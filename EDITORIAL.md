# Editorial manual

## 1. Purpose and authority

**1.1 What the site is.** This site publishes dated notes on science, technology, sport, business and public affairs. A post records what happened, how it was measured, and what remains unsettled, for a reader who wants the mechanism and not the announcement. The archive is a record rather than a service: there is no live feed, no duty to cover a subject because others are covering it, and no post that exists only to mark that something occurred.

**1.2 How the manual binds.** The manual governs form; the primary record governs fact. Where a clause here conflicts with the official usage of a body named in §13, that body's usage prevails for the name it owns, and this manual prevails everywhere else. Clauses carrying a numeral are checked mechanically before release; the remainder are editorial judgement and are checked by reading. A batch is not released until both kinds have been applied.

**1.3 Standing of the archive.** Rules bind new writing, and they bind every batch dated from 2022 onwards. A slug may be revised, and the address it replaces is retired rather than redirected.

**1.4 How examples are set.** Every example in this manual takes one of two forms, and a clause is never illustrated by paraphrase. A form the site uses is given alone, in monospace. A pair gives the rejected form first, then an arrow, then the accepted form, as `analyze → analyse`. Where a clause calls for more than one example, they are set as a list of at most five entries, one per line, each opened by a short italic label naming what it demonstrates; the label and the example are separated by a colon.

## 2. Selection

**2.1 The primary record.** Substance comes from the paper, the filing, the statute, the dataset, the transcript, or the institution's own statement. Journalism is a lead to that record and never the record itself, so a figure that appears only in a report is either traced to its origin or left out of the sentence.

**2.2 Saturation and angle.** A subject already carried by wires, papers, broadcasters and trade outlets is not disqualified, but it must be told at a depth or from an angle the coverage did not take, and the first two paragraphs must show that difference: the interval, the design, the cost, the mechanism, the confounder. Where a post would only restate what has been read everywhere, the slot is spent elsewhere. Coverage in any language saturates a subject equally; a result is saturated once several outlets have run it.

**2.3 Attribution and quotation.** A fact taken from a report is attributed in the sentence to the outlet that published it. Borrowed wording is kept under a sentence in length and set in single quotes. A news outlet never serves as the citation for a scientific result; §11 governs that form.

**2.4 Prohibitions.** A post does not reproduce a table, figure or screenshot from a report or a paper; where an image carries the argument it is redrawn from the primary data or omitted. It does not present an inference as a finding, and it does not forecast.

## 3. A month as a set

Each month is planned as a set. Day-by-day accumulation produces a pile with no shape, and the shape is what makes an archive readable later.

**3.1 Volume.** A month carries between **63 and 69** posts. The figure is chosen once for that month and then held; adjacent months take different figures, because a run of identical totals reads as a quota. Batches are dated across the month, so that no month opens with a crowd and closes with a pair, and new material never stacks on the last three days.

**3.2 Kinds.** A month leans away from political news and towards science, technology and the paper digest.

| Kind | Posts per month |
| --- | --- |
| Paper digests | at least 12, and the rest of the month |
| Technology | 8 to 10 |
| Sport | 3 to 4 |
| Business and economy | 4 to 6 |
| Culture and society | 2 to 3 |
| Essays | 2 to 5 |
| News, politics included, written as case notes | 3 to 4 |

Every row but the digest is a floor for the mix: it guarantees that the kind appears in proportion without capping it, and the digest row absorbs the remainder, so a month of 63 carries about 30 digests. Read as ceilings, the bands would allow at most about 44 posts in a month, which is below the volume 3.1 requires, and the archive has never been read that way: every month of 2026 carries 26 to 37 science posts and 4 to 6 essays, which is why the essay band runs from 2 to 5 here rather than from 1 to 3.

A digest states what was done, how it was measured, what the authors claim and what they leave open. It is the highest-value form here, it ages better than news, it can be sourced in bulk and dated honestly, and it therefore carries the largest share; a month that needs volume is met with digests rather than with commentary.

Essays stay scarce: at most one or two in a batch of twenty, and never the bulk of one. A piece that is neither report nor digest must be able to say what it adds.

**3.3 Politics as a case note.** A political post is written for political science or area studies. The event is the occasion; the substance is the institution, the incentive, the rule, or the bureaucracy that produced the outcome. It offers no editorial judgement and takes no side, including by implication; it aims neither praise nor blame at named figures and parties; it does not extend into adjacent or sensitive geopolitical disputes; it keeps to factual synthesis and institutional analysis, states the limits of what is known, and labels an inference as an inference.

## 4. Architecture of a post

**4.1 Length and paragraphs.** Five to seven paragraphs, six by default; **480 to 625 words** in the body. Five suits a compact result with few caveats; seven where the method or the background needs a movement of its own. Four or fewer is thin, eight or more is swollen, and a post that appears to need either is reorganised rather than stretched. A paragraph of 75 to 100 words is the working unit, and six of them land near the floor. Padding is never the remedy for a short draft: a body under 480 words is unfinished, not terse. The upper bound is soft, since a dense result occasionally earns the room.

**4.2 Movements.** Six, in their usual order: the situation before the result and why it was unresolved; what was done, with the journal and the date placed wherever that paragraph wants them; the central number with its interval or its comparison; the mechanism or the design that makes the finding credible; what the result does not show; and what would settle the question next or alter practice. The order may flex; the omissions may not. A digest without a limitations movement reads as publicity, and a digest without numbers reads as an abstract rewritten.

**4.3 The body.** No markdown headings, no bold, no bullet lists standing in for prose, no images unless the post is about the image, no links.

## 5. Sentences and paragraphs

**5.1 The definite article.** No more than **two paragraphs in six** open on `The`, and a month is not allowed to lean on it either. Where a paragraph would naturally begin with a definite subject, front the predicate, the result or the circumstance instead.

* *Fronted predicate*: `Supply is the difficult half`
* *Fronted complement*: `Worth naming are the limits`
* *Fronted nominal clause*: `Where the mission shows its character is the engineering`

Numbered scaffolding is a list in disguise: either number the items openly or drop the frame.

**5.2 Variety of openers.** Paragraph openings should differ in shape within a post. A fronted adverbial or prepositional phrase, a subordinate clause, a participle, an appositive, a comparative and, once or twice, a short declarative that acts as a beat: these are the available shapes, and a post that uses three of them is doing well. Two openers of one shape is the fault: two time phrases, two `Because` clauses, two clefts, two participle openings. An opener lengthened by a clause that carries no information is worse than the plain sentence it displaced. This clause governs new writing, and it carries the same weight in a revision of an older post.

* *Fronted time phrase*: `On the second night of the dome, the hospitals filled`
* *Fronted prepositional phrase*: `For a foundry that is sold out, the risk is demand`
* *Subordinate clause*: `When the committee met again, the forecast had changed`
* *Participle*: `Seen from the floor, the queue looked orderly`
* *Comparative, set against the beat*: `Four titles in six years, and the comparison follows`

**5.3 Habits on ration.** `rather than` appears about once in a post; it is a serviceable phrase that becomes a tic when every contrast reaches for it. `not ... but ...` is used only where the contrast is the point. Consecutive paragraphs do not open on the same grammatical subject, and a batch does not share a single sentence rhythm from first post to last.

**5.4 Rewriting.** A revised sentence is read against the one it replaces: the clause carrying the number, the caveat or the attribution must survive the revision. Bulk edits are verified by comparing word counts per changed line before anything is pushed.

## 6. Voice

**6.1 Register.** Formal, declarative, unexcited. The model is a departmental seminar note rather than a newsletter: statements are complete, verbs are active where the actor is known, and the tense is the present or the simple past unless the sequence requires otherwise. The first person appears where a judgement is genuinely the author's, and then once.

**6.2 Marks of machine prose.** A short list covers most of what damages a page. The triad that exists for rhythm rather than for content; the negated frame that announces what a story is not before saying what it is; the rhetorical question answered in the following clause; the closing sentence that restates the post; the verb that inflates a plain one; the metaphor imported from another domain; the empty intensifier standing where a number belongs. One instance in a paragraph is a lapse; three in a post is a failed draft.

* *Inflated verb*: `delve → examine`, `leverage → use`, `showcase → show`
* *Emptied intensifier*: `crucial → necessary`, `pivotal → decisive`, `significant → the figure and its interval`

**6.3 Honesty about certainty.** Hedging is reserved for what is genuinely unsettled: the interval, the unreplicated result, the estimate built on an assumption. Confidence tracks evidence, so a claim measured once is written as measured once, and a claim repeated by everyone is still written with its source.

## 7. Front matter and filenames

The filename is `<YYYY-MM-DD>-<slug>.md` and the key order is fixed:

```
---
slug: "the-slug"
title: "The title as displayed"
date: "2026-06-24T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "One plain sentence, no TeX, readable without the body."
---
```

* `slug`: lower-case ASCII, hyphenated, no date. Titles and slugs may both be revised; a slug that no longer names the post is changed rather than kept for the link, and the address it replaces is retired rather than redirected.
* `title`: roman text, no markdown italic, no formula notation.
* `date`: ISO 8601 with the `+08:00` offset, to the minute, varied across the working day.
* `categories`: exactly one, unquoted inside square brackets, from the nine listed below. No tags.
* `description`: one sentence of plain prose. No TeX, no backslash, no caret, no underscore notation, no abbreviated formula that a reader would have to decode. A description that needs notation is the wrong description; write the compound or the quantity in words. Italics are carried in markdown asterisks exactly as the body carries them (§9): journal titles, gene symbols and Latin binomials are italic; article titles, protein names, strain designations and programme names are roman. The page renders the description as markdown before it is displayed and strips the markup for the meta tags, so the asterisks cost nothing in a search preview. A description with a journal name set in roman is the commonest fault in this field.

The description is a sentence of narrative, not a citation. It says what happened; a journal is named inside that sentence, in italics, and never as an opening formula with a date attached.

* *Journal italic, as it should be written*: `A tantalum base layer lifts transmon lifetimes into the millisecond range, the group reports in *Nature*.`
* *Citation formula, rejected*: `In *Nature*, 13 November 2025, the group reports millisecond transmon lifetimes.`
* *Gene italic beside a roman protein*: `Loss-of-function variants in *ABCA7* leave the transporter missing ...`
* *Journal in roman, rejected*: `... the group reports in Nature.`
* *Notation smuggled into a description, rejected*: `... raises the coefficient above 10^{-9} ...` → `... raises the coefficient above one part in a billion ...`
* *The nine categories*: `news`, `science`, `economy`, `business`, `technology`, `essay`, `sport`, `culture`, `society`
* *A category as it appears in the file*: `categories: [science]`
* *A rejected form*: `categories: ["science", "news"]`

## 8. Titles

A title names the finding, the object or the turn, and it is read in a list of thirty others. Plain noun phrases, verb phrases and clauses do the work. Titles opening on `A`, `An` or `The` are limited rather than banned, and the `A/The [noun] that [verb]` shape is avoided because it reads as a catalogue entry.

A title is roman from the first word to the last. Anything the body would have to italicize — a journal title, a gene symbol, a Latin binomial, a variable, an algebraic formula — is kept out of the title rather than set in a form that cannot carry it. A formula in a title is a sign that the title is naming the notation instead of the finding.

* *Verb clause*: `Bicarbonate does not save lives in acidotic shock`
* *Subject and process*: `Warming turns wetlands from a sink into a source`
* *Colon, object and angle*: `Optical links in orbit: why satellites stopped calling home`
* *Catalogue shape to avoid*: `The paper that rewrote the field`
* *Variable kept out of the title, rejected*: `Six-sevenths of n colours suffice` → `Six-sevenths as many colours as vertices suffice`

A colon title is welcome where it carries both object and angle, and colon titles are capped at **a fifth of a month's posts**; the ceiling is a ceiling and not a target. Mechanical uniformity across a batch is a defect even where every individual title passes.

## 9. Italic and roman

**9.1 Journals.** The whole title is italic, including the definite article where the journal carries one in its official name. The `The` stays capitalized and inside the italics even mid-sentence, because it belongs to the name; a capitalized `The` outside the italics is the mark of a broken tag, and italicizing the word obliges it to keep the capital. Journals without an article take none, and no `The` is added that the masthead does not carry. Publishers, databases and preprint servers are not italicized as journals. Volume, issue, page and article numbers stay roman and outside the italics; article titles stay roman and, when quoted, sit in single quotes.

* *Article inside the italics*: *The Lancet*, *The New England Journal of Medicine*, *The BMJ*
* *Article outside the italics, lower case, rejected*: `the Lancet` → *The Lancet*
* *Journals without an article*: *Nature*, *Science*, *Cell*, *PNAS*, *JAMA*
* *Article title in roman, quoted*: 'Trends in wetland methane, 2000 to 2024'

**9.2 Genes and proteins.** Gene symbols are italic roman capitals without spaces, and mouse-style mixed case keeps its case. A substitution following a symbol leaves the symbol italic and the substitution roman. Protein names, complexes and families are roman. A superscript on a protein goes through TeX (§10).

* *Gene symbols*: *KRAS*, *TP53*, *FEM1B*, *CUL2*
* *Substitution*: *KRAS* G12C
* *Protein, roman, superscript in TeX*: `\(\mathrm{CUL2}^{\mathrm{FEM1B}}\)`
* *Protein family, roman*: mitofusin 1, p53, the BEX proteins

**9.3 Organisms.** Latin binomials and trinomials are italic, genus capitalized and species lower case, abbreviated after first mention. Higher taxa, strains, serovars and author designations stay roman. Virus species follow the italics of the ICTV code; virus names in ordinary prose stay roman.

* *First mention*: *Escherichia coli*
* *After the first mention*: *E. coli*
* *Strain, roman*: *E. coli* K-12
* *Higher taxon, roman*: Diptera

## 10. Notation

Anything carrying a subscript, a superscript, scientific notation or an index is set in TeX rather than typed with underscores, carets or spelled-out powers. Plain numbers, percentages and units in running text take no TeX and should not be given it.

Within TeX, single Latin and Greek letters are italic variables and are written bare; names of functions, units, acronyms and proteins are upright, and so are a differential and `pH`. A subscript that is a letter is italic; a subscript that is a word or a numeral is upright.

* *Variable, bare italic*: `\(a_\mu\)`
* *Upright formula name*: `\(\mathrm{PI}(3,4)P_2\)`
* *Scientific notation*: `\(1.16\times10^{-11}\)`
* *Upright subscript*: `\(x_{\mathrm{max}}\)`
* *A rejected spelling of the same formula*: `a_mu = 1.16 times ten to the minus eleven`

The chain runs from `hugo.toml`, which passes `\(` and `$$` through untouched, to `static/assets/css/math.css`, which maps every KaTeX glyph class onto Computer Modern Serif and restores italics on `.mathnormal` and `.textit`, to `static/assets/js/math.js`, which renders the blocks at load. Body text and mathematics share one family, so a variable stays slanted while a digit or a `\mathrm` word stays roman, and a rule that flattened either would be a regression. A post carrying formulas is not finished until the rendered page has been opened and the formula blocks counted.

## 11. Citations

Papers are cited by **journal and date only**. Running text carries no volume, issue, article number or page range, in any typography. Journal and date are enough for a reader to find the paper; the numbers only burden a sentence about the result. Do not include DOIs. Where a result is quoted from a preprint or a conference abstract, that is said plainly, since the journal-date form is not a licence to imply review that has not happened.

A venue is placed where the sentence needs it, and the place differs from post to post. It may arrive with the result as the result is first stated, ride on the authors in a subordinate clause, take the subject position itself, or wait for a later paragraph where what the journal accepted tells the reader how to weigh the claim. Four posts that each end on `was published in *X*` are the habit this clause exists to break; the closing form is allowed once in a batch and never as the default. Consecutive posts do not close the same way.

* *Result stated, venue in apposition*: `The group reports in *Nature* that the gap widens ...`
* *Venue as subject*: `*Science* carries the result this week ...`
* *Venue inside a condition*: `Work published in *Cell* in June puts the figure at ...`
* *Attribution trailing the finding*: `..., the authors write in a preprint posted on 2 August 2024`
* *The same closing sentence in every post, rejected*: four posts ending `was published in *X*`

## 12. Orthography

**12.1 Spelling families.** The `-ize` form is standard for the `-ise` family, and the `-yse` form is standard for the `-yse` family. The `-ise` form survives inside proper names whose own English name uses it, and the test is the body's own English name rather than house style. Vocabulary is British throughout.

* *`-ize`, accepted*: organize, recognize, criticize, summarize, standardize, prioritize, utilize, characterize, normalize, specialize, authorize
* *`-yse`, accepted*: analyse, analysed, analysing, analyser, catalyse, paralysed, electrolyser
* *Rejected spellings*: `recognise → recognize`, `analyze → analyse`, `organisation → organization`, a replacement that applies in prose only and never inside a proper name
* *Proper names that keep `-ise`*: *European Cancer Organisation*, *Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention*, *Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development*
* *British vocabulary*: `toward → towards`, `percent → per cent`, `program → programme`, `license as a noun → licence`, `defense → defence`, `story of a building → storey`

**12.2 Quotation and dashes.** Quotation marks are straight in the source, because the display layer curls them; the source never carries a curly apostrophe or a curly quote. Outer quotes are single and inner quotes double. Body prose carries no em dash and no en dash. Hyphens serve compounds, ranges and scores, and an en dash inside a proper name is written with a hyphen.

* *Outer single, inner double*: `'He called it "the last mile"'`
* *Rejected dash, one sentence instead*: `two teams [em dash] one city → two teams, one city`
* *Hyphen in a score*: `3-1`
* *En dash in a name, written with a hyphen*: `Snijders Blok-Campeau`

**12.3 Numerals, symbols and units.** Four-digit numerals and above take a comma every three digits, with years and code-like identifiers excepted. One to nine are words in ordinary prose and figures in measurements. Statistics are written with an italic capital *P*, and a sample size with an italic lower-case *n*. Units take a space and the correct case, and unit symbols take no plural; a unit written as a word pluralizes after a numeral and stays singular as a modifier. A unit built from two symbols keeps them apart, because a compound written as one token reads as an unfamiliar word rather than as two quantities multiplied, and the habit is especially strong in the amp-hour and watt-hour family.

* *Separated*: `1,044 h`, `12,500 tonnes`, `2,900,000 visitors`
* *Unseparated, rejected*: `1044 h → 1,044 h`
* *Year, exempt*: `1897`, `2025`
* *Thousand with a year's shape, rejected*: `1,997 → 1997`
* *Statistics, accepted*: *P*=0.03, *n*=3
* *Statistics, rejected*: `p value → *P* value`; a roman `P` → italic *P*
* *Charge capacity*: `mA h`, `A h`, never `mAh`
* *Energy*: `kW h`, `MW h`, `GW h`, `TW h`, never `kWh` or `MWh`
* *Power taken over the same hour*: `W h`, and `mW h` for the small draws

A unit as a modifier stays singular, which is why a panel is described as `a 1,000-nit panel` while the same figure stands alone as `1,000 nits`. Posts dating from before this clause carry the word forms `milliamp hours` and `kilowatt hours`; they are correct English and they are not symbols.

**12.4 Colons and semicolons, an advisory.** Both marks are punctuation of structure, and both are easy to overwork. A semicolon joining two clauses that would stand as sentences is usually a full stop the writer did not care to type, so one in a paragraph is worth a second look and two ask for a rewrite; a colon works best where it introduces a list, a definition or a consequence, and one per paragraph is enough, with the colon of a title counted separately under §8. The guidance is a preference rather than a gate: a post that reads well with a semicolon in each paragraph is published as it stands, and no batch is held back for punctuation of this kind.

## 13. Names

**13.1 The owner's spelling.** A proper noun is spelled as its owner spells it, checked against the owner's own English page before release. Where a body has an official English name, that name wins even where it breaks house rules. An official title carries its own punctuation the same way, as in the *Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act*. Institutions keep their own spelling, which is why the two examples below coexist in the same paragraph. A bulk spelling pass runs against a whitelist of proper nouns and its diff is read by hand, because a blanket substitution is how `Kennedy Space Centre` reaches the page.

* *All capitals*: NVIDIA, never `Nvidia`
* *Platform and company*: `bilibili` for the platform, `Bilibili Inc.` for the listed company, never `Bilibili` alone
* *Index operator*: `Nasdaq`, never `NASDAQ`
* *Companies and country*: `China's`, with the apostrophe; `Huawei`, `Tencent`, `Alibaba Group`, `TSMC`, `ASML`
* *Institutions, American spacing*: `Kennedy Space Center`; *British spelling, same paragraph*: `European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control`

**13.2 Naming bodies.** Where a discipline's own body has settled an English form, that form overrides British and American habit alike, and the outcome is not a drift towards American spelling since several bodies chose the British form. Enzymes follow IUBMB, gene and protein symbols follow HGNC or the relevant species database, viruses follow ICTV, astronomical designations follow the IAU, and organisms follow the nomenclature codes. Where no body has ruled, the question returns to §12 and British vocabulary decides.

* *Chemistry, IUPAC*: `sulfur`, `sulfate`, `aluminium`, `caesium`
* *Units, SI brochure*: `metre`, `litre`, `tonne`; a bare `ton` names two different non-metric units and never carries a figure
* *No authority, British vocabulary*: `orthopaedic`, because no body has fixed `orthopedic` and the dictionary does not decide

**13.3 Markets and eras.** Index names are proper nouns and take capitals on every word of the name. Where a piece turns on the index itself, the official long form is given at first mention and the trade short form afterwards. Names that a brand has restyled keep the form of their own era, and a post dated in an earlier era uses that era's form rather than retro-fitting today's capitals.

* *Index names*: `Shanghai Composite`, `Shenzhen Component`, `ChiNext Index`, `CSI 300 Index`, `STAR 50`, `Nasdaq Composite`, `Hang Seng Index`, `Hang Seng TECH Index`
* *Long forms at first mention*: `SSE Composite Index`, `SZSE Component Index`, `SSE Science and Technology Innovation Board 50 Index`, `CSI 300 Index`
* *The all-share measure, which is not the component*: `Shenzhen Composite`
* *Rejected*: `CSI300 → CSI 300 Index`, `Chinext → ChiNext Index`, `SHSE → SSE Composite Index`
* *The Spanish league, by era*: `La Liga` before 2015 → `LaLiga` from 2015 to 2023 → `LALIGA` from the June 2023 rebrand

## 14. Production

**14.1 Method.** For each batch: collect sources and hold a candidate list with journal, date and the figure the post will turn on, dropping a candidate whose figure cannot be verified; read the humanizer skill at `https://github.com/blader/humanizer/blob/main/SKILL.md` before drafting; write into `content/posts/<date>-<slug>.md` with the front matter order of §7; apply the checklist of 14.2 and repair the file rather than the checklist; build with `hugo --buildFuture --gc -d public` from the toolchain and run `python3 scripts/gen-json.py`, confirming that the search, featured and index counts move by exactly the number of posts added and that categories still report nine; open one or two rendered pages where a post carries formulas or unusual markup; commit `content/posts` and, where the change is technical, this manual and the static, layout or asset files, never the build output (`public/`, `_site/`, `vendor/`); push, wait for both workflows, then fetch each new URL once. A first failure followed by a success on the second attempt is a timeout; a persistent failure is a build fault to be fixed rather than retried.

Where a push is rejected, the branch is fetched and rebased before pushing again. Where a workflow fails, the content is corrected and a new commit is pushed; failed jobs are not re-run on the old tree. History is not rewritten by amendment or force.

**14.2 Checklist.** Form, per file: five to seven paragraphs and 480 to 625 words; at most two paragraphs opening on `The`; openers varied in shape, with no two of one shape and none padded; titles opening on a bare article kept within the limit, titles carrying nothing that would need italics, and colon titles within a fifth of the batch; one unquoted category from the nine, with the front matter keys in order and the description free of notation and set in the italics of §9, and with the venue not always closing the post.

Typography, per file: journal titles italic with their article inside the italics; gene symbols italic, proteins roman, binomials italic, higher taxa roman; symbols and indexed quantities in TeX with plain numbers left alone; four-digit numerals separated by commas, years and codes excepted; statistical symbols (`P` and `n`) italic; compound unit symbols spaced (`mA h`, `kW h`); `-ize` and `-yse` as in §12, with proper names keeping official spelling; straight quotes, singles outside and doubles inside, and no em or en dash in prose; `towards`, `per cent` and British vocabulary; house names as in §13.

Substance, per file: every figure traced to the primary record; borrowed facts attributed and borrowed wording short; the subject either unreported or told from an angle the coverage did not take, with the opening saying what the post adds; a limitations movement present; no reproduced figure or screenshot. Per batch: venues placed in at least three different positions across four posts.

Colons and semicolons fall under §12.4, which is advice and never grounds for rejection. Per batch: the month's total within 63 and 69 and different from its neighbours; dates spread; the mix of §3 holding with digests carrying the largest share; index counts moving by exactly the number added; every new URL answering 200 on the live site.

**14.3 The backlog.** The older archive carries standing defects: posts of four or fewer or eight or more paragraphs, posts below 480 words, titles opening on `A`, `An` or `The`, and paragraphs opening on `The` more than twice. They are a separate project, cleared in batches, with titles rewritten and slugs revised where the address misnames the post. New work must not add to the count.

Two slugs were published before §12 settled on `-ize` and therefore still carry `authorises` and `recognise`. Under §7 they may be revised along with their titles, descriptions and bodies, which already carry the house spelling.
