# Editorial rules

How posts are chosen and written. These rules apply to every batch added from September 2026 onwards.

## Current affairs are case notes, not a news feed

The site is not a rolling news service and will not grow a column for world affairs. There is no
"global politics" or "international briefing" section, and posts do not follow the news calendar
day by day.

When a post does touch on politics, it is written as a case note for political science or area
studies: the event is the pretext, and the substance is the framework. What is worth writing is
the institution, the mechanism, the incentive structure, the way a rule or a bureaucracy or an
electoral system produces an outcome. A post should be useful to a reader who wants to understand
how the machinery works, not to one who wants to know what happened this morning.

That framing carries constraints:

* no editorializing and no side-taking, including by implication;
* no lavish praise or blame aimed at named politicians or parties;
* no extension into adjacent sensitive geopolitical disputes;
* claims stay as factual synthesis plus institutional analysis, with the limits of what is known
  stated plainly;
* a post is not a forecast, and an inference is labelled as one.

## The mix

Across a full month of about forty-two posts, the balance leans away from political news and
towards science, technology, sport and paper digests.

| Kind | Rough share per month |
| --- | --- |
| Science and paper digests | 12 to 15 |
| Technology | 8 to 10 |
| Sport | 3 to 4 |
| Business and economy | 4 to 6 |
| Culture and society | 2 to 3 |
| Essays | 3 to 4 |
| News, politics included | 3 to 4, and written as case notes |

A digest is a reading note on a paper or a result: what was done, how it was measured, what the
authors claim and what they do not. It is the highest-value kind of post on this site and should
carry the largest single share.

## Standing style rules

The site conventions apply to all of the above: single category per post, no tags, no `<h2>` in
the body, ISO 8601 dates with `+08:00`, lowercase ASCII slugs, colon titles kept under a fifth of
a batch, British `towards`, `-ize` spelling, straight quotes with singles outside and doubles
inside, italic journal and book titles, and official spelling for proper nouns.

Market indexes. Index names are proper nouns and take capitals on every word that belongs to the
name: `Shanghai Composite`, `Shenzhen Component`, `ChiNext Index`, `CSI 300 Index`, `STAR 50`,
`Nasdaq Composite`, `Hang Seng Index`, and `Hang Seng TECH Index`, where the compiler writes
`TECH` in capitals. When a piece turns on the index itself, give the official long form at first
mention — `SSE Composite Index`, `SZSE Component Index`, `ChiNext Index`, `CSI 300 Index`,
`SSE Science and Technology Innovation Board 50 Index` — and the trade short form afterwards.
The Shenzhen composite and the Shenzhen component are two different measures, so `Shenzhen
Composite` is only correct for the all-share index and never for the 500-stock component. Write
no `CSI300`, no `Chinext`, and no `SHSE`; keep `index` lower case only when it is the common
noun rather than part of the name.

## Openings and titles

Two habits spoil a page of otherwise decent prose: every paragraph opening on "The", and every
headline built from "A something that does something".

Paragraphs. Keep the count of paragraphs whose first word is "The" to about a third of the
paragraphs in a post, and never let it dominate a month (the months of 2026-07 to 2026-11 were
brought down to between 24 and 35 per cent). When a paragraph would naturally start with a
definite subject, front the predicate, the result or the circumstance instead: `Supply is the
difficult half`, `Worth naming are the limits`, `Where the mission shows its character is the
engineering`, `Fourth comes the constraint that decides the outcome`. A run of "The first thing
is ... The second thing is ... The third thing is ..." is a list pretending to be prose; number
the items explicitly or drop the scaffolding.

Titles. Avoid the "A/The [noun] that [verb]" shape, which reads like a catalogue entry. Prefer a
plain noun phrase, a verb phrase, or a clause that names the finding. Colons are allowed and
welcome in moderation, since a colon title can carry both the object and the angle: `Optical
links in orbit: why satellites stopped calling home`. The ceiling is a fifth of a month's posts,
and it is a ceiling, not a target.

Grammar. Any rewritten sentence has to be checked against the sentence it replaces: the clause
that carried the number, the caveat or the attribution must survive the rewrite. Bulk edits are
verified by comparing word counts per changed line before anything is pushed.

Journal titles. Italicize the entire title, including the definite article when the journal
carries one in its official name: *The Lancet*, *The New England Journal of Medicine*, *The BMJ*,
*The Astrophysical Journal*, *The Cryosphere*. A journal's `The` stays capitalized and inside the
italics even mid-sentence, because it belongs to the name and not to the sentence; a capitalized
`The` sitting outside the italics is the sign that the markup is wrong, and italicizing the word
also obliges it to keep the capital. Journals without an article take none: *Nature*, *Science*,
*Cell*, *PNAS*, *JAMA*, *Physical Review Letters*, *Nature Medicine*, *The Lancet Oncology* and
*The Lancet Psychiatry* take the article their titles carry. Volume and page numbers stay roman
and outside the italics, and article titles stay roman, in single quotes.

Genes, proteins, organisms. Gene symbols are italic, roman capitals, no spaces: *KRAS*, *TP53*,
*BRCA1*, *FEM1B*, *CUL2*, *MFN1*, *OPA1*, *Serinc2* (mouse-style mixed case keeps its case). Protein
names are roman, including complexes and families: CUL2^FEM1B, mitofusin 1 and 2, OPA1 protein,
p53, the BEX proteins. A mutation written after a gene symbol leaves the symbol italic and the
substitution roman: *KRAS* G12C. Latin binomials and trinomials are italic, genus capitalized and
species lower case, abbreviated after the first mention: *Escherichia coli*, then *E. coli*;
*Homo sapiens*, *Homo longi*. Higher taxa, strain, serovar and author designations stay roman:
*E. coli* K-12, Diptera, *Salmonella enterica* serovar Typhimurium. Virus species follow the ICTV
italics, virus names in ordinary prose stay roman.

Notation. Anything with a subscript, a superscript or scientific notation is set in TeX rather
than typed with underscores, carets or spelled-out powers: \(\mathrm{PI}(3,4)P_2\),
\(\mathrm{CUL2}^{\mathrm{FEM1B}}\), \(a_\mu = 116\,592\,070.5(14.8)\times10^{-11}\),
\(1.16\times10^{-10}\). Single-letter variables and simple arithmetic belong in the same
treatment when they carry an index or a symbol; plain numbers, percentages and units in running
text do not.

Descriptions. The front-matter `description` is plain prose: no TeX, no carets or underscores, and
no formula-like name that would need them. Write the chemistry out in words (phosphatidylinositol
3,4-bisphosphate rather than the abbreviated lipid, a CUL2-based ubiquitin ligase rather than the
complex in superscript notation), and keep the sentence readable to someone who never sees the
body. TeX belongs to the body, where the markup renders.

Citations. Papers are cited by journal and date only: *Nature*, 6 May 2026; *The Lancet*, 2 May
2026. No volume, issue, article number or page range in running text, and no strings of the form
`Science 394, 6823`. Where a reader needs the paper itself, the date and the journal are enough to
find it, and the numbers only add noise to a sentence about the result.
