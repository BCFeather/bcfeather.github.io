The price of arabica coffee has been at or near records for two years, and the cause is a sequence of bad harvests in Brazil and Vietnam rather than anything that happened in a coffee shop. Brazil had a drought and then a frost, and the frost damaged trees that take three years to return to full production. Vietnam had a drought in the robusta belt, and robusta is the bean in the instant jar and the espresso blend. Two of the largest producers failed in the same window, and the price did what prices do.

The commodity is traded in dollars per pound, on a contract that a farmer in Minas Gerais has never seen. A roaster buying green beans in advance locks in a price and the hedge is part of the business model, which means the cost arrives at the consumer with a lag of six to twelve months. The lag is why the price of a flat white in a city café did not move when the news did, and why it is moving now.

The rest of the chain adds its own margins. Freight, insurance, the roaster's fuel, the paper cup, the rent of the shop and the wage of the person behind the counter. In a café the coffee itself is a small fraction of the price of the drink, which is why the bean price makes headlines and the drink price barely moves. A doubling of the bean price raises the drink by less than customers expect, and the shops raise it by more than the arithmetic requires because the rent and the wage moved too.

The producing side is where the pressure is hardest, and it is also the side with the least information, which is a durable feature of most commodity chains. Smallholders who sell to a cooperative at a price fixed weeks before the harvest do not capture the spike. The ones who sell at the spot price do, and the difference between the two determines whether the boom year pays for a new drying bed or goes to a trader's margin. The farm gate price is the number that determines whether the next harvest exists.

The climate is the part that will not go away. Coffee is grown in a band of altitude and temperature that is narrow, and the arabica varieties used in most of the world are sensitive to heat and to the timing of rain. The modelling suggests that the suitable area will move uphill and shrink, and that the shift is already visible in the lower farms of several countries, which have been abandoned or converted to something else.

What the price does is tell a story about the weather in two countries to a customer who is thinking about a meeting. The connection is real and it is invisible, and the only part of it a person can see is the number on the board behind the counter, which moved this month and will not move back for a while.
