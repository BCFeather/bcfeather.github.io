title: "The AI Act reaches its second deadline"
date: 2025-08-01 21:30:00 +0800
tags: [ai, regulation, europe, policy]
categories: [technology]
description: "On 2 August the European rules on general-purpose AI systems begin to bite, eight months after the first obligations and a year before most of the rest. What changes is mostly paperwork, and paperwork is how a law about models is actually applied."
---

The European regulation on artificial intelligence arrived in stages, and the second stage begins on 2 August. The first, which took effect in February, covered the prohibited practices and the general principles. This one reaches the general-purpose models, the large systems that are sold as a service and then adapted by everyone else. A year from now the obligations for high-risk applications arrive, and the timetable is designed so that each layer can be tested before the next one lands.

What changes on 2 August is mostly documentation. A provider of a general-purpose model must publish a summary of its training data, describe how it evaluates the model, maintain technical documentation for the regulator, and put in place a policy on copyright compliance. The heaviest obligation falls on the systems that the regulation calls systemic, which in practice means the largest few, and those must additionally assess and mitigate risks and report serious incidents.

The design of the regime is an admission that a law cannot inspect a model the way an inspector visits a factory. A model is weights and a training process, and the weights cannot be read. So the regulation governs the paperwork instead, on the theory that a firm that must describe its data and its evaluation has to know what it is doing, and that the description can be audited later. The theory is plausible and it has a known weakness, which is that documents can be written to satisfy an auditor without changing anything inside the system.

The copyright provision is the interesting one, and it was the subject of the longest argument during the drafting. Providers must publish a summary of the copyrighted material used for training, detailed enough for rightsholders to identify their work. The compromise is that the summary is a summary, and the level of detail is left to a template that the Commission is still writing. Lawyers on both sides have read the same sentence and drawn opposite conclusions, which is the normal state of copyright law in the decade of machine learning.

The enforcement machinery is thin. Each member state must designate a supervisory authority, several have not yet done so, and the coordination between them is a committee that meets periodically. A single market for models with a single regulator would have been simpler, and the settlement that produced the regulation was not aiming at simple.

The firms affected have responded in the way firms do. Several large providers published model cards before the deadline, under headings that look like the new requirements, and the cards are genuinely informative. Others have moved the models that they are least confident about out of the European market, which is a choice the regulation permits and does not discourage.

What the second deadline decides, in the end, is whether the paperwork becomes a language that everyone in the field speaks. If model documentation becomes a standard, the audit becomes cheap and the law has a chance. If every provider writes its own version, the regulator will be comparing documents that describe different things, and the eight months that follow will be spent harmonising templates.
