A summit in Paris produced a statement on artificial intelligence that most of the attending countries signed, and the two that declined were the United States and the United Kingdom. The meeting ran for two days at the Grand Palais, it gathered heads of government and the executives of the largest technology companies, and the document that closed it was a short list of intentions rather than a treaty.

The substance of the declaration is the part that the coverage skipped. It committed the signatories to an open and inclusive approach, to the development of the technology for the benefit of the many, and to a set of principles about the data and the safety. The verbs were soft and the list was long, which is the normal form of a communique that thirty countries can agree to.

The refusal is the interesting part. The American delegation argued that the text carried a regulatory implication that the new administration did not accept, and the British delegation said that the wording went further than the country could sign. The two positions were different and they pointed at the same question, which is whether a voluntary statement is a step towards a rule.

The summit also had a scientific track and a cultural one, and the parts that will matter in five years are probably there. The sessions on the evaluation of models, on the data that a model is trained on, and on the compute that a country needs to participate. The governance of the technology is being written in the technical committees rather than in the plenary hall.

The French hosts had their own agenda and they were open about it. The country has a research base and a set of model companies that are smaller than the American ones, and the argument that Europe should coordinate its compute and its rules is the argument that a middle-sized power makes when it cannot compete on scale.

The practical outcome of a summit like this is usually a set of processes. A working group, a review that will report next year, a fund that will be announced later. The value is the calendar that the process creates, because a government that has promised to report has a reason to collect the data.

What the two refusals show is a split that is now structural. The countries that want a rule before the technology spreads and the countries that want the technology to spread before a rule is written are both sincere, and the gap between them is not bridgeable by a communique. The next meeting will produce a longer list and the same split, and the list is the part that the process can deliver.

The summit also changed the way the subject is discussed in public. The sessions on safety, which dominated the previous meetings, were a smaller share of the programme this year, and the sessions on jobs, on health and on the use of the technology in public services were a larger one. That shift is a statement about the mood of the governments that attended, and it will be reflected in the communiques that follow.
