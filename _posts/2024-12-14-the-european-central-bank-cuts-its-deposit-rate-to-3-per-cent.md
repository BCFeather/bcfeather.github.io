---
title: "The European Central Bank cuts its deposit rate to 3 per cent"
date: 2024-12-14 21:00:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "The ECB lowered its key rates by a quarter of a point on 12 December, the fourth cut since June, and revised its growth forecast down as the euro area's recovery stalled."
---

European Central Bank lowered its three key rates by a quarter of a percentage point at its meeting on the twelfth of December, taking the deposit rate to 3 per cent. Main refinancing rate moved to 3.15 per cent and the marginal lending rate to 3.40. Decision was the fourth cut of the year, following reductions in June, September and October, and it was taken unanimously. Statement that accompanied it dropped the language about keeping policy restrictive for as long as necessary, which the market read as a signal that further cuts are coming.

Growth was the reason and the statement was explicit about it. Bank's staff revised its forecast for the euro area down to 0.7 per cent for the year, from 0.8, and to 1.1 per cent for 2025, and it said the risks to growth were tilted to the downside. Manufacturing has been in contraction for two years, the German industrial base is shrinking, and the surveys of purchasing managers showed services slowing in the autumn after holding up through the summer. Inflation, meanwhile, came in at 2.2 per cent in November, close to the target and below it on the measure that excludes energy and food.

Argument inside the governing council was about speed. Some members had argued for a half-point cut, given the weakness of the data, and the accounts of the meeting later showed that a minority had supported it. Others wanted to wait for the wage round in the first quarter of 2025, on the view that services inflation is still running above target and that a larger cut would be reversed if wages came in high. Compromise was a quarter point with guidance that leaned towards more easing.

Currency and bond markets took the decision as expected. Euro was little changed against the dollar, the German two-year yield fell to its lowest in two months, and the spread between Italian and German debt narrowed. European equities were mixed, with banks falling on the prospect of lower margins and utilities rising. Markets have priced in another two cuts during 2025, which would take the deposit rate to about 2.5 per cent by the summer.

Politics of the decision is a constraint that the bank does not talk about directly. Euro area governments are running deficits above the rules in several cases, and the fiscal framework has been loosened to accommodate defence spending. Rate cuts lower the cost of servicing that debt, which gives finance ministries an incentive to wait for the bank rather than to consolidate. Bank's independence is written into its mandate, and its president has been careful to say that decisions are taken on inflation and not on the budgetary needs of member states.

Difficulty ahead is structural rather than cyclical. Energy prices are higher than in the United States, the industrial base faces competition from China in the segments where European firms are strongest, and the demographic outlook limits the growth that monetary policy can produce. Cutting rates from 3 per cent to 2.5 per cent will help the sectors that borrow at floating rates and will do little for the ones that are closing plants. Bank's forecast assumes an investment recovery next year, and the assumption rests on demand from abroad that has not yet appeared.
