---
title: "Cyclone Chido devastates Mayotte and leaves thousands without shelter"
date: 2024-12-15 20:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Cyclone Chido struck Mayotte on 14 December with gusts above 225 kilometres an hour, the strongest storm to reach the French territory in at least ninety years, and left about 100,000 people displaced."
---

Cyclone Chido hit Mayotte on the fourteenth of December with sustained winds above 200 kilometres an hour and gusts that reached 226 at the airport on the island of Pamandzi. Meteorologists at Meteo-France described it as the strongest storm to strike the territory in at least ninety years, and the rainfall that came with it, 176 millimetres in twelve hours, turned the hillsides into slides of red mud. Islands were cut off, the airport closed and the power grid went down across most of the two main islands.

Damage was worst where the housing was worst. Much of Mayotte's population lives in informal settlements of tin and timber, and those neighbourhoods were described by officials as having been flattened. Prefecture reported 7,816 buildings damaged within the first days, 15,000 people without electricity, and about 100,000 displaced into seventy emergency centres, many of them schools and gymnasiums. Water and food distribution began immediately and was immediately overwhelmed.

Death toll was uncertain from the start and stayed that way. Official count reached 39 by the end of the month, and the authorities said repeatedly that the true number was much higher, because the informal settlements were not registered and because many of the dead were buried by families before they could be counted. French Red Cross and the prefecture both said the comparison with earlier storms on the island suggested a toll in the hundreds. Forty people were injured seriously enough to need evacuation to hospitals in France and Reunion.

Response was large and slow in equal measure. President Emmanuel Macron flew to the territory on the nineteenth of December and promised reconstruction within two years, and the government declared a state of natural disaster, which releases insurance and emergency funds. Ships and aircraft from Reunion brought water, generators and medical teams, and the army built field hospitals. Residents complained in the first week that little had reached them, and the prefect acknowledged that the logistics had been designed for a smaller event.

Place of Mayotte in French politics made the storm a political event as well. It is the poorest department of the republic, its population is mostly Comorian in origin, and its legal status has been contested for decades between those who regard it as French territory and those who regard it as occupied. Cyclone exposed the consequences of that arrangement: the housing that was destroyed was built because the state never provided enough of its own, and the island's hospital, the only one with a full surgical service in the region, was damaged in the storm.

Longer reckoning lies in the climate. Indian Ocean cyclones have become more intense as sea surface temperatures rise, and Chido intensified from a tropical storm to a category four equivalent in under a day before it reached the Comoros and then Mayotte. Reconstruction will be planned against the next storm rather than the last one, and the government's two-year timetable will be measured against an island where building regulations were rarely enforced and where the population has doubled in twenty years.
