---
title: "The jubilee marked seventy years of one reign"
date: 2022-06-02 23:10:00 +0800
categories: [culture]
description: "Four days of pageantry in London measured a reign that began with the accession in February 1952 and has outlasted fifteen prime ministers, the empire and most of the certainties the ceremony was built to display."
---

On the morning of 2 June, soldiers in bearskins and scarlet marched down the Mall while the Queen, ninety-six years old and standing on the balcony of Buckingham Palace, took a salute that her mother had taken before her. The occasion was the platinum jubilee, seventy years since she became sovereign on the death of her father at Sandringham in February 1952. Crowds along the route were thick from Admiralty Arch to the palace gates, and the weather, for once, held.

Four days followed in a deliberate order. Trooping the Colour opened the programme, a service of thanksgiving at St Paul's came next, then a concert in front of the palace and a pageant of streets and costumes on the Sunday. Street parties were held in thousands of towns and villages, many of them organized by people who had never attended one before and who found the template on a council website. Tea towels, biscuit tins and a commemorative coin appeared in quantities that recalled the souvenirs of 1953.

Numbers attached to the reign are the kind that courtiers like. Fifteen prime ministers have served under her, from Churchill to Boris Johnson, and the Commonwealth she inherited as a group of eight countries now counts fifty-four. Her coronation in June 1953 was watched by an estimated three quarters of the adults in Britain, most of them on borrowed sets, and the broadcast was the first to be carried around the world on the day it happened. The reign has also covered the end of the empire, the devolution settlements of 1998 and the departure from the union in 2020.

Politics sat underneath the bunting throughout. Republican sentiment in Britain is small but visible, and a handful of campaign groups used the week to argue that an inherited head of state is an anachronism. The cost of the celebrations, borne partly by the taxpayer, was questioned in the press, and the palace's refusal to say how the Queen's mobility was managed fed a familiar appetite for detail about her health. Newspapers that supported the monarchy printed supplements of photographs, and those that did not printed columns about the bill.

Personal losses shaped the mood. Prince Philip died in April 2021, and the Queen sat alone at his funeral because of the pandemic rules then in force. She appeared on the balcony twice during the jubilee, once with the working members of her family and once with three generations of heirs, and she stood rather than sat for the second appearance, which the newspapers read as a statement.

A jubilee measures continuity, and continuity is what the ceremony was for. The monarchy's political power has shrunk to a weekly conversation and a signature, and its public role has grown into something closer to a national habit: an institution that supplies dates, honours and a fixed point in the calendar. Whether that habit survives its current holder is the question the week raised without answering. A successor would inherit a smaller Commonwealth and a union that no longer holds together, which is the arithmetic the week invited.
