The countdown reached zero at 1.47 in the morning local time, and the rocket rose slowly, then faster than anything that had left the same pad before. It carried no crew, only a capsule called Orion, an attached service module and a great deal of instrumentation, and this was the first flight of a launch vehicle that had been in development for more than ten years. The sound reached the press site a few seconds after the light did, and the ground shook hard enough to rattle the windows of the viewing stands. Photographers on the roof had set their cameras hours earlier, and their long exposures caught the arc of the climb.

Delays had already cost the teams most of the autumn. Hydrogen leaks, a faulty sensor, a hurricane that came up the coast of Florida and a second storm had each pushed the date back by a week or more. The rocket had stood on the pad for months, and the crews had come to treat each scrub as a test of patience rather than of engineering. Loading the tanks required a careful sequence of cooling and pressure, and every attempt taught the launch team something the manuals had described less clearly than the practice did.

Two minutes into the flight the solid boosters separated, and the core stage continued to burn before it too fell away. The upper stage then performed the manoeuvre that put the capsule on a path to the Moon, and the spacecraft spent the following days in a slow outward spiral. Controllers followed the trajectory through a network of ground stations and tracking satellites, and the capsule's solar array unfolded on schedule. Pictures from the rocket's own cameras showed the coastline shrinking, then the curve of the earth, then black sky.

Planners chose a route that Apollo never used. Rather than repeating the free-return trajectory of the earlier programme, the mission sent Orion into a distant retrograde orbit, a path that loops around the Moon against its direction of rotation. That orbit is stable, it needs little fuel to hold, and it gives engineers a place to test the life-support and navigation systems for a long time. The capsule also carried mannequins fitted with sensors, so that the radiation inside could be measured for a future crew.

The capsule flew about one point four million miles before it returned, further than any spacecraft built for people had travelled. Its heat shield met the atmosphere at nearly twenty-five thousand miles an hour on the way home, close to Mach thirty-two, and the parachutes delivered it to the Pacific on the eleventh of December. Recovery crews had rehearsed the procedure for years, and the capsule came up on the deck of a ship that had sailed from San Diego to meet it.

A programme of this kind carries a date and a great deal of argument. A crewed fly-by is planned for the following year, and a landing on the lunar surface for a date that keeps moving. What the empty flight established is narrower and firmer. The rocket works, the capsule survives, and the ground teams can run a mission that lasts twenty-five days.
