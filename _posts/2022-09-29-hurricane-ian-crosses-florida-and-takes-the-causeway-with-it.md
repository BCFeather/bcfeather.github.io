---
title: "Hurricane Ian crosses Florida and takes the causeway with it"
date: 2022-09-29 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "A category four storm comes ashore near Fort Myers, cuts Sanibel off from the mainland and leaves millions without power."
---

Hurricane Ian came ashore on Wednesday afternoon near Cayo Costa, on the south-west coast of Florida, with winds of about a hundred and fifty miles an hour. The storm had crossed Cuba the day before and knocked out the island's electricity grid, and it strengthened over the warm water of the Gulf before it turned east. Storm surge of up to twelve feet pushed into the bays behind the barrier islands, and the water reached the second floors of houses that had stood through earlier storms. The centre of the storm moved inland overnight and weakened as it went.

Sanibel Island lost its connection to the mainland. The causeway that carried the only road was breached in three places, and the photographs taken the next morning showed the bridge decks hanging over water. The island's authorities said they could not reach residents by vehicle, and the rescue began with boats and helicopters. Fort Myers Beach, which had been built along a strip of sand, was reported to have lost most of its front row of buildings.

Power went out for about two and a half million customers, and the utility said that some of the substations would need to be rebuilt rather than repaired. Water systems failed in several counties, and the hospitals that remained open ran on generators. The governor declared a state of emergency before the landfall and asked for federal help, and the president approved the declaration the same day. The airports in Tampa and Orlando closed, and about two thousand flights were cancelled.

The forecast had been accurate about the track and about the intensity, and the warnings went out two days ahead. The evacuation orders covered the barrier islands and the low-lying districts, and the counties that issued them said that most residents had left. The people who stayed described the water rising faster than they had expected, and the rescue services took them off roofs in boats through the night. The state's building code, which was tightened after Hurricane Andrew in 1992, is credited with keeping newer structures standing.

The cost will be counted for months. Insurers have begun to estimate the losses at tens of billions of dollars, and the federal flood programme, which covers most of the homes in the affected areas, will be paying claims for years. The storm also arrived in the middle of a property insurance crisis in the state, where several companies had already failed and the premiums had doubled. Rebuilding will be slower than usual because the materials and the labour are more expensive than they were two years ago.

What the storm leaves is a coastline that has to be rebuilt and a question about how often it can be. Florida's population has grown by a fifth in a decade, and much of that growth is on land that is a few feet above the sea. The engineers who wrote the building code have said it works for wind and cannot work for water. The next storm will arrive with a different name and the same geography, and the argument about what to rebuild will begin before the debris is cleared.
