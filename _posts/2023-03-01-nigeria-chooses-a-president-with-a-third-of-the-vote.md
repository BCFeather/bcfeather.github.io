---
title: "Nigeria chooses a president with a third of the vote"
date: 2023-03-01 22:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The electoral commission declared Bola Tinubu the winner of Nigeria's presidential election on 1 March 2023, with 8,794,726 votes against 6,984,520 for the nearest challenger, after a count that took five days."
---

Four days after the polls closed, the chairman of the Independent National Electoral Commission read out the result at a press conference in Abuja, and the number that mattered was 8,794,726. That was the vote for Bola Tinubu of the All Progressives Congress, or about 36 per cent of the total, and it was enough to avoid a run-off in a system that requires only a plurality. Atiku Abubakar of the People's Democratic Party took 6,984,520 votes and Peter Obi of the Labour Party took 6,101,533, and the three of them accounted for more than ninety per cent of the ballots cast.

Tinubu also satisfied the second condition in the constitution, which requires a quarter of the vote in at least two-thirds of the states. He carried the north-west and the south-west, where he had been governor of Lagos for eight years, and he held enough of the north-central to cross the threshold. Obi won every state in the south-east and the south-south, and his margins there were large, which made the geography of the result the most discussed map of the year.

The count itself became the story before the numbers did. The commission had promised to upload results from each polling unit, and the system that was supposed to do it failed often enough that the parties spent the week issuing their own tallies. Observers from the Commonwealth and from the African Union described a competitive election with a flawed administration, and the opposition described a flawed election with a stolen result.

Turnout was the quiet number in the declaration. Fewer than a third of the registered voters cast a ballot, which in a country of more than ninety million registered electors meant that the winner's mandate rested on a fraction of the electorate. The campaigns had spent the previous year arguing that the young voters who had registered in large numbers would turn out for Obi, and the turnout suggested that they had registered and then stayed home. The commission's figures were disputed by the parties and accepted by the observers, which is the usual condition of a Nigerian result.

The economy that the new president inherited was in poor condition. Inflation was above twenty per cent, the currency had lost most of its value in the previous year, and the previous government had spent its last months managing a currency redesign that left shops without cash. Fuel subsidies that had cost billions a year were due to be removed, and the removal was the first argument that the new administration would have with the streets.

Legal challenges began within hours, as they had after every election since 1999. Obi's campaign said it would prove that the commission had violated its own procedures, and Atiku's party said the results from several states would have to be examined again. The tribunal had six months to hear the petitions, and the swearing-in was scheduled for 29 May, which meant that the country would have a president and a court case at the same time.
