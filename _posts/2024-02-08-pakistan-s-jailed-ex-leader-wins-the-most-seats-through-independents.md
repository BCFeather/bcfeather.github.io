---
title: "Pakistan's jailed ex-leader wins the most seats through independents"
date: 2024-02-08 22:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Pakistan voted on 8 February in an election held with Imran Khan in prison and his party stripped of its symbol, and candidates backed by him took 101 of the 336 seats in the National Assembly, leaving no party with a majority."
---

Pakistan held its general election on the eighth of February with the country's most popular politician in prison and his party denied the right to use its own election symbol. The count that followed, completed and published by the Election Commission on the eleventh, gave independent candidates a hundred and one seats in a three-hundred-and-thirty-six-seat National Assembly, and most of those independents were backed by Imran Khan's Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf. Pakistan Muslim League (Nawaz) took seventy-five, Pakistan People's Party fifty-four, and Muttahida Qaumi Movement seventeen. None of them had the hundred and thirty-four seats needed to govern alone.

Mechanics of the result explain the shape of it. PTI candidates had been forced to run as independents after the party lost its symbol, the cricket bat that appears on the ballot paper, and many of the party's senior figures were in jail or in hiding. In Pakistan, independent candidates cannot be allocated the reserved seats for women and minorities that are distributed to parties in proportion to their strength, so the largest bloc in the chamber will not receive its share of the seventy such seats. Parties that preserved their symbols will divide them instead, which means the arithmetic after the count does not match the arithmetic on election night.

Turnout was 46.9 per cent, according to the Free and Fair Election Network and figures later confirmed in the official record, down from 51 per cent in 2018 and 53 per cent in 2013. Of the 128,585,760 registered voters, more than half stayed home. Security was the stated reason in much of Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where polling stations were attacked on the day and mobile phone service was suspended. In the Punjab the reason was less often stated and more often understood, and turnout among women in some districts fell sharply.

Result produced an argument rather than a government. Khan's supporters said the count had been rigged in the hours after polling closed, when the commission stopped publishing results and the leading party's tally began to fall. The commission denied it and blamed a software problem. The military establishment, which had spent two years dismantling PTI, found itself with a parliament in which the party's candidates were the largest group and could not form a government, and with the parties it had been backing holding fewer seats than they expected. Nawaz Sharif, who had returned from exile in October expecting to win, invited the PPP and others into a coalition on the evening of the tenth.

By the end of the month the shape of the next government was clear enough. Shehbaz Sharif was nominated to return as prime minister, with the PPP and the MQM supporting him, and the PTI's independents prepared to sit in opposition under the protection of a speaker they did not choose. Negotiations over the reserved seats and over the provincial assemblies in the Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa were still going on, and those are the places where the next government will be tested first.

A question nobody in power had asked was answered by the count, and the answer was that the party they had tried to erase was still the most popular in the country. Whether that translates into anything is the part that remains open. A largest bloc that cannot form a government can still block legislation, and it can still win the by-elections that follow the reserved-seat allocation. Khan remained in prison through the spring, and his party remained the thing that every other party had to calculate around.
