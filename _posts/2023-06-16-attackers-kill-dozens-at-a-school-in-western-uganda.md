---
title: "Attackers kill dozens at a school in western Uganda"
date: 2023-06-16 21:10:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Fighters attacked a secondary school hostel in Mpondwe, in western Uganda, late on 16 June, killing at least forty-two people, most of them students, and kidnapping others across the border into the Democratic Republic of Congo."
---

Late on 16 June, fighters attacked a secondary school in Mpondwe, a town on Uganda's western border with the Democratic Republic of Congo, and set fire to a dormitory. By the following day the authorities had counted more than forty dead, most of them students, and said that several people had been abducted and taken across the border. The attackers were described by the Ugandan military as members of the Allied Democratic Forces, an armed group that has operated in eastern Congo for three decades and that has been linked to Islamic State.

The school is a few kilometres from the border, in a district that has absorbed refugees from Congo's wars for decades. The attackers arrived at about eleven at night and used petrol to set the buildings alight, which is why so many of the dead were burned rather than shot. Ugandan forces crossed into Congo in pursuit, and the army said it had rescued some of the kidnapped students from a nearby forest, while others were still missing. The government promised to hunt the group down and to reinforce the border district.

Founded in the 1990s as a Ugandan rebel movement, the Allied Democratic Forces has survived by relocating into Congo's eastern provinces, where the state is weak and where it has worked for years with local networks. It has carried out a series of massacres and bombings, including attacks in Kampala, and its links to Islamic State's central African operation have been acknowledged by international monitors. The group's finances come from gold and timber and from the territory it controls, and the Ugandan military's operations against it have displaced its base without destroying it.

This attack is the most lethal the group has carried out against a civilian institution in Uganda, and it has led to demands that the army and the police take the border districts more seriously. Uganda has deployed thousands of soldiers to Congo to hunt the group, and the results have been limited, which has become a domestic political issue for a government that presents itself as a regional stabiliser. The school will reopen, and the families will bury their children, and the argument about why the border is so open will continue.

There is a wider pattern that this attack belongs to. Schools have been attacked in Nigeria, in Cameroon, in Burkina Faso and in Kenya, and the pattern is that armed groups target places where children gather because that is where the damage lasts longest and where a community's future is stored. The attackers in Mpondwe did not choose the school because it was a military target; they chose it because it was undefended. Uganda's response will be judged by whether that changes, and by whether the next attack is prevented rather than condemned.

Ugandan forces said they had recovered some of the abducted students and were still searching for others. The district's schools will reopen under guard, and the parents who sent their children to a hostel a few kilometres from a war will be asked to do so again.
