---
title: "Boeing machinists end their strike and approve a thirty-eight per cent pay rise"
date: 2024-11-04 22:00:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "About 33,000 Boeing machinists voted on 4 November to accept a contract giving them a 38 per cent wage increase over four years, ending a seven-week strike that had halted production of most of the company's airliners."
---

Boeing machinists voted on the fourth of November to accept the company's latest contract offer, ending a strike that had run for seven weeks and had stopped production of most of its commercial aircraft. Union said 59 per cent of members who voted approved the deal, which gives about 33,000 workers a 38 per cent wage increase over four years along with ratification and productivity bonuses. Work resumed within days, and the company said it would restart assembly lines that had been idle since the middle of September.

Strike had begun on the thirteenth of September after members rejected an earlier offer by an overwhelming margin. Two further proposals were voted down in October, and the dispute became a test of the new chief executive, Kelly Ortberg, who had taken office in August and had promised to rebuild relations with the workforce. Union had asked for a return of the defined-benefit pension that was frozen a decade ago, and the final contract did not restore it, which is the part of the settlement that some members criticized.

Economic effects had been accumulating. Boeing had been burning through cash, and analysts estimated the strike was costing the company about a billion dollars a month in lost revenue and disruption. Suppliers had furloughed workers, and airlines that had ordered aircraft were told that deliveries would slip into 2025. Company had already announced job cuts of ten per cent across the workforce in October, and the two events together left it with a smaller headcount and a slower production plan than the one it published at the start of the year.

Financial position was weak before the strike and is weaker after it. Boeing has lost money in every quarter since 2019, when the grounding of the 737 Max began, and its debt has risen to levels that rating agencies have described as close to the threshold for a downgrade. Company raised capital in the autumn through a share sale and a convertible bond, which bought time, and it has said it will not return to positive free cash flow until production stabilizes.

Reaction was mixed and the shares moved little. Investors treated the settlement as the removal of a large uncertainty, and analysts noted that the wage bill will rise by more than two billion dollars over the life of the contract. Employees who had wanted the pension back said the deal was better than nothing, and the union's leadership said the vote showed that members would not accept a contract that gave away retirement security. Company said the agreement would help it to stabilize production and to deliver on a backlog worth more than half a trillion dollars.

Next stage is the part that has defeated Boeing repeatedly over five years. Restarting a production line is slower than stopping one, and the company has to do it while regulators limit output on the 737 Max and while its defence business runs losses on fixed-price contracts. Restoring the relationship with the workforce is a condition of doing that, and the new chief executive has said as much. Contract runs to 2028, which means the next negotiation will fall in the middle of the next decade's order cycle.
