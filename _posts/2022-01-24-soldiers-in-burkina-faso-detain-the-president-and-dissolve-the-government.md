---
title: "Soldiers in Burkina Faso detain the president and dissolve the government"
date: 2022-01-24 23:15:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Mutinous troops seized a barracks in Ouagadougou, arrested Roch Kabore and installed a lieutenant colonel, ending a presidency that had been losing ground to insurgents for five years."
---

Soldiers in Burkina Faso detained President Roch Marc Christian Kabore on 24 January at a barracks in Ouagadougou, and by the evening a group of officers had announced on television that the government was dissolved and the constitution suspended. The junta called itself the Patriotic Movement for Safeguard and Restoration and named Lieutenant Colonel Paul-Henri Sandaogo Damiba as its head. Its first budget cut spending on the ministries that deliver services, and it raised the share of the budget allocated to the army.

The coup had been building for months in the army's garrisons. Jihadist groups linked to Islamic State and to al-Qaeda had killed thousands of civilians, controlled swathes of the north and the east, and forced more than a million people from their homes. Soldiers had mutinied twice in the previous year over pay and equipment, and units in the field had complained that they were ordered into positions they could not hold with the vehicles they had. The army's own casualty figures had become a political issue, and the government had stopped publishing them in the autumn.

Kabore's government had tried a militia programme, armed volunteers in villages to defend themselves, and the militia had become another armed actor that the state could not control. The security forces had also been accused of abuses in the conduct of the campaign, which pushed civilians towards the insurgents and made the army's coercive power part of the problem. Human rights investigators documented killings in villages that the militia had been sent to defend, and the insurgents used those killings in their recruitment.

Regional response was quick and limited. The Economic Community of West African States suspended Burkina Faso from its institutions, as it had suspended Mali and Guinea after their coups, and the African Union followed. Both bodies demanded a return to constitutional order within a timetable, and both had learned from the previous two years that suspensions do not remove juntas. They also impose financial pressure that the poorest members of the bloc can least afford.

Junta's argument was about security, and its early moves were symbolic. Damiba promised to restore the constitution when the security situation allowed, appointed a government of officers and civilians, and made public appeals for dialogue with the insurgents. Within months the violence had not fallen, and the officers who had removed one president would face the same test from the officers below them. Eight months later another lieutenant colonel removed Damiba in a second coup and announced it on television with the same phrases about the failure of the political class.

A state that cannot protect its territory for five years will eventually be taken over by the institution that is supposed to protect it. Burkina Faso's coup was less a surprise than a schedule, and the sanctions applied by its neighbours address the form of the government rather than the capacity of the army that replaced it. The sanctions that followed cut the junta off from regional credit, and its answer was to send ministers to Moscow to ask for security support.
