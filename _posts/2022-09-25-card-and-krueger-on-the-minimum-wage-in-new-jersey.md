---
title: "Card and Krueger found that a higher minimum wage did not cost jobs in New Jersey"
date: 2022-09-25 21:40:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "Card and Krueger's famous natural experiment compared fast‑food work after New Jersey raised its minimum wage. The work challenged standard competitive labour market theory, drew sharp criticism and helped establish natural experiments as a core research tool in economics."
---

New Jersey raised its minimum wage from four dollars and twenty-five cents to five dollars and five cents on 1 April 1992, and Pennsylvania left its rate where it was. David Card and Alan Krueger published the comparison in the *American Economic Review* two years later, under the title 'Minimum wages and employment: a case study of the fast-food industry in New Jersey and Pennsylvania'. Two states share a border, a labour market and a set of restaurant chains, which is what makes the design possible. The method is the one the textbooks now call a natural experiment.

The paper actually did much solid work. The authors telephoned about four hundred fast-food restaurants in each state before the increase and again eight months after it, and asked for the number of full-time and part-time employees, the starting wage and the price of a meal. The response rate was high enough for the comparison to be made, and restaurants were matched by chain and by region. The estimate comes from the change across the border, not from a model of the labour market.

The esult was one the textbooks print. Employment in New Jersey rose relative to Pennsylvania, by an amount the authors could not distinguish from zero, and starting wages in New Jersey rose as the law required. The paper reports the estimates with standard errors and discusses the specifications it tried. Its conclusion is that the predicted job losses did not appear in the data, and that the competitive model of the low-wage labour market was too simple.

Criticism came from the economists who use payroll records. David Neumark and William Wascher re-examined the question with the employment figures that restaurants report to the government, and they found that jobs in New Jersey fell relative to Pennsylvania. The two teams disagreed about the sample, the timing and the measure of employment, and the argument lasted a decade. Later surveys and border designs produce estimates that cluster near zero, with a range that includes small negative effects.

The reason this paper matters has little to do with the size of the effect. It is the demonstration that a policy question can be answered with data the world produces rather than with a model the economist builds. The method spread to tax credits, immigration and health insurance, and it changed what a credible empirical paper looks like. Card and Krueger shared the Nobel prize in 2021, and the citation named the natural experiments rather than this paper alone.

My reading advice is to look at the table before the text, because the table of the employment change by state is the whole argument and the regression that follows controls for differences the table cannot. The appendix describes the survey and the non-response, which is where a sceptical reader should spend the time. Read the reply by the critics as well, since the two papers together show how a result is tested. The minimum wage remains a political question and the evidence remains contested at the margin, and this paper is the frame both sides now use.
