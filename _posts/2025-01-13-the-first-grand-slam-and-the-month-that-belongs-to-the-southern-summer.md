The Australian Open began in Melbourne on the twelfth of January, and for two weeks the tennis world works in a time zone that suits nobody else. The matches start at eleven in the morning local time, which is midnight in Europe, and the final is played on a Sunday evening that the rest of the world watches on a Monday.

The tournament is the first of the four grand slams in the calendar and the one with the shortest history. It moved to its current site in 1988, when the surface was changed to a hard court and the stadium was built beside a river, and the event has grown into a fortnight that the city treats as its own festival.

The heat is the condition that the players talk about. The temperature on the court can pass forty degrees in the afternoon, the surface radiates, and the rule that governs the suspension of play is written in a policy that the tournament reviews each year. The matches that are scheduled in the middle of the day are the ones that the players with the most experience try to avoid, and the ones who cannot avoid them are the qualifiers and the doubles teams.

The economics of the tournament are a lesson in the value of a month. The city fills its hotels, the television rights are sold to a hundred countries, and the sponsorship that surrounds the courts is priced for the audience that the final delivers. The tournament is a private business and a public event, and the balance between the two is negotiated every few years with the state government.

The players have their own calendar and it is the part that the public does not see. A player who reaches the final in Melbourne has played seven matches in fourteen days in the heat, and the season continues in February on a different continent. The ranking points that the tournament distributes decide the seedings for the rest of the year, and the physical cost is paid in the spring.

What the tournament does for the sport is to open the year with an argument. The young player who beats a champion in the fourth round is the story that the season carries, and the veteran who wins a fifth set is the story that the commentary repeats. The narratives that will be discussed in June and September usually begin here, and the first of them is always about a player nobody had ranked in the top ten in December.

For the viewer in the northern winter, the broadcast is the pleasure of the thing. The screen shows a bright court, a crowd in shirt sleeves and a sky that is the colour of a summer afternoon, and the contrast with the dark window behind the television is half the appeal. The tournament is a holiday that the calendar delivers in the coldest month, and the sport is the excuse for it, which is a trade that Melbourne has been making for thirty-seven years.
