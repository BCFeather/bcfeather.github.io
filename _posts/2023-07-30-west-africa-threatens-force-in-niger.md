---
title: "West Africa threatens force in Niger"
date: 2023-07-30 20:55:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Leaders of the Economic Community of West African States met in Abuja on 30 July and closed borders and froze assets against Niger, giving the junta a week to restore the president and warning that the use of force remains an option."
---

The heads of state of the Economic Community of West African States met in Abuja on 30 July and produced a communique with a deadline in it. They closed land and air borders with Niger, froze the accounts of the new military council and its members, suspended financial transactions between the bloc and the country, and gave the officers a week to restore President Mohamed Bazoum. If they did not, the statement said, the bloc would take all measures necessary, including the use of force.

Bola Tinubu, Nigeria's new president and the bloc's chair, said the organisation could no longer tolerate coups and that the region had to enforce its own rules. The bloc had already suspended Niger's membership, as it has suspended Mali, Burkina Faso and Guinea, and it has a standby force that has intervened in the past, in Liberia and Sierra Leone in the 1990s and in Gambia in 2017. Whether it can assemble and finance one now is a question that the communique did not answer. The commission was told to prepare the options, and defence chiefs were asked for a plan that could be executed within weeks.

Responses from the three neighbours were immediate. Mali and Burkina Faso, both run by officers who took power in the same fashion, said that any intervention in Niger would be treated as a declaration of war on them, and Guinea's leader said the bloc should negotiate instead. Their statement turned a sanctions dispute into a question about the survival of a set of governments that share the same origin and the same enemies.

Militarily, Niger bears little resemblance to Gambia. The country is landlocked, twice the size of France, and its army has been fighting insurgents in the west and the south-east for a decade. An intervention would need Nigerian troops in numbers, Nigerian money and Nigerian political will, and Nigeria's own parliament would have to agree, which its constitution requires and which its senators have so far declined to promise. Neighbours have closed their borders, and in a landlocked country the price of fuel will rise before the deadline does.

Diplomacy has continued alongside the threats. American and European officials have spoken to the new council, the United Nations has condemned the takeover, and Algeria, which shares a long border and a long history of mediation, has offered itself as a channel. The junta has said it will not be deterred and has accused France of planning an operation of its own, a charge Paris denies. It has named a prime minister from a small party that served the old government, which looks like civilian cover rather than a rupture.

The deadline expires next Sunday. Either Bazoum is released and restored, which the officers have given no sign of intending and which his party has demanded daily, or the bloc has to decide whether a threat it made in public is one it can carry out. In the Sahel, the credibility of the regional body is the thing that is actually being tested.
