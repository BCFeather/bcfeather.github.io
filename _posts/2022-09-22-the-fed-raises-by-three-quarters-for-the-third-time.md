---
title: "The Fed raises by three quarters for the third time and warns that the pain is coming"
date: 2022-09-22 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "Rates reach three point two five per cent, the projections point higher, and the chairman drops the language about a soft landing."
---

The Federal Reserve raised its policy rate by three quarters of a point on Wednesday, the third increase of that size in a row, and the target range now sits between three and three and a quarter per cent. It is the highest level since 2008 and the fastest sequence of increases since the 1980s. The projections published with the decision showed the median official expecting the rate to reach about four and a half per cent next year, which is higher than the market had priced. The statement said inflation remained elevated and that the committee was strongly committed to bringing it back to two per cent.

Inflation was 8.3 per cent in the year to August, and the measure that excludes food and energy rose faster than in the previous month. The labour market added jobs at a rate that is still too fast for the target, and the unemployment rate is 3.7 per cent. The bank's own summary of conditions describes an economy where demand has to fall before prices do, and it has been saying so since the spring. The question the meeting answered is how much of the work would be done at once.

Speaking after the meeting, the chairman used the word pain in his press conference, and he said that achieving a soft landing would be difficult. He also said that the alternatives were worse, and that a premature pause would allow inflation to become embedded. Markets read the combination as a signal that the bank would keep going until something breaks, and the two-year yield rose above four per cent before the close. Equity indices fell about two per cent, and the dollar reached its strongest level against the euro in twenty years.

Consequences outside the country are the ones that the bank does not have to consider. The dollar's rise makes imported energy and food more expensive for poorer countries, which have been borrowing in the currency that is now the most costly. The World Bank has warned that a global recession is possible next year, and the finance ministers of several emerging markets have said the rate path is exporting inflation rather than controlling it. The bank's mandate does not include those effects, and its officials have acknowledged them without changing course.

Housing is the first transmission channel that is visibly working. Mortgage rates have reached six per cent for a thirty-year loan, the highest since 2008, and existing home sales fell for the seventh month. The construction industry has begun to lay off workers, and the orders for new houses have fallen faster than the prices. That is what the bank wants to see, and the speed is what worries the people who remember 2007.

What the next meeting does will depend on two numbers that are published before it, one for prices and one for jobs. The bank has said it will look at the cumulative effect of the increases already made, and it has also said that it will not stop early. Both of those statements can be true at once only if the data turn, and the market has begun to bet on when. The rate path in the projections is a forecast rather than a promise, and the officials who wrote it have said as much.
