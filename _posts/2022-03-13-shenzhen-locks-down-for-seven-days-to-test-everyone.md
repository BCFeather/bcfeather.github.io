---
title: "Shenzhen locks down for seven days to test everyone"
date: 2022-03-13 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "A city of 17.5 million closes its offices, its restaurants and its factories and calls the arrangement slow living, while the port and the electronics plants keep running on a handful of staff."
---

Shenzhen announced on the evening of 13 March that it would close non-essential business for seven days and test its 17.5 million residents, and the measures took effect at midnight. Offices, factories, restaurants, cinemas and parks were shut in a city that produces a large share of the world's electronics, and residents were told to stay in their neighbourhoods except to buy food or to join a queue for a swab. The government described the period with a phrase that translates as slow living, which was chosen to avoid the word lockdown.

Testing was organized at a scale that had been rehearsed in other cities. Each district set up stations in residential compounds, the results went to a central system through a phone application, and a resident who wanted to enter a building or a metro station showed a colour on a screen. Three rounds were completed in seven days, and the city reported that it had tested more than 30 million samples, counting the repeats.

Factories did not all stop, and the way the ones that kept running did so showed how the system worked. The port at Yantian kept operating with a reduced crew living inside the terminal, the electronics plants that supply Apple and other customers ran on staff who slept on site, and a list of essential workers, from delivery riders to water engineers, was allowed to move. Companies that could not put their staff inside a closed loop stopped, and their customers found out about it in the following quarter.

The outbreak that caused the lockdown came from across the border. Hong Kong was in the middle of its largest wave of the pandemic, with tens of thousands of cases a day at its peak, and the truck drivers who carried goods between the two cities were the route by which the virus entered. Shenzhen closed the crossings to most traffic in February, moved freight onto a shuttle system with drivers who stayed on one side, and still found cases in the city that could not be traced to a known chain.

Implementation varied by neighbourhood. In the wealthier districts the compounds hired their own staff to organize the queues and delivered groceries to the door, and in the urban villages the residents queued in the street and the community volunteers did the work. Residents who tested positive went to a quarantine centre, their close contacts to a hotel, and the rest of the building stayed where it was and was tested again on the following day.

The measures were lifted on 21 March, a day later than announced, and the case count fell to a handful within a week. What the seven days showed was the capacity of the system and its price: a city could test itself faster than any other in the world, and the cost was paid by the small businesses that closed and the workers who lost seven days of pay. Shanghai began its own version two weeks later, and the contrast between the two cities became the argument that decided the policy for the rest of the year.
