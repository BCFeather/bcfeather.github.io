---
title: "Bitcoin lost twenty thousand dollars"
date: 2022-06-18 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "The price fell below the level it had first reached in 2017, touching about seventeen thousand six hundred dollars, as a lending platform froze withdrawals and leveraged funds began to fail."
---

Bitcoin fell below twenty thousand dollars on 18 June for the first time since late 2020, touching about seventeen thousand six hundred dollars during the afternoon in London. The price had been sixty-nine thousand in November, and the fall of roughly seventy per cent took it below the previous cycle's peak, which is a level that had never been breached in the asset's history. Ether fell below a thousand dollars in the same session.

Two forces combined to produce the move. Central banks were raising interest rates at the fastest pace in decades, which removed the cheap money that had funded speculative positions across the market, and the collapse of a large lending platform on 12 June had shown depositors that the firms holding their coins could stop paying them back. Once that became clear, selling fed on itself.

Leverage did the rest. Trading venues report that hundreds of millions of dollars of long positions were liquidated within hours, and each liquidation forced more selling. A crypto hedge fund that had borrowed from lenders across the industry, Three Arrows Capital, was reported to be unable to meet margin calls on positions in several tokens, and the lenders that had lent to it began to look for buyers.

Damage is visible in the aggregate numbers. The total value of all cryptocurrencies fell from about three trillion dollars in November to below one trillion during the third week of June, and the number of coins with any meaningful trading volume shrank. Mining companies that had borrowed to buy machines found that the price of bitcoin was below their cost of production in some regions, and several began selling reserves. Trading volumes on the largest venues fell by about half, and exchanges that had expanded during 2021 began to cut staff.

Retail investors were the last to arrive and the first to be hurt. Brokerage apps reported a surge in accounts opened during 2021, many of them holding sums small enough that the loss is personal rather than systemic, and surveys suggested that a large share of new buyers had entered near the peak. The asset that had been marketed as an inflation hedge fell during the worst inflation in forty years, which is a fact its advocates have yet to explain.

Regulators responded with inquiries rather than rules. Officials in several American states opened investigations into whether the yield products offered by lending platforms were unregistered securities, and the Treasury Department asked for information on how the sector's leverage was arranged. Legislation in Congress remained stalled, and the industry's lobbying apparatus spent the summer arguing that the failures were the fault of specific firms rather than of the structure. What the fall demonstrates is a property that bull markets obscure. Crypto prices are set at the margin by leveraged buyers, and when the marginal buyer disappears the price can move far below any estimate of fundamental value, because there is no cash flow to anchor it; the market spent two years learning that the opposite also holds.
