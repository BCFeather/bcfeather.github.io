Mid-term examinations ended on a Friday, and the papers were returned on the Wednesday after. Between those two days the corridors of the school had a different sound. Noise was louder and it carried a nervous edge, the way talk does when everyone is waiting for a number that has already been decided somewhere else. Boys who usually ran down the stairs stood in groups by the notice board instead, and the groups rearranged themselves each time a teacher came past carrying a folder.

It was the third year of middle school, and the year that everything before it had been preparation for. Teachers said this in September and said it again in October, and by November the sentence had become a kind of weather that hung over the lessons. Nobody argued with it. Homework simply grew, the evenings shortened, and the strip lights in the classrooms were switched on before the last period ended. The wooden desks carried the marks of two years of pencils, and the new ones had already been claimed by the people sitting nearest the window.

On the Sunday after the papers came back I walked home the long way. The road runs beside a row of small shops, and at half past five the shopkeepers were hosing down the pavement and putting out boards with prices chalked on them. A man was selling roasted chestnuts from a drum, and the smell of them followed me for a hundred metres. The sky above the roofs had the flat grey that comes an hour before dark in November, and the bicycles on the path already had their lights on.

That afternoon's results were neither good nor terrible. Two subjects had gone better than I expected, one had gone worse, and the total sat in the middle of the class. My mother read the sheet at the kitchen table and said there was still time. My father, who arrived on Saturday evening, said the same thing in fewer words, and then asked whether the bicycle chain needed oiling before the week began.

What I remember of that evening is the way the class talked about it. Someone had moved up, someone had moved down, and the order that had been fixed in September was moving for the first time. It was the first hint that the sorting at the end of the term would be decided by figures of this kind, and that those figures would follow the effort only loosely. Several people said they had known it all along, which is what people say when a result confirms what they already feared. Two of them brought their papers to the back of the room to compare marks line by line, and the comparison lasted longer than the lesson that followed.

The corridor emptied slowly that evening. Boys stood by the notice board near the stairwell and argued about a physics question that two teachers had answered differently, and the argument continued down the stairs and out of the gate. The wall behind them was covered with the red paper of an old campaign, and the paper had faded to a dull brick colour in the weather.
