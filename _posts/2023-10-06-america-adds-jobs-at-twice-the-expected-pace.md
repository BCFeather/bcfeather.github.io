---
title: "America adds jobs at twice the expected pace"
date: 2023-10-06 22:45:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "American employers added 336,000 jobs in September 2023 and unemployment held at 3.8 per cent, a labour report published on 6 October 2023 that came in nearly double the forecast and pushed bond yields higher."
---

September's employment report, published on 6 October, showed employers adding 336,000 jobs, close to double the consensus forecast of about 170,000, and revisions to the two previous months added another 119,000. Unemployment held at 3.8 per cent, and the share of adults in work or looking for work stayed at 62.8 per cent. Average hourly earnings rose two tenths of a per cent on the month, which left the annual rate a little below four per cent. The figure was the strongest of the year, and hiring had already been revised upward in each of the three previous months.

The gains were spread across sectors that had been cooling. Leisure and hospitality added 96,000 positions, health care added 41,000, and government payrolls grew by 73,000, a figure inflated by the return of striking workers in Michigan and by hiring in local government. Manufacturing added a small number, and the loss of jobs in the motion picture industry continued through the writers' and actors' strikes. Construction added 19,000 positions and professional services 21,000, while retail shed a few thousand as spending moved online.

The report landed awkwardly. Wage growth has slowed enough for the Federal Reserve to argue that the labour market is rebalancing, and the payroll number suggested the opposite, which pushed yields up across the curve and left traders pricing a higher chance of one more rate increase. Two-year Treasury yields moved to their highest level since 2006, and mortgage rates followed to levels last seen in 2000.

Economists offered competing explanations for the strength. Some pointed to the revisions, which have been positive for several months and suggest that earlier estimates were too pessimistic. Others noted that the survey of households showed a smaller gain than the survey of establishments, which is the pattern that appears when multiple job holding rises, and that the unemployment rate held steady only because more people entered the labour force. The household survey, which is smaller and noisier, told a weaker story, and the gap between the two surveys is one reason the report reads as ambiguous.

Politically, a labour market this tight after a year of rate increases complicates the argument that the economy is slowing towards a soft landing. Growth in the third quarter was tracking near four per cent, consumer spending held up, and the strike at the carmakers and the resumption of student loan payments were expected to subtract from the fourth quarter. Forecasters marked up third-quarter growth after the release, and several pushed their recession calls further out.

For households the picture is mixed. Hiring is easy for people with skills, and real wages have begun to rise as inflation falls, but borrowing costs have risen faster than at any time in a generation, and the sectors that added the most jobs are the ones with the lowest pay. A strong payroll number measures the number of jobs rather than their quality, and the wages in the report tell a quieter story than the headline.
