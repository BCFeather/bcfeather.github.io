---
title: "A week-long truce buys Sudan a little time"
date: 2023-05-23 22:15:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The two generals fighting for control of Sudan signed a seven-day ceasefire on 22 May, brokered by Saudi Arabia and the United States in Jeddah. It was the first agreement that both sides formally accepted, and the first test of whether either can control its own fighters."
---

The Sudanese army and the Rapid Support Forces signed a seven-day ceasefire in Jeddah on 22 May, with the mediation of Saudi Arabia and the United States, and the truce took effect on the evening of the same day. It was the first agreement that both sides had put in writing since fighting began on 15 April, and it included commitments to keep humanitarian corridors open, to allow aid to move, and to refrain from attacks on civilians. The previous attempts, several of them announced and none of them implemented, had lasted hours.

The war began as a dispute between two men who had been partners in the coup of 2021. General Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, the commander of the armed forces, and General Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, known as Hemedti, the leader of the Rapid Support Forces, had been arguing over the integration of the paramilitary group into the army, and over who would command what. The argument became a battle in the streets of Khartoum within a day, and the fighting spread to Darfur, where the Rapid Support Forces grew out of the Janjaweed militias that fought the rebellion of the 2000s.

Its human cost has been severe and badly counted. More than a thousand civilians have been confirmed killed, and the true figure is certainly higher, with reports of mass graves in El Geneina and of entire neighbourhoods emptied in Khartoum. About 1.4 million people have been displaced, including some three hundred thousand who have crossed into Egypt, Chad and South Sudan. Hospitals in the capital have been shelled and occupied, and the country's already fragile health system has in places stopped functioning.

Diplomatic architecture is odd and revealing. The two generals have both worked with outside patrons, and the outside patrons have interests that pull in different directions. Egypt and the United Arab Emirates have relationships with the two sides, Saudi Arabia has positioned itself as the mediator, and the African Union and the United Nations have been largely spectators. The Jeddah talks were a Saudi initiative that the Americans joined, which is a template for how the Gulf states now conduct regional diplomacy.

Whether a truce can hold depends on a question that neither general can answer. The Rapid Support Forces is a coalition of commanders with their own supply lines and their own grievances, and Hemedti's authority over units in Darfur has been uncertain since the fighting began. The army has its own divisions, including the Islamist brigades that were reactivated after the coup. An agreement signed in a hotel in Jeddah binds two men who may not be able to bind their own troops, and the first violations were reported within hours.

Its wider danger is that the fighting becomes a proxy war on the Red Sea. Sudan sits between Egypt, Ethiopia and the Gulf, and its collapse would send refugees into countries that are already struggling and weapons into a region that has plenty of them. The truce will be judged by whether aid reaches Khartoum and whether the two armies can pull back from the positions they have taken in the capital. A week is a short time to test that, and it is the only time either side has agreed to give.
