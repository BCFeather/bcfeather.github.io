---
title: "The Fed lifts rates to the highest level in twenty-two years"
date: 2023-07-27 22:05:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate by a quarter point on 26 July to a range of five and a quarter to five and a half per cent, the highest since 2001 and the eleventh increase in sixteen months."
---

The Federal Reserve raised its target range for the federal funds rate by a quarter of a point on 26 July, to five and a quarter to five and a half per cent, and left open the possibility of another increase this year. It was the eleventh rise since March 2022, when borrowing costs began from near zero, and the highest level for the benchmark since 2001. Jerome Powell told reporters that inflation had moderated but that the committee wanted to see more of it before it stopped.

Justification came written in the data. Consumer prices had risen three per cent over the year to June, down from nine per cent a year earlier, and the core measure that excludes food and energy was at four point eight, still too high for a central bank with a two per cent target. Unemployment was three point six per cent, the lowest in decades, and payroll growth had slowed but stayed strong. A labour market that refuses to soften makes a pause harder to defend. Food prices had fallen for months and energy was cheaper than a year earlier, while services inflation, which tracks wages, stayed above five per cent.

Powell's language has changed over the year. In 2022 he described the path to a soft landing as narrow and warned that households and firms would feel pain. On 26 July he said the staff's forecast no longer assumed a recession, and that a gradual cooling of hiring and prices without a sharp rise in unemployment was plausible. Economists who had predicted a contraction by now spent the week revising their models. He has also retired the word transitory, which he gave up two years ago after it became a liability.

Markets took the decision as expected and spent the day reading the next move. Bond yields fell modestly, stocks rose, and the dollar slipped, which is the pattern of a market that has decided the cycle is near its top. The committee's projections in June implied one more increase before the end of the year, and the language on 26 July preserved that option without committing to it. The statement repeated that the committee would weigh the lagged effects of past increases, a phrase that sounds technical and reads as a willingness to slow down.

Costs of the tightening are accumulating elsewhere. Credit card and auto loan rates are at their highest in years, mortgage rates have pushed towards seven per cent, and small businesses report that borrowing has become expensive enough to postpone investment. Commercial property, especially offices, is the sector most exposed, and regional banks hold much of that exposure. Lending standards for small firms have tightened for three quarters running, according to the Fed's own survey of senior loan officers.

September brings the next meeting, with two inflation reports and two employment reports in hand by then. A quarter of a point in July changes little on its own; the cumulative eleven changes have raised the price of money by more than five percentage points in sixteen months, which is the part that households will remember.
