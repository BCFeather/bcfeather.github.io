---
title: "Santiago Peña secures Paraguayan presidency as ruling Colorado party retains power"
date: 2023-05-02 01:02:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Santiago Peña, candidate of Paraguay's long-ruling Colorado Party, won the April 30 presidential election with 43% of votes. The ruling party kept control of Congress, while a third-place populist contender rejected results and sparked protests."
---

Paraguay's long-dominant Colorado Party claimed victory in the country's general election, with its presidential candidate Santiago Peña confirmed as president-elect by electoral authorities on Sunday night, local time. With nearly 99 per cent of ballots counted, the 44-year-old economist secured roughly 43 per cent of the vote, opening a clear lead over his closest rival Efraín Alegre, the standard-bearer of the opposition coalition Pact for a New Paraguay, who took 27 per cent. The populist outsider Paraguayo Cubas finished third with 23 per cent support.

The single-round election, held on 30 April, also filled seats in both chambers of Congress and elected 17 departmental governors. The Colorado Party maintained its legislative advantage, winning majorities in the Senate and the lower house, alongside 15 of the 17 governorships contested. The strong nationwide result extended the party's historic grip on Paraguayan politics; it has governed the landlocked South American nation for most of the past seven decades.

Peña, a former finance minister and ex-IMF economist, campaigned on promises of economic stability, job creation and cautious reform. In his victory speech to supporters, he framed the win as a mandate for national reconciliation rather than a personal triumph. 'We choose dialogue, peace and unity,' he told cheering crowds in central Asunción. He was scheduled to take office on 15 August for a five-year term, succeeding outgoing president Mario Abdo Benítez, also from Colorado.

Opposition groups had hoped voter anger over corruption, weak public health services and slow poverty reduction would end the party's long run in power. High turnout stood at around 63 per cent, reflecting intense public interest in the race. International observer teams from the European Union and the Organization of American States described polling day as generally peaceful and well-organized, while noting deep political polarisation and lingering public distrust in state institutions.

Still, the outcome was not universally accepted. Cubas quickly rejected the official tally and alleged electoral fraud, calling on his followers to stage street protests. Small demonstrations erupted in Asunción in the days after the vote, setting the stage for fresh friction between the incoming administration and anti-establishment voters. Analysts noted that the large share of votes captured by Cubas signalled a powerful undercurrent of anti-system sentiment that Peña would need to address once in office.

For Paraguay, the result meant continuity in its foreign and economic direction. The new president faced familiar structural challenges: narrow export bases, widespread informal labour and persistent pressure over organised crime and drug trafficking across its border regions.
