The twenty-third of April is the day for books, and the shops that sell them have spent a decade learning how to live with a platform that discounts every title by a third and delivers it in a day. The shops that survived did it by changing what they sell, from the book as a unit to the book as an occasion, and the change is visible in the layout of a shop that is still open.

The economics of a bookshop are thin. A shop buys a book at a trade discount of about forty per cent of the cover price, sells it at the cover price, and pays the rent and the staff out of the difference. A platform buys at a larger discount, sells below the cover price, and pays for the delivery with the volume. A shop cannot match the price, and a shop that tries loses money on every sale.

What the survivors sell is the part that a platform cannot. A shelf with a handwritten note from the person who read the book, a table of six titles that the staff chose, an evening with an author in a room above the shop, a recommendation that is specific to the customer rather than to a purchase history. None of it is expensive and all of it is labour, and the labour is the product.

The second change is the inventory. A shop that stocked everything now stocks deeply in a few areas and lightly in the rest, and the areas are chosen to match the customers who come. A shop near a university stocks the academic press and the poetry, a shop in a residential district stocks the children's books and the cookery, and the shop that tries to be general is the one that closes.

The third change is the coffee. The bookshop with a café in it earns a margin on the drink that is larger than the margin on the book, and the customer who comes for the drink is a customer who buys a book on the way out. The arrangement is a subsidy from the coffee to the books, and the shops that have it are the ones that can pay the rent, which is the test that decides whether a shop survives its second year.

What the day is good for is the argument about the price of a book, which is a argument about who pays for the discovery. A platform that discounts pays for the discovery with the data it collects, a shop pays for it with the shelf and the staff, and a reader who wants a recommendation is paying for it either way. The shop is the more expensive version and the more legible one, and the two can coexist as long as somebody chooses the expensive one, which is a choice that the shopper makes every time a book is bought as a present rather than as a restock, and the shops that understand this have stopped competing on the discount entirely.
