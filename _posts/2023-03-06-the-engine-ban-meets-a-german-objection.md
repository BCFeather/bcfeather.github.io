---
title: "The engine ban meets a German objection"
date: 2023-03-06 23:10:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "A scheduled vote on the European Union's plan to end sales of new combustion-engine cars in 2035 was postponed on 6 March 2023 after the German transport ministry objected, and the dispute was settled later in the month with a carve-out for synthetic fuels."
---

The vote that had been scheduled for 6 March in Brussels did not happen, because the German government said it could not support the text as written. The regulation would have required that every new car sold in the union from 2035 produce no carbon dioxide at the tailpipe, which in practice meant electric vehicles, and it had been agreed by the member states and by the parliament in 2022. The transport ministry in Berlin, which belongs to the Free Democrats, argued that the text would also ban cars running on synthetic fuels, and that the ban was therefore a decision about technology rather than about emissions.

Arriving late, the objection irritated the other governments more than its substance did. The compromise had been negotiated for two years, and the German government had voted for it in the council in June, and the ministry's argument had been made in the parliament's committee stage and rejected. The commission's president said the text would not be reopened, and then spent three weeks negotiating a declaration that reopened it in substance.

Behind the argument about synthetic fuels lies an argument about efficiency. A car running on fuel made from captured carbon and renewable electricity needs several times more electricity per kilometre than a battery car, and the fuels are expensive to produce. Their defenders say that they are the only way to decarbonize the existing fleet and the vehicles that cannot be electrified, and their critics say that a carve-out for them in the passenger car market would delay the shift to batteries at the moment when it had begun to work.

Agreed on 25 March, the compromise added a recital and a separate legal act that would allow vehicles running only on carbon-neutral fuels to be registered after 2035. The commission promised to propose the act within a year, and the German government said the promise was enough to lift its objection. The vote then took place in the last week of March, and the regulation passed with the votes that had always been there.

Across Brussels the episode was read as a lesson about how the union legislates. A text that had been agreed by twenty-seven governments and by a majority of the parliament was held up by one ministry that had lost the argument earlier, and the fix was a declaration that changed the meaning without changing the article. The institution's defenders said the process had worked, because the outcome was still a ban on combustion engines in 2035, and its critics said the process had produced a rule that nobody could describe in a sentence.

The car industry watched the argument with more interest than it showed. Manufacturers had already committed to electric model ranges, and the uncertainty about synthetic fuels mattered mostly to the suppliers and to the makers of expensive sports cars, who were the constituency that the German liberals were defending. The regulation passed, and the debate about the fuels continued in the separate act that the commission had promised, which is where the delay had been moved to.
