The first term ended in the last week of November, and the last thing I remember about it is the light. In September the five minutes home at half past five were still bright, the road white, the sports hall standing clear of the trees. By the middle of November the same walk happened in the dark, with the lamp posts along Shengjinhu Road on and the shop windows lit, and the classroom windows above the entrance glowing like a row of small screens.

September concealed the shape the term would take. For three years my work had been aimed at an examination in June, and every week of it could be counted down. The work now aims at an examination three years away, which means that a badly kept exercise book in September has consequences I cannot feel in November, and that a good one carries rewards which arrive too late to be enjoyed. Teachers said this in the first week, and I heard it as a speech rather than a fact.

The first set of marks arrived in October and did what marks do. I discovered that some of the methods which had carried me through the entrance examination fell short, and spent November learning to write down the steps I had previously done in my head. Others found the subjects they dreaded survivable, and one or two found a subject they had not expected to like. The ranking at the end of the term was published on a sheet of paper, and everybody in the room studied it and then said they had not.

What the term actually taught was a rhythm, and the rhythm belonged to the rented flat as much as to the school. Up at six, out of the door at twenty past, at the gate before seven, four classes before the break and two after, evening study until half past nine, home by ten. The flat was five minutes away, which made the difference: I could eat at home, sleep for forty minutes at noon, and go back for the evening session without a bus or a bicycle.

Weather helped the mood. Chizhou in late November is grey and damp, with the river low and the lake behind the school holding a mist that does not lift until ten. The classrooms are warm enough to steam the windows, and the walk between buildings is cold enough that everybody runs. There is a particular afternoon in that season when the sun comes out for an hour after the fourth class and the whole building seems to stop and look at it.

Three years remain, and the school has already begun to talk about them. The teachers mention the final examination the way people mention a distant city: often, without detail, as a fact of geography. In the last week of term the class cleaned its room, carried the books home in bags too heavy for one person, and stood at the gate saying see you in February. The road was dark by then, the windows were lit, and I did not think about June at all.
