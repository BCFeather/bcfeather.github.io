---
title: "Zimbabwe counts a disputed election"
date: 2023-08-27 22:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Emmerson Mnangagwa was declared the winner of Zimbabwe's presidential election on 26 August 2023 with 52.6 per cent of the vote, in a poll that regional and Western observers said fell short of the standards the country had promised."
---

Results were announced on the evening of 26 August: President Emmerson Mnangagwa had won 52.6 per cent of the vote against 44 per cent for Nelson Chamisa of the Citizens Coalition for Change, and his party had taken a majority in parliament. Turnout was 69 per cent, and the results were released five days after voting in most of the country, with a delay the commission attributed to the number of candidates. The opposition said the count had been manipulated in the townships and rural areas where its support is concentrated, and it published its own tally within hours of the announcement.

Observers were divided and unanimous at the same time. The Southern African Development Community's mission said the election had been peaceful and that it had concerns about the secrecy of the vote and about the use of state media. A European Union mission, invited for the first time in years, said the process had fallen short of international standards and that the playing field had been tilted by the state's control of broadcasting and by the police. American and British officials said the result could not be regarded as credible.

The campaign had already been contested. Dozens of opposition activists were arrested, several rallies were banned, and the Citizens Coalition for Change said its candidates were harassed by police and by supporters of the governing party. The party's structure has been unusual since its formation last year, with candidates chosen without primaries and a leadership that has refused to name a replacement for Chamisa when he is detained. After the count, the party said it would not accept the result and would take the matter to court.

Economics explains the stakes. Zimbabwe has used the American dollar since 2009, has reintroduced a local currency that lost most of its value within two years, and has debt that it has not been able to restructure for a quarter of a century. Inflation reached triple digits in 2022 and has come down only because the government has been limiting the money it prints. A government that wins a disputed election inherits an economy that needs foreign credit, and foreign credit requires the kind of election that the observers have just described as flawed. Talks with the International Monetary Fund have been suspended since 2019.

The result matters beyond the country's borders. Zimbabwe has been suspended from the Commonwealth since 2003 and has been negotiating its return, and the British government has said it will consider the observers' findings before deciding whether to invite the country back. South Africa's governing party, which has been the country's most important diplomatic sponsor, described the election as a success.

A president has been returned to office with a majority, an opposition has refused to accept the count and said it will petition the courts, and the observers have handed both sides a document that neither will like. Zimbabwe's next government will have to govern a country whose creditors have read the same report.
