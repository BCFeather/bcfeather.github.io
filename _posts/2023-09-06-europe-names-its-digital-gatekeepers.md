---
title: "Europe names its digital gatekeepers"
date: 2023-09-06 23:10:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "The European Commission designated six technology firms as gatekeepers under the Digital Markets Act on 6 September 2023, covering twenty-two services that must now comply with rules on how they treat competitors and customers."
---

Brussels published its first list under the Digital Markets Act on 6 September, naming Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, ByteDance, Meta and Microsoft as gatekeepers and listing twenty-two services between them. The rules apply to platforms with more than forty-five million monthly users in Europe and a market capitalization above sixty-five billion euros, and they come with a six-month deadline for compliance and fines of up to ten per cent of global turnover for failure, rising to twenty per cent for repeat offenses.

Obligations are specific. Gatekeepers must let users remove preinstalled apps, allow alternative app stores and payment systems, permit business users to access the data their own activity generates, and open messaging services to rivals who ask. They may not rank their own products above rivals' in search results, and they may not require app developers to use one payment channel. Each of those rules describes an argument that regulators have lost or won in other jurisdictions, and each has a counterpart in American, British and Japanese law that Brussels has been watching closely.

Apple is the firm with most at stake. Its App Store rules, and the commission it charges developers, are the subject of the new obligations, and the company has argued that its controls protect users from fraud and malware. iMessage appears in the list, which means the company must prepare to make it interoperable, a design change it has resisted. The company has appealed the designation of some of its services as gatekeepers, arguing that they fall below the size threshold.

ByteDance is the political case. TikTok is designated, and the company is the only name on the list that comes from outside America and Europe, which makes its compliance a test of how Europe treats a platform owned by a Chinese firm. The commission has been separately investigating TikTok's handling of minors and of advertising, and the act gives regulators a faster route than the older competition instruments.

Rules here bypass the courts. Instead of proving an abuse case by case, the act bans a list of practices and obliges firms to publish their compliance plans, and the commission can inspect and fine without going to court first. That design is a deliberate break with the pace of antitrust litigation, and it exists because European regulators concluded that the litigation took years, produced settlements and changed very little about how the platforms behaved. Officials have said the first compliance reports will be audited rather than accepted, and that audits may take most of next year.

The gatekeepers now have six months to change their products, and the commission will watch what they do rather than what they promise in a filing. A user in Europe should soon be able to delete some apps, use another app store, and send a message to a friend on a different service, and whether that happens will be the measure of whether a list can change a market, and of how much a deadline can achieve that a lawsuit could not.
