---
title: "A pound for a bank"
date: 2023-03-13 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "HSBC bought the British arm of Silicon Valley Bank for one pound on 13 March 2023, in a transaction arranged by the Bank of England and the Treasury over the weekend."
---

The British subsidiary of the failed bank was sold on Monday morning for a pound, and the buyer was HSBC, which is the largest bank in the country. The transaction was arranged by the Bank of England and by the Treasury over the weekend, and it was completed before the markets opened. The subsidiary was ring-fenced under British rules, which meant that its deposits and its loans sat in a separate legal entity from the parent, and that separation is what made a one-pound sale possible.

Insolvency was the alternative, and the authorities spent the weekend calculating what it would cost. Most of the subsidiary's depositors were companies, and the insurance scheme covered only a fraction of their balances, and a failure would have left hundreds of startups unable to pay wages. The Bank of England said the sale had secured the deposits, and it said the transaction had been conducted to avoid a drain on the insurance fund and on public money.

On the other side of the ledger the buyer took on about five and a half billion pounds of loans and about six and a half billion of deposits, and it paid a nominal price for the equity and received the difference in cash from the seller. HSBC's chief executive described the acquisition as a commercial opportunity, and the market treated it as a rescue that had been handed to a bank that did not need one. The bank's shares fell on the day, which suggested that the shareholders had read the deal as a cost rather than a bargain.

Within a week it was the second deal of its kind in Europe, and the third in a month. UBS had bought Credit Suisse the previous weekend, and the British transaction was designed to avoid the structure that the Swiss one had used, which had wiped out a class of bondholders and had been criticized for it. The British authorities structured the sale so that the bondholders and the shareholders of the parent were left with the loss, and the depositors and the employees of the subsidiary were protected.

Precedent was the part that the regulators did not want to discuss. A ring-fenced subsidiary with a foreign parent had been sold for a nominal sum in a single weekend, and the sale had been arranged by the state rather than by the market. The rules that were supposed to make a cross-border failure orderly had not been used, because there was no mechanism for one, and the mechanism that was used was a phone call and a purchase agreement.

The customers who opened their accounts on Monday found them working, which was the point of the exercise. The staff had been told on Sunday, the branches had been rebranded within a month, and the loans were serviced by a bank that had not wanted them. The one-pound price was a symbolic number and an accurate one, because the value of the franchise was negative and the value of the deposits to the system was positive.
