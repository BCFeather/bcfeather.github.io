---
title: "A door plug blows off an Alaska Airlines jet at 16,000 feet"
date: 2024-01-06 18:37:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "An Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9 lost a door plug minutes after take-off from Portland on 5 January, leaving a hole in the fuselage at cruising altitude with 177 people aboard and no serious injuries, and grounding 171 aircraft for inspection."
---

Six minutes after taking off from Portland on the evening of the fifth of January, at about sixteen thousand feet, the left side of Alaska Airlines flight 1282 opened. A panel roughly the size of a door, the plug that fills the space where a mid-cabin exit would be on a denser version of the aircraft, came away from the fuselage and disappeared into the night. Depressurization was instant, and the headset of the first officer was torn from his head, and the captain lost part of her hearing for the rest of the flight. Whatever was not strapped down in the two rows beside the hole went out of it, including a phone that was later found in a garden in Portland, and a boy's shirt. There were 177 people on board, six crew and 171 passengers, and none of them was seriously hurt.

Photographs taken by passengers made the case plain within an hour. The hole is a rectangle of dark sky beside row 26, with the seat backs of the row still in place and the seat cushions gone. Oxygen masks had dropped, the cabin had gone quiet in the way cabins do when people are waiting to find out how bad it is, and the aircraft turned back and landed at Portland without incident nineteen minutes after departure. Boeing's chief executive called it a consequence of an event, not an event that came from nowhere, and the phrase was repeated for a week. What it pointed at was the fact that the plug had been installed or re-installed at Boeing's own factory in Renton, where the fuselages arrive from Spirit AeroSystems in Wichita and the final assembly, including work on doors and plugs, is done.

The investigation moved quickly to the hardware. Investigators from the National Transportation Safety Board found the plug in a residential garden in Portland, and with it the answer to the most obvious question. Four bolts that should have held the plug to the frame were missing, and the marks on the frame showed that the plug had been held in place by nothing but the pressurized air pushing it against the fuselage, which is exactly the wrong way round. The board's chair said the plug had been opened at some point during assembly or inspection and not properly closed again. Spirit AeroSystems, which builds the fuselage, said the work was outside its scope and that it had been done on Boeing's line. Boeing said it would fix the process and would not say more while the investigation ran.

The regulator grounded the fleet within a day. The Federal Aviation Administration ordered the temporary grounding of 171 Boeing 737 MAX 9 aircraft operated by American carriers, and other regulators followed, grounding the type in their own jurisdictions. Alaska Airlines and United Airlines, which between them operate most of the MAX 9s in the United States, cancelled hundreds of flights and began inspections that took weeks rather than days, because the checks required opening the plugs and looking at the fittings. Passengers on the type spent the rest of January watching the registration of their aircraft on tracking sites, which is a habit that no airline wants to encourage.

Two questions sit underneath the mechanical one, and neither will be settled by the bolts. One is what happened to the culture of inspection at the manufacturer, which has now had five years of scrutiny since the two MAX crashes of 2018 and 2019 and still produced an aircraft that left the factory with its plug unlatched. The second is what the plug itself represents. It is a piece of structure that exists only because airlines wanted the option of fitting more seats later, and it was removed and refitted during the build, which means the aircraft carried a place where the assembly could be reopened. A design that leaves a panel to be closed twice has left a place where a mistake can be made twice, and the industry is now going to have to decide whether that convenience was worth what it cost on a Friday evening in January.
