---
title: "A stablecoin called Terra loses its dollar peg and takes 40 billion with it"
date: 2022-05-13 23:45:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "TerraUSD was designed to stay at a dollar through an algorithm and a sister token, and in the week of 9 May the algorithm ran in reverse, turning a stablecoin worth eighteen billion dollars and a token worth twenty billion into assets worth almost nothing."
---

TerraUSD, a stablecoin that was supposed to hold its value at one dollar through an algorithm rather than through reserves, broke its peg on 9 May and never recovered. The token that backs it, Luna, fell from about eighty dollars to fractions of a cent within four days, and the two together lost more than forty billion dollars of market value. The mechanism that was meant to keep the price stable worked exactly as designed, in the direction nobody had planned for.

The design was an arbitrage. One TerraUSD could always be traded for a dollar's worth of newly minted Luna, and one Luna's worth of TerraUSD could be burned, so any gap between the stablecoin's price and a dollar was meant to be closed by traders taking the profit. The system held while demand for both tokens was rising, and it held because a lending protocol called Anchor paid twenty per cent interest on deposits of TerraUSD, which brought in the deposits that made the whole arrangement look liquid.

Withdrawals started the run. Large holders pulled their deposits from Anchor and sold TerraUSD, the price slipped to sixty cents on some exchanges, and the arbitrage that was supposed to restore it instead created more Luna. Luna's price fell, which meant each dollar of TerraUSD required more Luna to redeem, which pushed Luna down further, and the loop ran until both tokens were effectively worthless. The supply of Luna went from about three hundred and sixty-five million tokens to billions in a few days.

Contagion moved faster than the blockchains. Funds that had lent against Luna collateral marked their positions down, a large crypto lender froze withdrawals, and bitcoin and ether fell with the rest of the market as leveraged holders sold whatever they could to meet margin calls. The total value of the crypto market fell by hundreds of billions of dollars in the same week, and the failure was being described by regulators as the first systemic event of the asset class.

Legal aftermath arrived slowly. The founder, Do Kwon, had built a following by mocking the sceptics who questioned the design, and the collapse turned that audience against him; investigations in South Korea and the United States followed, and a warrant was issued months later, while the tokens themselves continued to trade on exchanges at prices set by people betting on the next failed experiment. What the episode demonstrated was less a bug in one algorithm than the absence of any reserve behind a token whose only backing was its sister, so that confidence, once gone, could be met with nothing except a printing press that accelerated the fall.

Algorithmic stablecoins were sold as a way to have a dollar without a bank, and the pitch rested on the claim that the market itself would supply the stability a reserve would otherwise provide. What May established is that the dollar in them is a promise about the behaviour of other traders, and promises of that kind hold until the first large holder decides to test them.
