---
title: "The AI Pin arrives without a screen"
date: 2023-11-09 20:45:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "Humane unveiled its AI Pin on 9 November 2023, a screenless wearable that projects images onto the palm and answers questions through a subscription service, at a price that made reviewers question the category."
---

Humane, a startup founded by two former Apple designers, unveiled the AI Pin on 9 November after three years of secrecy and a series of teasing videos. The device is a small rectangle that clips to a shirt with a magnet, weighs about thirty-four grams, and has no screen. It answers questions through a language model, projects text and images onto the palm of the hand with a laser, and takes photographs with a camera that sits above the lens.

Its pitch is that the phone has become an obstacle. The founders argue that people look down at a slab of glass for hours and that a device which speaks, listens and shows a little could return attention to the world. The product responds to touch and to gestures, and a light on the surface, called the trust light, comes on when the camera or microphone is active. There is a monthly subscription of twenty-four dollars for the connectivity, storage and the AI services behind it.

It costs six hundred and ninety-nine dollars, plus that subscription, and began shipping in the spring. Reviews at the launch were cautious about the premise and harsh about the execution. Testers found the projector hard to read in daylight, the gestures unreliable, and the battery short of a full day, and several noted that the answers arrived slowly over a cellular connection that sometimes failed. The camera was praised for its quality and criticized for the awkwardness of holding a hand up to frame a shot.

The technology underneath is a set of partnerships rather than a breakthrough. Humane built the software and the hardware and relies on a language model from a partner, a custom chip, and a cellular carrier for data. That arrangement means the device's capability moves with someone else's roadmap, and its cost structure includes a monthly fee for connectivity that a phone hides inside a bundle. Analysts described the model as an attempt to sell a service in the shape of a gadget.

The launch also landed in a crowded argument about what comes after the smartphone. Meta and Apple are pushing headsets, Google is putting models on phones, and a dozen startups have proposed pins, rings and glasses. Most have failed on the same points: battery, latency, and the social awkwardness of wearing a camera. Humane's answer is that the device does less than a phone and therefore asks less of the wearer.

Whether that answer holds will be decided by a market that has been unkind to hardware which claims to define a new category. The company raised more than two hundred million dollars from venture investors before shipping a product, and its backers include some of the largest names in Silicon Valley. The founders have said the first version is for people who want to try something new, and that the second will be for everyone else, which is a reasonable thing to say about a device that has to be explained before it can be sold.
