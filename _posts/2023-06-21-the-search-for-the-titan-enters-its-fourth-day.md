---
title: "The search for the Titan enters its fourth day"
date: 2023-06-21 20:15:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Aircraft and ships from five countries searched the North Atlantic on 21 June for a missing submersible that had taken five people to the wreck of the Titanic, with the vessel's estimated oxygen supply due to run out the following morning."
---

On 21 June, aircraft and vessels from Canada, the United States, Britain, France and Germany searched a patch of the North Atlantic about seven hundred kilometres south of St John's, Newfoundland, for a submersible that had not been heard from since it began a dive on the morning of 18 June. The craft, named Titan and operated by the American company OceanGate, was carrying five people to the wreck of the Titanic, which lies at a depth of about three thousand eight hundred metres. Sonar picked up what the searchers described as banging sounds, and the search area was expanded and then narrowed as the acoustic data was analysed.

The vessel was small and experimental. Titan was a five-metre carbon-fibre cylinder with a viewport at one end and no ability to surface on its own if the crew was incapacitated; it relied on ballast that could be released and on a dead-man mechanism that was supposed to bring it up after a set time. Its communications were limited to text messages sent to the surface, and the last message received indicated that it was descending. The company had been criticized for years by marine engineers and by former employees for the design and for its refusal to have the craft certified by an independent classification body.

Searching at that depth demonstrates how difficult underwater rescue is. The vehicles that can dive to the wreck's depth are few, and the remotely operated vehicle that could reach it was still being transported when the search began. Aircraft dropped sonobuoys and ships towed sonar arrays, and the coast guard said the sounds might have come from marine life or from the wreck itself. The oxygen supply was estimated at ninety-six hours from the start of the dive, which put the deadline at the morning of 22 June, and the estimate became the clock against which the operation was measured.

Five people were on board: a British businessman, a French diver who had visited the wreck more than thirty times, a Pakistani businessman and his teenage son, and the company's chief executive. Their families were kept informed and asked for privacy, and the questions that were put to OceanGate in the first days were about why a craft without certification was carrying passengers to a depth that no rescue capability could reach. The company had argued that innovation in deep water required risk, and that its passengers understood what they were buying.

By the evening of 21 June the search had covered a larger area than anyone expected and had found nothing. The following day would bring the answer, and it would be an answer that ended the search rather than extending it. What the four days had already established was the size of the gap between the tourist industry that has grown around the Titanic and the rescue capacity that exists at that depth, which is a gap that no amount of sonar can close.

Coast guard officials said the search would continue through the night and that more vehicles would arrive within hours. The families were told to prepare for the worst, and the company that sold the tickets had already stopped answering questions about the design.
