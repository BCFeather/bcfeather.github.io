---
title: "The road into Bakhmut gets shorter"
date: 2023-02-09 22:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Russian forces pressed the encirclement of Bakhmut through February 2023, cutting the supply routes into the town and turning a fight over a small city into the defining battle of the winter."
---

Russian forces closed on Bakhmut from the north and the south through February, and the two roads that supplied the Ukrainian garrison came under fire. The town had been contested since the previous summer, and the fall of Soledar in January had opened the approach from the north-east. By the middle of the month Ukrainian units were moving in and out along a single route that soldiers described as the road of life and that artillery could reach.

The forces doing the attacking were mixed. Regular Russian units, the Wagner group's convict recruits and the two republics' militias all took part, and the coordination between them was poor enough that Ukrainian officers described the assaults as waves rather than as operations. Wagner's owner, Yevgeny Prigozhin, publicly complained about the supply of ammunition, which was the first sign of a rivalry that would become a mutiny four months later. Prisoners released in exchanges described assault groups that had been given a few days of training and a rifle, which was the labour the tactics required.

Kyiv's decision to hold was political as much as military. Bakhmut had no particular strategic value, and analysts on both sides said so, but it had acquired the value of a name that had been defended for months. President Zelensky described it as a fortress, and the commander in chief, Valerii Zaluzhnyi, argued that the attrition imposed on the attackers was worth the cost to the defenders. That trade was contested inside the Ukrainian command and it was contested afterwards.

The cost was measured in men and in shells. Ukrainian units were taking casualties at rates that medics described as the worst of the war, and the shell ratio favoured Russia by a factor of three or four at the height of the fighting. Western supplies had not yet arrived in the quantities that had been promised, and the shortage of artillery ammunition was the subject of the first public disagreement between Kyiv and its partners. Neither side published casualty figures, and the medics who worked in the town gave numbers that were lower than what the units described. The numbers that circulated came from medics and from families rather than from staff officers.

By February the town had ceased to exist as a place. Before the war it had about seventy thousand residents, a salt mine, a wine factory and a park, and by February the population was a few thousand people living in basements. Reporters who reached the edge of the town described streets that could not be identified from a map, and a landscape of ruins that had become the image the war was known by.

What the battle changed was the shape of the war rather than its outcome. It absorbed Russian capacity for months and produced a few kilometres of ground, and it absorbed Ukrainian capacity for the same period. Both sides would describe it afterwards as a necessary sacrifice, and the fact that both descriptions were sincere is the reason the war had become one of attrition rather than of manoeuvre.
