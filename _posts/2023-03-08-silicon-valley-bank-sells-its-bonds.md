description: "Silicon Valley Bank announced on 8 March 2023 that it had sold most of its available-for-sale securities at a loss of 1.8 billion dollars and would raise 2.25 billion dollars in new capital, and its shares fell by sixty per cent."
---

The bank that lent to startups announced a plan on 8 March to repair its balance sheet, and the plan was the thing that broke it. It had sold twenty-one billion dollars of securities, mostly mortgage-backed bonds bought when rates were low, at a loss of 1.8 billion dollars. It also said it would raise 2.25 billion dollars through a share sale and a convertible issue, and it disclosed that its deposits had fallen by more than it had expected.

Behind the announcement lay arithmetic that was simple and public. Deposits had grown from about fifty billion dollars in 2019 to nearly two hundred billion in 2021, and the bank had invested much of the inflow in long-dated bonds that yielded little and lost value when the Federal Reserve began raising rates. Those losses were unrealized while the bonds sat in a held-to-maturity book, and they became real when the bank had to sell to pay depositors who were leaving.

Customers were the second half of the problem. Its depositors were companies rather than households, and their balances were far above the insurance limit of 250,000 dollars, and they talked to each other in group chats and on investor calls. A few large venture firms had begun advising their portfolio companies to move money out in the previous week, and the advice spread faster than any regulator could have responded.

By the close the share price had fallen by sixty per cent, and the fall continued in the after-hours market, and the bank's attempts to reassure its clients did not work. Its chief executive appeared on a call and asked clients to keep their money in the bank, and the request was reported as a sign that the money was already leaving. By the evening the bank had hired advisers to consider a sale, and by the following morning the regulators had closed it.

The disclosure rules are the part that the post-mortems concentrated on. A bank that has to sell securities at a loss and raise capital has to tell the market, and telling the market is what starts a run when the depositors are concentrated and uninsured. The choice between disclosure and stability is a real one, and the bank made it in the direction that the rules required, and the rules were written for a world in which depositors did not share information in real time. The disclosures that arrived in the summer showed that the bank had sold assets it had described as liquid, and that the description had been generous.

Two things about the failure were new and both were about speed. The deposits moved in a single day, in a quantity that would have taken weeks by cheque, and the run was visible on social media while it was happening. A bank that had been solvent by the accounting of the previous decade was insolvent by the liquidity of the current one, and the distance between the two was measured in hours.
