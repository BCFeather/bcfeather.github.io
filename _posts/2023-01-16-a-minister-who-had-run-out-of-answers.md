---
title: "A minister who had run out of answers"
date: 2023-01-16 22:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Christine Lambrecht resigned as Germany's defence minister on 16 January 2023 after a year of public missteps, and Boris Pistorius, the interior minister of Lower Saxony, was named as her successor."
---

Christine Lambrecht asked the chancellor to dismiss her on 16 January, and by the afternoon the most exposed post in the German cabinet was vacant. She had held the defence ministry since December 2021, when the coalition took office promising a turning point in security policy after the Russian attack on Ukraine. She was fifty-seven and had spent most of her career in justice and family policy rather than in procurement. She had asked for the job when the coalition was assembled in 2021, and the chancellor gave it to her because the alternative was a man from a different party.

Her year in the job had been punctuated by episodes that the tabloids treated as evidence of a minister out of her depth. The worst came on New Year's Eve, when she published a message about the war in Ukraine that had been recorded in the middle of Berlin's fireworks, with the explosions audible behind her voice. The recording was criticized by her own coalition partners before the holiday was over. The ministry's communications had been handled by a spokesman who resigned in the autumn, and the gap was visible in the months that followed.

The substance behind the irritation was the state of the armed forces. The government had created a fund of one hundred billion euros for equipment, and the ministry had struggled to turn the money into orders that the industry could deliver. Ammunition stocks were described by officers as sufficient for days of intensive fighting, recruitment was falling, and the inspector of the army had spoken in public about gaps that the fund would take a decade to close.

Boris Pistorius, the interior minister of Lower Saxony, was named as the successor on 17 January and received his credentials from the president on 19 January. He was sixty-two, had run the police and the fire services of a large state, and had no record in defence procurement. The choice surprised his own party as much as the opposition, and it was read as a preference for a competent administrator over a specialist. He took over a ministry that had been reorganized twice in two years, with a procurement office that answered to no single official.

The politics of the moment were unforgiving. The coalition was in its second year, its three parties were losing support in the polls, and the defence ministry had become the place where the government's promise of a new security policy was judged. A minister who cannot explain where a hundred billion euros went is a liability in a way that a minister with a different portfolio might not be.

A ministry that spends a hundred billion euros needs someone who can say, in a sentence, what the money bought. The chancellor's calculation was that a state interior minister might be able to learn that sentence faster than a lawyer from Mannheim. Whether he can do it is the question the spring will answer, and the answer will be counted in deliveries rather than in speeches.
