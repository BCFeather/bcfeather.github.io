---
title: "Watson and Crick described DNA in a paper of nine hundred words"
date: 2022-08-19 21:40:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "A reading guide to the 1953 Nature letter, and to the two other papers in the same issue that made it possible."
---

Nine hundred words in Nature on 25 April 1953 described the double helix, and the letter is shorter than the abstract of a modern paper on the same subject. James Watson was twenty-five and Francis Crick was thirty-six, and neither of them had been working on the problem for long. The model was built from the X-ray photographs and from the chemical rules that Erwin Chargaff had found. It ends with the sentence that has been quoted ever since, about the copying mechanism that the pairing suggests.

Evidence appears in the letter as a paragraph rather than a section, and the coordinates that would let a reader rebuild the model sit in a separate paper by the same authors. The text says the structure is plausible and that it fits the data, and it leaves the proof to the crystallographers. A modern referee would ask for the density measurements, the bond lengths and the refinement statistics, and the authors had fewer of them than a reader expects. That thinness is the first surprise of the paper.

Two other papers in the same issue are what a reader should open first. Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling published the X-ray data for the B form, and Maurice Wilkins and his colleagues published the data for the A form. The photograph known as 51 was taken by Gosling in May 1952, and the measurements that came from it were the ones that showed the helix. Franklin's paper is written in the careful, quantitative style that the model paper lacks, and it reaches a conclusion about the symmetry that the model had to satisfy.

History around the letter has been told at length and it is worth knowing before the text is read. Franklin's data reached Watson and Crick through a report that Max Perutz showed to Crick, and the arrangement was made without her knowledge. She published her own interpretation of the structure in the same issue, and the model she described was close to the one in the letter. Credit for the discovery has been argued over ever since, and the argument is about the practice of science as much as about the paper.

Method is the part a student can still learn from. A model that satisfies the constraints of chemistry and of the diffraction pattern is a hypothesis, and the letter presents it as one. Predictions that follow are specific: the pairs of bases, the two strands, the direction of the helix, the spacing of the turns. Each of those was tested in the following decade, and the ones that failed were replaced by the ones the later experiments supported.

What the letter shows is a style of writing that has gone out of fashion. The authors state a structure, name the evidence and stop, and the reader is trusted to do the work. A modern paper would begin with a survey of the field and end with a discussion of the implications, and it would run four times as long. Look for the sentence about the copying mechanism, which is the only place where the authors speculate, and for the citation to the other two papers, which is where the evidence sits. The letter is short because the claim was small and precise, and that is the part a reader can copy.
