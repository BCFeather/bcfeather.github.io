description: "Japan's SLIM spacecraft touched down on the moon on 19 January, landing within about fifty-five metres of its target but tipped onto its nose with its solar panels facing the wrong way, and was switched off after two hours with its mission unfinished."
---

Shortly after eight in the evening Japan time on the nineteenth of January, the Japanese space agency's SLIM lander cut its engine and fell the last few metres to the surface of the moon, and the control room in Sagamihara received the signal that every mission of this kind waits for. Controllers had aimed it at a patch of ground the size of a football pitch, a deliberately difficult target that no previous lander had attempted, and it came down inside a hundred metres of it, with the agency later putting the miss at about fifty-five metres. Japan became the fifth country to land a spacecraft on the moon, after the Soviet Union, the United States, China and India.

The photographs told the rest of the story a day later. One of the two small rovers the lander had carried, a baseball-sized sphere built with a toy company's expertise, had been released during the descent and was sending back images of the lander itself. In them the spacecraft lies on its nose, its main engine pointing up at the sky and its solar panels turned away from the sun. The landing had been soft in the sense that the spacecraft had not been destroyed and its instruments were intact, and it had come to rest in an attitude that made its power supply run out faster than planned. JAXA shut the lander down about two hours and forty minutes after touchdown, with enough battery left to keep the option of a revival open.

Explaining the tilt is the more interesting part. SLIM, short for Smart Lander for Investigating Moon, was designed to demonstrate what the agency calls a pinpoint landing, using a camera that matches the surface below against maps built from earlier orbiters and adjusting the descent as it goes. Its main engine lost part of its thrust during the final phase, possibly because of a blockage in the fuel line, and the guidance software compensated by firing the two small attitude thrusters more than intended. One of them was already hot from the descent. It came down with a lateral drift that it could not fully correct, and it touched one of its legs first on a slope, which is enough to turn a landing into a tumble.

Science matters more than the landing pose. SLIM carries a multi-band spectral camera designed to look at the composition of the lunar mantle, the material that the giant impact that formed the moon is thought to have thrown up, and the site chosen for the landing, near the Shioli crater on the slope of a larger basin, is a place where that material is exposed. The two rovers, LEV-1 and LEV-2, are intended to move independently and to send images back through the lander. If the lander's battery can be revived when the sun moves round to the panels, there may still be data. If not, the mission will be remembered as a precise landing that ended on its face.

What the month shows, taken together with the American spacecraft that failed on the way to the moon a week earlier, is that lunar landing in the 2020s is a business of many attempts with narrow margins. Governments and companies are now sending small, cheap spacecraft to the moon at a rate that has not been seen since the 1970s, and the failure rate reflects that. Four of the six landing attempts of the past twelve months have ended in crashes or lost missions. The two that succeeded did so with difficulties that the mission teams are still working through, and both of them, in the end, put instruments on the surface and returned data that no telescope could have collected from orbit. That is the point of the exercise, and it is the reason the attempts will continue.
