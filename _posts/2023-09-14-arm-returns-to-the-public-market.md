---
title: "Arm returns to the public market"
date: 2023-09-14 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Arm Holdings priced its initial public offering at fifty-one dollars a share on 13 September 2023, valuing the chip designer at about fifty-four billion dollars, and began trading on Nasdaq the next day."
---

Arm sold shares at fifty-one dollars on 13 September, above the range it had been marketing, and the stock opened at about fifty-six dollars when trading began on Nasdaq the next morning. The offer valued the British chip designer at about fifty-four billion dollars, and SoftBank, which has owned the company since 2016, kept about ninety per cent of the shares and therefore control. The deal raised nearly five billion dollars, most of which went to the Japanese investor.

Its business is licensing designs rather than making chips. Arm develops instruction sets and processor layouts that other firms embed in their products, and it charges a licence fee and a royalty on each unit sold. Its designs are in nearly every smartphone, in cars, in data centres and in connected devices, and the royalties are small per chip and enormous in aggregate. The company reported revenue of about 2.7 billion dollars in its last fiscal year, with a third of it from China.

This listing bets on two markets. One is the mobile business that Arm has supplied for two decades, where Apple, Qualcomm and Samsung are licensees. The other is artificial intelligence and data centres, where Nvidia, which tried to buy Arm for forty billion dollars in 2020 before regulators blocked the deal, is building processors that combine Arm cores with its own graphics units. Arm has been signing deals with cloud providers and with carmakers, and it told investors that its royalties per device would rise as those markets grow.

SoftBank motives explain the timing, and so does the state of the market for chip stocks. The Japanese group bought Arm for thirty-two billion dollars and has been trying to capture some of that value, and it has been under pressure from investors after losses in its other funds. It kept a large stake so that it can still benefit from the upside, and it chose a Nasdaq listing because American investors pay more for chip companies than British ones do. The British government, which had wanted the company to list in London and had lobbied SoftBank for it, was disappointed and said so.

The risks are visible in the prospectus. Arm depends on a small number of customers, on licences that renew on multi-year terms, and on a Chinese joint venture whose governance the company does not fully control. A slowdown in phone sales reduces royalties, and a shift in how chips are designed could reduce the value of an instruction set that others must be persuaded to keep using. The valuation also assumes margins that Arm has not always delivered, and a growth rate that its own filings describe as uncertain.

A British company that designs the chips inside most of the world's phones has returned to the public market with an American listing and a Japanese owner. The offering will be judged by whether the artificial intelligence business grows fast enough to justify a valuation built on the phone business that made it, and by how much of that business SoftBank eventually decides to sell.
