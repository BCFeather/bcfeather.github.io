description: "A Japan Airlines Airbus A350 landing at Haneda on the evening of 2 January collided with a coast guard aircraft that had entered the runway, and the five crew members of the smaller plane died while all 379 people on the airliner escaped."
---

Shortly before six in the evening on the second of January, Japan Airlines flight 516 from New Chitose touched down on runway 34R at Haneda, the runway that faces north-northwest into Tokyo Bay, and within seconds its nose was on fire. Flight 516, an Airbus A350-900 carrying 379 people, had struck a Japan Coast Guard De Havilland Dash 8 that was standing on the same runway. Coast guard plane MA722 was based at Haneda and had been loaded with relief supplies for the earthquake zone on the Japan Sea coast. Five of its six crew died, and the captain, seriously injured, was the only survivor. All 379 passengers and crew on the airliner got out, most of them down the escape slides, in about eighteen minutes, with a handful of injuries and no deaths.

Videos that circulated within an hour showed the ordinary horror of the thing. It comes in on a normal approach, nose slightly high, and then a bright orange flash runs along the left side of the fuselage as the smaller aircraft is crushed underneath. It keeps rolling, stops, and the evacuation begins while the forward section burns. Fire crews were on the scene within three minutes, and the aircraft burned for hours, collapsing onto its belly as the fuselage aft of the wing gave way. Aviation photographers had been at the airport that evening for the New Year; within a day their pictures were the most-viewed images of the disaster, and within two days the wreckage had been lifted onto trucks and taken away.

Attention turned quickly to the question that always decides these cases, which is why two aircraft were on one runway. Japanese investigators, with Airbus and the French accident bureau assisting, began from the coast guard side, because the smaller plane had been cleared to hold at a point near the runway but had moved onto it. Recordings of the tower frequency circulated and were disputed. Recordings suggest the smaller aircraft was told to taxi to a holding position and to contact the tower again before entering the runway, and it appears to have entered anyway. Whether the phraseology was ambiguous, whether the crew was distracted by the urgency of the relief flight, or whether the controller lost situational awareness during a busy evening at one of the world's most congested airports, are questions that will take months to answer properly.

Haneda itself is part of the problem. It has four runways, sits on reclaimed land in the middle of a city of fourteen million people, and handles more than forty million passengers a year on a tight footprint. Its runway layout forces aircraft to cross active strips on the way to parking, and its curfew, traffic mix and short taxi distances compress the time in which controllers and crews have to make decisions. Every one of those constraints is known, studied and managed, and the airport has an excellent safety record. What the accident shows is that a system can be well managed and still contain a step where a single misheard instruction is enough.

A comparison with the earthquake is unavoidable, because the two disasters happened twenty-four hours apart and touched the same organisation. Those aboard the Dash 8 were on their way to Niigata with supplies for the communities cut off by the Noto earthquake. A plane that was part of the response to one disaster was destroyed by another, and the relief flight never left. That detail will appear in the accident reports as a line in the sequence of events, and it says something about how tightly coupled the infrastructure of a wealthy country has become. The runway, the relief flight, the earthquake and the airport's afternoon traffic were all in the same system, and the system failed at the point where two of its parts met.
