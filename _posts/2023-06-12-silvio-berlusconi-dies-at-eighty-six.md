---
title: "Silvio Berlusconi dies at eighty-six"
date: 2023-06-12 16:00:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Silvio Berlusconi, who built a media empire and used it to win three elections and to dominate Italian public life for three decades, died in Milan on 12 June at the age of eighty-six."
---

Silvio Berlusconi died at the San Raffaele hospital in Milan on 12 June, at the age of eighty-six, after a career that changed what an Italian politician was allowed to be. He was a property developer who built a national television business when the law gave him room, then entered politics in 1994 with a party he had invented a few months earlier, and won three elections with it. His funeral will be held in the cathedral in Milan, and the state has announced a day of national mourning.

His business career was the foundation of his politics. He assembled his television holdings in the 1980s while the rules that would have constrained him were being written, and he spent the following decade in litigation with the courts and with the European Commission over the size of his advertising market. When his political career began, he argued that the state had been captured by former communists and that he was the only person with the resources to take it back. That claim, delivered with the confidence of a salesman, proved durable enough to be repeated at every election he fought.

His governments were long on communication and short on reform. He cut taxes, changed the rules on his own conflicts of interest, passed laws that reduced his exposure to prosecution, and governed for a total of nine years across four administrations. He survived corruption trials, a conviction for tax fraud, a ban from public office that was later lifted, and a reputational campaign against him by much of the Italian press, and he remained the most recognizable politician in the country through all of it. His foreign policy was uncomplicated by comparison: he was a reliable American ally and a friend of whoever was in the White House at the time.

His personal conduct produced the scandals that his opponents expected and his supporters tolerated. The parties at his villas, the trials over payments to young women, the years of legal battles and the eventual conviction were all absorbed into a public image that had been built on the promise of a good time. He was, in a way that few leaders of a democracy have been, a figure who was judged on whether he made the country feel better, and enough Italians decided that he did to keep him in contention until his final years.

His legacy is contested in exactly the way he would have expected. He is credited with modernizing Italian politics and television, and blamed for the personalized, media-centred politics that followed him, which now belongs to parties he did not build. His company passed to his family with his death, and the coalition he founded continues under a prime minister who was his protégée. Italy has not finished with him, and it will not, because the style of politics of which he was the first practitioner is now the ordinary style of the age.

The funeral will be a state occasion, and the obituaries will disagree about the man in the way that his career invited. What nobody disputes is that he made Italian public life a performance, and that the performance has continued since he left it.
