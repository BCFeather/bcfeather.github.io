---
title: "Ukraine takes the war to Russian ports"
date: 2023-08-04 23:35:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Ukrainian sea drones damaged a Russian landing ship at Novorossiysk on 4 August 2023 and hit a tanker near the Kerch Strait the next day, extending the campaign that began after Russia left the grain deal."
---

Two sea drones crossed the harbour defences at Novorossiysk before dawn on 4 August and struck the Olenegorsky Gornyak, a large landing ship, which listed and was towed to the yard. The second came the next night, when a drone hit the Sig, a Russian chemical tanker under sanctions, near the Kerch Strait. Ukrainian officials claimed both, published the footage, and described the target set as the fleet and the fuel that supplies it.

The campaign is an answer to a decision. Russia left the grain corridor on 17 July and began striking Ukraine's ports, and Ukraine's reply has been to make the Russian side of the Black Sea unsafe in the same way. Its naval commander said in July that ships heading for Russian or occupied ports would be treated as military targets, and the drone raids are the part of that policy that can be photographed. The weapons are small, cheap and locally made, and they have done what a navy without ships can do.

Defences have adapted and not enough. Novorossiysk is the fleet's main base and its harbour is protected by booms, nets and gun crews, which did not stop two machines the size of a kayak. Russian military bloggers complained about the gaps, and the fleet has begun moving ships eastwards and drilling crews in anti-drone drills. Satellite images showed the damaged landing ship in dry dock within days, which is a fact the ministry did not publish.

Trade has been rearranged around the risk. Insurers have refused cover for the Russian ports, freight rates in the region have risen, and Russia has rerouted some oil cargoes. Ukraine's own exports restarted through a temporary corridor along the Romanian and Bulgarian coasts, with the first ship leaving Odesa in the middle of the month. The insurance for those voyages is arranged by brokers in London, which means the volume of grain that moves depends on a calculation made in an office far from either capital. The two countries are now conducting the same argument about who may sail where, and neither has a navy that can settle it.

The military effect is cumulative rather than decisive. Two ships damaged do not change the war, and they do change the calculations of every captain, insurer and port manager on both sides. Russia can absorb the losses and cannot easily protect a coastline of hundreds of kilometres against small craft launched from ashore. Ukraine can repeat the raids and cannot follow them with anything heavier. Officers in Kyiv describe the attacks as attrition against a fleet that cannot be replaced quickly, and they expect to repeat them.

Both sides now publish maps of the water they consider dangerous, and the two maps overlap. A grain deal ended in July and a shipping war began in August. The Black Sea has become a place where the weaker fleet sets the terms of insurance and the stronger one sets the terms of terror, and the ships that carry food and fuel are caught between the two.
