---
title: "Google's search monopoly goes on trial"
date: 2023-09-13 23:35:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "The American government's antitrust case against Google opened in Washington on 12 September 2023, with prosecutors arguing that the company used exclusive deals to keep rivals out of the market for search and search advertising."
---

Trial opened on 12 September in the federal courthouse in Washington, before Judge Amit Mehta, three years after the Department of Justice filed the case. Lawyers for the government argued that Google had maintained an illegal monopoly in two markets, general search and search advertising, by paying to be the default engine on devices and browsers and by signing agreements that made it the only search service on some screens. Google answered that its product won because people chose it, and that being the default can be changed by a user in a few taps.

The payments are the heart of the case. Google pays Apple billions of dollars a year to be the default search engine on the iPhone and Safari, and it has similar arrangements with phone makers and with browser developers. Internal documents introduced in court put the figure at eighteen billion dollars a year for the Apple deal, and lawyers for the government argued that the payment buys no service a customer would recognize, and instead buys the only distribution channel that rivals could have used.

Google's defence rests on quality and on choice. Its lawyers said that people use Google because it is better, and that the growth in queries and in advertising has come from that quality rather than from the deals. They also pointed out that Microsoft's Bing is installed on every Windows machine and has a small share of the market, which is the kind of evidence that defaults rarely decide the outcome. The judge will have to decide whether the default matters enough to count as exclusion.

Witnesses have been revealing about the business. Executives have described meetings in which the payment to Apple was discussed as the price of protection against a competitor who might otherwise take the space, and messages have shown executives worried about losing the deal. Former employees have described an internal culture that treated search as a property to be defended rather than a product to be sold, and the judge has been reading documents that Google would have preferred to keep sealed.

The case is one of several that the government has brought against large platforms. Suits against Meta and Amazon are before other judges, and the Federal Trade Commission is pursuing a case against Amazon over its marketplace practices, with a trial date that has not yet been set. The Google trial is the first to reach a courtroom, and its outcome will shape how the others are argued, because the legal question of what counts as a monopolist's conduct is the same in each.

A verdict is months away, because the judge has said he will hear the evidence in full before he rules, and a remedy, if there is one, could change how search appears on a phone that most Americans carry. The trial is about a default setting, which is the kind of thing that the law has been slow to see as a market, and about whether the biggest company in search can be made to compete for a screen it already owns.
