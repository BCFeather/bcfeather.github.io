---
title: "Six point five: a peak that had passed"
date: 2023-01-12 23:10:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "United States consumer prices rose 6.5 per cent in the year to December 2022, the sixth consecutive decline from the peak of 9.1 per cent in June, and the lowest rate since October 2021."
---

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on 12 January that consumer prices in December were 6.5 per cent higher than a year earlier, down from 7.1 per cent in November and from the peak of 9.1 per cent in June. Prices fell by 0.1 per cent on the month. The annual rate was the lowest since October 2021 and the sixth consecutive decline. The report came a day after the December payroll figures, which showed 223,000 jobs added and a slowing in wage growth.

The composition of the number mattered more than its level. Energy prices fell sharply, with petrol down more than a tenth over the year, and the fall in fuel did most of the work of bringing the headline rate down. Food prices were still rising, and shelter, which enters the index with a lag, was the largest single contributor to the monthly increase.

Core inflation, which excludes food and energy, was 5.7 per cent over the year. That figure fell more slowly than the headline, and it is the one the Federal Reserve watches when it decides how fast to raise rates. The central bank had raised its benchmark rate by half a point in December, after four consecutive increases of three quarters of a point, and the market read the December report as a reason for a quarter-point move at the next meeting. The ten-year Treasury yield fell by about ten basis points on the morning of the release, and the dollar weakened against the euro.

What 6.5 per cent does not say is that prices stopped rising. It says that they rose by less than they had been rising, and that a household buying the same basket as a year ago is spending about a sixteenth more. Cumulative inflation over two years was roughly eleven per cent, which is the number that appears in wage negotiations and in the complaints of shoppers. The index is a weighted average and its weights are revised every two years, which means the basket being compared is never exactly the same basket.

Labour market conditions were the reason the decline was expected to be slow. Unemployment was 3.5 per cent, near a fifty-year low, and the ratio of vacancies to unemployed workers was still close to two to one. Services inflation, which follows wages, was running at an annual rate well above the target, and the Fed's problem was that the part of the index that responds to interest rates was the part that had already fallen.

A peak is a direction and not a level. The report told the market where the year had turned, and it left open how long the descent would take and what it would cost in employment to make it faster. The descent had taken seven months to travel from nine per cent to six and a half, and the last stretch to two per cent was the one the Fed had warned would be slow.
