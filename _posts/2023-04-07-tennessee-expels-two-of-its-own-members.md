---
title: "Tennessee expels two of its own members"
date: 2023-04-07 22:20:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Tennessee House expelled two Democratic members on 6 April 2023 over a protest against gun laws after a school shooting, the first expulsions of legislators in the state in decades, and both were returned to their seats within a week."
---

Tennessee's House of Representatives voted on 6 April to expel two of its Democratic members, Justin Jones of Nashville and Justin Pearson of Memphis, for breaking the rules of decorum when they led a protest from the well of the chamber a week earlier. The vote against Jones was 72 to 25 and against Pearson 69 to 26, both well above the two thirds required. A third member, Gloria Johnson of Knoxville, survived by a single vote, which she attributed to the colour of her skin.

A protest followed the shooting at a private Christian school in Nashville on 27 March, when six people were killed, three of them nine years old. Thousands of students walked out of schools in the following days and gathered at the Capitol, and the three members joined them with a megaphone and then brought the chant into the chamber. Republican leaders said the members had brought disorder into a place that runs on rules and that the punishment was proportionate.

The expulsions were the first in the state since 1866, and the process took less than a week. Each member was given a hearing of a few minutes, no witnesses were called, and the debate on the floor lasted about an hour. The speaker described the vote as a matter of order rather than of politics, and the expelled members described it as a matter of politics dressed as order, which is the argument that both sides needed to make. The chamber had not expelled anyone since 1866, and the precedents it cited came from the Reconstruction era.

Public reaction was immediate and large. The Vice President visited Nashville on the Friday, the President telephoned the three members and invited them to the White House, and donations to the two expelled members passed two million dollars within a week. County commissions in Nashville and Memphis appointed them back as interim representatives, one on 10 April and the other on 12 April, and both said they would run in the special elections that follow.

The constitutional question is narrow and unresolved. The state constitution allows the House to expel a member with a two-thirds vote, and it does not say for what, which is a power that most legislatures hold and almost none uses. A federal lawsuit filed by one of the members argued that expelling elected representatives for the content of their speech violates the First Amendment, and the case will proceed whether or not its subject is sitting in the chamber.

What the week demonstrated was the distance between procedure and legitimacy. Every step the House took was authorized by a rule that had existed for a century and a half, and the result was two members removed from a body where their constituents had placed them by a margin of thousands of votes. The rules were followed, and the outcome was judged illegitimate by a large part of the public, which is a description of a problem rather than a remedy.
