---
title: "Trump threatens tariffs of twenty-five per cent on Canada and Mexico"
date: 2024-11-26 20:50:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "The president-elect said on 25 November that he would impose a 25 per cent tariff on imports from Canada and Mexico on his first day in office and add ten points to existing duties on Chinese goods unless the three countries acted on drugs and migration."
---

President-elect Donald Trump said on the twenty-fifth of November that he would sign an order imposing a 25 per cent tariff on all imports from Canada and Mexico on his first day in office, and that he would add ten percentage points to existing tariffs on Chinese goods. Announcement was made on his social media account and tied the measures to fentanyl trafficking and to migration across the two land borders. Mexican and Canadian governments responded within hours, and financial markets moved on the news before American trading opened.

Legal basis for the promise is thinner than its political weight. Tariffs on this scale would normally require an investigation and a finding under the trade statutes, and the incoming administration has said it will use emergency powers that allow the president to act when a national emergency is declared. That route has been used for targeted measures and has not been tested for across-the-board duties on allies, and litigation from affected industries is expected whatever the justification offered.

Economic effect would fall on three countries at once. Canada and Mexico send about 40 per cent of their exports to the United States, and the integrated supply chains of the car industry cross the borders several times before a vehicle is finished. American importers would pay the duty and pass on much of it, which is a price increase in the year the administration has promised to reduce prices. Peso and Canadian dollar both fell against the dollar, and shares of carmakers with plants in Mexico dropped.

Threat fits a pattern that began in the first term and has been extended since. Tariffs on Chinese goods, imposed in 2018 and retained by the following administration, cover more than three hundred billion dollars of trade. New measures announced during the campaign included a ten per cent baseline tariff on all imports and duties of up to twenty per cent on goods from countries with large trade surpluses, and the announcement of the twenty-fifth of November is the first specific commitment since the election.

Response from the two neighbours was measured and pointed. Canadian prime minister said the two countries' economies are integrated and that a tariff would raise costs for American consumers, and he noted that Canada supplies a large share of the crude oil, electricity and critical minerals the United States buys. Mexican president said the country would respond with its own measures if the threat was carried out, and pointed to the shared border and to the migration cooperation that the tariff is meant to compel.

Question that remains open is whether the announcement is a negotiating position or a policy. Both neighbours have already agreed to tighten border enforcement and have been doing so for years, which gives them little room to offer more. If the tariffs take effect in January, they will be the largest increase in American duties since the 1930s and will be met with retaliation. If they are withdrawn in exchange for concessions, the announcement will be remembered as leverage. Markets have priced a probability rather than an outcome.
