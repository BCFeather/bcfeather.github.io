title: "The start of spring on the calendar and the cold outside"

The solar term that opens the spring falls on the third of February this year, and the day is marked by a household ritual that has nothing to do with the weather. A thin pancake is made, a plate of shredded vegetables and a sweet sauce are laid out, and the family rolls the vegetables in the pancake and eats it standing in a kitchen that is still heated.

The term is one of twenty-four in a calendar that divides the year by the position of the sun rather than by the moon. The spring begins when the sun reaches a particular point in its path, and the date falls between the second and the fifth of February. The calendar is agricultural in origin and it is still used to schedule the planting in the countryside, which is why the term appears in the almanac and the weather forecast.

The food of the day is a pun as much as a dish. The pancake is the spring roll in its earliest form, the radish is eaten raw for the bite it gives, and the name of the practice means to take a bite of the spring. The point of the ritual is to eat something fresh in a season when nothing is fresh, and the vegetable that is used is often the one that was stored in a cellar.

The weather that the day produces is usually the coldest part of the winter. The solar term is a mark on the sun's path rather than a description of the temperature, and the frost that arrives in the first week of February is often the hardest of the year. The gap between the calendar and the weather is a national joke, and it is also the reason the almanac gives a second date for the warmth.

The agricultural logic of the term is a set of windows. The wheat is sown after the spring begins in the northern plain, the rice is raised in a nursery in the south, and the orchard is pruned in the week that the term falls. The dates are old and they have been adjusted by the agronomists, and the adjustment is usually a fortnight later than the traditional date.

The ritual has been revived in the cities as a food festival. A restaurant sells a spring pancake in February, a supermarket puts the radish in a display at the entrance, and a school asks the children to draw the character for spring. The revival is thin and it is real, and the day is one of the few in the solar calendar that a young person in a city can name.

What the term shows is that a calendar can be a promise rather than a description. The spring is officially here and the thermometer disagrees, and the household that eats the pancake is choosing to side with the calendar. The choice is old and it is the reason the date has survived the weather.
