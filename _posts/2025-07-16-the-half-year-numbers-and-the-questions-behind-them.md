The second-quarter figures were published in the middle of July, and the headline was respectable. Growth for the quarter came in above five per cent, the half-year total was in line with the target, and the usual round of commentary followed. The interesting information sat underneath the headline, in four numbers that receive less attention and explain more.

The first is the split between production and consumption. Industrial output grew faster than retail sales, which has been the pattern for two years. A country that produces more than it consumes has to sell the difference abroad or store it, and the trade surplus has been absorbing a large part of it. That works until it does not, and the constraint this year has been the tariff schedule rather than the factories.

The second is the price numbers. Producer prices have been falling for a long stretch, and consumer prices have been close to zero. Falling prices at the factory gate mean that the firms at the bottom of the supply chain are absorbing a margin squeeze, and their response has been to cut costs by cutting hours rather than by cutting staff. A shorter working week shows up in the statistics as resilience in employment and as weakness in household income.

The third is property. The sector has been shrinking for four years, and the official numbers this quarter showed a smaller decline in investment and a continued decline in sales. The mechanism is well understood now. Local governments that relied on land sales have less money, so infrastructure spending has slowed, and the households that own a flat whose value is falling feel poorer and spend less. Both effects are slow and both are real.

The fourth is exports. Shipments to Asia and to Europe have grown and shipments to America have fallen, which is what a tariff does when it is applied. Firms have rerouted, moved final assembly to third countries, and renegotiated. The adjustment is expensive and it has worked well enough that the export volume has not collapsed.

The question the numbers cannot answer is whether the economy can rebalance from investment and exports toward household spending. The instruments for that are fiscal rather than monetary. A central bank can cut a rate and cheapen credit, and cheap credit in a country with a high savings rate tends to go into assets rather than into consumption. Transfers to households, better social insurance and a smaller burden of precautionary saving would work through a different channel, and they are all budget decisions.

There is also the question of what the numbers are measured against. A growth rate that is respectable in a country with a young population is a different achievement from the same rate in a country whose workforce is shrinking, and the second is the situation here. The published figures are an audit of the past quarter. The decisions that matter for the next two years are being made in a different building, and they will not appear in a statistics release until they have already had their effect.
