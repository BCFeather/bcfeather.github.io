---
title: "India lands a rover near the lunar south pole"
date: 2023-08-23 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "The Indian Space Research Organisation's Chandrayaan-3 mission set its Vikram lander down near the lunar south pole on 23 August 2023, making India the fourth country to land softly on the moon and the first to reach that region."
---

The Vikram lander touched down at 18:04 Indian time on 23 August, after a descent of about nineteen minutes that the space agency broadcast live to an audience of millions. It came to rest on a plain at roughly sixty-nine degrees south, closer to the pole than any spacecraft has landed, and it began sending pictures within hours. India is now the fourth country to put a machine on the moon, after the Soviet Union, the United States and China, and the first to do so at that latitude.

Chandrayaan-3 is small and cheap by the standards of the competition. Chandrayaan-3 carries a rover and a set of instruments that will measure the soil's composition, its temperature and the plasma around it, and it cost about seventy-five million dollars, less than a Hollywood film about space. It was built largely from the spare hardware of Chandrayaan-2, the orbiter that reached the moon in 2019 and whose lander crashed on the final descent.

The south pole is the prize. Permanently shadowed craters there are thought to hold water ice, and the water would supply fuel, oxygen and drinking water for any long stay. The Indian instruments are designed to look for hydrogen and to measure the temperature of the top layer of soil, which no probe has done at that latitude. Every agency with a lunar programme now aims at the same region, and the interest has turned a scientific question into a territorial one, with the United States leading a group of signatories to its Artemis accords and China building its own coalition around a research station.

Setbacks preceded the success. Russia's Luna-25, its first lunar mission since 1976 and launched earlier in August, crashed into the surface two days before the Indian landing, and the failure was reported in Moscow with unusual candour. The Indian mission itself had to be delayed by the pandemic and by tests that followed the 2019 crash, and the agency's chairman said the landing had been rehearsed through a series of software changes meant to survive any sensor failure.

Politics at home followed the landing immediately. The prime minister watched the descent at a space centre in Bengaluru and told the control room that the country had reached a place no other had reached, and newspapers compared the landing with the moon mission that the country's first prime minister had funded in the 1960s. India's space budget has grown, and the agency has been told to prepare a crewed mission and a Venus orbiter within a decade, with a second lunar sample return in between.

A rover is now driving on a plain near the pole, and its instruments will spend a lunar day, which is fourteen Earth days, measuring what is there before the cold night ends the mission, as it ended the previous rover's after two weeks. India has bought itself a place in the next round of lunar exploration, and the round after that will be about who gets to stay.
