---
title: "Finland boards a tanker suspected of cutting a Baltic power cable"
date: 2024-12-28 21:05:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Finnish police and border guards boarded the tanker Eagle S on 26 December and took it into custody after the Estlink 2 power cable between Finland and Estonia broke on Christmas Day, treating the case as aggravated criminal mischief."
---

Finnish border guard boarded the tanker Eagle S on the twenty-sixth of December and brought it under control near the port of Porvoo, a day after the Estlink 2 electricity cable between Finland and Estonia failed. Cable, which carries several hundred megawatts between the two countries, broke on the twenty-fifth of December, and the transmission operator Fingrid reported it to the authorities that afternoon. Police took charge of the investigation and said the case was being treated as aggravated criminal mischief.

Evidence pointed to the anchor. Investigators said the vessel's port-side anchor was missing and that a drag mark had been found on the seabed along the cable route, and the border guard published photographs of the ship after it was detained. Ship's track shows it passing over the cable in the early hours of Christmas Day at low speed, and the cable operator said the damage was consistent with a heavy object being dragged across it. Repair was estimated at months rather than weeks, and the two countries were able to keep supply balanced in the meantime.

Vessel itself is part of the story. Eagle S sails under the flag of the Cook Islands, was carrying a cargo of unleaded petrol from a Russian port, and belongs to a class of ageing tankers bought after the invasion of Ukraine to move Russian oil outside the western insurance and price-cap system. Crew were questioned, and the ship was held while the investigation continued. Finnish authorities said they had acted under national law on critical infrastructure and that the boarding had been carried out with the consent of the master.

Response from governments was immediate. Estonian and Finnish prime ministers issued a joint statement describing the incident as part of a pattern of hostile activity in the Baltic, and NATO said it would increase patrols in the Gulf of Finland. European Commission president called it a deliberate attempt to damage critical infrastructure, and the EU's foreign policy chief said the episode showed how exposed the region's cables and pipelines are. Russian authorities said the ship had nothing to do with the damage and that the detention was unlawful.

Context matters more than the single case. Several cables and a gas pipeline in the Baltic were damaged in the preceding two years, including the Balticconnector pipeline in 2023, and each incident was followed by the same questions about attribution and about the legal basis for stopping a ship in international waters. Region's infrastructure sits in shallow water, is surveyed by few vessels, and is protected by rules written for a world in which shipping was mostly commercial and mostly benign.

Broader consequence is the shift in how the Baltic is policed. Navies and coast guards in the region have begun to treat cable routes as objects of surveillance rather than as passive infrastructure, and a NATO mission announced in January will station ships and drones along them. Legal question of what a coastal state may do to a foreign-flagged vessel suspected of damaging a cable remains unsettled, and Finland's answer, that it may board and detain one, will be tested in court long before it is settled in law.
