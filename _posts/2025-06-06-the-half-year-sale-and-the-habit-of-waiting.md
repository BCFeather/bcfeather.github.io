The mid-year sale opens its pre-sale window in the middle of May, a full month before the date it is named after. Shoppers put down small deposits to hold a price, collect coupons from three different places inside the same app, and wait for a midnight that the platform has decided is the real one. The date itself, the eighteenth of June, marks the founding of JD.com in 1998, and a company anniversary has become a national shopping season with a membership wedded to it.

The arithmetic is the interesting part. A discount is rarely a single discount. It is a deposit that doubles in value, a platform coupon with a threshold, a store coupon with a different threshold, a rebate paid after the fact and a payment method that subtracts a few more yuan if the shopper uses the right wallet. The customer who can hold all of that in their head saves money. The customer who cannot saves less, and occasionally pays more than they would have in April. Consumer groups have complained about this for years, and the rules on promotional pricing have tightened, and the arithmetic has survived in a slightly shorter form.

Retailers learn the same arithmetic from the other side. Inventory has to arrive before the pre-sale window opens, which means factories plan production in February for a sale in June. Warehouses hire temporary staff weeks early. The logistics network reaches its peak twice a year now, in June and in November, and the difference between a good peak and a bad one is measured in the number of parcels that arrive a day late.

What has changed lately is the mood of the shopper rather than the structure of the sale. Trade-in programmes for appliances and phones now sit inside the platform checkout, and the subsidies that were meant to be a stimulus have become part of how the discount is calculated. People buy the larger item and keep the smaller one another year. Big-ticket categories have grown, the small impulse purchases have not. The shopper who once filled a cart with phone cases and socks now waits for a subsidy before replacing an air conditioner.

There is a version of the sale that is purely a ritual. The numbers announced afterwards are huge and slightly opaque, the marketing is relentless for six weeks, and the actual saving on most goods is modest. There is another version in which a household replaces a washing machine it had been meaning to replace since March, and gets a fair price for the old one, and that is a genuine improvement in how the year is organised.

Both versions are true at once, which is why the festival survives. It gives the half-year a shape, a moment when the country's shopping is concentrated into a few days and the warehouses run at their limit. Whether that shape is worth the coupon arithmetic is a question the shopper answers every year, by deciding how many platforms to check before midnight.
