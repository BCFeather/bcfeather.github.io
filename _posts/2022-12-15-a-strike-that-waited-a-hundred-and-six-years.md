---
title: "A strike that waited a hundred and six years"
date: 2022-12-15 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Nurses in England, Wales and Northern Ireland walk out for the first time in the history of the Royal College of Nursing."
---
Picket lines went up before dawn on 15 December, outside hospitals that had been told which services would be exempt. Members of the Royal College of Nursing left their posts for the day, and the college said that around a hundred thousand of them had voted to take part. It was the first national strike in the college's history, which dates from 1916, and the first of two days that month. Banners were printed with the names of wards rather than slogans, and the queues at the hospital gates were photographed from the road.

The dispute is about pay, and the argument about the number is as much about arithmetic as about principle. The college asked for a rise of five per cent above inflation, which at the time would have meant something close to nineteen per cent. The government had awarded a rise of roughly four per cent through the pay review body, and it said that anything more would have to come out of the health budget itself. Both sides used the word fair, and the word meant different things in each of their sentences.

Staffing is the other half of the case, and it is harder to put on a placard. Nurses described wards where a single nurse was responsible for a dozen patients, and shifts that ended with the work unfinished. Vacancies had been filled by agency staff at rates several times the standard, and the difference was paid from the same budget that funds the permanent posts. The college published a survey of its members in which most of them said they had considered leaving the profession during the year.

Ministers argued that the strike would harm patients, and the college argued that understaffing harms them every day. Life-preserving care was exempt, and cancer treatment, intensive care and emergency departments ran with senior staff drafted in from elsewhere. Operations were postponed in their thousands, and appointments were canceled in a way that had become routine during the pandemic. The health service said that the impact had been less severe than it feared, which both sides claimed as evidence.

Public argument about who was to blame turned on the word independent. The pay review body is described as independent, and its recommendations are accepted or rejected by the government of the day. Unions pointed out that the body had been given a remit that assumed a particular level of spending, and that a review under those terms could produce only one answer. The government replied that the process had been followed, and that a rise above the recommendation would require money that had not been voted.

By the afternoon the second day had been announced for 20 December, and further dates were being discussed for January. The college's leadership said it would keep talking, and the government said the same, and both statements were issued within an hour of one another. Nothing in either suggested a way out before Christmas, and the emergency departments were preparing for a holiday period that is always the hardest of the year.
