---
title: "France bars under-fifteens from social media and Europe follows"
date: 2026-07-21 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Parliament passed the ban on 21 July, with new accounts blocked from September and existing ones closed from January, and the age check will run through the state's digital identity system."
---

France's parliament passed a law on 21 July that bars children under fifteen from social media, and the timetable attached to it is unusually short. From 1 September the platforms will be required to refuse new accounts to anyone who cannot show that they are fifteen or older, and from January existing accounts held by younger children will be suspended or closed. President Macron called the vote a big step forward, and the minister responsible for digital affairs asked the platforms to take their share of the responsibility. The vote followed a debate that had run for most of the parliamentary session, and the majority was comfortable.

Enforcement rests on identity rather than on self-declaration. The law relies on the state's digital identity system, which lets a user prove an age without disclosing a name or an address, and it obliges platforms to use an approved method rather than the age field that users fill in themselves. Platforms that fail face fines that scale with their revenue, and the regulator can order changes to a service rather than only impose a penalty. The regulator has published a list of approved providers and has been testing them since the spring.

Background to the vote includes a 2023 law that required parental consent for users under fifteen, which proved almost impossible to apply. The consent screen was easy to bypass and the platforms had little incentive to check, so the earlier rule produced a checkbox rather than a barrier. The new law replaces consent with a prohibition, which is a different instrument and one that the platforms cannot delegate to parents. The earlier law's failure was documented in a review that found the consent screen had been applied inconsistently.

European direction of travel is the same. Several member states have set thresholds at fifteen or sixteen, and the commission has been pressing platforms to introduce age assurance across the single market. Germany and the Netherlands have taken a narrower approach, limiting features such as infinite scroll and private messaging for minors rather than banning the service outright.

Problems with the model are real. A child can use an account registered by an older relative, and a determined teenager can reach a service through a network that hides location. Age checks also create a database of identities that becomes a target, which is why the French system was designed to return a yes or no rather than a date of birth. Age assurance companies have been selling services across the bloc, and their methods differ in cost and in accuracy.

Rights groups have made the other argument, which is that the state is removing a place where teenagers find each other. They point to evidence that isolation and bullying happen offline as well, and they argue that digital literacy and design rules would serve children better than exclusion. The law will be judged by whether the September deadline produces fewer accounts or only fewer visible ones. The government has promised a review after the first year, which gives the argument a date.
