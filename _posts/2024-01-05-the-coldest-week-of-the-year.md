---
title: "The coldest week of the year"
date: 2024-01-05 19:04:00 +0800
categories: [essay]
description: "A quiet essay about the first week of January in Chizhou, when the river runs low and grey, the market sells salt fish and coal, and the term's last examinations are close enough to be felt."
---

Winter here does not begin with the calendar. It begins on the first morning when the window glass is wet on the inside and the water in the kettle is cold before the kettle is switched on, and this week it began. The first of January was mild enough that people walked the riverside with their coats open, and by the fourth the wind had turned to the north and every doorway in the town had a curtain of thick cloth hung across it. By six in the evening the streets are almost empty except for the delivery men on their electric scooters, hunched and wrapped, and the shops along the market road keep their lights on later than they do in summer, as if the light were part of the heating.

The river has thinned to its winter self. In July it runs brown and full and pushes at the embankment steps; now it runs clear and low, and the sandbars that the summer flood built are visible again in the middle of the channel, pale and ribbed, with the gulls standing on them in the morning. Barges still move, but slowly, and the men on them wear the same padded jackets they wore last year, so that the whole river looks like a photograph from an earlier decade. In the early morning a mist lies on the water and the far bank disappears, and the first bus over the bridge sounds its horn twice before it reaches the middle.

In the market, the inventory changes with the weather. Vegetable stalls put the cabbages and radishes at the front and the tomatoes at the back, where they are expensive and few. Salted fish and cured pork hang from the frames above the counters, which is the beginning of the work for the New Year even though the festival is a month away. Somebody has brought out the charcoal braziers, and the men who sell them sit behind them with their hands held towards the heat. The woman who sells noodles has moved her pot to the inner side of the stall so that the wind does not take the steam, and she sells more in a morning now than she did in October, which is what cold does to the appetite of a town.

School is what the weather is measured against. The term ends in the middle of the month, and the last two weeks of it are given over to revision, to papers and to the careful business of finding out what has been forgotten since September. Classroom windows are shut now, and the radiators make a noise like a kettle at the far end of the room, and the teacher writes with his coat on because the heating in the older building does not reach the board. In the evenings the study room fills early, and the boys at the back of it keep their gloves on while they write, which makes their characters slow and deliberate. The examinations have not started, and already the whole rhythm of the day has bent towards them.

What I like about this week is how honestly the season asks to be noticed. There is no pretending in January. Mornings are dark, the river is cold, the shops sell what the cold makes people want, and everybody's hands are busy with the same small adjustments, the extra layer, the closed window, the earlier night. Nothing about the town is decorative at this time of year, and nothing about it is idle. By the middle of the month the examinations will be over and the Spring Festival will begin to show in the shop windows, and this plain, cold, working week will be the last stretch in which the year feels entirely ordinary.
