---
title: "COP28 opens with a fund and a fight over the wording"
date: 2023-11-30 23:55:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "The climate summit opened in Dubai on 30 November 2023 by making its loss-and-damage fund operational on the first day, with pledges above 400 million dollars, in a conference that would be judged by whether it called for the phase-out of fossil fuels."
---

Governments opened the twenty-eighth climate conference in Dubai on 30 November, with nearly two hundred governments, a venue built on the edge of a city that produces oil, and a presidency led by the head of the national oil company. The first decision of the conference made the loss-and-damage fund operational, a year after it had been agreed in principle at Sharm el-Sheikh, and pledges announced on the day passed four hundred million dollars.

A fund this size is small against the sums involved. Estimates of the damage that climate change inflicts on vulnerable countries run into hundreds of billions of dollars a year, and the pledges amount to a fraction of the cost of a single large disaster. The United Arab Emirates and Germany each put in a hundred million, the United Kingdom sixty, and the United States seventeen and a half, a figure that drew criticism from delegates who noted the size of the American economy and its historical emissions.

Its main business was the first global stocktake, the formal assessment of progress since Paris. The science is unambiguous: the world is on track for roughly 2.5 to 2.9 degrees of warming under current policies, well beyond the 1.5 degree goal, and the gap between pledges and action has widened every year since 2015. The stocktake's text was supposed to say what governments would do about that gap, and the argument was about a single verb.

A fault line ran between countries that want language calling for a phase-out of fossil fuels and those that want to speak of a phase-down, or of unabated emissions, or of transition. Saudi Arabia and other producers argued that the text should focus on emissions rather than on the fuels, and a group of countries including small island states said anything short of a phase-out was a failure. The presidency, which had promised an inclusive process, spent the second week trying to find a formula that both could accept.

Other parts of the agenda moved faster. Pledges to triple renewable capacity and double the rate of efficiency improvement by 2030 were endorsed by more than a hundred countries, and a declaration on food systems attracted signatures from a similar number. A group of governments and development banks announced guarantees for private finance in emerging markets, and the methane pledge gained new members, though the largest emitters remained outside it.

The venue became part of the story, too. More than eighty thousand people registered, the largest conference of its kind, and the oil company sponsorship and the presence of fossil-fuel lobbyists were criticized by campaigners who said the process had been captured by the industry it was meant to regulate. The presidency answered that the only way to reach an agreement among producers was to include them, and the argument will be settled by the text that the conference adopts. The conference ran into mid-December, and the text it finally adopted was fought over until the last night, with the verb that had divided the parties replaced by a formula that satisfied nobody entirely.
