---
title: "Finland votes and the governing coalition loses its majority"
date: 2023-04-02 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Finland's parliamentary election on 2 April 2023 gave the National Coalition Party a narrow first place, ended Sanna Marin's coalition and left a government to be built around a party whose programme is built on spending cuts."
---

Finland voted on 2 April and the result was decided by a few thousand ballots. The National Coalition Party took 48 seats with 20.8 per cent of the vote, the Finns Party took 46 with 20.1, and the Social Democrats of the outgoing prime minister took 43 with 19.9. Turnout was 72 per cent, the highest in two decades, and the three parties were separated by less than two percentage points. The result was the closest three-way contest since the 1990s, and it left no party with a plausible claim to govern alone.

Sanna Marin had governed since December 2019 at the head of a five-party coalition, and she became the youngest prime minister in the world at 34. Her government steered the country through the pandemic, through the application to NATO that followed the invasion of Ukraine, and through an energy crisis that arrived on the Russian border. What it did not do, in the judgment of the electorate, was stop the growth of public debt, which had reached about 70 per cent of output.

Petteri Orpo, the leader of the National Coalition, campaigned on a simple arithmetic. He argued that the deficit of about ten billion euros a year had to be closed, that the tax burden was already among the highest in the OECD, and that spending would have to be reduced rather than borrowed. The Finns Party ran on a harder version of the same case and added immigration, which it wanted restricted on the argument that the welfare state could not be sustained otherwise.

NATO did not feature in the campaign, because there was nothing left to argue. Finland applied in May 2022, was admitted in April 2023, and every party in the chamber supported the membership by the time of the vote. Foreign and security policy barely appeared in the televised debates, which the commentators treated as a sign of consensus and which the smaller parties treated as a sign that the consensus had been arranged.

Government formation began the following week and was expected to take a month. Orpo had to choose between the Finns Party, with whom he agreed on fiscal discipline and disagreed on immigration, and the Social Democrats, with whom he agreed on neither. A coalition of the National Coalition, the Finns and two smaller parties would command 109 seats in a chamber of 200, enough to govern and narrow enough that one defection could break it.

What the election changed was the direction of a country that had spent four years being described as a model. Finland had entered the pandemic with a strong fiscal position, left it with a debt ratio a third larger, and joined an alliance whose defence commitments will cost more than the savings the new government hopes to find. The voters gave the National Coalition a mandate to do arithmetic that no Finnish government has managed since 2008, and the first test of it will be the budget the new government presents in the autumn.
