---
title: "The first quarter grew and the question is what grew"
date: 2025-04-16 21:30:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "The statistics office reported growth of 5.4 per cent for the first quarter on the sixteenth of April, above the target for the year. The composition of the number is the part that the target does not describe."
---

The statistics office reported growth of 5.4 per cent for the first quarter on the sixteenth of April, which is above the target of around five per cent that was set in March and above what most of the forecasts had expected. The headline number was reported as a strong start, and the interesting part of the release was the table underneath it, where the components tell a different story.

The component that carried the quarter was the export, brought forward. The tariffs that the American administration had announced were expected to raise the cost of Chinese goods from the summer, and the importers who expected that cost placed orders earlier, which pulled the shipments into the first quarter. A number that includes goods that were moved forward is a number that has borrowed from the next quarter, and the borrowing is visible in the month-by-month data that the customs administration publishes, where March stands out against a flat January and February.

The consumer part was weaker than the headline. Retail sales grew, and the growth was concentrated in the categories that the trade-in subsidy covers, the cars, the appliances, the home renovation materials. A subsidy that pays a household to replace a machine produces a sale that would have happened later, and the same arithmetic that applies to the export also applies to the appliance. The categories that the subsidy does not cover grew more slowly, which is the part of the retail data that the analysts watch.

The investment part was mixed and the property part was still negative. The manufacturing investment grew, the infrastructure investment grew, and the residential construction kept shrinking, at a slower rate than last year and still a rate. The property sector is large enough that a decline in it subtracts from the total, and the policies that have been introduced, the purchase restrictions that were lifted and the financing that was extended, have stabilised the sales without reversing the construction, which is a distinction that the monthly data make clear and the quarterly commentary often blurs.

What the release shows is the difficulty of reading a quarterly number. The same figure can describe an economy that is recovering, an economy that is borrowing from the future, or an economy in which the state is spending and the household is not, and the three are distinguished by the components rather than by the total, which is the part of the release that the statistics office publishes in the same document and that the headline ignores. A reader who stops at the headline has learned a fact about the quarter and nothing about the year, and the year is what the policy is aimed at.

The next release is due in the middle of July, and by then the tariff will have taken effect and the borrowing will have to be repaid. What the quarter means will be clearer then, and the clarity will arrive after the market has already traded on the number, which is the normal condition of anyone who reads economic statistics for a living.
