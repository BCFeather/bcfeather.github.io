description: "The European Space Agency's Euclid telescope, designed to map the dark matter and dark energy that make up most of the universe, was launched on 1 July from Cape Canaveral aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket."

Shortly after four in the afternoon on 1 July, a Falcon 9 rocket lifted off from Cape Canaveral carrying a spacecraft that the European Space Agency hopes will explain what the universe is mostly made of. Euclid, a telescope of 1.2 metres across, is the agency's most ambitious science mission in a decade, and its job is to survey a third of the sky with enough precision to trace the shape of the invisible. An hour after launch the spacecraft separated from its second stage and began the long coast towards its operating orbit.

Dark matter and dark energy are the terms physicists use for two problems that have become the same problem. Galaxies rotate faster than their visible mass allows, clusters bend light more than their stars can account for, and the expansion of the universe has been accelerating since well before anyone was watching. Planck's measurements of the cosmic background put ordinary matter at about five per cent of the contents of the universe, dark matter at twenty-seven per cent, and dark energy at the rest. Everything telescopes have ever photographed is a rounding error in the ledger.

Euclid's method is statistical and patient. Its instruments, one working in visible light and one in the near infrared, will photograph billions of galaxies and measure the shapes of the images with a precision of a fraction of a pixel. Weak gravitational lensing distorts those shapes by tiny amounts, and the pattern of distortion carries the distribution of the mass in front of the galaxies, most of it invisible. A second measurement uses the way galaxies cluster at different distances, a relic of sound waves in the early plasma, to map how the expansion of the universe has changed over time.

The mission has been slow to arrive. It was approved in 2011 as part of the agency's Cosmic Vision programme, built by a consortium led by Thales Alenia Space in France with instruments from a dozen countries, and named after the Greek mathematician who wrote the first textbook of geometry. Launch was pushed back repeatedly, most recently by the war in Ukraine, which cost the mission its Russian Soyuz rocket and forced a switch to a commercial American launcher. Engineers have spent the past two years testing the telescope's focus to a tolerance measured in microns.

The rival instruments answer different questions rather than competing for the same one. The James Webb Space Telescope looks further back and sees fainter objects with sharper images, but it looks at small patches of sky. Euclid trades depth for breadth, and its wide survey is what a statistical measurement of dark energy requires. The two missions share a scientific community that expects to combine their catalogues for years, and the first Euclid data release is expected in the middle of next year.

Six years of observation lie ahead, and the results will be argued over long after that. What the mission can settle is whether dark energy behaves as a constant, as the standard model assumes, or changes with time, which would require new physics. What it cannot settle is what either component actually is. Mapping the shape of ignorance with unprecedented accuracy is a real achievement, and it is the one that the next generation of theorists will have to work with.
