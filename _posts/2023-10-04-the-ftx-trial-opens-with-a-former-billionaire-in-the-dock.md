---
title: "The FTX trial opens with a former billionaire in the dock"
date: 2023-10-04 21:35:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "Sam Bankman-Fried went on trial in Manhattan on 3 October 2023, accused of fraud and money laundering over the collapse of FTX, which failed in November 2022 with about $8 billion of customer money missing."
---

Sam Bankman-Fried sat in a Manhattan courtroom this week, less than a year after the exchange he founded filed for bankruptcy with a shortfall that prosecutors put at roughly eight billion dollars of customer deposits. He faces seven counts of fraud, conspiracy and money laundering, and the case has become the most closely watched white-collar trial since Theranos. The judge has denied him the accommodations he requested, including a laptop and a released-on-bail arrangement, and he has been preparing his defence from a jail outside New York.

Prosecutors told the jury that he had treated customer money as his own, moving deposits from FTX into Alameda Research, the trading firm he also owned, to cover losses and to fund investments, loans and political donations. The government's case leans on three cooperating witnesses, including Caroline Ellison, who ran Alameda and has pleaded guilty, and Gary Wang and Nishad Singh, both of whom worked inside FTX. Each is expected to describe instructions from the top and spreadsheet entries that were changed to hide the movement of funds. The exhibits include internal spreadsheets, chat messages and a ledger that prosecutors say records the transfers.

The defence argues that the decisions were made in good faith by a young founder who took risks with borrowed money and lost, and that the line between careless management and criminal intent has to be proved rather than assumed. His lawyers have signalled that they will attack the credibility of the cooperators, who face sentencing recommendations in exchange for testimony, and that they will present the collapse as a run on a firm whose books were thin because of market conditions.

The trial matters beyond the defendant because it tests how far the crypto industry's practices can be described as ordinary finance. Customer assets were pooled, collateral was rehypothecated, and the same small group of people ran the exchange, the market maker and the venture fund on the other side of the trades. None of that was disclosed to depositors, and the question for the jury is whether disclosure failures of that kind are fraud or merely the sloppiness that regulators tolerated for years.

Donations to campaigns have drawn their own scrutiny. Bankman-Fried gave tens of millions of dollars to campaigns and to causes on both sides of American politics, and prosecutors say some of that money came from customer accounts. Two of the recipients have since returned the funds or pledged to, and the question of who knew what has reached congressional campaigns in several states.

A verdict will take weeks, and the collapse has already produced consequences outside the courtroom. FTX's new management has recovered assets and negotiated returns for some creditors, the industry has been pushed into offshore jurisdictions by American enforcement, and the exchange's brand sits alongside Celsius and BlockFi in the list of firms that failed in 2022. The trial is the reckoning for the man who sold the brand. Sentencing, if there is a conviction, would come months later.
