Every August a market appears in the space between the students who have finished a course and the students who are about to start it. The inventory is a shelf in a dormitory, a stack of books carried down four flights, a photograph taken on a bed and posted to a group chat with a price. The trade is small, informal and extremely efficient, and it exists because a new copy of a textbook costs five times what a used one does.

The price of a used book is set by the syllabus. A text that is required by three hundred students and printed in an edition that has not changed in five years holds its value. A text whose professor has written his own notes and no longer assigns the book collapses to almost nothing, and the students who bought it in June discover this in September. The market is therefore a bet on a document that the seller has read and the buyer has not.

The platforms have moved into the gap, and they have changed the shape of the trade in ways that nobody predicted. Several second-hand book apps now handle the transaction, take a commission, and arrange delivery, and they have made the trade bigger and less personal. The old version of it was a noticeboard near a canteen and a meeting at a gate. The new version has ratings, disputes and a refund policy, and it costs the seller a slice of a price that was already low.

The economics of the textbook itself are the underlying problem, and the problem is a print run that is too small for the price to fall. A publisher cannot spread a fixed cost over copies that do not exist. A publisher that sells a few thousand copies of a specialised title has to recover its costs from those copies, and the price per unit is high because the print run is small. The students respond by buying used, which reduces the print run, which raises the price, which pushes more students to the used market. The loop has been running for twenty years and nobody has broken it.

Some universities have tried. A library that holds ten copies of a required text for a class of three hundred is not a solution, and a licensing arrangement that lets the library distribute digital copies is closer to one and has been slow to negotiate. The publishers are not wrong to protect the edition, and the students are not wrong to refuse a price they cannot pay.

What the August market shows is that the formal arrangement is not working for the people it is meant to serve. A textbook is a fixed cost that a student cannot avoid and a publisher cannot reduce, and the second-hand trade is the informal mechanism that both sides use to live with the difference. It is a small piece of improvisation, conducted on a bed with a phone, and it does the job that the market for new books cannot.
