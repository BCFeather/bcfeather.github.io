Rooms within walking distance of an exam centre cost several times their normal rate in the first week of June. A hotel that charges two hundred yuan a night in May charges six hundred in the days of the exam. A guesthouse that has never raised its rate raises it for the same week.

The demand is small and it is certain. A family that lives forty minutes from the centre by car will book a room to avoid the morning traffic. The number of such families on a given street is known months ahead, because the centre's allocation is published and the candidates are assigned by address.

The supply is fixed. The hotels near a school were built before the school became a centre, and nobody builds a hotel for three nights a year. The rooms that exist are the rooms the market has. A price that triples is the mechanism that decides which family gets one.

The platforms have made the number visible and unstable. A rate set by an algorithm rises with each booking, and a family that refreshes the page watches it climb through an afternoon. A hotel that holds rooms back for a direct booking keeps a margin the platform does not take.

The alternative that families use is the short let. An apartment in the same block is rented for a week through an agent, at a price that is negotiated rather than listed. The short let costs less than the hotel and it is less predictable. The family that takes one is buying a risk along with a bed.

Cities have tried to cap the rate in some years. A notice that limits the price near a centre is issued, then enforced unevenly. Hotels that comply lose the margin. Hotels that do not are reported by a competitor. The cap has never survived a full season.

The three nights produce their own routine. A family arrives on the evening before the first paper and leaves after the last one. Breakfast is eaten early. The corridor is quiet by nine. The hotel that hosts these guests each June keeps the lift free in the morning without being asked.

The price says something about scarcity in a narrow window. The same street in July offers a room for a fifth of what it cost in June. The difference is the calendar. The market reads the calendar and takes its profit in three days.

Schools make their own arrangements beside the hotels. A centre opens its canteen for the candidates who live far away, provides a hall for the wait between papers, and lets a family sit on a bench in the shade. The service is free and it is uneven. A school with a large staff does more of it. A school with a small one does less. The family that cannot afford the room depends on which of the two it was assigned, and the assignment is made by a computer that has never seen the street.
