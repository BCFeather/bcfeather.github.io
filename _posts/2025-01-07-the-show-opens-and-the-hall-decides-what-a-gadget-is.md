The electronics show opened on the seventh of January, and the phrase that appeared on the most booths was the artificial intelligence that had been added to something ordinary. A refrigerator that plans a menu, a pram that watches a baby, a lawn mower that maps a garden, a laptop with a chip that runs a small model offline. The products were different and the vocabulary was the same.

The chip announcements are the part of the show that the industry watches first, because a chip decides what the next year of devices can do. The graphics processor that was announced this week is the fourth generation of a line that has become the engine of the data centre as well as the games console, and the laptops that will carry it are the ones that can run a model without sending the prompt to a server.

The show also marks the point at which a category becomes ordinary. A television with a bright panel and a fast refresh rate is no longer news, and the coverage of the televisions this year was about the software that arranges the channels. The same has happened to the phone, the camera and the headphone, and the companies now compete on the service that surrounds the object rather than on the object.

What the hall shows about the industry is its centre of gravity. The robots are in the north hall, the cars are in the west, the chips are in the south, and the walking of the floor is a lesson in where the capital has gone. The companies that make the components are the ones with the queues, and the companies that make the finished goods are the ones with the carpets.

The demonstrations have a grammar that the visitors learn quickly. The product is shown doing something that the visitor could not do, and the something is repeated until the visitor has filmed it. The demonstration is the unit of coverage, and a product that cannot be demonstrated in fifteen seconds is a product that will be described in words rather than in a video.

The distance between the show and the shop is the part that the coverage rarely states. A product that is on a stand in January is a product whose tooling is being ordered, whose certification is being scheduled and whose price is being negotiated with a retailer. The version that ships in the autumn is often cheaper and smaller, and the feature that the demonstration depended on is sometimes the one that was cut. A visitor who remembers the stand and forgets the date is the visitor the show is designed for.

What the show is for, in the end, is to make a year of technology legible in four days. The industry needs a place where the suppliers, the buyers and the reporters are in the same building, and the calendar needs a week in which the year's arguments begin. The hall is that place, and the phrase on the booths is how the argument is named.
