The spring travel period opens on the fourteenth of January and runs for forty days, until the twenty-second of February. The first morning is quiet in most stations, because the passengers who travel early are the ones who have finished their work for the year, and the peak that the newspapers photograph arrives in the last week before the holiday rather than on the first day.

The period is a calendar invention as much as a transport one. The holiday itself is a week, the factories close for a variable stretch around it, and the schools have a longer break. The forty days are the window in which the country's movement is expected, and the operators schedule the extra trains, the extra flights and the extra staff inside that window rather than across the year.

The numbers that the transport ministry publishes each January are estimates made in December, and they have been accurate enough to plan with. The total in recent years has passed four billion passenger journeys, of which the railway carries a fraction and the highway carries most. The share that the highway carries is the part that has grown, because the car has made a journey that used to take two days into one.

The railway's part of the period is the one that gets the attention. The tickets for the busiest routes sell out in minutes, the extra trains are scheduled overnight, and the stations run a queuing system that the staff rehearse in December. The capacity is stretched and it is also finite, and the finite part is the track.

What the period does to the places at both ends is a change that the local economies plan for. The coastal cities lose a share of their workforce for two or three weeks, the restaurants and the building sites slow down, and the inland counties that receive the travellers have their busiest retail month. The money that is earned in one place is spent in another, and the period is the mechanism.

The first day is also the day on which the year's travel habits show themselves. The travellers who booked in December have a seat and a plan, the ones who did not are watching the waiting list, and the ones who drive have a route and a flask. The station in the morning is a place where three different strategies are visible at once, and the strategies are usually inherited rather than chosen.

What the forty days produce is a country that moves and returns. The movement is older than the railway and the calendar that organizes it is newer, and the two have settled into a shape that the operators can forecast within a few per cent. The first morning is quiet because the shape is known, and the quiet is the sign of a system that has been doing this for a long time. The forty days that follow are the test of that forecast, and the test is passed every year in a way that nobody notices unless it fails.
