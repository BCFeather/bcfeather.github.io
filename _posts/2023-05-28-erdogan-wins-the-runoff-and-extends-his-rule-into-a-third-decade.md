title: "Erdogan wins the runoff and extends his rule into a third decade"
date: 2023-05-28 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Recep Tayyip Erdogan won the second round of Turkeys presidential election on 28 May with 52.2 per cent of the vote, defeating Kemal Kilicdaroglu and extending a rule that began when he became mayor of Istanbul in 1994."
---

Recep Tayyip Erdogan won the second round of Turkey's presidential election on 28 May, taking 52.2 per cent of the vote to Kemal Kilicdaroglu's 47.8, with a turnout above eighty-four per cent. The result was narrower than any of his previous victories and comfortable enough to be called a mandate. It gives him a term that runs to 2028 and, if he completes it, a quarter century in national office, which no Turkish leader since Ataturk has approached.

The runoff campaign was conducted in the shadow of the first round's disappointment. Kilicdaroglu had campaigned as the candidate of a six-party alliance and had promised a return to parliamentary government, a reset with the west and an independent central bank. In the two weeks between the votes he moved sharply towards the nationalist right, promising to expel refugees and attacking the Kurdish party that had supported him, which appears to have cost him more support among his own voters than it gained among Ogan's.

Erdogan's campaign was built on control of the state's instruments. He used the public broadcaster, the religious networks and the state's own relief programmes, and he cast the opposition as the instrument of foreign powers and of a terrorist organization. The message worked in the interior and in the pious working-class districts of the cities, which is the coalition that has carried him since 2002, and it held even though the economy was visibly struggling.

Its economic inheritance is the hard part. The lira has lost more than three quarters of its value against the dollar in five years, inflation has been running near forty per cent, and the central bank has spent reserves to keep the currency from falling faster than the government could bear. The policy that produced this, an insistence on low interest rates in the face of high inflation, was maintained by a president who has called high rates the mother of all evil, and which the next government will have to reverse.

The foreign policy question is what a victorious Erdogan will do with the leverage he has accumulated. Turkey has been the broker of the Black Sea grain deal, the guarantor of Sweden's path into NATO, and the mediator between Russia and Ukraine, and it has done all of that while buying Russian air-defence systems and refusing to join sanctions. A president who has just won an election without needing anyone's approval has more room to bargain, and less incentive to compromise.

Opposition now faces the question that it postponed during the campaign. The alliance that Kilicdaroglu assembled includes nationalists, Islamists, secular republicans and Kurds, and it was held together by the prospect of defeating one man. With that prospect gone, the parties will return to their own agendas, and the mayor of Istanbul, who was the candidate the polls preferred and the government blocked, will be the obvious standard-bearer for the next round. Turkey has five years of a president with a divided parliament and a damaged currency, and the country's institutions have shown that they can survive that. Whether they can improve on it is the question the election did not answer.
