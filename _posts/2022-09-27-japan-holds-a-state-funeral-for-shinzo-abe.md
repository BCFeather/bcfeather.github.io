---
title: "Japan holds a state funeral for Shinzo Abe and the country argues about the bill"
date: 2022-09-27 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Four thousand guests fill the Budokan, the prime minister promises to inherit the agenda, and a crowd outside asks why the ceremony is paid for."
---

The state funeral for Shinzo Abe was held at the Budokan in Tokyo on Tuesday afternoon, and about four thousand guests filled the hall where the judo competitions of the 1964 Olympics had taken place. The former prime minister was shot in the city of Nara on 8 July while campaigning for an election, and the man accused of the killing was arrested at the scene. The ceremony was the second, after a private funeral at a temple in the days following the death, and it was the first state funeral for a former leader since 2007.

Guests included the vice president of the United States, the prime ministers of India and Australia and the president of the European Commission. Leaders who had worked with Abe in the group of seven came, and so did the emperor and the empress in a private capacity. The Russian president did not attend, and the seating plan was arranged with the diplomatic consequences in mind. The American delegation arrived on a military aircraft and stayed for six hours.

A eulogy that mattered came from the current prime minister, who had been Abe's foreign minister and who has described himself as a disciple. Fumio Kishida said the government would carry the agenda forward, and he listed the security legislation, the economic policy and the diplomatic initiative that Abe had built. The speech was an attempt to claim the inheritance in a party that has never settled the question of who owns it. Two factions have been arguing about that since the funeral was announced.

Public argument is about money. The ceremony cost about one point six billion yen, most of it for security and for the venue, and the opposition parties said the spending was excessive in a year when prices were rising. Protesters gathered outside the hall with banners, and the opinion polls showed that a majority would have preferred a smaller event. The government's answer was that the funeral was an obligation of the state rather than a tribute from the party.

Security was the largest the city had mounted for years. Roads around the Budokan were closed, snipers were on the roofs, and the police deployed units that had been brought in from other prefectures. The shooting in July had exposed a gap in the protection of former leaders, and the force had promised a review. The suspect's weapon was a homemade firearm, and the investigation has been looking at how he was able to approach a former prime minister in an open space.

What the ceremony did was close a period in Japanese politics without settling its argument. Abe was the longest-serving prime minister in the country's history and the most divisive of the post-war era, and the funeral was designed to present him as an institution rather than as a politician. His faction remains in the party and his policy remains in the statutes, and his absence has not resolved the question of who will lead the conservative movement next. The next election will be fought over the inheritance, and it will be fought by people who spoke at his funeral.
