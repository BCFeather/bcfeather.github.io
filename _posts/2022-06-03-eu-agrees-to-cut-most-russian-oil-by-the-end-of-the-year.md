---
title: "The EU agreed to cut most Russian oil by the end of the year"
date: 2022-06-03 21:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "A sixth package of sanctions bans seaborne crude in six months and refined products in eight, leaves the Druzhba pipeline running for three landlocked states, and covers about ninety per cent of the union's imports."
---

Ambassadors in Brussels approved the sixth package of sanctions against Russia on 3 June, after four weeks in which the union's unity had been tested more seriously than at any point since February. The package bans imports of Russian crude carried by sea from 5 December and refined products from 5 February, and it removes Sberbank and two other banks from the international messaging system that most cross-border payments rely on.

Ninety per cent of the oil the union buys from Russia falls under the ban. About a tenth arrives through the Druzhba pipeline, which runs overland to refineries in Hungary, Slovakia and the Czech Republic, and that flow was exempted to keep those three governments on side. Hungary's prime minister, Viktor Orbán, had refused to sign anything that would raise fuel prices at home, and his veto held for most of May. The three landlocked states had argued that no alternative pipeline could reach them before the end of the decade.

Other measures reach further than the oil ban. European insurers and brokers are now barred from covering shipments of Russian crude anywhere in the world, which complicates sales to buyers in Asia, and a ban on providing services to Russian oil firms covers financing, technical help and shipping. The union also agreed to expand a list of individuals subject to asset freezes and travel bans, and to tighten export controls on goods that can be used by the military.

Prices had already done much of the work. Brent crude traded near 120 dollars a barrel in early June, up by about sixty per cent since the start of the year, and diesel in Europe rose faster than crude because refineries in Russia supply a large share of the continent's middle distillates. The ban will tighten supply into the winter, and the union has been negotiating with producers in the Gulf and in West Africa to replace the barrels it is giving up. Shipowners in Greece and Cyprus, whose tankers carry much of the trade, lobbied hard against the insurance clause and lost.

Russia has been redirecting exports to China and India, often at a discount of thirty dollars a barrel, and the revenue loss is smaller than the volume figures suggest. Indian refiners have bought cargoes that European buyers turned away and sold the products back into Europe, which the sanctions package does not stop. Officials in Brussels acknowledge the gap and say that closing it would require measures aimed at refined products rather than at crude.

The package establishes a schedule rather than a settlement. Six months is long enough for Hungary to stock up and short enough for the market to price the loss, and the exemptions write into the sanctions a permanent argument about who pays for them. The union has now committed itself to a date, and the politics of the winter will decide whether that date holds. Member states will revisit the exemptions in the autumn, when winter demand makes the arithmetic harder to hide.
