---
title: "BHP walks away from its bid for Anglo American"
date: 2024-05-29 22:30:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "BHP withdrew its takeover proposal for Anglo American on 29 May after the third offer, worth about thirty-four billion pounds, was rejected, ending a six-week campaign that would have created the largest copper miner in the world."
---

BHP told the market on the twenty-ninth of May that it would not pursue its takeover of Anglo American, hours before a deadline set by the British takeover code, closing a campaign that had begun in April with an approach worth thirty-one billion pounds. Third and final proposal had valued the company at about thirty-four billion pounds, or forty-nine billion dollars, and it was rejected by Anglo American's board on the morning of the twenty-ninth on the ground that it still undervalued the business and imposed unworkable conditions.

Structure of the offers was the source of most of the disagreement. BHP wanted Anglo American to spin off its platinum and iron ore businesses in South Africa before the merger, and it made completion conditional on those separations being carried out first. Anglo American's board said the condition transferred the risk of the separation to its shareholders and would take more than a year, during which the company would be constrained in how it ran its assets.

Valuation gap was the other obstacle. BHP's final proposal offered 0.886 of its own shares for each Anglo American share, plus a share of the value of the two businesses to be divested. Anglo American argued that the package valued it at about twenty-nine pounds a share when the market and its own copper projects justified more, and several of its large shareholders said publicly that they would support a higher offer.

Copper was the reason for the pursuit. Anglo American owns mines in Chile and Peru that would combine with BHP's operations to create a producer large enough to supply a significant share of the metal that the energy transition requires. Demand for copper is expected to rise as grids, vehicles and data centres are electrified, and the largest deposits are becoming harder to find. Consolidation among the big miners has been predicted for a decade, and the failed bid was the closest it has come.

Anglo American's own response was a restructuring plan published a week before the final deadline. Company said it would spin off or sell its platinum business, divest its nickel operations, and separate De Beers, the diamond company it has owned since 1926. It also said it would slow spending on a fertiliser project in Britain. Plan was presented as a way to focus on copper and iron ore, and analysts said it was designed to show shareholders that the value BHP claimed to be buying could be realised independently.

Consequence of the withdrawal is that both companies remain independent and both are under pressure. Anglo American must deliver the restructuring while its shares trade below the level of BHP's final offer, and it faces questions about whether it can do so without a buyer. BHP has to explain why it spent six weeks and a great deal of money on a bid it could not complete, and its shares rose on the news of the withdrawal, which suggests that investors were not convinced the deal was worth the price. The next attempt at consolidation in the industry will be harder to start.
