---
title: "Starship reaches space and then loses both of its halves"
date: 2023-11-18 22:30:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "SpaceX's second integrated Starship flight on 18 November 2023 achieved the first hot-stage separation of booster and ship, then lost the booster in an explosion and the ship to its flight-termination system about eight minutes after launch."
---

SpaceX launched the second integrated flight of Starship from Boca Chica on the morning of 18 November, and the vehicle cleared the pad, passed maximum aerodynamic pressure and separated its booster from the ship for the first time. The separation worked as designed, with the ship's engines igniting while the booster was still firing, a manoeuvre the company calls hot staging. For about three minutes, both halves flew as intended.

Then the booster failed. After separation it was supposed to flip, relight a subset of engines and return towards the coast for a controlled splashdown in the ocean, and instead it broke apart shortly after the turn, several thousand feet above the water. The ship continued upwards on its own, reached space above the Karman line, and was lost at about eight minutes into the flight, when the autonomous flight-termination system ended the mission. SpaceX said the ship's telemetry stopped near the end of the planned burn.

What was achieved matters because it was the first time the full stack had operated in the configuration that the company intends to fly. The April flight had failed to separate, had damaged the pad and had tumbled for several minutes before being destroyed. Engineers changed the staging design, added a water deluge system under the pad, and rebuilt the flight-termination system whose earlier version had taken longer than expected to act. The pad survived this launch intact.

The loss of the vehicle is part of the development method. SpaceX builds prototypes, flies them, and treats the data as the product, in contrast with programmes that test components on the ground for years before a first flight. The company said it had gathered data on staging, on the behaviour of the ship's engines in vacuum, and on the loads that the structure carried, and that the next vehicle was already being prepared.

Starship is central to two programmes. NASA has contracted a version of the ship as the lunar lander for the Artemis missions, and the agency's schedule depends on orbital refuelling, which has never been demonstrated at this scale. SpaceX also intends to use the vehicle for its own satellite launches, and the economics depend on rapid reuse of both stages, a capability that remains unproven.

Regulators have tightened in step with the flights. The Federal Aviation Administration requires a mishap investigation after each loss, and the next launch cannot proceed until the agency accepts the findings and the corrective actions. That process took months after the first flight and is likely to take weeks after this one. The company has said it wants to fly more often in 2024, and the pace will be set as much by paperwork as by engineering. Engineers also reported that the ship's engines had run longer than on the April attempt and that the whole stack had passed the point of maximum aerodynamic pressure without the structural failure some had predicted, data the company will use to refine the next vehicle's flight profile.
