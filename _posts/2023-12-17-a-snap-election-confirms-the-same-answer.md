---
title: "A snap election confirms the same answer"
date: 2023-12-17 22:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Serbia held early parliamentary elections on 17 December 2023, two years ahead of schedule, and the party of President Aleksandar Vucic won again, in a vote that international observers criticized for the advantage the state gave the incumbent."
---

Serbia voted on 17 December in parliamentary elections called two years early, alongside local elections in Belgrade and 64 other municipalities, and the Serbian Progressive Party of President Aleksandar Vucic won with about 46 per cent of the vote. The coalition around it, which includes the Socialist Party and the pensioners' party, secured a majority in the 250-seat assembly with roughly 129 seats. Turnout was about 59 per cent, and the result was declared by the state election commission late on the night of the count.

The snap vote was called in October, three months after a shooting at a school in Belgrade and a second at a village near Mladenovac, which produced the largest protests the country had seen in a decade. The opposition demanded elections on the theory that the protests had changed the mood, and the government agreed, on the calculation that the opposition would split and that the state's advantages would carry the day. Both were right: the opposition ran as two blocs and the machinery worked.

Observers from the OSCE and from the Council of Europe spent the day in polling stations and published a report that described a campaign in which the governing party had a systematic advantage. The report cited pressure on public sector employees, the use of state media as a campaign channel, the distribution of cash payments in the weeks before the vote, and a voter register that contains more names than the country has residents. Domestic monitors reported the bussing of voters to Belgrade from other towns and a pattern of family voting in rural stations.

Belgrade is the result that matters, because the city government has been the only large institution the opposition has run in a decade. The Progressive Party and its partners claimed a majority in the capital by a margin of a few seats, the opposition claimed that the count in some polling stations had been manipulated, and a repeat vote was ordered in a handful of places. The city assembly is where the contracts for land, waste and transport are awarded, and the parties fought the campaign as if the national result were already settled.

Foreign policy was barely debated, which is itself the outcome of a decade of positioning. Serbia has refused to join the sanctions on Russia, has voted against Russian resolutions at the United Nations, has bought gas at prices below the market, and has opened 22 accession chapters with the European Union, none of them closed. The European Commission's annual report said the country had met the political criteria in part, and the government's answer was that it would hold a general election first and decide afterwards.

The vote changes little of the machinery it was fought over. The same party governs with a slightly smaller majority, the same president speaks for the country, and the same newspapers that supported it will report that the country has chosen stability. What changed is the record: a snap election held under protest, observed by international organizations, and won by a party whose advantage in the campaign has now been documented rather than suspected, which is a fact that the next report will carry.
