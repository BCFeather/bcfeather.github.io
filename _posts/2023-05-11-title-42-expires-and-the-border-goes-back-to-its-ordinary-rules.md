---
title: "Title 42 expires and the border goes back to its ordinary rules"
date: 2023-05-11 22:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The pandemic-era order that allowed American border agents to turn asylum seekers away expired on 11 May. The Biden administration replaced it with a rule of its own, and both the arrivals and the lawsuits began the same day."
---

The public health order known as Title 42 expired at midnight on 11 May, ending three years in which American border officials could expel migrants to Mexico without letting them lodge an asylum claim. The order had been issued in March 2020 by the Trump administration, on the argument that the pandemic required the immediate removal of people arriving at the southern border, and it had survived into the Biden presidency because the numbers at the border kept rising and no replacement had been written.

The replacement is a rule that presumes most asylum seekers who arrive without documents and without passing through a third country are ineligible, unless they can show that they face a particular risk and that they applied through an app before reaching the border. Officials argue that the rule channels people into legal pathways and deters the ones who are choosing a smuggler over a queue. Immigration lawyers argue that it does the same thing that Title 42 did, with a different legal justification, and the first suits were filed within hours.

Numbers behind the change are large and awkward. Encounters at the south-western border reached about two hundred and fifty thousand in the last fiscal year and have been running higher in the first months of this one, with families and unaccompanied children forming a growing share. The holding facilities in border towns have been over capacity for a year, and the cities on the Mexican side have filled with people waiting for an appointment that the American system could not give them while Title 42 was in force.

Political arithmetic is unforgiving in both directions. The Republican governors of border states have spent the past two years sending buses of migrants to northern cities, and they greeted the expiry by promising more. The administration, which had promised a humane system, has been forced to keep a version of the restrictive machinery it once condemned, and the left of the party has said so publicly. In Congress, the House has passed a bill that would restore a Title 42 style expulsion and add detention, which will not pass the Senate and will be used as an advertisement.

Its legal future is likely to be decided in the same courts that handled the pandemic order, where the state of Texas has been the most persistent litigant. The administration's rule will be challenged on the ground that it conflicts with the statute that governs asylum, and the challengers have won on similar ground before. A judge in Louisiana or Texas could suspend the rule before the summer, which would leave the border with no operative policy at all, and that prospect is what makes the first weeks after 11 May a test of administration rather than of principle.

What the expiry settles is only that the emergency justification is gone. The underlying question, which is how a rich country with a labour shortage and a broken asylum system should treat people who arrive without permission, has been deferred again. The people in the queue will not be deferred. They will arrive under whichever rule is in force on the day they reach the river, and that rule is now the subject of a fight that will run through the election.
