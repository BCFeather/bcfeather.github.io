---
title: "Click chemistry wins the Nobel, twenty-one years after the phrase was coined"
date: 2022-10-05 22:10:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "Bertozzi, Meldal and Sharpless share the chemistry prize for reactions reliable enough to run inside living cells."
---

The chemistry prize went on Wednesday to Carolyn Bertozzi, Morten Meldal and Barry Sharpless for work that made chemical reactions reliable enough to use inside living cells. Bertozzi, who turned fifty-six on the day of the announcement, works at Stanford. Meldal, sixty-eight, works at the University of Copenhagen, and Sharpless, eighty-one, at Scripps Research in La Jolla. The committee described the award as one for click chemistry and bioorthogonal chemistry.

Its idea is that a reaction should work the same way every time, in water, at room temperature, without leaving a mess of by-products. Sharpless set out those rules in 2001, and he borrowed the name from the way a seat belt clicks. In the same period Meldal, working separately, found that an azide and an alkyne will join in the presence of copper and will do so while almost nothing else reacts. The reaction has become the standard way of linking two molecules in a laboratory, and drug discovery now uses it to build compounds that would be tedious to assemble otherwise.

Bertozzi's contribution was to make the reaction safe inside a living organism. She attached the azide to sugars that cells put on their surfaces, and then used a copper-free version of the reaction to add a dye or a drug to those sugars. The technique lets a biologist watch a cell change its coat without killing it. Her laboratory used it to study how tumours disguise themselves from the immune system, and the method became a tool in immunology. Her group later used the reaction to attach a drug to a tumour-seeking antibody, which is how the technique reached clinical trials.

Applications have spread further than the citation suggests. Pharmaceutical companies use the reaction to attach payloads to antibodies, which is the basis of several drugs in trials. Materials scientists use it to make gels that heal themselves, and diagnostic laboratories use it to label DNA. The reaction also appears in the synthesis of new polymers and in the tagging of proteins for mass spectrometry. Firms that sell diagnostic kits use it to bind an enzyme to a bead, which is cheaper than the older chemistry it replaced.

Sharpless is the second person to win the chemistry prize twice, after Frederick Sanger, and his first came in 2001 for work on chirally catalysed oxidation reactions. He has said that he prefers the phrase 'click chemistry' to any of the names the textbooks use. Meldal describes himself as a peptide chemist who stumbled on the reaction while looking for something else, and he had never met his co-laureates before the announcement. He said in a telephone interview that he had been at a conference when the call came and had assumed it was a colleague with a question about a peptide.

Worth ten million kronor, the prize is shared three ways. What the three of them did was turn a laboratory convenience into a method that works in the most inconvenient place there is, which is a living body. The announcement noted that the reactions are used in the development of cancer drugs and in the study of how cells communicate. Chemistry prizes often wait for an application, and this one arrived with several already in the clinic.
