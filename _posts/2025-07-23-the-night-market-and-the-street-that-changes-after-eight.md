In July the night market takes over streets that are ordinary roads during the day. By eight the barriers are up, the generators are running, the smell of oil and charcoal has settled over two hundred metres of asphalt, and the crowd is moving at the speed of a queue. The stalls sell the same things they sold last year, at prices that have risen less than the price of the ingredients, and the margins are thinner than the atmosphere suggests.

The format's economics are simple and unforgiving, and they explain most of what the market looks like from the outside. A stall pays a fee for its pitch, which is usually modest, and it takes in revenue for perhaps four hours a night. A vendor selling grilled skewers might clear a few hundred yuan on a good evening and nothing on a wet one, and the weather decides the month. The people who run these stalls are not entrepreneurs in the sense the business pages use. They are households supplementing a wage, and the supplement is seasonal and unreliable.

What the market does for the street is harder to price, and the argument for it is usually made in exactly those terms. A road that is dead after seven becomes a place where a family with a child can walk at ten at night, which is a change in what the city offers rather than a change in what it earns. The nearby shops stay open later than they would otherwise, the taxi rank at the corner does a better trade, and the residents above the shops acquire a month of noise and a month of cheap vegetables.

The complaints are as regular as the market, and they are mostly made by the people who live directly above it. Noise, litter, the smell, the blocked entrance to a building, the scooters parked on the pavement. The organizers have learned to answer them, with a cleaning contract, a sound limit, a curfew at eleven, and a phone number that residents can call. The answer holds for a season and is renegotiated each year, which is how most urban arrangements actually work.

The format has been proposed as a solution to several problems it cannot solve. It does not create many permanent jobs, it does not revive retail in any structural sense, and it does not make a district cheaper to live in. What it does is give a few hundred households a summer income and give a street a few hours of life, and those are modest achievements that happen to look good in a photograph.

Whether it lasts is a question about the city's tolerance rather than about demand. A night market needs a street that can be closed, residents that will accept four weeks of noise, and a district office willing to renew a permit. In a neighbourhood that is being redeveloped, all three disappear at once, and the market moves two kilometres east to a street where the buildings are lower and the complaints are quieter.
