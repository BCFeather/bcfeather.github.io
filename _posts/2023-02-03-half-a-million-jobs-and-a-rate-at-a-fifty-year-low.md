---
title: "Half a million jobs and a rate at a fifty-year low"
date: 2023-02-03 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "American employers added 517,000 jobs in January 2023 and the unemployment rate fell to 3.4 per cent, the lowest since 1969, in a report that unsettled the assumption that tightening had begun to bite."
---

Employers added 517,000 jobs in January, according to the report published on 3 February, and the unemployment rate fell to 3.4 per cent, its lowest level since May 1969. The consensus forecast had been about 185,000, and the previous two months were revised up by a further 71,000. The labour force participation rate rose slightly to 62.4 per cent, which meant that the jobs were being filled by people who had not been counted as looking for work a month earlier.

The composition was as striking as the total. Leisure and hospitality added 128,000 jobs, health care 58,000, and construction 25,000, in weather that had been mild enough for sites to stay open. Average hourly earnings rose 4.4 per cent over the year, slower than the 5 per cent of the spring, and the average workweek edged up. Nothing in the report pointed to the recession that the surveys of purchasing managers had been predicting since the autumn. The share of workers holding part-time jobs for economic reasons fell, which is the measure that usually rises before a downturn.

The puzzle was how the number could be so large when the headlines were about layoffs. Technology firms had announced more than 100,000 cuts since the previous autumn, and the banks were beginning to prepare for a slowdown in dealmaking. Those cuts were concentrated in a sector that employs about 2 per cent of the workforce, and the workers who lost those jobs were being hired elsewhere within weeks, which showed up in the claims data rather than in the payroll survey.

Markets read the report as bad news, which was the convention of the moment. Futures moved to price a higher peak for the policy rate, the two-year yield rose above 4.3 per cent, and the dollar strengthened. The idea that good economic news was bad market news had become a cliche by then, and the cliche was accurate: a labour market this tight gave the Fed no reason to stop.

Politics read the report in the usual two directions. The White House pointed to the unemployment rate as evidence that its programme was working, and Republicans pointed to the participation rate as evidence that it had not, because participation remained below its level before the pandemic. Both claims were true of a single number, which is what makes monthly reports useful in arguments and unreliable as verdicts.

What the report did to expectations lasted about six weeks. By the middle of March the same market that had priced a higher peak was pricing a lower one, because a bank had failed and the conversation had changed. The January payroll figure went unrevised, and the strength it described was real, but the frame in which it mattered had been replaced before anyone had finished arguing about it. Positioning in the rates market had become so one-sided that a single release could move the whole curve by twenty basis points.
