An eighteen-year-old gunman entered Robb Elementary School in Uvalde, Texas, at about 11.33 on the morning of 24 May and fired more than a hundred rounds into two adjoining classrooms. Nineteen children, most of them nine and ten years old, and two teachers were killed, and seventeen other people were wounded. The gunman was shot dead by a Border Patrol tactical team at about 12.50, seventy-seven minutes after he entered the building, and by then the rooms he had fired into held the dead and the wounded together.

Before the school he had shot his grandmother in the face at the house where he lived, and she survived and called the police. He crashed a pickup truck into a ditch behind the school, fired at two people outside a funeral home, and walked in through a door that had been left open. The district had installed fencing and a lock system after earlier shootings, and the door he used had been propped open by a teacher who was retrieving something from her car.

The delay is the part that the investigations have centred on. Officers from the city police, the county sheriff's office and the Department of Public Safety arrived within minutes, took rounds from the gunman, and then held the corridor, treating the incident as a barricade rather than an active attack. Children inside made at least eight calls to emergency numbers, one of them telling a dispatcher that eight or nine of her classmates were still alive. A supervisor eventually decided that the door had to be tried, a team entered, and the shooting ended.

By the end of the week there were 376 law enforcement officers on the scene or in support, from federal, state and local agencies, and accounts of what each had done were being contradicted by body-camera footage released piece by piece. The director of the Department of Public Safety called the decision to wait the wrong call and said so publicly, the local police chief was placed on leave, and the state's governor said he had been misled about the response in briefings he gave in the first hours.

The political argument that followed was the one America has had after every school shooting for a decade: a bipartisan group of senators began meeting to discuss a narrow package on background checks and red-flag laws, the Senate's Democratic majority said it would hold votes, and the governor of Texas said the answer lay in mental health and school security rather than in gun laws. The state had loosened its rules on carrying handguns the previous year, and the rifle used in Uvalde had been bought legally days after the gunman's eighteenth birthday.

Nineteen children died in two rooms in a school that had been prepared for an attack and whose door, on that morning, stood open. The town of fifteen thousand people spent the following weeks at twenty-one funerals, and the country spent them arguing about a corridor, a door and seventy-seven minutes that no report has yet fully explained.
