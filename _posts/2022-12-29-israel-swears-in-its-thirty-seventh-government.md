---
title: "Israel swears in its thirty-seventh government with sixty-four seats"
date: 2022-12-29 22:00:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Israel swears in a coalition of sixty-four seats, with Ben-Gvir at national security and Smotrich at the treasury."
---
Knesset approval came on the evening of 29 December, after a session that ran for hours and a vote that fell along the coalition's narrow majority. Netanyahu returned to the office he had held for twelve years, less than eighteen months after he had lost it. The government is the thirty-seventh in the country's history, and it was assembled from four lists that had campaigned separately. The ceremony was held late, and the ministers took their oaths in a chamber that was half full.

Arithmetic is sixty-four seats out of one hundred and twenty. Likud holds thirty-two of them, and the rest belong to Religious Zionism, Shas and United Torah Judaism. The partners were given ministries that matter to them: the police and the ministry of national security went to Itamar Ben-Gvir, the treasury went to Bezalel Smotrich, and the interior ministry went to Shas. In return, the coalition agreed to a set of understandings on religion, on the courts and on the settlements.

Guidelines were published on the day it was sworn in, and the document was longer than usual. It promised to advance a reform of the judiciary, to expand the settlements, and to change the way the legal advisers of ministries are appointed. Those clauses had been the subject of argument during the coalition talks, and they were the reason the government was described as the most right-wing in the country's history. Critics described the guidelines as a plan to weaken the courts, and the opposition promised to fight them in every forum available.

Netanyahu's trial continues, and that fact hangs over the arrangement. His partners had promised to change the legal framework in which the case is being heard, and the reforms they described would touch the powers of the attorney general and the process by which judges are chosen. Supporters describe the changes as a correction of an over-mighty court, and critics describe them as a way of ending a trial. The prime minister denies the charges and says the reforms are unrelated to his case.

Security is where the coalition will be judged first. The army is engaged in the occupied West Bank in a way it had not been for years, and the new minister for national security has authority over the police and over the border police. His first statements promised a harder line, and the Palestinian Authority said the new government had closed the door on any political process. The American administration said it would judge the government by its actions. Officials in the defence ministry had asked for the guidelines to leave the operational arrangements alone, and the text does not name them.

By midnight the ministers had taken their seats and the prime minister had given his first speech in the new term. He promised stability, economic growth and a restoration of deterrence, and he thanked the partners who had spent two months bargaining over ministries. The coalition that resulted has the narrowest of majorities and a long list of promises that no previous government had made.
