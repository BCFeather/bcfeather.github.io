title: "The equinox and a day of equal lengths"

description: "The sun crosses the celestial equator on the twentieth of March, and the day and the night are supposed to be equal. They are not quite, and the reason is the way the atmosphere bends the light and the way a day is counted."
---

The sun crosses the celestial equator on the twentieth of March, and the date is called the equinox because the day and the night are supposed to be of equal length. The word is accurate enough for a calendar and inaccurate for a clock. A place at the latitude of Beijing receives about twelve hours and ten minutes of daylight on the equinox, and the extra ten minutes are a consequence of the atmosphere and of the definition of sunrise.

The atmosphere bends light. A ray from the sun that passes through the lower layers of the air is refracted upward, so the sun is visible a few minutes before its disc has geometrically risen and a few minutes after it has set. The effect is about the same at both ends of the day and it adds several minutes to the daylight that a table of sunrise times records. The same refraction is why a sunset looks flattened at the horizon, and why the moon appears larger than it is.

The definition matters too. Sunrise is the moment the upper edge of the sun appears rather than the centre, and sunset is the moment the upper edge disappears, and the two definitions together lengthen the day by the diameter of the sun divided by the rate at which it moves. A day measured to the centre of the disc is shorter than a day measured to the edge, and the difference is a couple of minutes at this latitude.

The equal day arrives a few days early at the equator and a few days late further from it, and the geography of the effect is neat. At the equator the day and night are nearly equal all year and the equinox adds almost nothing. At a high latitude the sun moves across the horizon at a shallow angle, the refraction buys more minutes, and the date on which the day and night are truly equal can be a week away from the equinox. At the poles the whole idea stops working, because there is one sunrise and one sunset in the year.

What the equinox does reliably, whatever the clock says, is mark the point at which the northern hemisphere begins to gain more daylight than it loses. From this date until the solstice in June, every day is longer than the one before, and the rate of the gain is fastest at this moment and slows as the summer approaches. The plants respond to the length of the day rather than to the temperature, which is why the flowering of a species is stable from year to year while the warmth is not.

The date is also the one on which a person can stand a raw egg on its end, according to a claim that circulates every year and is false in a way that is easy to test. The egg will stand on any day with enough patience, and the equinox has nothing to do with it. The claim survives because it is a good story about a day that otherwise passes without notice, and the notice is what the equinox is short of.
