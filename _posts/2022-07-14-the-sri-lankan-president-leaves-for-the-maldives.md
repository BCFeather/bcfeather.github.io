---
title: "The Sri Lankan president leaves for the Maldives and promises to resign"
date: 2022-07-14 23:55:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Gotabaya Rajapaksa flies out on a military aircraft before the curfew, and the speaker says the resignation will come on Wednesday."
---

President Gotabaya Rajapaksa left Sri Lanka early on Wednesday morning on a military aircraft and arrived in the Maldives, and the speaker of the parliament said he had agreed to resign later in the day. The departure came four days after the protesters took his residence in Colombo, and it was announced by the air force without a statement from his office. The prime minister was sworn in as the acting president, and the curfew that had been imposed on Monday was extended. The opposition said the flight was an admission that he had lost the country.

Whatever happens next, last week will be the week the country remembers. The crowd occupied the palace on Saturday, the president's whereabouts were unknown for two days, and the speaker told the parliament on Monday that the president would resign. The immigration officers at the airport stopped him on Monday night, according to the officials, and he left on Wednesday on a plane that the air force provided. The Maldivian government confirmed the arrival and said it had not been asked for asylum.

The lawyers have spent the week on one question: what happens when a president resigns from abroad. The constitution allows the parliament to elect a president from among its members, and the speaker has said the vote will be held within a week of the resignation. The prime minister is the acting president until then, and he has said he will serve for the shortest possible time. The opposition has asked for an early election, and the parties that supported the president have said the constitution does not allow one.

Whoever takes office will find the treasury empty. The country has suspended the payments on its foreign debt, and the reserves are enough for a few weeks of imports. The talks with the International Monetary Fund have reached the stage where a staff-level agreement is possible, and the fund has asked for a budget and for a plan to restructure the debt. The creditors include China and India, and both have lent more than the fund's rules would normally allow.

Anger here runs deeper than the shortages. The family that has held the presidency and the government for most of the last two decades is seen as corrupt, and the protests have been organized by young people who have no memory of the war that ended in 2009. The crowd that took the palace included students, monks and professionals, and the slogans were about accountability rather than about a single policy. The political parties that joined the protests have been unable to agree on a programme.

The country is left with a parliament that must elect a president within a week, and with a crisis that will outlast the election. The president's resignation was promised and not yet delivered, and the crowd that took his house has said it will stay until it is. The fund will lend only to a government that can sign, and the government that can sign has not been formed. The next week will decide whether the transition is orderly, and the answer will depend on the parliament rather than on the street.
