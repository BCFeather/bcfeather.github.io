---
title: "The Nikkei closes above its 1989 record"
date: 2024-02-24 20:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Nikkei 225 rose 2.19 per cent to 39,098.68 on 22 February, passing the closing high set on 29 December 1989 and ending a wait of more than thirty-four years."
---

Nikkei 225 closed at 39,098.68 on the twenty-second of February, up 2.19 per cent on the day and above the record set at the height of the bubble economy on the twenty-ninth of December 1989. Previous closing high was 38,915.87, and the index had come close to it several times since the start of the year without passing it. Intraday record of 38,957.44, also set in 1989, was broken in the morning session. By the twenty-seventh the index had reached 39,239.52, its high for the month, and it passed 40,000 in early March.

Wait was thirty-four years, and the reason it took so long is a story about what happens after a bubble. Index peaked in December 1989 and then fell by more than sixty per cent over the following two years, and it spent the next two decades in a range that never came close to the old high. Japanese banks carried bad loans for years, companies carried overcapacity, and prices fell for long enough that the country built an entire policy vocabulary around the problem. Return to the 1989 level is therefore not a recovery of lost ground in real terms. It is a nominal price finally reaching again a number that had become a national memory.

Rally that carried it there had specific causes. Japanese corporate profits rose to records, helped by a weak yen that increased the value of overseas earnings and by the end of years of deflation that had kept companies from raising prices. Foreign investors bought Japanese equities heavily, drawn by governance reforms that pushed companies to return cash to shareholders and to unwind cross-shareholdings. Semiconductor investment in Kyushu and the prospect of interest rates staying low for longer added to it. Heavy weights in the index, including the trading houses and the chip equipment makers, led the advance.

Comparison with 1989 is the one that everyone made, and it is misleading in an important way. In 1989 the index was driven by property speculation, bank lending against inflated collateral and a stock market in which the value of the Imperial Palace grounds was said to exceed that of California. Earnings this time are real, the banks are better capitalised, and the market's valuation on a price-to-book basis is still below the levels of the bubble. What the two moments share is the psychology of a market that has finally done something it had stopped believing it could do.

Whether the level holds is another question. Bank of Japan began to move away from negative interest rates in March, and a stronger yen would cut the overseas earnings that have driven the profit gains. Rise has been narrow enough that a handful of companies account for much of it, and the domestic economy slipped into a technical recession in the fourth quarter before the figures were revised. Record was a fact on the twenty-second of February. What it means about the Japanese economy is still an argument.

Individual investors were the ones who had waited longest. Japan's retail investors hold a large share of the market directly and through tax-advantaged accounts, and the generation that bought at the peak in 1989 has been the cautionary example in every conversation about the stock market since. Brokers reported a surge in inquiries from younger buyers during February, and the financial media in Tokyo ran the same photograph of the 1989 trading floor that it runs every time the subject comes up. For the people who had given up on the market, the record was a reason to come back, and that is the part of the rally that will be tested when the first correction arrives.
