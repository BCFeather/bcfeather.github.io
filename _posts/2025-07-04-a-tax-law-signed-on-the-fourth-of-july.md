The American tax and spending law was signed on 4 July, on a stage, in front of an audience, with a marching band somewhere in the background. The date was chosen for the photographs, and the photographs were the point of the event. The law itself, which the president called the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and which everyone else has shortened to an acronym nobody enjoys typing, is a collection of changes that will arrive over several years and will be measured in instalments.

The core of it is an extension of tax reductions that were passed in 2017 and were scheduled to expire. Extending them keeps the status quo for most households and businesses, which is an odd thing to hold a ceremony for. A tax cut that is already in place and merely stops expiring does not produce a cheque in anyone's letterbox, and it does not show up in the wage packet. It shows up in the baseline, which is the number the government uses for everything else.

The offsets are the interesting part. To keep the headline cost within a range that enough legislators could accept, the law reduces spending on health insurance subsidies for low-income households and tightens eligibility for food assistance. The reductions are phased in, with work requirements and reporting requirements that take effect at different dates, and their effect on the number of people covered will be known only after the first full year of implementation. Supporters argue that the programmes were being used by people who should not have qualified. Critics argue that the reporting requirements will remove people who qualify but cannot produce the paperwork on time.

There is an energy provision in it as well, which removes several of the tax credits that had been created to encourage wind and solar construction. The credits had been the primary federal instrument for renewable deployment, and their removal changes the arithmetic of projects that are already financed and not yet built. The industry's response has been quiet, which is what an industry does when it has lost an argument it expected to win.

The macroeconomic reading is contested and genuine. The law increases the deficit relative to the baseline, at a moment when the debt is already large and interest costs are rising. Whether that matters depends on a view about growth, and the two views are held by serious economists who have looked at the same numbers. The market's view, as usual, was expressed in the bond yield and will be revised several times.

What the fourth of July signing actually decides is the shape of the American fiscal argument for the next three years. The next budget will be fought on the terrain this law created, with the health provisions as the first line and the deficit as the second. The ceremony is over. The instalments start in the autumn, and each one will arrive with its own set of numbers, its own chorus of experts and its own anniversary.
