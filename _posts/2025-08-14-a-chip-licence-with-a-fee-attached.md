---

NVIDIA agreed in August to pay the American government a share of the revenue from sales of the accelerators designed for the Chinese market, in exchange for export licences that had been withheld for several months. The reported figure is fifteen per cent. The arrangement is unusual in trade law and it has no close precedent. Export controls normally work by prohibition, and a prohibition that can be bought out for a share of the takings is a different instrument.

The background is a series of tightening rules that have been applied in stages since 2022. Each restriction cut the performance of the chips NVIDIA is permitted to sell into China, generation after generation, and each new part number was designed to sit just under the threshold. The H20 was the result of that exercise, and it was the last design that could be sold at all, and then it was not. For a quarter the inventory sat in warehouses and the company wrote down the value of it.

The commercial logic of the arrangement is straightforward for both sides. For the company, a licence with a fifteen per cent levy is better than no revenue and a stock of unsold inventory. For the government, a share of the revenue converts a policy of denial into a policy of extraction, and it produces a number that can be described as a payment from a foreign firm rather than a subsidy to one.

The strategic logic is less straightforward. If the purpose of the controls is to slow the development of Chinese computing capacity, then selling working accelerators, even degraded ones, works against that purpose. If the purpose has shifted to extracting value while the domestic industry catches up, then the arrangement is coherent. The two purposes have been stated at different times by different officials, and the policy reflects both.

The Chinese response has been to discourage purchases. Regulators have told the large platform companies that they should not buy the H20, and the reasoning is a mixture of security concern and a desire to direct demand toward domestic parts. Whether the advice becomes a rule is unresolved, and a rule would change the arithmetic for both firms.

What the arrangement demonstrates is the extent to which the export control regime has become a bargaining instrument. A licence that can be negotiated is a licence that can be repriced, and every firm now knows that the price is a percentage rather than a prohibition. That is a durable change in how the rules work, and it will outlast this particular chip and probably the next three, because the precedent is now on the record and the next negotiation will start from it.

For the domestic chip industry the effect is indirect and probably helpful, though the domestic parts remain behind on the memory bandwidth that training workloads need. A market in which the American part is expensive, uncertain and politically discouraged is a market in which the local alternative has room to be adopted, and adoption is what a chip programme needs more than a subsidy.
