title: "What the school start time does to a teenager"
date: 2025-08-27 21:00:00 +0800

description: "The evidence on adolescent sleep and school start times is unusually consistent, and the policy has moved slowly anyway. A bell at half past seven is a decision about buses and parents as much as about biology."
---

The evidence on adolescent sleep and school start times is unusually consistent and has been for twenty years. At puberty the circadian rhythm shifts, and a teenager's body does not release melatonin until later in the evening than a child's or an adult's. Waking at six in the morning is therefore not a matter of discipline. It is a matter of biology, and the biology moves in a direction that no timetable can reverse.

The recommendation that follows is straightforward. Start secondary school at half past eight or later. Several countries and a number of American districts have done it, and the results are documented in a way that few education interventions are. Attendance improves, lateness falls, and the self-reported sleep duration rises by close to an hour in the first year. The academic effects are modest and positive. The effects on mental health are larger and harder to measure, and the studies that have looked report fewer students in the clinical range for depression.

The obstacle is a schedule, and a schedule is a set of agreements between people who have other things to do. A start time is a system, and the system includes the bus schedule, the parents who leave for work at seven, the teachers who finish at four and the after-school care that begins when the last bell rings. Moving the bell by forty minutes moves all of those, and each of them has someone who will object. The objections are practical rather than scientific, and they are correct on their own terms.

The counter-argument that gets raised in every school board meeting is that the students will simply go to bed later. That argument was tested and it failed. The studies show that bedtime moves by a few minutes and wake time moves by the full amount, which is exactly what the biology predicts. Teenagers who are given an extra forty minutes in the morning sleep for most of it.

The second counter-argument is about the primary schools. If secondary schools start later, the buses have to serve the two groups in a different order, and the primary children end up starting earlier and waiting at home in the afternoon. The primary schedule matters too, and the research on young children is less clear, and a district that rearranges one bell is rearranging two.

What the debate shows is how a well-established finding survives in a system that is organised around something else. The timetable is not really about learning. It is about the coordination of a few thousand households, and a change to it is a change to the shape of a working day for the whole district.

The schools that have moved the bell have not gone back. That is the most useful piece of evidence in the file, because it is the evidence that the disruption is survivable and the benefit is persistent, and districts that have not moved are still arguing about a change that other districts have already absorbed.
