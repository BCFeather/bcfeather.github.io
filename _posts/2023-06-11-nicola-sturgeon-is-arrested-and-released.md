title: "Nicola Sturgeon is arrested and released"
date: 2023-06-11 18:20:00 +0800

description: "Nicola Sturgeon, the former first minister of Scotland, was arrested on 11 June by police investigating the finances of the Scottish National Party, and released the same day without charge pending further inquiry."
---

Nicola Sturgeon was arrested on 11 June at her home near Glasgow by detectives investigating the finances of the Scottish National Party, and was released without charge some hours later pending further inquiry. The arrest was the third in a case that has been running since 2021, following the detention of her husband, Peter Murrell, the party's former chief executive, and of Colin Beattie, its former treasurer. Police have been examining whether money that was earmarked for a referendum campaign was used for other purposes, and whether the party's accounts were kept properly.

Sturgeon was first minister for nine years and led the party to three consecutive election victories, and she resigned in February, saying that she no longer had the energy to carry the argument at the pace she had. Her departure left the party without the most effective advocate it has had for independence, and it placed her in the awkward position of being a former leader who cannot easily distance herself from the organization that is under investigation. The timing of the arrest, shortly after her memoir was announced, made the story harder for her to control.

The political damage is cumulative rather than acute. The SNP has been in government in Scotland for sixteen years, and its dominance has rested on the perception that it is more honest than the Labour and Conservative parties it replaced. Each stage of the investigation has eroded that perception, and the arrest of the person who embodied it is the most serious blow yet. The party's opponents have been careful not to comment on the substance, and contented themselves with the observation that the police would not be involved if there were nothing to investigate.

Legally, no one has been charged, and the investigation may end without any prosecution. The police have searched the party's headquarters and Murrell's home, seized documents and computers, and interviewed a long list of officials; a report has been sent to the procurator fiscal, who decides whether charges follow. That process is slow and quiet, and it will run through the next general election and the next Scottish parliamentary campaign whatever the outcome.

What the case means for independence is debated more than it is understood. The movement has always been a coalition of people who disagree about almost everything except the destination, and Sturgeon's skill was to hold that coalition together while making it look easy. The arrest removes her from that role, and it invites the argument that the cause was damaged by the people who led it. The polls have moved little through the affair, which suggests that Scottish voters distinguish between the party and the question, and that distinction will be tested at the next election more sharply than it has been so far.

No deadline binds the procurator fiscal, and the party has a leadership contest to run in the meantime. Both will proceed on the assumption that nothing has been decided, which is the only assumption the law allows.
