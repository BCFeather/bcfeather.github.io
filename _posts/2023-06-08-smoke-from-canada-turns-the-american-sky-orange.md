---
title: "Smoke from Canada turns the American sky orange"
date: 2023-06-08 15:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Wildfires in Quebec sent a plume of smoke across the north-eastern United States on 7 June, pushing New York's air quality index above 400 and grounding flights, while more than four hundred fires burned across Canada."
---

On 7 June, smoke from hundreds of wildfires in Quebec moved south and settled over the north-eastern United States, and the sky over New York turned a colour that photographs could record and the British vocabulary could not describe. The city's air quality index went above four hundred, the worst reading it had ever logged, and local officials advised everyone, including the healthy, to stay indoors. Flights into and out of the region were grounded by visibility and by the smoke's effect on engines, and schools cancelled outdoor activity.

The fires behind the smoke were Canadian and largely out of the reach of American firefighting. Quebec had recorded an exceptionally dry spring, and lightning in early June had ignited hundreds of fires in the boreal forest, many of them far from roads and settlements, where the strategy is to let them burn rather than to try to fight them. There were more than four hundred fires burning across Canada, and the smoke moved east on a weather pattern that carried it over the most densely populated corridor on the continent.

Air quality is now a measurable part of climate policy, and this week produced the sort of numbers that policy is written about. Fine particulate matter from wildfire smoke penetrates deep into the lung, and studies have linked short episodes to hospital admissions for asthma and to cardiac events. The United States has spent decades cleaning its industrial air and has reduced the emissions it controls, and then it received a reminder that a great deal of the particulate it breathes in summer arrives from forests it does not manage, in a country that is warming at twice the global rate.

Fires have a longer tail than the smoke. Canada's forestry industry depends on a boreal forest whose fire season is lengthening, and the communities in the north, many of them indigenous, bear the direct cost of evacuation and of the loss of hunting and trapping grounds. The reconstruction of the burned areas is slow, the replanting is expensive, and the carbon released by the fires contributes to the conditions that make them more likely. None of that fits in a smoke forecast, and all of it is the reason the smoke was so thick.

A photograph of an orange New York is the sort of image that enters the public memory and shapes it. It arrived in the same month that global average temperatures set records and that the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere reached its highest level in millions of years, and it made a global phenomenon visible in a city that had been arguing about whether to phase out gas stoves. Whether it changes anything depends on whether the summer repeats, and the forecast for the rest of the season is for more of the same.

Smoke moved on within days and the fires did not. Quebec's fire agency said it expected to be fighting into July, and the province's timber industry is already counting the loss in years of supply rather than in hectares.
