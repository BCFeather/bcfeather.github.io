---
title: "Liz Truss becomes prime minister and promises to act on energy bills within a week"
date: 2022-09-06 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "She wins fifty-seven per cent of the party membership and takes office at Balmoral, with a cabinet built around tax cuts."
---

Liz Truss won the leadership of the Conservative Party on Monday with fifty-seven per cent of the members' vote against Rishi Sunak, and she was appointed prime minister by the Queen at Balmoral on Tuesday. The appointment happened in Scotland rather than in London because the Queen had been advised not to travel, and it is the first time in her reign that a prime minister has been sworn in outside a royal palace in the capital. Truss is forty-seven and has been in the cabinet for ten years. She arrived in Downing Street in the rain and promised a package on energy bills within a week.

Her campaign was built on the promise of immediate tax cuts and on the argument that the orthodoxy of the Treasury had held the country back. She said during the summer that she would cut national insurance, reverse the rise in corporation tax and review the mandate of the Bank of England. Her opponent, the former chancellor, argued that unfunded cuts would push up borrowing costs, and he was supported by most of the party's members of parliament. The members, who are older and more numerous in the south of England, chose otherwise.

A cabinet formed the same evening is the part that tells the story. Kwasi Kwarteng, who had backed her from the start, became chancellor; Suella Braverman became home secretary; James Cleverly became foreign secretary. The three are among the most loyal of her supporters and among the least experienced in the departments they now run. Several of the ministers who had served under Boris Johnson lost their posts, and the new team was described by one of its own members as a government of the campaign rather than of the party.

Problems waiting for her are the ones the campaign did not discuss in detail. Inflation is at ten per cent, the energy regulator's price cap is due to rise by eighty per cent in October, and the health service has a waiting list of more than six million. The pound had fallen against the dollar through the summer, and the yield on ten-year gilts had risen to a level last seen in 2011. A recession is forecast by the Bank of England for the end of the year, and the budget will be presented by the new chancellor in the last week of September.

The energy package she promised is expected to freeze household bills at about the current level for two years, at a cost that the Treasury has put at sixty billion pounds. The money would be borrowed, and the borrowing would come on top of the tax cuts, which is the combination that the markets have been watching since the summer. Officials have said the support would be paid for by borrowing rather than by a windfall tax, and the opposition has called that a choice to make the public debt absorb the price of gas.

What happens next will be decided within weeks, and everybody involved knows it. The party has to hold a conference in the first week of October, the chancellor has to present a budget in the last week of September and the Bank has to decide on rates in the same period. Truss campaigned as a candidate against the institutions she now leads, and the first test of her government is whether the institutions accept the argument. The members who elected her did so on the promise that the country would grow, and the evidence for that promise arrives with the gilt market.
