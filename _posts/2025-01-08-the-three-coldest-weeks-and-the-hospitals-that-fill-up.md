The coldest stretch of the Chinese winter begins on the eighth of January, in the counting that divides the period after the solstice into nine-day blocks. The third block is the one the almanac calls the depth of the cold, and it is the fortnight in which the night temperature in the north stays below minus ten and the rivers that have not frozen are covered.

The cold is a settled fact and the illness that arrives with it is the part that fills the hospitals. The paediatric wards in the northern cities report their peak in the same weeks, and the queue at an evening clinic in January is a queue of children with a fever and a parent with a coat. The viruses that cause it are the seasonal influenza, the respiratory syncytial virus and the mycoplasma that has been circulating since the autumn.

The two are connected by the way a person behaves in the cold rather than by the temperature itself. A week of minus ten sends a family indoors, the windows stay shut, the classrooms keep the same air, and a virus that would spread slowly in the open air spreads quickly in a room. The cold also dries the air, and a dry nose is a nose whose first line of defence is weaker.

What the hospitals do about it is a scheduling problem that the health system solves every year. The paediatric staff are rostered for the peak, the beds in the respiratory wards are opened in December, and the clinics extend their hours into the evening. The system that works is the one that plans for the peak in October, and the system that struggles is the one that discovers the peak in the corridor.

The advice that the doctors give is unglamorous and effective. A flu vaccination in the autumn, a mask in a crowded clinic, a hand washed after the school pickup, a child kept home for a day rather than sent to a class with a fever. The measures are cheap and the compliance is partial, which is the normal condition of public health.

The cold itself is also a risk that the statistics record separately. The deaths from cardiovascular events rise in the same weeks, the elderly who live alone are the ones most exposed, and the city that runs a warm room for the winter is a city that has understood the arithmetic. The heating bill and the hospital bill are the same bill seen from two institutions.

What the fortnight shows is that the winter is a season with a shape. The cold begins and ends on a schedule that the almanac predicted, the illness follows it, and the institutions that plan for the shape are the ones that can absorb it. The almanac is old and the schedule still holds, and the hospital that reads it is the hospital that has beds in February, which is the difference between a plan and a scramble in a corridor.
