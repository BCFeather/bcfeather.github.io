---
title: "Charles is crowned in a service of old rites and new arithmetic"
date: 2023-05-06 22:10:00 +0800
categories: [culture]
description: "On 6 May Charles III was crowned at Westminster Abbey in a ceremony built on a thousand years of liturgy and adjusted for a country that no longer believes in it. The pageantry was genuine; so was the effort to keep it smaller."
---

Westminster Abbey held the coronation of Charles III on the morning of 6 May, seventy years after his mother was crowned in the same building and eight months after her death. The service ran for two hours, kept the medieval structure that has been used since 973, and added a few things that had never been there before: a hymn sung in Welsh, a Greek choir, a gospel passage, and a moment in which the congregation was invited to swear allegiance, rather than only the bishops and the peers.

The ritual itself is the point of the thing, and the details are worth listing. The king was anointed behind a screen, in the part of the service that cameras could not film, and received the orb and the sceptre, objects that carry an imperial history the monarchy has spent decades trying to interpret. The crown that was placed on his head, and then on the head of the queen, was the same one that had been used in 1937, taken out of the Tower and adjusted for a head it had not fitted before. A stone that Scotland had lent to the abbey in 1996 was in the chair beneath him.

Its arithmetic was the more interesting story. The guest list was cut to two thousand from the eight thousand who attended in 1953, and the invitations went to a wider set of people, including community volunteers and charity workers, and to a narrower set of peers, with fewer of the hereditary kind. The procession was shortened to a single route of two kilometres and the military contingent to about seven thousand. A ceremony that once displayed the reach of an empire was arranged, this time, around a country that wanted the display and did not want the bill.

Party politics was present without being dominant. The first minister of Scotland attended in a kilt; the leaders of the Commonwealth came; the American president did not, and sent a delegation instead. Two members of the royal family were absent, one of them the son whose relationship with the institution has been the subject of a book, and his presence in the abbey for the ceremony was itself a piece of news. Protests were held along the route, and a small number of arrests were made.

The question that the ceremony could not answer is the one that matters for the institution. A coronation is a ritual of continuity, and continuity is precisely what the monarchy now has to argue for. The polling that accompanied the day put support for the monarchy at a respectable level and enthusiasm among the young at a much lower one, and the shape of the service, with its nods to other faiths and its trimming of the aristocracy, was a response to that gap. Whether a thousand-year rite can be adjusted into a modern argument is the experiment that began on Saturday.

Camilla became queen in the same ceremony, and the heir became the prince of Wales in a title that had been his since the previous autumn. The next coronation, whenever it comes, will be in a country whose relationship with the monarchy will have been decided in the meantime. Saturday was the last easy ceremony of the old kind, and the people who organized it knew that better than anyone in the abbey.
