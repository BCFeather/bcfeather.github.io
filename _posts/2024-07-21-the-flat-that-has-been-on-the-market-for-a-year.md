A seller who listed a flat last summer has cut the price four times. The first cut was two per cent, the last was eight. The flat is in a block built fifteen years ago with a lift and a car park. It is now priced below what the same unit sold for in the spring.

The viewings have stopped. An agent who showed the flat to eleven families in September showed it to two in March and none in June. The phone still rings with people who want to know whether the price is negotiable, which is the question that a seller answers last.

The stock has grown. A district that had four hundred listings three years ago has a thousand. The ones that are priced correctly sell in six weeks. The ones that are not stay on the page and become part of the statistics that a buyer reads before making an offer.

The buyers are waiting for something. A household that sold its old flat and is renting has no reason to buy while the price falls by a per cent a month. The expectation of a further fall is the mechanism that produces the fall.

The agent has started to call the neighbours. A block with one flat for sale often has three within a year. An agent who knows that a family is thinking of moving can list the unit before it is advertised. The call is the cheapest form of marketing in the trade.

The developer is the other seller. A project that was launched in 2021 has unsold units in 2024. The company cuts the price by more than a household can. A buyer who compares a new unit with a resale one finds the new one cheaper, which is why the resale market is slow.

The rent has held. A flat that cannot be sold is rented instead. The rent in the district has risen by a few per cent, because the families that would have bought are renting. The seller becomes a landlord by default.

The land sale is the part that the city notices. A plot that would have sold for a large sum two years ago receives no bid. The budget that was written on the assumption of the sale has to be rewritten. The flat and the plot are the same market.

The school district is the part that holds the price. A flat in the catchment of a good primary school sells at a premium that survives every other fall in the market. A family that buys it for the school sells it to another family that wants the same place, and the chain repeats.

The mortgage is the constraint on the buyer. A loan is approved against an income and a valuation, and a bank that values the flat at last year's price lends less than the seller expects. The gap is paid in cash or the deal does not close. The listing page keeps its own history, and a buyer who scrolls back can see every cut the seller has made.

The flat will sell eventually. A price that is low enough finds a buyer in any market, and the question is only how much the seller will accept before the family that needs the money accepts it for them.
