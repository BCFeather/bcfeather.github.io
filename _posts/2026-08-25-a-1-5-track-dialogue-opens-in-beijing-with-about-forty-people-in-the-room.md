---
title: "A 1.5-track dialogue opens in Beijing with about forty people in the room"
date: 2026-08-25 23:20:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "About forty former officials, scholars and business figures from China and the United States met in Beijing on 25 August for a dialogue organized by the Chinese Association for International Understanding and the Asia Society, the third such meeting in the series."
---

About forty people gathered in Beijing on 25 August for a dialogue that its organizers call track 1.5, meaning that the participants hold no official rank but speak with the people who do. The Chinese Association for International Understanding hosted the meeting together with the Asia Society, and the theme on the agenda was constructive strategic stability. The agenda was circulated to participants in advance, a courtesy that the earlier sessions did not extend and that organizers described as a sign of seriousness. Roughly half the delegation had served in government, and the other half came from universities, think tanks and companies with cross-border exposure.

The format has a purpose. Delegates who negotiate treaties cannot say anything new in public, and delegates who speak only for themselves cannot promise anything. A meeting of former officials sits between the two, and its value lies in the sentences that begin with the phrase my understanding from my time in government. Track 2, the purely academic version of the same conversation, has run for longer and produces papers rather than understandings, and the 1.5 format exists because papers have stopped travelling. A retired ambassador can say that his successor will not compromise on a particular point, and the sentence carries weight that a scholar's version does not.

Small-scale meetings of this kind have become the main channel between the two countries in a year when the formal ones have been difficult. Tariffs, export controls and the Taiwan question have kept the cabinet-level exchanges sparse, and both governments have used unofficial gatherings to test ideas before they become policy. The working channels on climate, counternarcotics and aviation have continued through the tariff fight, and officials on both sides point to them as proof that the relationship has floors. A meeting in Beijing also allows Chinese participants to brief a wider circle of American visitors than a formal negotiation would, which is part of why the format persists.

The agenda covered the familiar ground: nuclear risks, artificial intelligence in military systems, trade in critical minerals, and the conditions under which a summit could take place. Chinese participants were reported to stress that the relationship needs guardrails, while American participants were reported to stress that the guardrails need to be verifiable. One proposal reported from the discussions was a notification arrangement for military exercises, a modest idea that would reduce the chance of an accidental encounter. Another was a standing experts' group on artificial intelligence in weapons systems, which both governments have discussed at the United Nations without agreeing on language.

The timing has its own meaning. The meeting came two weeks after the collapse of the Canadian trade talks and a week after the new Iran sanctions, two events that pushed the alliance system and the sanctions regime to the front of the agenda. A room in Beijing is a useful place to ask whether either of them has a limit. The Canadian dispute offers an obvious lesson for the room: retaliation that is announced in advance gives the other side time to prepare, while a quiet one creates a crisis. The Iran sanctions offer the opposite lesson: measures that accumulate without a negotiation attached tend to be absorbed rather than answered.

Such dialogues do not produce agreements. They produce a shared vocabulary, a set of names each side can telephone, and a rough map of where the other side will not move. In a year with few meetings, that is the most valuable output available, and the third session in the series suggests both governments found the first two worth continuing. Organizers said a fourth meeting would be held in the United States next year, subject to the usual scheduling and to a relationship that has cancelled more events than it has held. Participants left with telephone numbers and a shared glossary, which is what these gatherings produce and, in a year of few meetings, what they are for.
