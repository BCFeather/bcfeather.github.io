description: "In late August the new first-years arrived at Chizhou No. 1 Middle School, a morning of name lists on notice boards, uniforms bought one size too large, and a building that seemed much bigger than the school they had left in June."
---

The notice boards went up on the wall beside the main gate on the last few days of August, and by the morning of 1 September the lists had already been photographed and sent to a dozen group chats. Twelve classes, fifty-odd names in each, arranged in an order that nobody could explain and that every family studied for information about who would be in the same room for the next three years. The first-years arrived in white shirts and dark trousers that had been bought a size too large, on the advice of relatives who understood how fast a fifteen-year-old grows.

The building is on a hill, and the walk from the gate to the first-year classrooms takes four minutes if the corridor is empty and ten when it is crowded. On the first morning it was crowded. Parents carried bedding and buckets and mosquito nets up the stairs, older students directed traffic with the confidence of people who had done this once themselves, and the teachers stood at the doors of their rooms with a list in one hand, ticking names. Somewhere on the third floor a boy discovered that his room had a window facing the playground and told everyone within reach.

A first class meeting lasted most of the morning and covered almost nothing academic. The rules: when to arrive, how to leave, what to do with a phone, who to tell if something happens. The seating was arranged by height, then rearranged by eyesight, and by the end of the hour the room had found its first jokes and its first silence. The teacher wrote her name and the room number on the blackboard and said that the homework would begin on Monday.

September brought a timetable unlike the one the class knew in June: more subjects, longer days, evening study, and a month that arrives with the heat still in it. Chizhou No. 10 Middle School had been a short walk from home for most of the children in that room, and Chizhou No. 1 Middle School requires a bus, or a bicycle, or a parent with a car and a schedule that can absorb twenty minutes twice a day. That arithmetic, more than the syllabus, is what the first week teaches.

By the second week the corridors had their own traffic. The canteen queue had a rhythm, the shop by the sports field had a queue at break, and the library, which nobody had mentioned during the enrolment talk, turned out to be the quietest place on the hill. The first-years learned which staircase was faster, which teacher collected homework on Monday and which on Friday, and that the bell in the older building rings four seconds later than the one in theirs.

Six weeks later, in the middle of October, the class had stopped being a list on a wall. It had a leader, a seating plan that everybody accepted, three running jokes and one argument that had been settled by the teacher. The uniform had been washed enough times to fit properly. The walk up the hill had become ordinary, which is the thing that a first morning never promises and that every term eventually delivers.
