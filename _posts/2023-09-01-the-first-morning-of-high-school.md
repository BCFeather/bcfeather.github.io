date: 2023-09-01 21:20:00 +0800

description: "In late August the new first-years arrived at Chizhou No. 1 Middle School, a morning of name lists on notice boards, uniforms bought one size too small, and a building that seemed much bigger than the school they had left in June."

The class lists went up on the wall beside the main gate in the last few days of August, and by the morning of 1 September I had photographed mine and forwarded it to a dozen group chats. Twenty classes, fifty-odd names in each, arranged in an order nobody could explain and every family studied for information about who would share a room for the next term. I arrived in a white shirt and dark trousers bought a size too large, on the advice of relatives who knew how fast a fifteen-year-old grows.

The school sits on flat ground at number 698 on Shengjinhu Lu, on the edge of the Pingtian Lake scenic area, with the city's sports hall a few hundred metres away and open land on the other side. The campus is wide enough that the walk from the gate to my classroom takes four minutes at an empty hour and ten when the corridors are full.

My family rented a flat instead of sending me to a dormitory. It stood in a residential block ten minutes on foot from the gate, three rooms let by the year to a landlord who had housed families like ours for a decade. My mother stayed there with me and cooked, and my father came at the weekend. The block filled up in August and emptied in June, and by the end of September I knew the names of four neighbours' children, all of them first-years at the same school.

A first class meeting lasted most of the morning and covered almost nothing academic. The rules: when to arrive, how to leave, what to do with a phone, who to tell if something happens. Seating was arranged by height, then rearranged by eyesight, and by the end of the hour the room had found its first jokes and its first silence. The teacher wrote her name and the room number on the blackboard and said that homework would begin on Monday.

September brought a timetable unlike the one I knew in June: more subjects, longer days, evening study, and a month that arrives with the heat still in it. What changed most was the distance. I had walked to Chizhou No. 10 Middle School in ten minutes; now the day began and ended in a rented room with a desk, a kettle and a window facing another block of flats, and the only journey was the five-minute bike ride between that room and the gate.

By the second week the corridors had their own traffic. The canteen queue had a rhythm, the shop by the sports field had a queue at break, and the library, which nobody mentioned during the enrolment talk, turned out to be the quietest place on the ground floor. I learned which staircase was faster and which teacher collected homework on Monday. Six weeks later, in the middle of October, the class had stopped being a list on a wall: it had a leader, a seating plan everybody accepted, three running jokes, and one argument that the teacher had settled.
