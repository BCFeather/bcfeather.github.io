---
title: "OSIRIS-REx brings home a piece of an asteroid"
date: 2023-09-24 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "A capsule from NASA's OSIRIS-REx spacecraft landed in the Utah desert on 24 September 2023 carrying about two hundred and fifty grams of rock and dust from the asteroid Bennu, the largest sample ever returned from such a body."
---

The capsule entered the atmosphere at about half past eight in the morning local time, opened a parachute at thirty thousand metres and landed on the Utah Test and Training Range within a few minutes of its predicted time. Recovery teams reached it by helicopter, confirmed that it was intact and carried it to a portable clean room, where they measured gases released from the sample. The spacecraft itself, which had released the capsule three hours earlier, diverted on a course that will take it past the sun and then towards a rendezvous with another asteroid in 2029.

Sample mass is about two hundred and fifty grams, more than the mission had hoped for and twice what Japan's Hayabusa2 brought back from a different asteroid. OSIRIS-REx launched in 2016, reached Bennu in 2018, spent two years mapping it, and collected the material in 2020 with a device that fired nitrogen gas at the surface and caught what came up. The capsule travelled about six billion kilometres to return it, and the mission cost about a billion dollars, which is a fraction of what a crewed mission would cost.

Bennu is a rubble pile of carbon-rich material that dates from the formation of the solar system. Scientists expect the sample to contain clay minerals that hold water, organic molecules including amino acids, and grains that have not been heated since the first solid bodies formed, which is why the mission was sent to this asteroid rather than to a larger one. The mission's principal investigator has said the sample will keep laboratories busy for a generation, and that most of it will be held for researchers who have not yet been born.

The recovery was careful because the sample is fragile and because contamination is permanent. The capsule was sealed and shipped to a laboratory in Houston, where it will be opened in a nitrogen atmosphere, and the material will be divided among teams in several countries. The mission's own instruments were designed to measure the sample in space, and the ground analysis will test whether those measurements were right. A portion of the material has been set aside for the mission team, and the rest will be divided among laboratories that applied for it.

Planetary defense was a secondary purpose. Bennu is one of the asteroids whose orbits bring it close to Earth, and the mission measured its shape, its spin and the forces that change its path with a precision that improves the forecast of where it will be in the next century. The probability of an impact remains small, and the data has narrowed it to a range that planetary scientists describe as the best constrained of any near-Earth asteroid.

A capsule has landed in a desert with dust older than the planet inside it, and laboratories will spend decades reading it. The mission has done what the science requires, which is to bring something back and to leave the explanation to people who never saw it arrive.
