At its developer conference on 9 June, Apple did two things that will be remembered differently. It moved every platform onto a single numbering scheme, so that iOS, iPadOS, macOS, watchOS, tvOS and visionOS are now all version 26. It also introduced a design language it calls Liquid Glass, in which menus, buttons and panels become translucent, refract what sits behind them, and behave as though they were made of a liquid that has been persuaded to hold a shape.

The numbering change is housekeeping and slightly overdue. Apple's operating systems had drifted out of alignment, with some on version 18, some on 15, one on 2 and the computer on 15 as well. The mismatch confused the ordinary buyer and made every announcement a small lesson in product taxonomy. Moving to a year-based number fixes that, and it has a marketing advantage that the company did not need to state. A version number that matches the calendar makes each release feel like a new model year rather than an increment.

The design change is the substantive one. Apple has been building screens that look flat and calm since 2013, when it abandoned the imitation of physical materials in favour of thin type and white space. That style aged, as styles do, into something that feels closer to a document than to an instrument. Liquid Glass reverses the direction without returning to the old one, and the reversal is visible everywhere at once because it touches the layer that sits above every app.

Translucency is a technically expensive decision. A panel that shows the content behind it has to be rendered with the background, blurred at the edges, lit consistently, and composited at a rate that does not drop frames. It also has an accessibility cost, because legibility depends on what happens to be underneath, and a menu over a busy photograph is a different object from the same menu over white. Apple has dealt with this before, in the glass effects of an earlier era, and the tools it built then are more capable now.

The strategic reading is less interesting than the aesthetic one. Every platform release is now accompanied by a paragraph about artificial intelligence, and this keynote was no exception, with the usual claims about assistant features and privacy. Those claims are real and they are also the least visible part of the update to anyone holding a phone. The glass is visible in the first second, and the assistant is visible only after a question has been asked of it.

Whether it works will be settled in the autumn, when the betas leave and the ordinary user meets a translucent control panel over a photograph of a beach. Some will find it beautiful. Some will find it hard to read. Apple has, historically, been willing to accept the second group in order to get the first, and the company's design history is a record of that trade succeeding more often than not, largely because it has been willing to soften an idea in the second beta.
