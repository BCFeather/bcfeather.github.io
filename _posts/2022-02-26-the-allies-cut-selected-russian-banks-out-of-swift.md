---
title: "The allies cut selected Russian banks out of the SWIFT network"
date: 2022-02-26 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Seven governments agree to remove banks from the messaging system and to freeze the central bank's reserves, and the rouble falls when the markets open."
---

The leaders of the United States, the European Union, Britain, France, Germany, Italy and Canada issued a joint statement on 26 February committing themselves to remove selected Russian banks from SWIFT, the messaging system that banks use to instruct each other about payments. The same statement promised to freeze the assets of the Russian central bank and to set up a task force to find and hold the property of sanctioned individuals. The measures were the first coordinated attempt to cut a major economy out of the infrastructure of international finance. The carve-out for Sberbank and Gazprombank, the two that handled the energy trade, was deliberate, because Europe bought about a quarter of its oil and a third of its gas from Russia.

The choice of banks mattered more than the choice of mechanism. The list that the European Union published on 2 March named seven institutions, among them VTB, Bank Otkritie, Novikombank, Promsvyazbank and Rossiya, and it left out Sberbank and Gazprombank, the two that handle most of the payments for oil and gas. What the exclusion does, technically, is stop a bank from sending the messages that instruct a correspondent bank to move money. The payment itself still has to travel through the correspondent relationship, which means that a bank cut from SWIFT can still trade if it finds a partner willing to settle through a channel of its own. Russia had built such a channel, the System for Transfer of Financial Messages, and it had spent eight years persuading its trading partners to use it.

Freezing the reserves carried more weight. The central bank held about 630 billion dollars of foreign assets, most of them in dollars, euros and pounds in accounts at other central banks and at commercial banks abroad, and the sanctions made those unusable. The bank's response was to raise its policy rate from 9.5 per cent to 20 per cent on 28 February and to impose controls on the movement of capital, and the rouble fell by about 30 per cent against the dollar before the controls took effect.

Effects spread beyond Russia. Commodity traders had to find new ways to pay for Russian oil, and the discount on Russian crude widened to 30 dollars a barrel within a month as buyers demanded compensation for the difficulty. European companies with Russian subsidiaries wrote off their investments, and insurers stopped covering cargoes in the Black Sea. The price of wheat rose by a third, and the countries that import it, many of them in North Africa and the Middle East, began to look for other suppliers.

Tightening continued through the year. Sberbank was added to the list in April, the European Union banned the import of Russian coal in April and of seaborne oil in December, and a price cap on Russian crude was agreed in the autumn. Each step was debated for weeks, and each step was designed to hurt the Russian budget without raising the price that European consumers paid.

One lesson of the joint statement concerns the limits of financial weapons. Cutting a bank out of a messaging system is an act of coordination among states that share a financial system, and the states that do not share it can build another one, more slowly and at a higher cost. The sanctions of February 2022 made Russia poorer, and they did not make it stop.
