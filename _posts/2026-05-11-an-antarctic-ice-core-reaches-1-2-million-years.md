A consortium of ten European institutes reported on 5 May that the ice core drilled at Little Dome C, on the high plateau of East Antarctica, has been dated continuously back to about 1.2 million years before the present. The drill reached bedrock at a depth of two thousand eight hundred metres in January of last year after four summers of work, and the team has now published the age model that turns a column of ice into a calendar. It is the oldest undisturbed climate record ever recovered from ice, and it extends the reach of ice-core science by roughly half a million years.

The prize the drillers were chasing is a period called the Mid-Pleistocene Transition, when the rhythm of the ice ages changed. Before about nine hundred thousand years ago, glaciers advanced and retreated every forty-one thousand years, following the tilt of the earth's axis. After that transition they settled into a hundred-thousand-year cycle, with colder and longer glaciations, and nobody has a satisfactory account of why the period doubled. A continuous record that spans the transition, with the atmospheric composition trapped in its bubbles, is the evidence that the argument has been missing.

The ice itself is a compressed archive. Snow that fell on the plateau has been buried and squeezed until the layers are thinner than a fingernail, and the deepest ice has been flattened to a small fraction of its original thickness, which is why two thousand eight hundred metres of core covers more than a million years. The team dated it by counting annual layers in the upper part, by matching volcanic ash horizons to known eruptions, and by tuning the isotopic composition to orbital cycles in the deeper part, where annual layers can no longer be resolved.

The first results concern the air, not the temperature. Bubbles in the ice sample the atmosphere of the time, and the consortium reported that carbon dioxide stayed within a narrow band across the whole record, never exceeding about three hundred parts per million, which is well below the four hundred and twenty-five of the present atmosphere. The finding does not explain the transition, and it does rule out the simplest candidate explanation, that a fall in greenhouse gas drove the cooling. Methane and nitrous oxide are being measured next.

There is an uncomfortable symmetry in the result. The record shows a planet that oscillated between ice ages and warm periods for a million years with a stable atmosphere, and it shows how much the stability depended on a carbon cycle that people have since disturbed. The core is also a test of the models that are used to project the next century, since a model that cannot reproduce the transition is a model whose warnings deserve a discount or an explanation. Ice cores rarely settle arguments; this one gives the argument better evidence, which in this field is the most a core can do. The core is stored at a facility in Bremen at minus twenty degrees, in sections a metre long, and the consortium has said that the deeper sections will be sampled by three independent laboratories before the carbon dioxide record is published in full.
