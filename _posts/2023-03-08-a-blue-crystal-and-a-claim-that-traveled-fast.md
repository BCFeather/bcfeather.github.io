---
title: "A blue crystal and a claim that travelled fast"
date: 2023-03-08 23:20:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "A paper in Nature on 8 March 2023 reported that nitrogen-doped lutetium hydride conducted electricity without resistance at 294 kelvin and near-ambient pressure, and physicists began trying to repeat the result within days."
---

Superconductivity at room temperature has been the field's standing promise since the 1980s, and every claim of it has arrived with a condition attached. The condition has always been pressure, usually millions of atmospheres, which is why the materials that broke temperature records have stayed in the laboratory. The paper published on 8 March claimed to have removed the condition, and the claim was what made it news.

The material was a compound of lutetium, hydrogen and nitrogen, made by placing a thin foil of lutetium in a diamond anvil cell and adding a mixture of the two gases. The authors reported that the sample became superconducting at 294 kelvin, which is about twenty-one degrees Celsius, at a pressure of roughly one gigavalent, or about ten thousand atmospheres. Ten thousand atmospheres is still a pressure that only a diamond anvil can produce, and it is far below the millions that the previous hydride superconductors had needed.

What the group offered as evidence was the standard set: a sharp drop in resistance, a change in the magnetic susceptibility that excludes field lines, and a critical field. The resistance data showed a transition that began at the reported temperature and fell to a value that the instruments could barely measure. The authors also published the colour change that gave the material its nickname in the press coverage, a blue that turned red under pressure and then back again when the pressure was released.

The context is that the same group had published a claim of superconductivity in a hydrogen-sulfide compound in 2020, and that paper had been retracted by Nature in 2022 after an investigation into its data. The retraction was in the news when the new paper appeared, and the new paper was read by many physicists through the old one. The authors said the new data had been collected and analyzed with more care, and the journal said the paper had been reviewed by referees who knew the earlier case. Readers had raised questions about the resistance plot in the earlier paper, and the questions took eighteen months to resolve.

Reproduction is the test that the field applies, and it began immediately. Groups in China, in Germany and in the United States published attempts within weeks, and their results were mixed: some reported no superconductivity at all, and others reported transitions at lower temperatures. The disagreement was about the sample rather than about the theory, because the synthesis is difficult and the amount of material produced is tiny.

Whatever the outcome of those attempts, the paper changed the conversation. A room-temperature superconductor at a pressure that a laboratory can reach would be a material with obvious uses, from magnets in scanners to cables in grids, and the search for one had been conducted with the assumption that it required impossible pressures. The claim set a target, and the attempts to hit it became a subject in their own right, which is a kind of progress even when the target moves.
