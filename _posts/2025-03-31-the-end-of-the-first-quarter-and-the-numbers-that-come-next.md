The first quarter closed on the thirty-first of March, and the numbers that describe it will arrive in the middle of April, when the statistics office publishes the growth figure and the customs administration publishes the trade figures and the companies publish their results. The fortnight in between is the period in which the market forms a view, and the view is formed from the monthly indicators that arrived during the quarter rather than from the quarter itself.

The indicators that matter most are the ones that are published early and are hard to revise. Electricity generation is one, because a factory that runs uses power and a factory that does not, does not. Freight volume is another, because goods that are made are goods that move. The purchasing managers' index is a third, and it is a survey rather than a count, which makes it faster and less reliable. A person who watches all three can form a reasonable estimate of the quarter before the quarter's own number is published.

The quarter itself had a shape that the monthly data described. January was the month of the holiday, with the factories closed and the consumption concentrated in travel and restaurants. February was the month of the return, with the labour coming back and the production restarting. March was the month in which the export orders were tested against the tariffs that had been announced in the preceding weeks, and the test is the part that the April numbers will answer.

The financial markets have their own calendar and it runs a little ahead. The quarter-end is a date on which the funds rebalance, the banks window-dress their balance sheets and the index providers reweight, and the effect on the prices in the last week of March is a technical one that the analysts know to discount. The first week of April often reverses it, and the reversal is not a signal about the economy.

What the quarter leaves is a set of questions that the year will answer slowly. Whether the consumer is spending, whether the exporter can find new markets, whether the property sector has stopped shrinking, whether the local governments can fund the infrastructure they have promised. Each of those is a number in a table in April, and each of them will be revised twice before the year is out, and the revisions are part of the answer rather than a correction of it, because a first estimate is a guess that the later data are allowed to improve.

The habit of waiting for the numbers is worth examining. A quarter is an accounting period, and the economy does not stop at the end of March to be counted. What the period does is impose a rhythm on the argument, and the rhythm is useful because it forces the people who disagree to agree on a date at which they will look at the same figures. The figures will not settle the argument. They will move it, and the moving is the business of the year.
