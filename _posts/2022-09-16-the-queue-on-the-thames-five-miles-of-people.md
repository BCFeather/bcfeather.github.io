title: "The queue on the Thames, five miles of people waiting to walk past a coffin"
date: 2022-09-16 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [essay]
description: "A crowd that has been standing for twelve hours for a few seconds in a hall, and the small economy that grew up around it."
---

The queue begins at the Albert Embankment and runs upstream along the river, and on Friday afternoon the end of it was somewhere past Southwark Park, about five miles from the door. People joined it with folding chairs, thermos flasks and sleeping bags, and the stewards handed out numbered wristbands and told them to expect a wait of twelve hours. The hall opens at five in the morning and closes at half past ten at night, and the line moves at a walking pace for every hour it is open. The weather has been dry and mild, which is the one mercy the stewards could not arrange.

The people in it form no sample of the country. They are older and quieter than the crowd at a football match, and they came from towns that have not been in the news for decades. A woman near the front said she had taken two buses and a train and had brought a sandwich, and that she expected to be inside before midnight. Behind her, a group of nurses in uniform had come off a night shift and joined the line without going home. The stewards had stopped letting people leave and rejoin, so the queue had the discipline of a place where nobody wants to lose their position.

Logistics of that kind are a piece of staff work that the army and the police have been rehearsing for years. Barriers went up along the route, the bridges were closed to traffic, and volunteers in tabards walked the line with water and foil blankets. The local councils opened toilets in libraries and churches, and the shops along the route stayed open through the night. A queue of five miles has to be fed, warmed and kept from crushing itself, and the plan for that is about as long as the plan for the funeral.

What the waiting does to people is the part the cameras cannot show. Twelve hours in a line is a long time to talk to a stranger about a monarch, and the conversations reported from the route were about the war, about the price of heating and about the last time the country did something together. There were children who had been told by their parents what the Queen did during the war, and there were people who had come because their own parents would have come. The queue turned into a place where the country said things to itself that it does not say in ordinary weeks.

When the line reaches the hall, each person is given a few seconds in front of the coffin and then moved on. There is no pause for a photograph and no chance to say anything, and the stewards keep the flow with a hand at the elbow. People come out through a side door into the daylight and stand on the pavement for a while, and some of them cry and some of them look as though they have finished a long journey. A few seconds is a small thing to wait twelve hours for, and nobody in the line described it as small.

By Tuesday the queue will be gone, and the barriers will come down, and the route will go back to being a road. What it has shown is a capacity for patience that the country does not usually display, and a need for ceremony that the political class had underestimated. In a week when the pound was falling and the gas bill was rising, five miles of people stood still and waited their turn. It is hard to say what that means, and it was clearly worth doing.
