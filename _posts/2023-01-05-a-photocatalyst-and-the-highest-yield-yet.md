A paper in *Nature* on 5 January reported a photocatalytic system that splits water into hydrogen and oxygen using sunlight, and reported an efficiency higher than any previously published for the approach. The work appears in the journal's first issue of the year, at page 66 of volume 613, and it belongs to a line of research that began in 1972. The efficiency figure is the claim that will be quoted, and the conditions of the measurement are set out in the supplementary material. The claim, in the language of the field, is a record rather than a discovery.

In that year two researchers at the University of Tokyo, Akira Fujishima and Kenichi Honda, showed that a titanium dioxide electrode illuminated with ultraviolet light would split water. The experiment was elegant and the economics were hopeless, because ultraviolet is a small part of the solar spectrum and titanium dioxide absorbs little of the visible range. Fifty years of work have gone into finding materials that absorb more of the spectrum and hold on to the charge carriers long enough to use them. The Japanese work was published in 1972 and was ignored for a decade, which is the usual fate of a result that is correct and impractical.

Solar-to-hydrogen efficiency is the number the field argues about, and it is the fraction of the energy arriving as sunlight that ends up stored in the hydrogen-oxygen bond. Researchers have generally treated ten per cent as the level at which the process might begin to interest an industrial partner, and most published systems have stood well below it. Many of them also relied on sacrificial electron donors, which is a way of measuring a reaction that would not run on its own.

The new system belongs to the class known as particulate photocatalysts, in which tiny crystals of a semiconductor are suspended in water and each particle does the whole job of absorbing light, separating charge and catalysing both half-reactions. The difficulty with the design is that the two half-reactions must occur on the same particle without the products recombining, which is why co-catalysts are deposited on the surfaces. The particles are typically a micrometre across, and the co-catalysts are islands of metal or metal oxide deposited on their surfaces.

Reported alongside the efficiency was a stability measurement long enough to show that the gain had not been bought with the catalyst's lifetime, which is the trade this literature usually makes. It does not report a reactor of any size, and the gap between a beaker of suspended powder and a plant that produces hydrogen at a useful rate is measured in orders of magnitude.

Sunlight is free and the reactor has a price. A record efficiency is a reason to keep building reactors, and it is a long way from being a reason to stop building something else. The next problem is the one that every solar chemistry faces, because a reactor has to survive weather and weather is the part that no catalyst can be designed around.
