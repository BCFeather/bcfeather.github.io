The northern cities heat their flats from the middle of November to the middle of March, and the coldest week of January is when the system is tested. The pipe that runs through a district is fed by a boiler house that burns gas or coal, the water arrives at a temperature that the operator sets each morning, and the flat that is too warm in December is often the flat that is too cold in January.

The bill is the part of the system that a household can see. In most northern cities the heating charge is calculated by the floor area of the flat rather than by the heat that is used, and it is paid once, before the season, at a rate that the municipal price bureau sets. The consequence is that a family pays the same whether it opens the window in a warm week or keeps it shut in a cold one, and the incentive to save heat is therefore absent at the point where the heat is consumed.

The metering reform has been the attempt to change that. A building that installs a heat meter and a valve on each radiator can charge by consumption, and the households that participate pay less when they turn the valve down. The reform has been slow because the pipe network in an old building was designed for a single flow, and the household at the end of the corridor is the one that suffers when the neighbours turn their valves down.

The gas behind the heat is a national question. The winter load curve in the north rises sharply in the coldest weeks, and the supply is met by the pipeline gas, the imported liquefied gas and the storage that the operators fill in the summer. The storage is the cheapest insurance and it is also the least visible, and the cities that built it are the ones that can hold the temperature when a cold front arrives for a week.

The cold week also produces the complaints that the hotline records. A radiator that is cold in one room, a building that is heated at six in the evening and cold at six in the morning, a charge that is levied on an empty flat. The complaints are about the service and the service is about the pipe, and the pipe is a piece of infrastructure that the city laid decades ago and cannot easily change.

What the season shows is that heating is a public service delivered through private rooms. The comfort of a household depends on a boiler house it never sees and on a gas contract signed by a company it never meets, and the bill arrives as a fixed number that describes none of that. The flat is warm and the arithmetic is hidden, which is the normal condition of a utility.

For the household, the useful knowledge is the small kind. A radiator that is cold at the top and warm at the bottom needs to be bled, a room that is too dry in a heated flat needs a bowl of water on the radiator, and a valve that is turned down in an unused room saves nothing on a fixed charge. The system is large and the household can only work at the edges of it.
