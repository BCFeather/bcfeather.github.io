---
title: "The Bank of Japan loosens its grip on bond yields"
date: 2023-07-31 21:20:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Bank of Japan kept its policy rate at minus 0.1 per cent on 28 July but allowed ten-year government bond yields to rise to one per cent, relaxing a cap it had defended since the end of last year."
---

The decision was small in the wording and large in the effect. At the end of its two-day meeting on 28 July, the Bank of Japan kept its short-term policy rate at minus one tenth of one per cent, where it has been since 2016, and changed the way it manages long-term interest rates. The cap that had kept ten-year government bond yields within half a percentage point of zero became a reference rather than a limit, and the bank said it would tolerate moves up to one per cent, buying bonds only when yields threatened to go further.

Governor Kazuo Ueda described the change as an improvement to the bank's tools rather than the beginning of a tightening cycle. Traders read it differently. Yields rose to their highest since 2014, the yen jumped and then gave back the gain, and Japanese bank shares rallied on the expectation of better margins. The bank also raised its forecast for inflation this year and said that wage growth was becoming more durable. He added that the spring wage negotiations, which set the pattern for the year, would be watched closely.

Arithmetic had made the policy untenable. Japan's inflation has run above the bank's two per cent target for more than a year, and core measures are near four per cent, which is why the gap between Japanese and American yields has widened enough to make hedging a Japanese bond an expensive exercise. Every time the bank defended the cap it had to buy more bonds, and the more it bought, the more it owned, until its holdings exceeded the size of the country's gross domestic product. It now owns more than half of all outstanding government bonds, a position that makes an exit hard to design and easy to postpone.

Global consequences run through the currency. A world in which Japanese investors can earn more at home is a world in which they repatriate capital, and Japanese institutions are the largest foreign holders of American and European debt. The rise in yields has been gradual and the change was widely anticipated, which has kept the spillover modest so far, but the direction is now established.

Households will feel the change in their mortgages and their savings. Most Japanese mortgages are variable, tied to short rates that the bank has not moved, and the burden will grow only if a rate rise follows. Pension funds, which had bought bonds for their certainty, now hold assets whose value moves, a novelty in a market that spent two decades assuming it did not. Insurers will have to mark long bonds to market for the first time in years, and their capital will move with them.

Future meetings will be judged by whether the bank lets the new band do the work or defends it with purchases. Ueda has said the change is technical, and the market has decided it is the beginning of something, which is how these things usually start, and how they usually end.
