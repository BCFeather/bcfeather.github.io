---
title: "Lilly caps the price of insulin"
date: 2023-03-01 23:20:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "Eli Lilly announced on 1 March 2023 that it would cut the list price of its most prescribed insulins by seventy per cent and cap the monthly cost at thirty-five dollars, a decision taken under political pressure and against a background of falling margins."
---

Eli Lilly said on 1 March that it would cut the list price of Humalog and its other rapid-acting insulins by seventy per cent, and that it would cap what a patient pays at thirty-five dollars a month. The company also said it would lower the price of its older human insulins to the same monthly figure. The announcement arrived eleven days after a similar move by Novo Nordisk, and the two companies together make most of the insulin sold in the United States.

The list price is the number that the industry had been defending for two decades. It is what a patient without insurance is charged and what an insurer uses as the starting point for a rebate negotiation, and it had risen several hundred per cent since the 1990s while the cost of producing the molecule fell. Patients who bought the drug at the list price had been crossing the border into Canada and into Mexico for years, and the practice had become a political symbol before it became a business problem.

In Washington the pressure behind the change was legislative as well as moral. The Inflation Reduction Act had capped insulin at thirty-five dollars a month for people on Medicare, and the cap had taken effect in January, which removed the most profitable part of the market from the old pricing model. California had announced that it would contract for its own generic insulin at a lower price, and several states were preparing similar plans.

Inside the company the business case was the part that its executives emphasized. Lilly's insulin franchise had been losing share to cheaper biosimilars and to pharmacy benefit arrangements that pushed patients towards other products, and a lower list price was a way of competing on the price that patients see rather than on the rebate that they never see. The company's gross margin on the product would fall, and its volume would rise, and the arithmetic of that trade was the one its investors were asked to accept.

Roughly a million Americans with type 1 diabetes depend on insulin to stay alive, and the cost of it had become the most cited example of a drug price that bears no relation to the cost of making the drug. The announcement was read by patient groups as a genuine reduction and by critics as a concession designed to head off a worse one. Both readings described the same event, which is what happens when a price is set by regulation and negotiation rather than by a market. By then the price a patient paid at the counter had become a subject that every pharmacy chain in the country had a policy about.

The effects took a year to appear in the data. List prices fell, some patients paid less, and the rebate system that had obscured the old prices began to be questioned by insurers and by employers. The two companies had moved first among the large manufacturers, and the smaller ones followed within a year, which suggested that the competitive pressure had been real and that the political pressure had made it visible.
