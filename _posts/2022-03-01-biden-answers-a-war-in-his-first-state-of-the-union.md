---
title: "Biden answers a war in his first State of the Union"
date: 2022-03-01 23:45:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "An address drafted for inflation and a pandemic is rewritten in ten days to meet the first Russian columns, and the chamber stands for a flag that few of its members had thought about."
---

President Biden delivered his first State of the Union address on 1 March, and he began with a war that had been going for six days rather than with the economy that had been going badly for a year. The speech had been drafted for a recovery and rewritten as the Russian army advanced, so that a passage on roads and bridges sat beside a passage on artillery. Members of both parties wore blue and yellow ribbons, and the Ukrainian ambassador watched from the gallery as the guest of the first lady.

Sanctions came first. Mr Biden listed what the allies had done in a week: Russian banks cut off from the dollar, the airspace closed to Russian aircraft, export controls on technology, and the release of 60 million barrels of oil from the reserves of thirty countries. He promised that the Nord Stream 2 pipeline would be shut, and he said the Russian president had miscalculated the response. 'He was wrong,' he said of the calculation that the West would watch.

On prices he offered a list rather than a theory. The plan promised to cut the cost of prescription drugs, to cap insulin at 35 dollars a month, to fund child care and care for the elderly, and to build houses and roads with American materials. He called it a plan to build a better America, and he asked the companies that had made record profits to accept a minimum tax and a levy on share buybacks.

The pandemic section was shorter than the one drafted a month earlier. Cases had fallen from the peak of January, the mask guidance had been loosened, and the president announced a programme that would let people who tested positive collect antiviral pills at a pharmacy. He said the country was reaching a point where the virus would be managed rather than confronted, which is a sentence a president uses when an emergency is winding down and he does not want to say so.

The Republican answer came from Governor Kim Reynolds of Iowa, who spoke in a kitchen in Des Moines and said the party in power had spent a year on government overreach. She blamed the president for the price of petrol, the state of the border and the shortages in shops, and she said the answer was to produce more at home. Neither speech acknowledged the other's strongest point: the sanctions that the president had announced were raising the price of the fuel that the governor blamed him for.

What the address fixed was the terms of the year. A president elected to end a pandemic and repair an economy now led a coalition at war with a nuclear state, and the domestic programme he had promised in 2020 became a list of items to be funded after the emergency. His approval had fallen to about 40 per cent, and the speech did not move it. Six days of war had taken the agenda he had, and he spent the rest of the year asking Congress for money for a country he had never intended to defend.
