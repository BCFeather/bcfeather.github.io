The Tour de France ends in Paris on the last Sunday of July, and the final stage is a procession that turns into a sprint on the Champs-Élysées in the last hour. By the time the riders reach it, the race has been decided for a week, and the last week is a formality conducted in heat and in front of crowds that have been standing at the roadside since morning.

The race itself has changed more than its route, and the change is mostly about attention. Twenty years ago the coverage was a summary on the evening news and a results table in the newspaper. Now it is a live stream from the first kilometre, telemetry on every rider, a camera on a motorcycle and a commentary that runs for five hours a day. The audience for the middle of a flat stage, which is several hours of riders moving at a steady speed across farmland, is a genuinely new thing in sport.

What the roadside shows is different again, and it is the part of the race that the television cameras cover least. The crowds are not spectators in the sense that a football crowd is. They arrive in camper vans the day before, they occupy a field, they wait for a two-minute window in which the peloton passes, and then they pack up and drive to another field. The economics of a small town hosting a stage finish are favourable for one day and unpredictable for the rest of the year.

The sport's own problems are old and unresolved. Doping controls have improved and the records of the 1990s and 2000s remain the shadow over the palmarès. Team budgets are concentrated in a handful of countries. The calendar is brutal in a way that no other sport quite matches, with three-week races that require a rider to hold a peak for twenty-one days and a support team that must do the same.

The ascent that decides the race is usually one of the big alpine or Pyrenean climbs, and the deciding move is made by a rider who has been conserving energy for two weeks and who attacks with four kilometres to go. The gap that opens is a minute and a half, which is the margin on which a three-week race turns, and the rider who loses it will describe the day for years afterwards.

The riders themselves have become more open about the costs. Retirement comes early, crashes are frequent, and the peloton's willingness to talk about head injuries has changed in the last five years. None of this is unique to cycling. Every sport has a calendar, a history and a commercial structure. What is unusual is the way the race uses public roads, so that the spectacle and the country occupy exactly the same space for three weeks, and a field in the south of France becomes, for one afternoon, the centre of a sport watched by millions of people who will never stand in it.
