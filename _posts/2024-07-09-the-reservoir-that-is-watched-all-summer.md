A reservoir above a flood plain holds water back in July and releases it in September. The dam that does this is operated to a rule. The rule is written as a table of levels that match the inflow against the forecast.

The flood control volume is the part that is kept empty. A reservoir of a hundred metres of depth may hold twenty metres of it in reserve during the wet season, and the reserve is the space that a flood can be absorbed into without touching the gates.

The forecast decides how much of the reserve is used. A typhoon that is expected to bring two hundred millimetres of rain in three days is a signal to release water early, before the rain arrives, so that the space is available when the flood comes.

The release is unpopular downstream. A river that is drawn down in the middle of a dry week looks like a mistake to the people who live beside it. The operator publishes its reasoning. The reasoning is ignored until the rain falls.

The dam also generates power. A turbine that runs at full output in July earns the revenue that pays for the maintenance of the year. A release made for flood control is passed through the turbines when the level allows, which recovers part of the cost.

The reservoir is one of a chain. A river with five dams is operated as a single system. Water released from the upper one arrives at the lower one two days later. The coordination is done by a dispatch centre that sees all five levels at once.

The sediment is the slow problem. A river carries silt into the reservoir and the silt settles behind the dam, which reduces the volume that the table assumes. A survey every few years measures the loss and the table is adjusted.

The drought years put the same table under strain in the other direction. A reservoir that is asked to supply irrigation in a dry spring enters the wet season with less reserve than the table wants, and the operator has to choose between a smaller flood margin and a smaller harvest.

The summer passes with the level in a band that nobody notices. The work is done in the numbers. Those numbers are reviewed in a room that the public never sees.

The dam is inspected every year by a team that walks the galleries inside it. A crack of a tenth of a millimetre is measured and recorded. A seep that was not there last year is mapped and watched. The record is a book of measurements that goes back to the year the concrete was poured.

The downstream levee is the part that people see. A bank of earth that was raised in the last century protects a town of fifty thousand. The levee is inspected in April, repaired in May, and watched in July by a team that walks it with a torch at night.
