A reservoir above a flood plain holds water back in July and releases it in September. Operators work it to a rule. A table of levels, matching inflow against forecast, is how the rule is written. What is kept empty is the flood control volume. A reservoir of a hundred metres of depth may hold twenty metres of it in reserve during the wet season, and the reserve is the space that a flood can be absorbed into without touching the gates.

Forecast decides how much of the reserve is used. A typhoon that is expected to bring two hundred millimetres of rain in three days is a signal to release water early, before the rain arrives, so that the space is available when the flood comes. The release is unpopular downstream. A river that is drawn down in the middle of a dry week looks like a mistake to the people who live beside it. Operators publish their reasoning. Reasoning is ignored until the rain falls.

Power is generated there too. A turbine that runs at full output in July earns the revenue that pays for the maintenance of the year. A release made for flood control is passed through the turbines when the level allows, which recovers part of the cost. The reservoir is one of a chain. A river with five dams is operated as a single system. Water released from the upper one arrives at the lower one two days later. Coordination is done by a dispatch centre that sees all five levels at once.

Sediment is the slow problem. A river carries silt into the reservoir and the silt settles behind the dam, which reduces the volume that the table assumes. A survey every few years measures the loss and the table is adjusted. The drought years put the same table under strain in the other direction. A reservoir that is asked to supply irrigation in a dry spring enters the wet season with less reserve than the table wants, and the operator has to choose between a smaller flood margin and a smaller harvest.

Every year a team walks the galleries inside the dam. A crack of a tenth of a millimetre is measured and recorded. A seep that was not there last year is mapped and watched. A book of measurements, going back to the year the concrete was poured, is the record.

Downstream, the levee is the part people see. A bank of earth that was raised in the last century protects a town of fifty thousand. Levee inspection comes in April, repair in May, and a night watch in July by a team that walks it with a torch. The summer passes with the level in a band that nobody notices. Work is done in the numbers. Those numbers are reviewed in a room that the public never sees. By the end of September the level is where the plan wants it, the galleries have been walked for the last time this year, and the town downstream has forgotten the number that was quoted in July.
