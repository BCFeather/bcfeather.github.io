The Park Fire began on the twenty-fourth of July in a park north of Chico, in northern California. By the tenth of August it had burned more than four hundred thousand acres, which makes it the fourth largest in the state's recorded history.

The ignition was deliberate. A man pushed a car that was already burning down a slope into a gully. The grass at the bottom caught within minutes. He was arrested the same week and charged with arson.

The fire ran north through the foothills for eleven days. It crossed two highways, jumped a river, and burned into a forest that had not burned in sixty years. A fire that has that much fuel does not need wind to spread. The wind arrived anyway.

The smoke went east. A plume that reached fifteen kilometres into the atmosphere crossed the Rockies and dimmed the sky over the Great Plains. Air quality alerts were issued in four states that had no fire of their own.

The evacuation was ordered in stages. A town of six thousand was emptied in an afternoon. The fairground became a shelter with cots and a generator. The animals were the harder problem. A fairground with a thousand horses in it needs water that the town supply cannot provide.

The crews worked a twelve-hour shift on and twelve off. A hand crew cuts a line with a saw and a shovel. The line is only as good as the wind allows. Aerial tankers drop retardant in lines that a ground crew then holds.

The cost is counted in three parts. The suppression bill for a fire of this size runs into the hundreds of millions, the structure losses are counted house by house. The timber loss is counted in a decade of growth.

The insurance market has already repriced. A county that had one large fire in twenty years has had four in eight. The companies that write policies in the foothills have raised the premium or withdrawn.

The forest will come back and it will come back differently. A severe fire removes the seed source. A slope that burns twice in a decade converts from conifer to shrub. The conversion is the part that the state's carbon accounting does not capture.

The utility cut the power to a thousand kilometres of line before the fire reached it. A public safety shutoff is unpopular with the customers who lose refrigeration for three days. It is the cheapest way to stop a fire that starts at a downed conductor.

The burn scar is the problem after the fire. A slope that has lost its vegetation sheds water instead of absorbing it, and a storm in the following winter can move more sediment in an afternoon than the fire moved in three weeks.

The fire was contained in the last week of August. Containment is a line around the perimeter, which does not mean the fire is out. A stump can hold heat for weeks, and a wind in September can carry it over a line that took three weeks to build.
