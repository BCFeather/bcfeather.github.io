---
title: "Guatemala's election sends two outsiders to a runoff"
date: 2023-06-26 15:20:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Guatemala's first round on 25 June put Bernardo Arevalo, a centre-left candidate whose party was polling in single digits, into an August runoff against Sandra Torres, after a count that displaced the establishment candidates."
---

Guatemala counted the votes of its general election on 25 June and produced a result that no poll had predicted: Bernardo Arevalo, a former diplomat and the son of a president who was overthrown in 1954, finished a strong second and qualified for the runoff in August against Sandra Torres, a former first lady who has run three times before. The candidates who had been expected to fill the two places, including a former general and a mayor of the capital, fell behind, and the count moved slowly enough that the result went unconfirmed until the following day.

Arevalo's party, Movimiento Semilla, was formed by a group of academics and activists and had been polling in low single figures for much of the campaign. Its appeal was built on anti-corruption rather than on ideology, and it drew support from young voters in the capital and from a wider electorate that has grown tired of the parties that have shared power since the return to democracy in 1986. The party's candidate for vice-president was a doctor who had worked in rural health, and the campaign was run on a budget that its opponents described as negligible.

The election was conducted under unusual conditions. Three candidates who had been leading in the polls were excluded by the electoral tribunal before the vote, on grounds that ranged from registration errors to accusations of corruption, and one of them, a businessman who had been first in most surveys, was barred just weeks before the ballot. The exclusions drew criticism from international observers and from the United States, and they shaped a field in which the remaining establishment candidates divided the vote that had been expected to carry one of them into the runoff.

Torres is the better known of the two finalists and the more experienced campaigner. She was first lady during the presidency of her former husband, Alvaro Colom, and she has built a party organization that has survived his death and her previous defeats. She is associated with the social programmes that the country's poor have relied on, and with the political networks that Arevalo has campaigned against. The runoff will therefore be a referendum on whether the country wants a government that breaks with those networks or one that manages them.

Stakes are higher than the office. Guatemala has been through a period in which prosecutors and judges who investigated corruption were removed or driven into exile, and the next president will decide whether that process continues. Arevalo has said he would restore the independence of the courts and support the return of the prosecutors who left; Torres has said she would govern with the institutions as they are. The runoff is in August, and the country will spend the intervening weeks deciding whether the surprise of 25 June was a beginning or an accident.

Arevalo now has three weeks to turn a protest vote into a coalition, and Torres has three weeks to persuade the country that experience is what the moment requires. The runoff is on 20 August, and the pollsters who missed the first round will be watched more closely the second time.
