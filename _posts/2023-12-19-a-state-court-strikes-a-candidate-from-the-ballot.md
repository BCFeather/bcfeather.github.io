---
title: "A state court strikes a candidate from the ballot"
date: 2023-12-19 23:45:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Colorado Supreme Court ruled on 19 December 2023, by four votes to three, that Donald Trump is disqualified from the state's Republican primary by the insurrection clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, a decision stayed until January and appealed to the federal Supreme Court within a week."
---

The Colorado Supreme Court ruled on 19 December that Donald Trump is ineligible to appear on the state's Republican primary ballot, applying a clause of the Fourteenth Amendment that bars from office anybody who took an oath to support the Constitution and then engaged in insurrection against it. The majority was four judges to three, all of them appointed by Democratic governors, and the opinion is the first from a state supreme court to reach that conclusion. The court stayed its own order until 4 January, which gave the candidate and the party time to appeal.

Written in 1868 for former Confederates, the clause has been used rarely and never against a president. A group of Republican and unaffiliated voters in Colorado brought the case in September, arguing that the events of 6 January 2021 met the definition, and a district court agreed after a five-day trial that the former president had engaged in insurrection but held that the clause did not apply to the presidency. The supreme court reversed the second half of that reasoning, reading the text to cover every office under the Constitution.

Dissent argued that the question belongs to Congress, that the procedure denied the candidate due process, and that the clause requires legislation to be enforced. The majority answered each point: the clause is self-executing, it was applied without legislation after the Civil War, and the trial court had heard the evidence and made findings of fact. What the majority did not answer is the practical question of what happens if a candidate who is leading every poll is removed from a ballot in one state and stays on in others.

Republican politics responded within hours. The state party called the ruling election interference and said it would file an appeal; the campaign described it as an attack on democracy; the primary field, which had spent the autumn arguing about who could beat him, closed ranks. The appeal reached the federal Supreme Court within a week, and the justices took the case on an expedited schedule, which put a decision on the national ballot question before the primaries in March.

Legally the effect may be narrow, and politically it may be enormous. Colorado holds 37 delegates of the 2,429 that will attend the Republican convention, and a candidate removed from its primary loses a small number of them. Maine's secretary of state reached the same conclusion on 28 December, which added one more state and one more legal theory to the pile, and the pattern that matters is that the question is now being answered by officials in different states in different ways.

The country has been here before in a different form. In 2000 the Supreme Court stopped a recount and settled a presidential election with a five to four vote, and the institution's standing has been questioned in every cycle since. A decision on whether a leading candidate can appear on the ballot is the kind of question that a court can answer and that a country may not accept, and the appeal filed in December asks nine justices to settle something that half the electorate has already decided.
