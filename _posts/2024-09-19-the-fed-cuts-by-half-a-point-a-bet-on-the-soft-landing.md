On the eighteenth of September the Federal Reserve cut the benchmark interest rate by half a percentage point, to a range of 4.75 to 5.00 per cent. It was the first reduction since March 2020 and twice the quarter point that most forecasters had pencilled in. Jerome Powell, the chair, called the decision a recalibration of policy. The word was chosen to smooth the passage from inflation-fighting to labour-market defence.

Substance was plainer. Officials believe that the inflation of 2021 to 2024 is over. They are now worried, openly, about jobs.

Inflation peaked above nine per cent in 2022 and has fallen to about 2.5 per cent, close enough for the committee to declare victory. Employment has been cooling for months. Unemployment has drifted up. Revisions to earlier payroll data showed that job growth over the past year was weaker than anyone thought when the figures were published. A dot plot pointed to another half point of cuts by December.

Risk being managed now is the oldest one in monetary policy, that of being late. A cooling labour market left alone becomes a recession. The cut is insurance against that outcome rather than a reward for a won fight.

Markets read the news with the ambivalence the moment deserved. Stocks rallied, then fell back, as investors weighed support against the signal that weakness was serious enough to need it. Bonds absorbed the move without drama. The dollar drifted. Transmission takes a year, and by the time this cut is felt it may be the smallest of several.

Politics was unavoidable. Donald Trump, who had spent months demanding cuts and attacking the chair personally, called the move political and timed for the Democrats. The White House presented it as proof of competent management. An institution that says it takes no account of politics is telling the truth and answering nothing, because a cut eight weeks before an election will be read politically whatever the intent.

Judgement underneath it will be debated for years. In 2021 the bank could treat the surge as temporary or act early, and it waited. Inflation then required the fastest tightening in four decades, and the tightening has been followed by half a point at the first sign of cooling, in an election year. Whether that describes a bank that learned its lesson or one overcorrecting in the other direction is a question the unemployment reports of the next year will answer. Credibility built over decades survives this episode, probably. It will be tested again before the year is out. The bet was announced on the eighteenth of September and the outcome is booked for 2025. Whether the landing is soft will be read in the payroll reports of the winter, which are the only evidence that counts. Two decades of low inflation had made the bank slow to believe that prices could run, and the cost of that delay is now being paid in a labour market that looked calm until it stopped adding jobs at the rate the country had come to expect.
