---
title: "Thirty per cent of the land and the sea"
date: 2022-12-19 20:00:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "Nearly two hundred parties adopt the Kunming-Montreal framework, with twenty-three targets for 2030 and a long argument about money."
---
The gavel came down at three in the morning in Montreal, after two weeks of negotiation and a final night that nearly ended without an agreement. Nearly two hundred parties adopted a framework that had been drafted in China and negotiated in Canada, because the first half of the conference could not be held in Kunming during the pandemic. The text carries four long-term goals and twenty-three targets, most of them dated 2030. Delegates from the countries that had blocked language about consumption applauded the adoption and then complained about it in the corridor.

One target has been quoted more than the rest: thirty per cent. By 2030 the parties are meant to protect at least thirty per cent of land and thirty per cent of the ocean, and to restore thirty per cent of degraded ecosystems. At the moment about seventeen per cent of the land and eight per cent of the sea are protected in some form. The gap is large, and the money to close it was discussed in the same document.

Money was the dispute that held the conference together and nearly broke it. The framework calls for at least two hundred billion dollars a year by 2030 from all sources, and for twenty billion a year from rich countries to poor ones by 2025, rising to thirty billion by 2030. A new fund is to be created under the financial mechanism, and the text asks for the money to be delivered before the next conference. Several African delegations said the figures were too small and the timetable too slow. That figure was the one most often quoted by the delegations that had come to Montreal asking for a new fund.

Another contested subject was the language about damage. Targets call for the reduction of the rate at which species are lost, of the risk from pesticides, and of the subsidy that damages nature by at least five hundred billion dollars a year. That number had been fought over for months, and it survived in the final text with a qualification about the most harmful subsidies. Brazil and Argentina had objected to language about sustainable diets, and that language was rewritten.

Indigenous rights appear throughout the document, and that was a victory for the delegations that had argued for them in every session. Several targets require that protected areas be governed with the consent of the communities that live in them, and the framework recognizes traditional knowledge as a source of information. Conservation groups said afterwards that the words would matter more than the numbers if the money reached the ground. Some of that language had been removed from an earlier draft at the request of several governments, and it returned after a night of negotiation.

Implementation is the part that no conference can guarantee. The framework has no binding sanctions, and its targets are to be reported through national plans rather than enforced by a court. The previous set of targets, agreed in Aichi in 2010, was missed on almost every count. Delegates left Montreal with a document and a deadline, and with the memory of the last set still in the room.
