---
title: "The most distant star torn apart by a black hole, caught in visible light"
date: 2022-11-30 19:30:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "Astronomers report the most distant tidal disruption ever seen, and the first one found with visible light."
---
In February a survey telescope in California noticed a new point of light in a patch of sky that had been unremarkable the night before. The alert went out automatically, and telescopes in Chile and elsewhere were turned towards it within days. The object was given the name AT2022cmc, and at first it looked like a gamma ray burst, the brightest class of explosion in the universe, because it was luminous and it faded quickly. A team that had been hunting for something else entirely took the data and began to argue about it.

The observations that followed pointed to something rarer. A star had wandered too close to a supermassive black hole, been torn apart by tidal forces, and part of its remains had been thrown out in a jet. Only about one per cent of such disruptions produce jets, and the ones known before had been found by X-ray and gamma-ray instruments, which see them as short, hard flashes. This one was found in an optical survey, which meant a whole class of events had been sitting in the data unseen. The team that published the result had been looking for exactly this kind of event in the archive of the same survey.

Distance was the second surprise. The light from AT2022cmc left when the universe was about a third of its present age, which makes it the most distant of these events ever measured. That measurement came from a telescope in Chile, whose spectrograph read the redshift of the light and converted it into a distance. The number was large enough that the event had to be extraordinarily bright to be visible at all. A second observatory measured the same redshift a month later, and the two figures agreed within their errors.

Twenty-one telescopes contributed data, from gamma rays to radio waves, and the case that the team built rests on the shape of the whole spectrum. A jet aimed almost directly at the Earth amplifies the light and stretches it across every band, which is why an event so far away could be seen. The jet itself was narrow, and the alignment was a coincidence that astronomers could not have arranged.

The paper appeared in Nature with a title about a luminous jet from the disruption of a star by a massive black hole. Behind the title is a modest gain: a new way of finding these objects, and a reminder that the sky is surveyed nightly by machines that do not know what they are looking for. Several similar events have since been reported from the same survey.

Whether the result changes anything practical is a question for the next decade. What it changes now is the census of these events, which had been built on instruments that see only the brightest and the nearest. A survey that sees in visible light can find the ones that point at us from much further away, and the count of those will grow. The next survey telescope, due to begin work within a few years, will cover a much larger area of sky every night.
