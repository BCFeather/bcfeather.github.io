April is the season of the dust, and the dust that arrives in a Chinese city in April usually began somewhere else. The source regions are the dry basins of the Mongolian plateau and the deserts of the northwest, where the soil is loose after a winter without vegetation, and the trigger is a cold front with a strong pressure gradient that lifts the particles and carries them east. A city a thousand kilometres away gets a sky the colour of a paper bag and an air quality index that is driven by particles rather than by the gases.

The measurement of it is different from the measurement of ordinary pollution. The monitors that report the index count particles below ten micrometres and below two and a half, and a dust event produces a huge number of the coarse fraction and a moderate number of the fine one. The coarse particles are the ones that reduce visibility and stain the windows, and the fine ones are the ones that reach the lungs, and the two fractions travel differently and are counted separately for that reason.

The forecasting has become the part of the science that the public actually uses. The models that predict a dust event combine a weather forecast, which gives the wind, with a land surface model, which gives the erodibility of the soil, and the output is a plume that can be tracked from the source to the city. The skill of the forecast has improved to the point where a warning can be issued a day ahead, and the warning is used by the schools, the airports and the hospitals in the way that a storm warning is used.

What the dust leaves behind is a cleaning bill. The particles settle on the windows, the cars and the laundry, and the fine fraction settles in the lungs of the people who cannot avoid being outside. The advice that the health authorities give is the one that works, close the windows, run the purifier, wear a mask if you must go out, and the advice is followed unevenly because a delivery rider cannot choose to stay indoors.

The longer record is the part that matters for the policy. The dust events of the past two decades have been less frequent and less intense than those of the 1980s, and the reason is the vegetation in the source regions rather than the strength of the wind. The programmes that planted grasses and restricted grazing across the dry north have reduced the erodibility of the surface, and the reduction is measurable in the satellite record of the dust index, which is one of the few environmental programmes whose effect can be seen from space within a decade.

What the event shows is that the air a city breathes is a shared object. The particles were lifted by a wind in one jurisdiction and are the problem of another, and the only policy that works is the one that changes the surface at the source. A city can filter its own air and it cannot stop the sky from arriving yellow.
