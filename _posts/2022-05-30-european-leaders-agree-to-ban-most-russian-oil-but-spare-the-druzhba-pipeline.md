---
title: "European leaders agree to ban most Russian oil but spare the Druzhba pipeline"
date: 2022-05-30 23:45:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "After four weeks of argument led by Hungary, the European Council agreed late on 30 May to cut seaborne imports of Russian crude within six months and refined products within eight, leaving the pipeline that feeds central Europe exempt and the package covering about ninety per cent of the trade."
---

European Union leaders agreed late on 30 May to ban most imports of Russian oil, ending a month of argument that had stalled the sixth package of sanctions against Moscow. The deal prohibits seaborne crude within six months, refined products within eight, and leaves crude delivered by the Druzhba pipeline to Hungary, Slovakia and the Czech Republic exempt, which means the embargo covers about ninety per cent of the oil the bloc buys from Russia. The package was formally adopted on 3 June, after technical work on the legal text.

The exemption was the price of Hungary's signature. Viktor Orbán had refused to accept any measure that would raise the cost of the crude his country's refineries are built to process, and he had argued that a landlocked state with no access to the sea had no alternative supplier. Hungary's foreign minister compared the proposal to an atomic bomb dropped on the country's economy, and the negotiations moved through several designs, including a scheme to compensate Hungary for the price difference and one that would have exempted the pipeline for a fixed period.

Slovakia and the Czech Republic were granted temporary derogations of their own, Bulgaria secured an exemption from the ban on refined products for a limited period, and Croatia obtained a similar allowance for the vacuum gas oil its refinery uses. The result was a package designed to look uniform from Brussels and to be tolerable in five capitals, with the largest share of the trade covered and the part that could not be replaced left alone.

Other parts of the package were less contested. Sberbank, the largest Russian bank, was excluded from the international payments system, three state broadcasters were banned from European platforms, and a further list of individuals and entities was sanctioned. Insurance issues, which had complicated the shipping of Russian oil for weeks, were addressed with a provision meant to allow Greek and other European tankers to keep carrying cargoes to third countries.

The economics of the ban depend on where the barrels go. Russia had been selling about half of its crude to Europe, and the loss of that market would force it to discount to buyers in India and China, which it had already begun to do. Europe would have to replace the volumes with crude from the Middle East, west Africa and the Americas, and the price of that substitution is paid by consumers who were already facing record fuel costs and inflation above eight per cent.

What the agreement showed was the limit of unanimity. A single member state with a pipeline and no coast could hold twenty-six others for a month, and the compromise that ended the argument kept the pipeline running and the ban partial. The package took effect on the day it was published, and the question it left was whether the remaining ten per cent would be addressed in the next round, when the same member would have the same pipeline and a stronger case for keeping it.
