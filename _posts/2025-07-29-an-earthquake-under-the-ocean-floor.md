A very large earthquake struck off the Kamchatka Peninsula on 29 July, in one of the most seismically active corners of the Pacific. The magnitude was reported above eight, which places it among the strongest events of the decade, and the shaking was felt across the peninsula and in the Kuril Islands. Japan issued evacuation orders along parts of its northern coast, and the Pacific warning system put several countries on alert.

The geology of the region explains the size, and the history explains the fear. Kamchatka sits above a subduction zone, where the Pacific plate slides beneath the Okhotsk plate at a rate of several centimetres a year. The interface between the two plates is locked for decades, accumulates strain, and then releases it in a single rupture that can move the sea floor by metres. When the sea floor moves, the water above it moves with it, and the wave that results travels across an ocean at the speed of an airliner.

The tsunami that followed was smaller than the warnings had anticipated. The waves that reached the coast were modest, the evacuation orders were lifted within hours, and the damage from the shaking itself was local. That outcome is common in these events, and it is also the reason tsunami warnings are sometimes discounted by the public. A warning that turns out to be unnecessary is correct science and poor politics.

The measurement of an event like this is a distributed exercise, and the disagreement between agencies in the first hour is normal rather than alarming. The magnitude is estimated from seismometers within minutes, refined over hours, and revised for days as more data arrive. The tsunami is forecast from models that assume a particular shape of the rupture, and the shape is not known until the wave is observed. A deep earthquake that displaces little water and a shallow one that displaces a great deal can produce the same seismogram and very different oceans.

The history is a sequence of events that arrived without warning and were recorded by whoever survived to write them down. The long record for the region is written in the sediment and in the accounts of earlier events. Kamchatka has produced magnitude nine earthquakes in the past, with tsunamis that crossed the Pacific and reached Japan. The pattern is well documented, and the exposure is not. Coastal settlements in the region have grown since the last large event, and the evacuation routes have been designed for a population that no longer matches the current one.

None of this makes the region unusual. The same arithmetic applies to every coast that faces an ocean trench, and the same gap exists between what the science can say and what a village can do about it. What the 29 July event provided was a rehearsal. The sensors worked, the warnings travelled, the evacuations were carried out, and the wave arrived and did less than feared. The next one may not be so generous.
