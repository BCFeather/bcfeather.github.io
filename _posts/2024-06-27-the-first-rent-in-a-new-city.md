---

June ends with the rental market of a university city turning over. Halls close on the thirtieth, and a graduate with a job elsewhere has four days to find a room, sign a contract and move a suitcase. Nearly twelve million people left university this summer. Most of them took the job nearest the life they had already built, so the share that moved to a new city is smaller than the headlines suggest. The ones who did move had a starting salary high enough to cover a deposit in a place where they knew nobody.

Three flats in an afternoon is what an agent offers. All of them sit within a kilometre of a metro station. One is a room in a shared flat with a mattress on the floor. One is a studio whose landlord wants a year paid in two instalments. The last is two rooms above a shop, priced at the top of the budget.

Cash, rather than rent, decides the choice. A standard contract asks one month as a deposit and three months in advance, which is four months of money before the key is handed over, on a salary that has not been paid once. An agency fee comes on top, usually half a month of rent and sometimes a whole one. The fee is negotiable on a street with nine agencies. A graduate who arrived yesterday negotiates nothing, because the alternative is another afternoon of viewings.

Distance from a station is the variable that no budget can fix. A flat ten minutes from one costs more than a flat twenty minutes away, and the difference in rent is smaller than the difference in time. Someone starting at nine in the centre takes the outer district with a direct line and spends fifty minutes each way on a train that is full at eight. Sharing is where most of them end up. Two or three people who met on a platform rent a larger apartment and divide it by rooms. Rent falls and the risk rises, because a flatmate who leaves in November takes a third of the payment with him and leaves two tenants a month to find a replacement.

Sharing is where most of them end up. A block built under one lets to graduates below a price ceiling, on a three-year contract with a condition on income. Places are ordered by the date of application, so someone who applies in June sits behind everyone who applied in March. Moving costs a day and almost nothing. A suitcase, a mattress bought on credit, a desk that arrives in a box. The van cannot park at the entrance, so the boxes are carried in from the road while somebody holds the lift for the afternoon.

Nothing in the arithmetic is as tight as the gap between the first rent and the first salary. A parent covers it, and that transfer is the part of the graduate economy that no statistic records. By the second year the arithmetic loosens, when the deposit has become the tenant's own money and the salary has caught up with the rent.
