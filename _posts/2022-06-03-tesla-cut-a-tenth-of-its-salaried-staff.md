---
title: "Tesla cut a tenth of its salaried staff"
date: 2022-06-03 23:20:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "An email from the chief executive told salaried employees that the company would shed about ten per cent of them and stop hiring, weeks after he had described the economy as a correction that might become a recession."
---

An email sent to executives on 2 June and reported the next morning told Tesla's salaried employees that the company would cut about a tenth of them. The company employed roughly a hundred thousand people at the time, and the reduction falls mostly on office staff rather than on the lines in Fremont and Shanghai. A separate message suspended hiring worldwide, and recruiters were told to cancel interviews already scheduled. The reductions came in the same week that the company's shares lost about a tenth of their value.

The email gave macroeconomic reasons. The chief executive, Elon Musk, had told an audience at a conference in mid-May that the American economy was in a correction and that a recession was possible, and the note to staff repeated that the company had grown too fast in some functions and had too many people in the wrong places. He added that the cuts would leave the hourly workforce larger than it had been a year earlier.

Tesla had been expanding faster than any carmaker of its size. Deliveries in the first quarter of 2022 were a record, and the company opened a plant near Berlin and another in Texas within a month of each other. Shares had fallen by about forty per cent since January as investors marked down every growth company that depended on cheap money, and the announcement pushed them lower again. Production in Shanghai had been disrupted by the city's lockdown, which shut the plant for three weeks in April.

A second message required office staff to return for at least forty hours a week. Musk wrote that remote work was no longer acceptable and that anyone who preferred to stay away could consider working somewhere else. The demand put Tesla alongside a small group of employers that had decided the pandemic had ended the argument about where white-collar work happens. The policy matched what other manufacturers had begun to ask, though most of them kept a hybrid arrangement.

Similar notes went out across the technology sector in the same week. Coinbase withdrew job offers it had already made and extended a hiring freeze, and Meta told managers to identify low performers. Crypto firms had started cutting in May, when the price of bitcoin fell through the floor of its recent range, and lenders that had grown quickly on the back of customer deposits began to look fragile.

Cutting ten per cent of a salaried workforce is a small move in absolute terms for a company that added tens of thousands of jobs during the pandemic. What makes it worth recording is the timing: a growth company telling its staff to expect a downturn while its order book remained full and its factories were still ramping. Investors read the email as a signal about margins, and the shares, which had lost a third of their value in six months, fell further on the day. Analysts said the saving would be small against the cost of the factories the company was building, which is why the message was read as a signal rather than as a plan.
