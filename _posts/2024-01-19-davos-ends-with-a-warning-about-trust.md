---
title: "Davos ends with a warning about trust"
date: 2024-01-19 21:26:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The World Economic Forum's annual meeting in Davos ran from 15 to 19 January under the theme of rebuilding trust, with wars in Gaza and Ukraine, the Red Sea attacks and the governance of artificial intelligence dominating an agenda that had little room for economics."
---

Delegates met in Davos from the fifteenth to the nineteenth of January for the World Economic Forum's annual meeting, under a theme that the organisers had chosen a year in advance and that the world had made ironic by the time the delegates arrived. Rebuilding trust was the phrase on the badges and the banners. What the participants actually discussed, in the panels and in the corridors, was war. Fighting in Gaza, the war in Ukraine, the attacks on shipping in the Red Sea and the possibility that the conflict could spread were the subjects that filled the main hall, and the economic sessions drew smaller audiences than they have in years.

About twenty-eight hundred people attended, from a hundred and twenty countries, including sixty heads of state and government. President Zelenskyy of Ukraine came in person for the first time since the invasion, and used the visit to press for continued military support at a moment when the American and European funding packages were stuck in their respective parliaments. President Herzog of Israel spoke, as did the prime ministers of Qatar and Jordan, and the conversations among them were reported as being more substantive than the public sessions. The Chinese premier, Li Qiang, attended and made the case for his country's economy to an audience of investors who have spent two years reducing their exposure to it.

Artificial intelligence produced the most concrete argument on the agenda. The forum published a report warning that the gap between the capabilities of AI systems and the institutions meant to govern them was widening, and that the risk lay less in a single catastrophic event than in a slow erosion of the distinction between what is generated and what is true. Several governments used the meeting to announce or preview legislation, and the conversation had moved, in the space of a year, from whether rules were needed to what the rules should contain. Companies that had spent 2023 lobbying against restrictions were now asking to be regulated, which is usually a sign that they have worked out where the rules will fall.

Red Sea shipping came up in almost every session about the economy. Shipping executives described the rerouting of container traffic around the Cape of Good Hope as a permanent change in costs rather than a temporary one, and insurers said the premiums for the route would not return to their old levels while the attacks continued. Strikes by American and British forces on Houthi targets, which had happened a week earlier, were discussed less as a military question than as an illustration of how quickly a regional conflict can become a supply-chain problem for companies that have no position in it.

What the forum is for has been argued about for fifty years, and the argument has become sharper as the institution has grown. It is not a negotiating body and it produces no agreements. What it produces is a week in which the people who make decisions in different countries are in the same small town, eating the same food and walking the same street, and a great deal of business is done in the margins of that. Organisers will say that this year's meeting was a success because the people who needed to talk to each other did. The harder question, which the snow and the security barriers kept out of the main halls, is whether the trust the theme referred to can be rebuilt at all, or whether the best that can be managed is a better understanding of why it broke.
