---
title: "American and British aircraft strike Houthi targets in Yemen"
date: 2024-01-12 22:58:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "American and British aircraft and warships struck more than sixty Houthi targets across Yemen on the night of 11 January, the first direct military response to a campaign against shipping that has diverted trade around the Cape of Good Hope."
---

Late on the night of the eleventh of January, aircraft and warships from the United States and Britain struck more than sixty targets at sixteen sites across Yemen held by the Houthi movement, hitting radar installations, drone and missile launch sites, and the coastal positions from which the movement had been firing at ships. Explosions were reported in Sana'a, Hodeidah and Dhamar. Tomahawk missiles were fired from a warship in the Red Sea, aircraft flew from bases in the region, and British aircraft flew from Akrotiri in Cyprus. The strikes were the first direct military action by the two governments against the group that had spent two months attacking commercial shipping in the Bab al-Mandab, and they were announced by President Biden and Prime Minister Sunak within minutes of each other.

What they were answering had begun in November. Houthi forces seized the Galaxy Leader and its crew on the nineteenth of November, shortly after the war in Gaza began, and then began firing ballistic missiles and drones at ships they judged to be linked to Israel. In practice their targeting was loose, and the ships they hit and damaged included vessels with no Israeli connection at all. Several crews were taken hostage. Container lines that had been routing through the Suez Canal since the 1860s began, within weeks, to send their ships the other way, around the Cape of Good Hope, adding about ten days and a great deal of fuel to every voyage between Asia and Europe. Insurance rates for the Red Sea rose to levels not seen since the tanker attacks of 2019, and by January the traffic through the canal had fallen by more than a third.

A day earlier, the Security Council had settled the legal argument, with a resolution demanding that the Houthis stop the attacks. Russia, China and Algeria abstained rather than veto, which gave the resolution the votes it needed and gave the two governments a document to cite. The text did not authorise the use of force, and both capitals were careful to describe the strikes as self-defence of shipping rather than as a punitive operation. That distinction matters in international law and matters more in the politics of the region, where every government is watching to see whether the American commitment extends beyond a single night of bombing.

What the strikes could not do was obvious by the following morning. Houthi officials said five fighters had been killed and promised retaliation, and the movement's ability to fire at ships does not depend on the sites that were destroyed, because its launchers are mobile, its drones are cheap, and its supply from Iran is a matter of small boats and long borders. The group has survived eight years of bombing by a Saudi-led coalition with far more aircraft and far less restraint. A campaign of a few nights, conducted by governments that have said they do not want a wider war, is unlikely to change a decision made in Sana'a about whether to keep firing.

The consequences are therefore mostly economic, and they will be felt in Europe before they are felt anywhere else. Longer voyages mean higher freight rates, higher fuel bills and later arrivals, and the shipping industry has already begun to build those costs into contracts for the second half of the year. European manufacturers that run on just-in-time deliveries are the most exposed, and car plants in Germany and Slovakia have already had to adjust schedules because parts were on ships that turned around. The war that began with a raid on a music festival in October has now acquired a maritime front, and the price of it is being paid in ports from Rotterdam to Shanghai, by companies that have nothing to do with any of the parties fighting it.
