title: "Debris from the Titan is found and five people are dead"
date: 2023-06-22 21:30:00 +0800

description: "Search vehicles found debris from the Titan on the seabed about five hundred metres from the bow of the Titanic on 22 June, and the operator said the submersible had suffered a catastrophic implosion that killed all five people aboard."
---

A remotely operated vehicle found debris from the Titan on the seabed on the morning of 22 June, about five hundred metres from the bow of the Titanic, and the operator, OceanGate, said in a statement that the submersible had suffered a catastrophic implosion. The pieces recovered included parts of the pressure hull, and the coast guard said they were consistent with a sudden failure under pressure rather than with a gradual loss of power or communications. All five people on board were killed, and the search became a recovery operation.

An implosion at that depth happens in milliseconds. The pressure at three thousand eight hundred metres is about four hundred times atmospheric, and a hull that fails at that depth collapses faster than the human nervous system can register. The acoustic sensors that the navy had deployed in the area, which were intended to detect the vessel's signals, recorded a sound consistent with an implosion shortly after communications were lost on 18 June, and that information was held while the search continued because the sound could not be confirmed as the Titan's.

The five were Stockton Rush, the company's founder and chief executive; Paul-Henri Nargeolet, a French diver and Titanic specialist; Hamish Harding, a British businessman and aviator; and Shahzada Dawood, a Pakistani businessman, and his son Suleman. Their families were notified before the public announcement, and the company's advisers said the families had been prepared for the possibility during the search. The recovery of the remains is expected to be partial and may take weeks, and the authorities have said they will investigate the accident.

Investigators will focus on the design and on the decisions that led to the dive. Titan had not been certified by an independent classification society, and its hull was made of carbon fibre whose behaviour under repeated deep dives had never been well characterized. The company had been warned in a 2018 letter by the Marine Technology Society, whose members questioned the lack of third-party testing, and former employees had raised concerns about the craft's viewport and its ballast system. The chief executive had argued publicly that the industry's certification requirements were unsuited to innovation.

A wider consequence is for the small industry that takes paying passengers to the wreck. Several operators said they would review their procedures, and insurers said they would look again at the cover they write for deep-water tourism. The Titanic lies in international waters, and the rules that govern visits to it are a patchwork of national and voluntary arrangements. The wreck has been visited by fewer than three hundred people since it was found in 1985, and it will be visited again, because the desire to see it has not diminished and the technology that takes people there has not been made safe by this week.

OceanGate has suspended all operations, and its chief executive is among the dead, which means the questions about certification will be answered by a board and by investigators rather than by the man who made the decisions. The Titanic now has one more wreck beside it.
