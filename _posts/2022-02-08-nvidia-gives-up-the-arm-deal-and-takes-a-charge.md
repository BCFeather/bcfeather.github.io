---
title: "NVIDIA abandons its bid for Arm and takes a charge of 1.36 billion dollars"
date: 2022-02-08 23:45:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "Regulators in three jurisdictions block a deal that would have put the designs inside most of the world's phones under one owner, and SoftBank prepares an offering instead."
---

NVIDIA and SoftBank Group announced on 8 February that they had terminated the agreement under which NVIDIA would have bought Arm, the British designer of the processor cores inside most of the world's phones. The parties said the transaction had run into significant regulatory challenges, and they ended it rather than fight the objections in court. The deal had been signed in September 2020, and its value had grown with NVIDIA's shares to about 66 billion dollars, which would have made it the largest acquisition ever attempted in the chip industry.

Regulators in three places had taken the same view of the combination. The Federal Trade Commission in Washington sued to stop it in December 2021, the British competition authority moved the case to an in-depth inquiry, and the European Commission opened its own investigation. The objection was structural: Arm licenses its designs to everybody, including the companies that compete with NVIDIA, and a single owner with an interest in its own chips could decide which customers received the next generation first.

Customers said so in public. Qualcomm, Microsoft, Google, Amazon and Samsung all depend on Arm cores, and several of them told the regulators that the neutrality of the licensor was worth more to them than any promise NVIDIA could give about the future. Arm's business is small in revenue, a few hundred million dollars a quarter in licence fees and royalties, and its influence is large because the instruction set it controls is the floor under mobile computing.

The financial terms of the failure were fixed in advance. SoftBank keeps the 1.25 billion dollars that NVIDIA paid at signing, and the American company said it would record a charge of 1.36 billion dollars in the first quarter of its next financial year. NVIDIA also keeps the twenty-year licence it had negotiated, and Jensen Huang said the two companies would continue to work together as partners.

SoftBank now intends to take Arm public, and it said the offering would be prepared within the fiscal year that ends in March 2023. A listing would return the company to the market it left in 2016, when SoftBank bought it for 32 billion dollars, and it would give the British government the outcome it said it wanted, which was a British company with an independent board rather than a division of an American one.

The industry that watched the deal fail drew a conclusion about scale. Growth in chips now comes from designing systems rather than from buying rivals, and the largest companies will spend the next few years building around the licences they already hold. NVIDIA's shares rose after the announcement, because investors had priced the deal as unlikely and because a company that does not have to defend a merger can spend the money on its own products. Arm, which had been promised a bigger budget and a wider reach inside the American company, now has to make the case for its valuation to the public investors it will meet in the autumn.
