---
title: "Boris Johnson resigns after fifty-nine ministers leave his government"
date: 2022-07-07 23:55:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The prime minister gives up the party leadership and stays in office until the autumn, while the contest to replace him begins."
---

Boris Johnson announced on Thursday lunchtime that he would resign as leader of the Conservative Party, and he said he would stay in Downing Street until a successor had been chosen. The statement came after two days in which fifty-nine members of his government resigned, beginning with the health secretary and the chancellor on Tuesday evening. He accepted that the arithmetic in the parliamentary party had turned against him, and he spent part of the speech listing the achievements that he said had been overlooked.

A scandal about a deputy whip was the trigger, and the man had been appointed in February despite having been accused of misconduct. The allegations became public at the end of June, and the explanations that the whips gave changed several times in a week. The resignations that followed came without coordination, according to the ministers who sent them, and the speed with which they arrived showed that the party had lost confidence in the man rather than in a single decision.

What is unusual is the timetable. A leader who resigns usually leaves office within days, and this one will remain until the party has elected a replacement in September. The cabinet that stays is made up of people who were loyal or who have nowhere else to go, and the opposition has said the country will have a caretaker government for two months in the middle of an energy crisis and a war. The government's answer is that the machinery of the state continues to run.

A contest to replace him has already begun and it is crowded. The bookmakers' favourite is the foreign secretary, and the former chancellor who was the first to resign is also standing. The rules of the parliamentary party require the candidates to be reduced to two by the members of parliament, and then the party membership, which is older and more right-wing than the country, will choose between them. The first ballots will be held in the week after the resignation.

His legacy is being written in the past tense already. He won the largest majority for his party in thirty years and he used it to leave the European Union, and he also presided over a government that broke the rules on parties during the lockdown. The inquiry into those gatherings is still sitting, and its report may be published before the new leader takes office. The members of parliament who removed him said they were protecting the party's reputation, and the voters will decide whether they were right.

What the resignation does is leave the country with a government that cannot make difficult decisions and an economy that needs them. The energy regulator will announce a new price cap in August, the inflation figure is heading for double digits and the Bank of England has begun to raise rates. The candidates to replace him have promised tax cuts and spending, and none of them has said how it will be paid for. The caretaker will keep the seat warm, and the winter will arrive before the new prime minister has settled in.
