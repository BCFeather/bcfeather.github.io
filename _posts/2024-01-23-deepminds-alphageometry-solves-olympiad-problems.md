title: "DeepMind's AlphaGeometry solves olympiad problems"
date: 2024-01-23 21:14:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "AlphaGeometry, published in *Nature* on 17 January, solved twenty-five of thirty olympiad geometry problems without human demonstrations, matching the average gold medallist by pairing a language model with a symbolic deduction engine."
---

A paper published in *Nature* on the seventeenth of January describes a program that solves olympiad geometry problems at roughly the level of a gold medallist, and the interesting thing about it is how little it resembles the systems that have been trying to do mathematics by learning from human proofs. AlphaGeometry, built by a team at Google DeepMind led by Trieu Trinh, was given thirty problems from recent International Mathematical Olympiads. It solved twenty-five of them within the standard time allowed. The best previous system solved ten. Average human gold medallists, working under competition conditions, solve somewhere between twenty and twenty-five of the same set.

The training data is the part that deserves attention. Geometry has always been the poor relation of automated reasoning, because the proofs in textbooks are written for people, with diagrams and intuitions that a machine cannot use, and the number of formally verified geometry proofs in existence is small. AlphaGeometry does not learn from those proofs. It learns from a synthetic corpus, built by taking random configurations of points and lines, running an exhaustive deduction engine over each one, and keeping the results. From that process come millions of theorems and proofs, at every level of difficulty, none of them written by a person. Once the corpus exists, a language model trained on it learns to suggest the construction that a human geometer would add to a diagram, the auxiliary point or the circle that makes the proof go through.

At test time the system alternates between two parts. The neural model proposes constructions, and a symbolic engine checks whether the resulting proof is valid by deduction. If the proposal is wrong, the engine reports the failure and the model tries again. That architecture is what allows the output to be checked. The proofs the system produces are not opaque scores or probability distributions. They are sequences of steps that a mathematician can read, and the paper reports that the system found a proof of every geometry problem from the 2000 and 2015 olympiads under expert review, and discovered a more general version of a theorem from 2004.

The limits are as clear as the result. Geometry is a narrow branch of mathematics, and the problems in the test set are those that can be stated and solved without analysis, algebra or combinatorics. The symbolic engine is the part that guarantees correctness, and it works because plane geometry has a complete decision procedure; most of mathematics does not. Moving from this to a system that can help with research mathematics would require the same trick to work in domains where the rules are not decidable, and nobody has shown how.

What the paper does establish is that the division of labour between learning and logic can be arranged differently from the way the field had assumed. Systems that try to prove theorems with language models alone tend to produce fluent nonsense, and systems that try to prove them by search alone tend to drown. Putting a model in charge of the intuition and a formal engine in charge of the checking is a division that humans have always used, and it turns out to be one that can be engineered. That is a small result in the history of mathematics and a large one in the history of machines that do it.
