---
title: "The Supreme Court ends race-conscious admissions"
date: 2023-06-29 20:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Supreme Court ruled on 29 June that the admissions programmes at Harvard and the University of North Carolina violated the equal protection clause, ending more than forty years in which universities could consider race as one factor among many."
---

In a ruling on 29 June, the Supreme Court held that the race-conscious admissions programmes at Harvard and the University of North Carolina violated the equal protection clause of the fourteenth amendment, and effectively ended the practice at universities across the country. The decision, written by the chief justice and joined by the court's five other conservative members, held that the programmes used race in ways that were insufficiently specific and that amounted to a stereotype. The three liberal justices dissented in two separate opinions, and the dissent by the first black woman to serve on the court, Ketanji Brown Jackson, was among the most pointed in recent memory.

The ruling does not forbid universities from considering race in every circumstance. It says that admissions officers may consider an applicant's discussion of how race affected their life, which is the opening that institutions have already said they will use, and it leaves open the possibility that the military academies could make a case for a national security exception. What it forbids is the systematic use of race as a factor in a scoring process, which is the way that most selective universities have pursued diversity since the Bakke decision of 1978.

Effects on admissions will be uneven and will be measured over several years. The most selective institutions, which have the largest pools of applicants and the most sophisticated recruitment, will have to rebuild their pipelines and will lose some of the black and Hispanic students they currently enroll. The states that had already banned affirmative action, including California and Michigan, offer evidence about what happens: at the most selective campuses, the share of black students fell sharply and did not recover at the same rate for years.

It is also a statement about how the court reads the constitution. The majority held that the equal protection clause is colour-blind in the context of university admissions, and it dismissed the universities' argument that diversity is a compelling interest that justifies the use of race. The dissenters argued that the clause was written to protect a people who had been enslaved and that reading it as colour-blind ignores the history that produced the inequality the programmes were trying to address. Both opinions claim the legacy of the amendment, which is what the argument has been for fifty years.

Consequences will be political as much as legal. Universities have said they will comply, and several have announced changes to their essay prompts and to their recruitment budgets. The decision energises a debate about legacy admissions, about the children of donors, and about the structures that produce advantage in a country where the Supreme Court has now said that race may not be one of them. The ruling ends a policy; it does not end the inequality that the policy was meant to address, and the country will argue about that for the next generation.

Universities have said they will comply and will look for other ways to build a diverse class, and the litigation over those alternatives has already begun. The ruling is the most consequential in the field since Bakke, and it will be cited in every admissions office in the country for the next generation.
