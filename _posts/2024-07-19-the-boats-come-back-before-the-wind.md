A typhoon that forms in the western Pacific gives a fishing port three days to bring its fleet home. The notice is issued by the marine authority when the storm passes a line on the chart. The line is drawn to allow for the slowest boat.

The harbour that receives the fleet is smaller than the fleet. A port built for two hundred vessels takes four hundred. The extra ones moor three abreast against the quay. A crew that cannot find a berth anchors in the bay and keeps a watch through the night.

The crews sleep on board. A fisherman who has been at sea for a fortnight does not go home for two days of wind, because the boat has to be ready when the storm passes. The families come down to the quay with food instead.

The market closes for the duration. A fish market that opens at four in the morning is shut when the storm is within a day. The catch that was landed the evening before is sold at a discount to whoever will take it.

The ice plant is the constraint on the second day. A boat that cannot ice its catch cannot go back out. The plant runs at full capacity for the week after the storm. The queue at the plant is longer than the queue at the fuel dock.

The typhoon also brings the season's best fishing. The water that the storm stirs up carries nutrients. The boats that go out on the third day come back with a fuller hold than they had all month. The danger and the profit arrive together.

The port repairs what it can before the next one. A quay that lost a section of railing is patched in the autumn. A breakwater that was damaged in the last storm is surveyed and scheduled. The schedule slips when the budget is short.

The forecast has improved and the losses have not fallen as fast. A storm that is tracked accurately still closes a port for three days. The cost of three days is counted in a season that has forty weeks in it.

The insurance is sold by the season. A boat that stays in port for a storm collects nothing and pays nothing. The premium is calculated on the days at sea. A skipper who goes out on the third day pays for the risk that he took.

The cooperative keeps a list of the boats that are at sea when the notice is issued. A boat that does not answer the radio is reported to the marine police, and the police send a cutter. The list is written by hand on a board in the office, and the board is photographed and sent to the county. A port that has lost a season to a single storm remembers the date, and the board in the office is kept from year to year.

The boats go back out when the signal is lowered. The crews who stayed aboard for two days wash the decks, check the nets, and leave on the evening tide.
