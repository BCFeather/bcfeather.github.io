---
title: "Iranian missiles hit American bases in Jordan and Bahrain, then Washington pauses"
date: 2026-07-21 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Revolutionary Guard said it destroyed a radar and an aircraft in Jordan and struck sites in Bahrain, and four days later the American president ordered a halt to further strikes on Iran."
---

Iran's Revolutionary Guard said on 21 July that it had fired missiles at an American base in Jordan and at American facilities in Bahrain, claiming to have destroyed a missile-defence radar and an F-15 parked in a shelter. American officials did not confirm the damage, and the Jordanian government said only that it had intercepted some of the projectiles. The attack was the first Iranian strike on American bases since the exchanges at the start of the month. The Guard's statement was carried by Iranian state media and included a list of the targets it said it had hit.

Sequence that led to it began on 1 July, when the two governments met indirectly in Doha and failed to convert the session into direct talks. American aircraft struck targets inside Iran in the following week, and the Iranian foreign ministry condemned the raids on 9 July as a breach of the United Nations charter and of the memorandum both sides had signed. Each round has been calibrated to stop short of a general war, and each round has raised the price of stopping. Both sides have kept their public accounts vague, which is how a limited exchange stays limited.

Regional hosting arrangements make the exchange dangerous. Jordan and Bahrain both host American forces under agreements that their governments defend as defensive, and an Iranian strike on those bases turns a host into a participant. Gulf states have been urging Washington to keep the retaliation inside a narrow band, and their air defences were active through the week.

Shipping stayed the immediate economic channel. Britain and France issued a joint statement on 3 July saying they would work with Oman to protect shipping in Omani waters, and both sent additional naval units to the region. Insurance rates for tankers crossing the Gulf rose again after the missile attack, and several operators rerouted cargoes around the Cape rather than through the strait. Tanker rates for the Gulf route had doubled since June, and several insurers stopped writing new policies.

Then the shooting stopped, at least towards Iran. President Trump ordered a pause in further strikes on Iran around 25 July, according to officials briefed on the decision, and American aircraft instead struck Houthi targets in Marib and al-Jawf provinces in Yemen. The pause was presented as a chance for diplomacy, and it was read in the region as a recognition that a fourth round of exchange would be harder to control than the first three. Officials in the Gulf said they had been told of the decision before it was announced.

A pause is a decision that lasts until someone tests it. The memorandum remains in force on paper, the carriers remain in the Gulf, and the Houthis remain able to close the Red Sea route that the world's container ships use. What changed in July is that both governments have now used force directly against each other and have both decided, for the moment, not to do it again. Implementation of the memorandum, meanwhile, has not advanced since the Doha round.
