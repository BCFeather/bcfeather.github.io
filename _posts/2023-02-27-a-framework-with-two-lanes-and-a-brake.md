---
title: "A framework with two lanes and a brake"
date: 2023-02-27 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Britain and the European Union agreed the Windsor Framework on 27 February 2023, replacing the Northern Ireland protocol with a system of green and red lanes and a mechanism for Belfast to object to new EU rules."
---

Rishi Sunak and Ursula von der Leyen announced the Windsor Framework in Windsor on 27 February, after four months of talks that had begun when the British government decided to renegotiate the protocol rather than to legislate around it. The agreement replaced the protocol's arrangements for goods moving from Britain to Northern Ireland with two lanes: a green lane for goods staying in Northern Ireland, subject to light checks, and a red lane for goods that might enter the European market. The text ran to more than seventy pages and was accompanied by a political declaration that both sides described as the part that would be tested first.

A brake was the second element. The framework gives the Northern Ireland assembly a mechanism to object to new European rules that would apply in the province, and if an objection is supported by a majority of both communities, the British government can suspend the rule. The mechanism is limited to goods and to a defined set of rules, and it is described as an emergency brake rather than as a veto.

Reducing the role of the European court was the third element. Disputes about goods would be settled in Northern Ireland's own courts, and the European court's jurisdiction would be confined to questions about the interpretation of European law. That concession was the one the union resisted longest, because it touched the principle that the single market has one court.

The politics in Belfast were the part that remained unresolved. The Democratic Unionist Party, which had refused to form a government for a year over the protocol, said the framework was an improvement and that it would take time to consider. The party's seven tests for any settlement included the removal of the European court's role and the absence of a customs border in the Irish Sea, and the framework met the first of those and softened rather than removed the second.

The economics of the agreement were smaller than the politics. Trade between Britain and Northern Ireland had been disrupted by the protocol's checks, and the framework was expected to reduce the cost of moving goods within the United Kingdom by a meaningful amount. Businesses welcomed the green lane and asked about the paperwork, and the paperwork was the part that every previous agreement had also promised to reduce. Businesses in Northern Ireland had been paying to move goods that never left the United Kingdom, and the green lane was designed to end that.

What the framework demonstrated was how much of the argument was about symbols. The protocol had been described by its opponents as a border in the Irish Sea and by its defenders as the only way to keep the single market intact, and the framework changed the machinery while keeping both descriptions usable. The deal was ratified in March, and the institutions in Belfast were restored in February 2024, on the strength of it. Its opponents in Belfast said the machinery had changed and the frontier had not, which was the argument that kept the institutions closed for another year.
