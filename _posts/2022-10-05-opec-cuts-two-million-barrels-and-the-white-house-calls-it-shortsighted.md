---
title: "OPEC cuts two million barrels and the White House calls it shortsighted"
date: 2022-10-05 22:40:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "The group agrees its largest reduction since 2020, five weeks before the American midterm elections."
---

Ministers met at the group's headquarters in Vienna and agreed to take two million barrels a day off the market from November. The cut is the largest since the emergency of 2020, and it arrives nine months after the group had been raising output. Brent crude rose above ninety-three dollars on the news, and the American benchmark followed. The meeting lasted less than an hour, which the group's critics took as evidence that the outcome had been settled in advance.

Delegates described the decision as a response to a weakening economy and to uncertainty about demand. The real reduction will be smaller than the headline, because several members had been pumping below their quotas and the new targets are set against those quotas. Analysts put the actual loss at about one million barrels a day. The group's own documents put its idle capacity at about four million barrels a day, most of it held by three producers. That capacity is the argument the group makes when it is accused of pushing prices up.

The White House called the decision shortsighted and said the president had been told it reflected the interest of a cartel rather than the state of the market. Officials had spent the summer asking for more oil, and the requests had gone to Riyadh in particular. The timing, five weeks before the midterm elections and with petrol prices already falling, made the decision a political event in Washington. The advisers had also warned that another release from the strategic reserve would leave the stockpile at its lowest level since the 1980s. The administration said it would review its relationship with the kingdom and would keep the option open.

Riyadh and Washington had been close for seventy years on the understanding that oil would flow and security would follow. That understanding has been tested before, in 1973 and in 2020. This year's version is harder because the war in Ukraine has made energy a weapon and because the kingdom's budget needs a price above eighty dollars. The crown prince has also been pursuing a foreign policy of his own, and the American president had said in July that he would pay a price for it.

Its next meeting was set for December, and the statement left the door open to further reductions. Producers outside the group, from the American shale fields to Guyana, have been adding capacity, and the International Energy Agency has cut its forecast for demand growth twice this year. The agency now expects demand to grow more slowly next year than this, and its director said the group was risking a price spike that would destroy the demand it says it wants to protect.

For consumers the arithmetic is simple. A cut of one million barrels a day is about one per cent of world supply, and the price effect depends on how much spare capacity sits behind it. Every dollar on a barrel reaches the pump within about six weeks, which puts the timing of the cut squarely in the campaign season. The group says it holds the spare capacity and will use it if the price runs too high. The market spent the day deciding whether to believe the promise or the precedent.
