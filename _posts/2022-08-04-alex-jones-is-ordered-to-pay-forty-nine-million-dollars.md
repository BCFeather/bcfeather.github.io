---
title: "A jury orders Alex Jones to pay forty-nine million dollars to the parents of a murdered child"
date: 2022-08-04 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "A Texas jury adds punitive damages to a compensatory award, and the conspiracy broadcaster says the verdict will bankrupt him."
---

A jury in Austin ordered Alex Jones on Thursday to pay about forty-nine million dollars to the parents of a child who was killed at Sandy Hook, and the total was made up of four million in compensation and forty-five million in punitive damages. The parents, Scarlett Lewis and Neil Heslin, had testified that they were harassed for years by people who believed the broadcaster's claim that the shooting was staged. The jury deliberated for about a day before it returned the first figure and for a day more before it returned the second.

The compensatory award was smaller than the plaintiffs had asked for. Their lawyers had described the abuse that followed the broadcasts, including messages that said the child had never existed and a man who filmed the family's home. The jury heard that Jones had said on air that the parents were actors and that the government had staged the deaths of twenty children. He later conceded that the shooting had happened, and he did not repeat the concession in court.

The punitive figure is the one that matters for the company. Texas law allows punitive damages to be awarded when the conduct is found to be malicious, and the jury was told that the broadcasts had continued after the parents asked for them to stop. Jones's lawyer argued that the company was a small business with a large audience and that a large award would end it, and the jury rejected that argument. The broadcaster said outside the court that he could not pay and that the verdict was a joke.

At the same time, the case is one of several that the families have brought. A jury in Connecticut has heard a similar claim, and the company that owns the site has filed for protection from its creditors in another court. The amounts in the Texas case are the first that a jury has fixed, and the plaintiffs' lawyers have said they will pursue the assets rather than the statements. The audience that made the broadcasts profitable is the audience that the company now says it cannot afford to compensate.

Meanwhile, the wider question is about the platforms that carried the claims. The families' suits named the social networks as defendants in some of the filings, and the companies have argued that they carry what their users post rather than publish it. The courts have begun to disagree in some cases, and the argument has been taken up in the legislatures. The verdict in Austin does not settle that question and it makes it more urgent.

What the case shows is the cost of a business built on a lie. The broadcaster sold supplements and survival gear to an audience he told was under attack, and the audience paid for the products and for the subscriptions. The jury has now put a price on the harm, and the price is small compared with the revenue and large compared with the company's accounts. The next jury will decide whether the total is enough, and the appeals will decide whether it survives.
