---
title: "The ruling that Google had been waiting for"
date: 2024-08-06 21:00:00 +0800
tags: [technology, antitrust, google, law]
categories: [technology]
description: "A federal judge found that Google had illegally maintained a monopoly in search. The remedy is still to be decided, and the case will outlast the judge who wrote the opinion."
---

A federal judge in Washington found that Google had illegally maintained a monopoly in general search. The opinion runs to two hundred and eighty-six pages and it was released on a Monday afternoon.

The finding rests on the deals that the company signed with phone makers and browser developers. Those deals made Google the default search engine on devices that ship by the hundred million. The judge treated the default as the moat rather than the product.

The defence argued that a user can switch in four taps. The court accepted that a user can switch and found that almost none do, which is the difference between a choice that exists and a choice that is exercised.

The payments behind those deals were the evidence that mattered. Google paid billions of dollars a year for a default placement. The judge reasoned that a company does not pay for something that a user would choose anyway.

The remedy will be decided in a separate hearing. The options run from a ban on the payments to a requirement that a user be offered a choice screen at setup. A structural break-up was discussed in the opinion and was not ordered.

The appeal will take years. The case was filed in 2020 and the trial ran for ten weeks in 2023. The appellate court will read the same record with a different standard.

The other case is the one to watch. A second suit, filed over advertising technology, is scheduled for trial later. It asks for a divestiture rather than a behavioural remedy.

The ruling arrives in a year when the search business is being reshaped by a chat interface. A competitor that answers a question in a paragraph does not need a page of links, and the moat that the judge described protects a product that is changing under it.

The payment to Apple was the single largest item in the record. Analysts put it at twenty billion dollars a year, which is a quarter of the revenue that the search business earns from the devices that Apple sells.

The judge found that Google had also raised the price of advertising without losing share, which is the classic test of market power. The evidence was a set of internal documents in which the company modelled a price increase and found that the customers stayed.

Microsoft argued that its own search engine was a better product and that the default was the only reason it failed. The court accepted the first half of the argument and rejected the inference, because a product that cannot be reached is not a product that has been tried.

The opinion is written for the appeal. Judge Mehta set out the market definition first, then the evidence of exclusion, then the harm, and each section answers a question that the appellate court will ask in the same order.

The company said it would appeal and it said the decision would be reversed. A spokesperson used the word disappointed four times in a statement of five sentences, which is the tone that a large company adopts when it has lost and intends to fight.

The precedent is older than the internet. A monopoly that is maintained by contracts rather than by a better product is the pattern that the antitrust statutes were written for, and the judge quoted a case from 1911 in the first paragraph.
