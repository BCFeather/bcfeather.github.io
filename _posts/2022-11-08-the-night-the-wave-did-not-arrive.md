---
title: "The night the wave did not arrive"
date: 2022-11-08 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Americans vote in the midterms, and a Republican sweep predicted for months fails to appear in the early counts."
---

Polls closed on the East Coast at seven, and the first returns came in from Kentucky and Indiana within the half hour. By nine the picture was already strange, because seats that Republican strategists had expected to take comfortably were close, and in a few places the Democratic incumbent was comfortably ahead. The television studios had prepared graphics for a wave, and those graphics sat unused while the anchors read numbers that refused to behave as the models had promised. Producers began to telephone their analysts to ask whether the samples had been wrong, and the analysts said that the samples had probably been wrong for months.

The Senate was the place to watch. Pennsylvania, Georgia, Nevada and Arizona held the majority in their hands, and the margins in all four were thin enough to be counted in thousands of votes. In Pennsylvania the Democratic candidate, recovering from a stroke, won a seat his party had been afraid of losing since the spring. In Georgia neither candidate reached fifty per cent, and the contest went to a runoff in December that would decide the chamber on its own. In Nevada the count took days, and the result was decided by postal ballots from a single county that nobody had thought to watch.

The House was slower to resolve, because several states count mail ballots over days and some of them accept ballots that arrive after election day. The Republican advantage in the national vote was real, and the party did pick up seats, though not the dozen or more that its leadership had promised in the summer. New York and California, where courts had redrawn the maps after the census, produced results that nobody had forecast with any confidence. By the following week the majority had been called for the Republicans, and it was small enough that a handful of members could stop almost anything the leadership wanted.

Two things appeared to be doing the work. The end of the constitutional right to abortion in June had put the question on ballots in several states, and where it did, turnout among younger voters rose sharply. Michigan, California and Vermont passed measures protecting reproductive rights, and Kentucky rejected an amendment that would have declared no such right existed. In Kansas, where the question had been put to a vote in August, the result had already shown how much the issue could move people who usually stayed at home.

Candidates who had built their campaigns around the claim that the 2020 election had been stolen did less well than the polls suggested. Several lost close races, and the officials who certify results in their states were on the ballot as well. In Michigan and in Arizona the people who had refused to certify in 2020 were replaced by people who said they would sign whatever the count produced. The claim had become less useful as a campaign message than it had been two years earlier, and the candidates who used it most loudly lost by the largest margins.

By the small hours the count was unfinished and the shape of the result was clear enough. A wave had been promised for a year, and no wave arrived. What came instead was a narrow country, a Senate balanced on a single seat in Georgia, and a House whose majority would be decided by a few thousand ballots in districts nobody had thought about.
