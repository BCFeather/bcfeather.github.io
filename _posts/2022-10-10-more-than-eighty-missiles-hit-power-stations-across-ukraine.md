---
title: "More than eighty missiles hit power stations across Ukraine in the morning rush"
date: 2022-10-10 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Kyiv, Lviv, Dnipro and Zaporizhzhia are struck at the start of the working day, and the grid operator announces cuts."
---

Air raid sirens sounded across Ukraine at about eight in the morning, and missiles arrived in wave after wave for more than two hours. Kyiv, Lviv, Dnipro, Zaporizhzhia, Kharkiv and several smaller cities were hit, and the targets were power stations, substations and a railway junction. The interior ministry put the dead at nineteen and the injured at more than a hundred by the afternoon. The strikes came at the hour when the roads are full and the schools are opening.

The commander of the armed forces said more than eighty missiles had been launched and that slightly over half had been brought down. Rescue workers dug through the rubble of a power plant in Kyiv and of a residential block beside it, and the city's metro stopped running because the power had gone. Officials in Lviv said the city had lost hot water and part of its electricity, and the regional governor said the system would need days to recover. The national grid operator announced scheduled outages in every region and stopped exporting electricity to Europe.

President Vladimir Putin said at a meeting in Moscow that the strikes were a response to the explosion on the bridge across the Kerch Strait, which he blamed on Ukrainian services. He said the army would continue to hit infrastructure that supports the armed forces, and he promised that the campaign would be felt. The Russian defence ministry published a map with lines drawn to the targets, and the lines included cities far from the front.

Officials in Kyiv described the timing as deliberate, since the missiles arrived as people were driving to work and children were walking to school. The foreign minister said the strikes had no military logic and that they would strengthen the case for more air defences. The prosecutor's office opened a case on the basis that the attacks targeted the civilian grid rather than an army. Germany's chancellor promised additional systems within weeks, and the leaders of the group of seven met by video to discuss it.

The grid had already been damaged by a month of smaller strikes, and engineers had been repairing substations faster than they could be counted. Winter comes early in this part of Europe, and heating depends on the same network that the missiles were aimed at. Municipal authorities in Kyiv began distributing firewood and generators, and hospitals ran on back-up power while the repairs went on. The energy minister said the country would need imports as well as repairs before the cold, and he put the cost of restoring the grid at several billion euros.

For the cities the immediate effect was a queue at every petrol station and a queue at every shop that sold power banks. Nobody could say how long the pattern would continue, and the sirens had become so routine that some people stopped going to the shelter. What the morning showed is that a campaign against the grid is a campaign against the ordinary week, and that the ordinary week is what a war has to hold together.
