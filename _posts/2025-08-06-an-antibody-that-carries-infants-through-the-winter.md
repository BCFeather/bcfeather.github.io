title: "An antibody that carries infants through the winter"
date: 2025-08-06 19:45:00 +0800
tags: [medicine, infants, viruses, health]

description: "Respiratory syncytial virus fills paediatric wards every winter, and the newest protection for infants is a single injection of antibodies rather than a vaccine. The clinical case is strong, and the obstacle in most of the world is cost and timing."
---

Respiratory syncytial virus is the most common reason a small infant is admitted to hospital in winter, and it has no vaccine for the child who most needs one. A vaccine given in infancy would have to work in the presence of maternal antibodies that neutralise it, and the immune system of a two-month-old responds weakly to most vaccines. The solution that has emerged in the last few years is not a vaccine. It is an injection of ready-made antibodies, one dose, given at birth or before the winter season starts, which protects the infant through the months when the virus circulates.

The product most widely used is a monoclonal antibody engineered to have a long half-life, so that a single dose covers the whole season rather than requiring a monthly interval. The trials reduced hospitalisation for lower respiratory infection by the majority of cases in the treated group, and the safety profile was unremarkable, which is the result everyone wanted and few expected.

The economics are the difficulty. A dose costs more than the entire childhood immunisation schedule in most middle-income countries, and the health systems that would benefit most are the ones with the least to spend. The manufacturer has offered a tiered price in some markets and has not in others, and the negotiation is conducted country by country, quietly, by procurement officials who are used to arguing about vaccines that cost a dollar.

Timing is the second problem. The virus arrives in temperate climates in November and leaves in March, and an injection given in September must hold its protection through the whole period. In tropical climates the season is less defined and the optimal month is not obvious. A programme that gives the dose at birth is simpler to run and produces a weaker match with the local season.

There is a third option that is being used more widely than the antibody. A vaccine given to the mother in late pregnancy transfers antibodies to the infant through the placenta, protecting the child for the first months of life. The two approaches overlap rather than compete. The maternal vaccine is cheaper and depends on the mother receiving it in the right weeks. The infant antibody is more expensive and works regardless of the mother's vaccination status.

What all of this produces is a familiar shape in global health. A product exists, works well, and reaches the children whose families can pay for it. The mechanism to extend it to the rest is a purchasing agreement, a price tier and a donation programme, and each of those is a negotiation rather than a fact.

The virus itself is not going away. It has been circulating for as long as anyone has measured it, it reinfects adults every few years, and the burden falls almost entirely on the very young. The intervention available in 2025 is good enough that a paediatric ward in a rich country has begun to notice the absence of it in January. That is the kind of result that takes a decade to appear in the statistics.
