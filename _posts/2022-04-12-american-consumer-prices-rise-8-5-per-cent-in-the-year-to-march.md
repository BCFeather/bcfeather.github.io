---
title: "American consumer prices rise 8.5 per cent in the year to March"
date: 2022-04-12 22:30:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "The fastest annual rate in four decades came from petrol, groceries and rent at once, and it landed four weeks before the Federal Reserve's next meeting, which had already been expected to raise rates aggressively."
---

The Labour Department reported on 12 April that its consumer price index had risen 8.5 per cent over the twelve months to March, the fastest annual pace since 1981. The monthly increase was 1.2 per cent, driven by a jump in energy costs after the invasion of Ukraine, and the index for all items less food and energy rose 6.5 per cent, the highest in forty years. The monthly increase was the largest since 2005, and the energy component rose more than ten per cent in a single month.

Composition matters more than the headline. Petrol prices climbed to a national average above four dollars a gallon, groceries rose at the fastest rate since 1981, and shelter costs, which lag in the index, began to reflect the previous year's surge in rents and house prices. Used vehicles, which had driven much of the inflation of 2021, stopped leading the index. Food eaten at home rose more than ten per cent over the year, and the indexes for cereals, dairy and meat each set records of their own, with eggs and flour among the sharpest movers.

Three forces run through the number and they respond to different treatment. Pandemic-era demand met constrained supply as spending shifted from services to goods, and that mismatch is fading slowly. Energy and food prices rose because of the war and because of sanctions on Russian exports. Fiscal support and monetary policy kept household balance sheets strong enough to absorb the price rises without pulling demand down.

Politics around the figure are simpler than the economics. Republican candidates had planned to campaign on it before it was printed, and the White House, which had spent months calling the increase transitory, now described it as the product of Putin's war and pointed to the million barrels a day being released from the strategic reserve. Both descriptions contain something true, and neither survives contact with the details: prices had been rising for a year before the invasion, and energy accounts for only part of the index.

Federal Reserve officials had already abandoned gradualism. It raised rates by a quarter point in March and signalled a faster path, and this report made a half-point increase at the May meeting close to certain. Bond yields rose, mortgage rates crossed five per cent for the first time since 2018, and the dollar touched a two-decade high against a basket of currencies on the expectation that American rates would outrun European ones.

Whether that tightening can bring inflation down without a recession is the unresolved question of the year. The labour market was still adding hundreds of thousands of jobs a month, wages were rising, and the very strength of the jobs data made the inflation problem harder to solve, which is an awkward position for a central bank that has two mandates and only one instrument. Core prices rose faster than average hourly wages for a sixth month running, and that gap is the arithmetic that decides how the year ends, for households and for the committee alike.
