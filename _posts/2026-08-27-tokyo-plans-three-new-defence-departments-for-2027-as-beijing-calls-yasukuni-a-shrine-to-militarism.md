The Chinese defence ministry's routine briefing on 27 August devoted most of its Japan section to two subjects. The first was Yasukuni, which the spokesman described as a shrine that glorifies a war of aggression, and the second was a set of Japanese plans reported for 2027: three new departments, long-range missiles for unmanned underwater vehicles, and a hypersonic test agreement with Australia. The briefing was the last routine one before the autumn session, and spokesmen tend to use those occasions to set out positions that will be quoted for months. Japanese legislators visited the shrine in mid-August, as they do every year, and the Chinese statement named the practice rather than the building.

The combination matters. A statement about a shrine is a statement about history, and a statement about three new departments is a statement about budgets. Beijing put the two in the same paragraph, which is exactly how Japanese domestic critics have long described the problem: a security policy that grows while the reckoning with the past stays unfinished. The shrine honours convicted war criminals alongside the war dead, a fact that Japanese governments have acknowledged in statement after statement since 1985. A country can argue that its security policy has no connection to its history, and its neighbours will judge the argument by the ceremonies it holds in August.

The reported plans themselves would extend the Self-Defence Forces into areas that the defence white paper of 4 August gestured at. Unmanned underwater vehicles with long-range missiles would give Japan a submerged strike capability it has never had, and a hypersonic testing arrangement with Australia would tie Tokyo to a weapons programme that the region will notice. Submerged strike capabilities change the arithmetic of a blockade, because a boat that can sit under the water for weeks is harder to find than a missile battery. An Australian testing range offers space that Japan lacks, and Japanese ranges offer Australia depth that it lacks, which is why the arrangement has been discussed for years.

Australia has become the closest partner in that effort. The two governments have been working on long-range strike and hypersonic technology for several years, and an agreement on testing would allow Japanese ranges to be used for Australian programmes and the reverse. Technical cooperation of that kind outlasts the governments that sign it. The Australian and Japanese governments have also agreed on joint work on undersea surveillance, a domain where both have invested since 2023. Technical agreements of this kind are signed by ministers and implemented by engineers, and the engineers keep working after the ministers change.

The spokesman referred to the Cairo and Potsdam declarations and to the commitments Japan made on the anniversary of the surrender, and he said the country should stay on the path of peaceful development. The standard formulation also serves a domestic purpose, because it frames Japan's spending as a choice about the past rather than a response to the present.
