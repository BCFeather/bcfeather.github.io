---
title: "The House finally elects a speaker"
date: 2023-10-25 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Mike Johnson of Louisiana was elected Speaker on 25 October 2023 by 220 votes to 209, ending twenty-two days without a speaker and installing a conservative who had voted against certifying the 2020 election."
---

Republicans elected Mike Johnson of Louisiana as Speaker on 25 October, with 220 votes to 209 and no defections, ending a vacancy that had lasted twenty-two days and paralyzed the House. Johnson was the fourth nominee the conference tried, after Steve Scalise, Jim Jordan and Tom Emmer each failed to reach a majority on the floor, and he won on the first ballot because the factions that had blocked the others could agree on him.

His record is short and unambiguous. He entered Congress in 2017 after a career as a constitutional lawyer, he led the amicus brief signed by more than a hundred Republicans in a Texas case that sought to overturn the 2020 election, and he voted against certifying the results in Pennsylvania and Arizona. He has opposed abortion rights without exceptions, sponsored a national abortion ban, and voted against the spending deals and the aid packages that the previous Speaker had negotiated. He had never held elected office before the seat he won in 2016. He has also worked as a lawyer for a conservative advocacy group and as a talk-radio commentator.

The timetable is crowded. Government funding runs out on 17 November, and the House has passed none of its twelve appropriations bills. The Senate has moved several with bipartisan support, and the two chambers are far apart on topline spending, on the border and on the supplemental request for Israel and Ukraine that the White House sent in October. Johnson has said he will move the Israel money separately and has been vague about Ukraine. The Senate's version includes the Ukraine money, and the House has no plan to move it before the deadline.

The removal clause that ended McCarthy's speakership remains in place. Johnson inherits the same rule, and the members who used it have said they will use it again if he relies on Democratic votes. The difference is that the eight who voted against McCarthy are now dispersed across a conference that wants to move past the fight, and the new Speaker has the advantage of having been everyone's second choice.

Governing with a majority of four was always going to require the opposition. The previous Speaker lost his job for doing exactly that, and his successor has promised not to. The contradiction will surface at the first must-pass bill, and the deadline is three weeks away. Members who have never served under a Speaker with a majority this narrow are finding the arithmetic unforgiving.

Johnson presented himself as a steady hand and as a constitutional conservative, and the elevation of a little-known backbencher to second in line to the presidency happened because the better-known candidates had made too many enemies. The House has a Speaker again, and the price of the deal is a leader whose positions on the last election and on abortion are now attached to the institution. He is the least experienced Speaker in more than a century, and he takes office with the rule that removed his predecessor still in force.
