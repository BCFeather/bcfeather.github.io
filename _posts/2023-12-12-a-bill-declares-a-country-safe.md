---
title: "A bill declares a country safe"
date: 2023-12-12 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Rishi Sunak won a Commons vote on 12 December 2023 for a bill that declares Rwanda safe for asylum removals, by 313 votes to 269, after a day in which his own party threatened to defeat the central claim of his immigration policy."
---

Rishi Sunak won the vote on the Safety of Rwanda bill on the evening of 12 December, 313 to 269, after a day of meetings in which two factions of his party demanded changes that pulled in opposite directions. The bill declares Rwanda a safe country for the purposes of British asylum law, and instructs courts to treat it as safe, which is the device the government found after the Supreme Court ruled the policy unlawful on 15 November. The majority of 44 was produced partly by members who voted for the bill while saying they would vote against it at the next stage.

Judges had given three reasons for the November judgment, and the bill answers each of them in the text rather than in the facts. The court found that Rwanda could return asylum seekers to the countries they had fled, that its asylum system lacked the capacity to process the numbers involved, and that the evidence of its practice fell short of what the court required. The bill overrides the first finding by declaring that Rwanda will not remove anybody, establishes a monitoring committee to report on the second, and tells courts to disregard the third unless new evidence arrives.

The politics around the vote were nearly fatal. The right of the party wanted the bill to disapply the Human Rights Act and the convention outright, and about thirty members said they would vote against anything weaker; the centre wanted a bill that could survive in the courts. A compromise was negotiated in the morning, the government won, and both wings claimed that they had been promised the next stage. The Lords will send amendments back within weeks, and the bill may return to the Commons three or four times before Easter.

Nothing has flown yet, and the timetable is the part that matters to the people in the hotels. The first removal flight was blocked in June 2022 by the European Court of Human Rights, then by the British courts, and the government has now spent roughly 240 million pounds on the scheme, including payments to Rwanda that the National Audit Office described as not fully accounted for. The Home Office has not said how many people it expects to remove in 2024, and its own impact assessment puts the capacity of the Rwandan system at a few hundred a year against tens of thousands of arrivals.

The scheme's logic has always been deterrence rather than removal, and the evidence for that has never been produced. Small boats crossing the Channel fell by about a third in 2023, which the government attributes to the policy and its critics attribute to weather, to French policing and to the cost of the crossings. Every flight cancelled by a court produces a story about lawyers rather than about the people who did not board, which is the political product the policy has reliably delivered.

For the government, the vote bought time rather than a solution. The election must be held by January 2025, the Rwanda plan is the only remaining promise from the 2019 manifesto that has not been abandoned, and the party's poll deficit has widened through the autumn. Ministers now describe the bill as the strongest possible version, which is what they said about the last version, and the courts will have another year in which to disagree with them.
