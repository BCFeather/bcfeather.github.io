---
title: "Israel passes a law to curb the Supreme Court"
date: 2023-07-25 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Knesset passed a law on 24 July stripping the Supreme Court of power to rule government decisions unreasonable, by sixty-four votes to nil after the opposition left the chamber, the first stage of a judicial overhaul that has divided the country."
---

Members of the Knesset approved the first part of the government's judicial overhaul on 24 July, by sixty-four votes to zero. The opposition had walked out before the vote, and the empty benches were the point: the coalition of Benjamin Netanyahu passed the law with its own majority and without a single opposing member present. Police cleared protesters from outside the building with water cannon, and several were carried away in stretchers. The law was written to take effect at once, and ministers said they would use the new powers within weeks.

What passed is a clause that removes the courts' power to judge a government decision unreasonable, a doctrine that the attorney general and the Supreme Court have used to block appointments and policies in cases where the law was technically satisfied. Critics describe it as a small technical change that in practice removes the last check on ministers who appoint whoever they wish to ministries they control. Supporters describe it as restoring the primacy of elected officials over unelected judges.

The fight has been going on since January, and it has reached parts of Israeli society that used to stay out of politics. Reservists in elite units, including pilots and cyber specialists, said they would stop volunteering, and the army's chief of staff warned about readiness. High-tech firms moved money abroad, the shekel fell, and the country's credit outlook was cut. Foreign investors sold Israeli bonds, and the central bank drew on its reserves to steady the currency. Doctors joined protests, and so did lawyers, teachers, and the historians who wrote the country's founding documents.

Netanyahu's coalition had paused in March to allow for talks, then resumed in the summer as the talks failed. His partners in the Religious Zionist and Jewish Power parties have their own priorities, including changes to the way rabbinical courts and the police are run, and they have made the overhaul the price of their support. The prime minister himself is on trial for corruption, which is the reason his critics give for his interest in weakening the courts and the reason his supporters give for the prosecution they describe as political.

In September the Supreme Court hears petitions against the law, with a full panel of judges and a decision that will test whether a court can strike down a law that limits its own power. Meanwhile the fight over the composition of the committee that appoints judges remains unresolved, and the government has said it will return to it in the autumn session.

A law passed by sixty-four votes in a chamber of a hundred and twenty has changed the balance between two branches of the state, and the argument about it has moved from the Knesset to the courthouse. What the court does next will decide whether the summer's protests were the crisis or its rehearsal, and the answer may arrive with the reservists before it arrives with the judges, whose ruling will be appealed by whichever side loses it.
