---

The scores are published in the last week of June, and then the country enters a five-day negotiation. A household sits around a table with a printed list of universities, a set of historical admission scores, a phone running three apps that predict the probability of admission, and a set of opinions from relatives who have not applied to anything since 1998. The deadline is fixed and cannot be missed, and the decision allocates four years of a young person's life.

The information is genuinely public. Every province publishes the scores of every admitted candidate from previous years, which makes the historical thresholds knowable with more precision than almost any other market in the country. The complication is that the threshold moves with everyone else's decisions. If a thousand families decide that the same university looks like a safe bet this year, its threshold rises, and the bets fail together.

The prediction apps exist to manage that. They model the last three years of data, add a coefficient for how popular a school has become, and produce a probability. The numbers look scientific and are, in the narrow sense, accurate. They cannot know how many other families will read the same number, which is the only variable that matters. Economists call this a coordination problem. Parents call it luck.

The choice itself is usually presented as a choice between a school and a city, and it is actually a choice between three or four other things. Whether the subject is one the student can survive four years of. Whether the diploma opens a door in the province where the family has connections. Whether the accommodation is tolerable. Whether the tuition is affordable, which matters less than it used to, and whether the campus is close enough for a visit in the spring.

The negotiation has its own gender of authority. The student usually holds the subject preference. The parents usually hold the ranking preference. The relatives contribute a piece of local knowledge, often outdated. In many households the resolution is a compromise that satisfies nobody, and in a smaller number the student wins outright because the alternative is a child who resents every term of it.

The provincial systems differ, and that difference matters more than most families realise when they begin. Some provinces let a student list dozens of choices in rank order and assign them by algorithm, which rewards careful ranking and punishes sentiment. Others require a shorter list, which rewards judgement and punishes indecision. A family that moves between provinces in the same week, as some do when a child studies away from home, is working with two different games at once. Five days is a short time for a decision with a four-year consequence, and the shortness is deliberate. A longer window would allow more research and also more argument, and the system has been designed so that the decision is made quickly and then closed. By the first week of July the forms are submitted, and the household goes back to its ordinary business, and the outcome arrives in August in a message that cannot be negotiated.
