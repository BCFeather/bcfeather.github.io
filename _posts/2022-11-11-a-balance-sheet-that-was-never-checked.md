---
title: "A balance sheet that was never checked"
date: 2022-11-11 22:00:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "FTX collapses into bankruptcy in Delaware, its founder resigns, and the largest trust in the crypto industry turns out to have rested on very little."
---

On the morning of 11 November the company posted a short statement on its own account. FTX Trading, its American subsidiary, the trading house Alameda Research and roughly one hundred and thirty other entities had begun voluntary proceedings under Chapter 11 of the bankruptcy code in Delaware. The founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, had resigned, and a man named John J. Ray III had been appointed to run what was left. The statement said that the group would review and monetize its assets for the benefit of stakeholders, which is the language of a liquidation rather than of a rescue.

The week before had been noisy in a way that financial collapses usually avoid. A rival exchange had published a report questioning the value of the tokens that backed the balance sheet of Alameda, customers began to withdraw, and the withdrawals exceeded the reserves. A rescue by a larger competitor was announced on a Tuesday and abandoned by Wednesday afternoon. That competitor walked away after looking at the accounts, and its chief executive said afterwards that the numbers had been worse than anything he had expected.

The bankruptcy petition put the case into the hands of a man who had spent his career winding up failed companies, including Enron. Ray had experience of institutions whose books did not show what their officers said they showed, and he was appointed on the same day that customers were told their deposits were now part of an estate. His first filing described a company without reliable records, and he asked the court for permission to reorganize the group before the assets lost the value they still had.

Size alone made the case extraordinary. FTX presented itself as an exchange where customers kept their own assets, and in practice the money had been lent into the group's own trading operations. Those loans had been recorded with a care that a bankruptcy examiner would have to reconstruct from emails, spreadsheets and the memories of people who had already left. Some of the lending had gone to affiliated companies that existed mainly to hold the loans, and their accounts were kept in the same loose way.

Contagion ran through the market. Other platforms halted withdrawals, several lenders that had lent to the group wrote down their positions, and a token presented as a stablecoin lost its peg for a while. Bitcoin fell below sixteen thousand dollars, a level it had not seen since 2020, and the mood among retail holders turned from disappointment to anger within a week. Prices of smaller tokens moved by double figures in a single day, and some exchanges stopped quoting them altogether.

For regulators the case was a gift and a warning at once. Offshore exchanges had been offering services that resembled banking while claiming the rules of banking did not apply to them. The failure made it very hard to keep arguing that those rules were unnecessary, and several authorities opened investigations within days of the filing. The question that remained was how much of the rest of the industry had been built on the same arithmetic.
