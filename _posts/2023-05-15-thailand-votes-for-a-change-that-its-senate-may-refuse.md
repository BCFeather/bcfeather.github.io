---
title: "Thailand votes for a change that its senate may refuse"
date: 2023-05-15 23:20:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Thailand went to the polls on 14 May and delivered a decisive verdict against the military-backed government. Move Forward and Pheu Thai between them took most of the seats, and the appointed senate still stands between them and a prime minister."
---

Thailand held a general election on 14 May and produced the strongest result for the opposition in a decade. Move Forward, a party founded four years ago by a group of activists who had led the street protests of 2020, won the largest share of the constituency seats and, with Pheu Thai, the party of the Shinawatra family, controls well over half of the elected lower house. The parties aligned with the prime minister who took power in a coup in 2014 lost heavily.

The arithmetic has a catch. Under the constitution that the army wrote after that coup, the prime minister is chosen by a joint sitting of the elected house and a senate of two hundred and fifty members, all of them appointed by the junta and none of them elected. A party needs three hundred and seventy-six votes in that joint sitting. Move Forward and Pheu Thai can assemble perhaps three hundred and twelve among the elected members, and the senate has shown no inclination to help them.

Campaigning turned on two issues that the constitution was designed to keep off the agenda. The first is the monarchy, and the law that makes criticism of it a criminal offence with penalties of up to fifteen years. Move Forward campaigned on amending that law, which no previous party had done, and its leader, Pita Limjaroenrat, made the question of the army's role in politics a central theme. The second is the economy, which has recovered from the pandemic more slowly than its neighbours, and where household debt has climbed to about ninety per cent of output.

Young voters explain the result. Move Forward drew its support from voters under thirty-five and from Bangkok and the southern cities, and its use of social media reached an electorate that the older parties had treated as an appendage of their own machines. Turnout reached a record of more than seventy-five per cent, and the party's surprise sweep of constituencies in the capital was the single most striking number of the night. Analysts who had expected a Pheu Thai landslide were left describing a generational shift.

What happens next is a negotiation with a body that has no electoral mandate. Pita has said he will seek the votes of the senate and has asked the other parties to sign a memorandum on a common programme, which includes a commitment to revisit the lese-majeste law. The senate's members have said publicly that they will not support a prime minister who proposes to amend it. A deadlock would push the country towards a non-elected prime minister drawn from the senate's own ranks, which is the outcome the constitution was written to permit.

The economy and the courts add their own constraints. The election commission has been asked to rule on whether Pita, who holds shares in a media company that was at the centre of an earlier court decision, is disqualified, and a ruling against him would remove the only candidate with the numbers. Thailand has seen twelve coups and more constitutions than elections, and the country's institutions have a long record of reversing verdicts they did not like. The vote on 14 May was clear; whether it governs depends on institutions that did not appear on the ballot.
