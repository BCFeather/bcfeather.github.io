---
title: "Triangle of Sadness takes the Palme d'Or at Cannes"
date: 2022-05-28 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [culture]
description: "Ruben Östlund's satire of a luxury cruise, its passengers and the crew who serve them won the top prize at the seventy-fifth festival on 28 May, a second Palme for a director whose films have made a habit of humiliating the comfortable."
---

Ruben Östlund won the Palme d'Or at the Cannes film festival on 28 May for Triangle of Sadness, a satire set largely on a luxury cruise ship whose passengers are wealthy and whose crew is paid to pretend to like them. The jury was chaired by the French actor Vincent Lindon, and the award was announced at the closing ceremony after a festival that had run for twelve days in the usual heat and the usual arguments about whether cinema belongs on streaming platforms.

Östlund had won the same prize five years earlier for The Square, a film about a museum curator and an art installation that goes wrong, and the two films share a method: take a group of people who believe themselves to be civilized, put them in a closed setting, and let the arrangement collapse. In the new film the setting is a yacht in the Mediterranean, the passengers are arms dealers and tech entrepreneurs, and the sequence that the festival audience discussed most is a dinner in heavy seas that turns the ship's plumbing and the guests' manners inside out.

The rest of the prizes spread across the programme. The Grand Prix was shared between Close, a Belgian film about two boys whose friendship ends, and Stars at Noon, Claire Denis's adaptation of a novel set in Central America. Park Chan-wook took the best director prize for Decision to Leave, Song Kang-ho the best actor prize for Broker, and Zar Amir Ebrahimi the best actress prize for Holy Spider. The festival's seventy-fifth edition had been preceded by two years of disruption, and the size of the crowds on the Croisette, thick with buyers and students, was read in the trade press as a recovery.

Cannes in May is also a market, and the market was nervous. The festival's own finances had been strained by the pandemic, the streaming services that had kept the industry alive were cutting spending, and the war in Ukraine had removed the Russian delegation and its money from the programme. The films that sold well were the ones with stars attached, and the ones that did not were the ones that had been financed by the platforms that used to buy everything.

Östlund's win surprised those who had followed the betting, which had favoured a more solemn entry, and confirmed what everyone who had seen the film already suspected. The director used his acceptance speech to talk about the cinema as a collective experience and about the auditorium as a place where a stranger's laugh tells you something about your own joke, a theme he had developed in interviews throughout the fortnight.

The prize was handed out on a Saturday night to a film about people who mistake their comfort for a right, and the festival that awarded it was itself a place where comfort and rights are negotiated every year. Cannes has always liked films that bite the hand that feeds the audience, and this year it gave the biggest prize to one that bites it twice.
