title: "Modi addresses Congress and courts American business"
date: 2023-06-23 17:00:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Narendra Modi addressed a joint session of Congress on 22 June during a state visit to Washington, the second Indian prime minister to do so, and used the trip to sign deals on jet engines, chip packaging and defence cooperation."
---

Narendra Modi addressed a joint session of Congress on 22 June, becoming the second Indian prime minister to do so after Manmohan Singh in 2010, and used the speech to present India as an indispensable partner rather than a country that needs help. The visit was a state visit, with a White House dinner and a series of announcements attached, and it was the third meeting between Modi and President Biden in less than a year. The Indian flag was flown on Pennsylvania Avenue, and the crowd outside the Capitol was large and mostly enthusiastic.

The deals announced were concrete and cover the sectors where the two countries have decided their interests overlap. General Electric and Hindustan Aeronautics signed an agreement to co-produce engines for Indian fighter aircraft, with a technology transfer that GE had resisted for years. Micron said it would build a semiconductor assembly and test plant in Gujarat, and Applied Materials announced an engineering centre near Bangalore. The two governments agreed to work on critical minerals, on quantum and on the standards that govern artificial intelligence, and they signed a defence cooperation framework that simplifies the sale of American equipment to India.

Strategy explains the trip. India wants technology, capital and a hedge against China, and the United States wants a partner in the Indo-Pacific that can operate at scale and that shares its concerns about Chinese expansion. The two countries have been discussing these questions for two decades, and the difference now is that both governments have decided that the arrangement is worth paying for. India's purchases of Russian oil during the war in Ukraine were noted and forgiven, which is the clearest evidence that the Americans have decided that India's value outweighs its non-alignment.

Modi also carried a domestic message in the speech itself. He described India's economy as the fastest-growing large economy in the world, its technology sector as a source of talent for American firms, and its diaspora as a bridge between the two countries. He did not mention the restrictions on the press, the treatment of religious minorities or the state of the opposition, and the members of Congress who criticized those issues did so outside the chamber rather than in his presence.

What the visit changes is the framework rather than the facts. India's tariffs remain high, its defence industry is still mostly state-owned, and its record on intellectual property is the reason that technology transfers have been so slow. The agreements signed this week are the beginning of a long negotiation about how much of the American supply chain will move to India and how quickly, and the answer will be decided by the companies rather than by the leaders. Modi left Washington with more access than any Indian leader has had, and with a clearer sense of what the Americans want in return.

Delivery will take years, and the engines that General Electric will build in India will not fly before the end of the decade. What was settled this week is the direction of the relationship, which is now closer than at any time since the two countries began their nuclear rapprochement.
