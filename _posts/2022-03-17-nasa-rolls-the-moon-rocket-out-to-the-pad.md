---
title: "NASA rolls the Moon rocket out to the pad"
date: 2022-03-17 23:45:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "A crawler carries 98 metres of rocket six kilometres at walking pace, and a machine assembled from the budgets of forty states begins the rehearsals that will decide when it flies."
---

The crawler-transporter began moving the Space Launch System out of the assembly building at Kennedy Space Center on the evening of 17 March, and the journey to pad 39B took about ten hours at a speed that a person could walk. The rocket stood on a mobile launcher with its umbilicals attached, and the whole assembly weighed more than 5,000 tonnes. Crowds watched from the causeway, and the agency streamed the rollout live, which is what an agency does when the machine has cost more than the science it carries.

Numbers describe a rocket built to a specification written in 2011. The Block 1 version stands 98 metres tall, produces 5.75 million pounds of thrust at liftoff from four engines and two solid boosters, and can send 27 tonnes towards the Moon. The core stage holds liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, the upper stage is a modified Delta Cryogenic Second Stage, and the payload is the Orion capsule, which was designed for four astronauts and tested without any aboard on this flight.

The purpose of the first flight was to fly the hardware rather than to do science. Orion would spend about 25 days in space, enter a distant retrograde orbit around the Moon that took it tens of thousands of kilometres beyond the surface, and return to splash down in the Pacific. The capsule carried mannequins fitted with sensors in place of crew, and the heat shield was to be tested at a re-entry speed that only a lunar return produces.

Cost had followed the rocket since the beginning. The inspector general of the agency reported that the first four flights would cost about 4.1 billion dollars each, and that the programme had spent more than 20 billion dollars before the first launch. The rocket reuses technology from the shuttle, which was meant to save money and which carried the cost of the contractors and the districts that built it. Members of Congress defended the programme on the ground that it preserved jobs and capabilities that no commercial company had.

Rehearsals took longer than the rollout. The wet dress rehearsal in April stopped three times on problems with valves and with the supply of liquid hydrogen, and the launch attempts in the late summer of 2022 met a hydrogen leak, a faulty sensor and a hurricane. The rocket finally flew on 16 November, and the capsule returned on 11 December after a flight that the agency judged a success and that had cost the equivalent of a small mission in its own right.

What the rollout showed was how a rocket is made in a democracy. The machine that rolled out at walking pace was the product of a coalition of states, a set of contracts and a promise about the Moon that had survived four administrations, and its schedule was set as much by the calendar of appropriations as by the physics. The vehicle worked, and the question that it left was whether a programme designed to be un-cancellable can also be cheap.
