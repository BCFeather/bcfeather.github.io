title: "The south bank of a river retaken"
date: 2022-11-11 23:20:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Ukrainian troops enter Kherson city eight and a half months after it fell, and the Russian army withdraws across the Dnipro."
---

Reports came before noon, and they were cautious. Ukrainian units were moving along the roads west of the city, and Russian forces had been pulling back for two days. By the afternoon the defence ministry's intelligence directorate announced that the regional capital was back under Ukrainian control, and that its soldiers had entered the streets. Residents who had stayed through the occupation came out of their buildings to meet them, and videos of that meeting travelled faster than any official statement could.

Kherson had been the only regional capital taken by the Russian army in the first weeks of the invasion. It fell in early March, and for eight and a half months a city of nearly three hundred thousand people lived under an occupation that included a referendum, a change of currency and a promise of permanent incorporation into Russia. Teachers were told to use Russian textbooks, and those who refused left the profession or left the city altogether. The occupation administration moved its offices east in October, and the flags came down with it.

The withdrawal was announced on 9 November by the commander of Russian forces in Ukraine, who told his minister that the army could no longer be supplied across the river. Bridges over the Dnipro had been damaged for months, and every ton of ammunition and food had to come across on pontoons or by ferry under fire. The retreating troops took what they could carry, and they cut the power lines and the water works as they went.

What the Ukrainians found was a city emptied of about half its people, without reliable power, water or heating, and with a great deal of evidence of what had happened in the cells of its police station and in the yards of its villages. Demining began at once, and officials warned that the retreating army had left traps in apartments, on roads and in the woods. Engineers started work on the substations before the shelling had stopped, and the first train arrived a week later with food, blankets and a generator for the hospital. Volunteers came in from Mykolaiv with water, and the queues at the collection points stretched around the block.

The river is the front now. Russian artillery sits on the far bank, and the distance from the water to the edge of the city is short enough for ordinary shells to reach. The Ukrainian president called it a historic day, and said in the same breath that the work of restoring the city would be enormous. He also warned that the soldiers on the eastern bank would keep firing at whatever moved in the streets below them.

Militarily, the gain is a bridgehead and a threat. The land north of the Dnipro that the Russians had held is gone, the canal that supplies Crimea is in Ukrainian hands, and the front has shortened by some hundreds of kilometres. Politically, the return of a provincial capital is the kind of fact that no communique can argue away, and it arrived on the same day that the government in Kyiv published the terms on which it would negotiate.
