---
title: "A moon landing that ran out of time"
date: 2024-01-09 21:06:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "Astrobotic's Peregrine lander launched towards the moon on 8 January and lost propellant within hours, ending the first American commercial attempt to reach the lunar surface before it left Earth orbit."
---

Peregrine launched on the morning of the eighth of January from Cape Canaveral aboard a Vulcan Centaur, the new rocket that United Launch Alliance had spent a decade building, and for the first few hours the mission looked like the beginning of something. The spacecraft separated cleanly, powered up, and began talking to its controllers through NASA's Deep Space Network. Then the trouble started. A valve that should have stayed closed had opened, helium had pushed its way into a line of oxidiser, and the pressure in the propellant tank rose past the point where the structure could hold it. By the afternoon the team had lost the ability to point the spacecraft at the sun with certainty, and by the evening it had become a question of how long the battery could keep the heaters alive.

Astrobotic published the sequence as it happened, which is not the usual practice in a business where every failure is a share price. The company said the anomaly had probably been caused by the valve, that the propellant was leaking at a rate it could not stop, and that the landing on the moon was no longer possible. Over the following days the spacecraft, still under control, was turned towards the sun to keep its solar panels charged, and the team used the remaining time to test the instruments that were meant to be used on the surface. Cameras took pictures. A NASA instrument that measures the plasma around the spacecraft returned data. The lander carried payloads from six countries and several universities, along with a small rover built by Carnegie Mellon, and none of them would ever be deployed.

On the eighteenth of January the company ended the mission, after the spacecraft had been guided into a re-entry over the South Pacific. What burned up was about a tonne of hardware and the ashes of people whose families had paid for a memorial flight, a detail that turned the failure into something more uncomfortable than an engineering problem. The cause of the failure was traced to a pressure relief valve that had been supplied for the helium line, and the company said later that a test of that valve before launch had not caught the condition that opened it. That is the kind of finding that closes a report and does not close the question of whether private companies should be carrying other people's remains to the moon on a first flight.

The mission's fate says something about the programme it belonged to. NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services pays companies to carry instruments to the moon, at prices far below what a government-built mission would cost, on the understanding that some of the attempts will fail. Astrobotic's contract was worth about a hundred million dollars, and the agency's administrators have said repeatedly that they expect a mixed record. The same month, a Japanese mission reached the surface with a different set of problems, and a second American lander, built by Intuitive Machines, was waiting for a launch window in February. The pattern of the next two years is already visible. Several small spacecraft, each with a narrow margin for error, each paid for by a customer who has accepted the risk, and a public that will judge the programme by how many of them land rather than by how much they cost.

For the engineers who built Peregrine, the month ended with a different kind of lesson. A spacecraft that never reached the moon still operated for ten days, kept its attitude, ran its instruments and delivered data, which is more than several lunar attempts of the past managed. The failure was in one valve, on one line, in a vehicle that otherwise worked. Spacecraft are unforgiving in exactly this way. A design that is right in a thousand places is still lost if one of them is wrong, and the cheaper the mission, the fewer the places where that one can be found before launch.
