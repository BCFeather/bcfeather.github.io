---
title: "Shehbaz Sharif becomes prime minister of Pakistan"
date: 2024-03-03 22:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Shehbaz Sharif was sworn in as prime minister of Pakistan on 3 March after a coalition of parties agreed to support him, inheriting an economy constrained by an IMF programme and a parliament whose largest party alleges the election was rigged."
---

Shehbaz Sharif took the oath of office as prime minister of Pakistan on the third of March, returning to a post he had held for sixteen months before the election of the eighth of February. He was nominated by his brother, Nawaz Sharif, whose party, the Pakistan Muslim League (Nawaz), is the largest in the coalition that formed the government. Vote in the National Assembly was 201 in his favour against 92 for the candidate backed by the party of Imran Khan.

Coalition behind him is an arrangement of convenience. Pakistan Muslim League (Nawaz) won 75 seats, and its partners, including the Pakistan People's Party and several smaller groups, brought the total to a working majority. The Sunni Ittehad Council, which most of Khan's supported candidates joined after they were elected as independents, holds the largest single bloc of seats and sits in opposition. Government's first task was to agree on a cabinet, and it took several days to allocate the ministries among the parties.

Economy that he inherits is in the middle of a difficult adjustment. Pakistan is in the final months of a nine-month standby arrangement with the International Monetary Fund worth three billion dollars, and it must negotiate a longer programme to avoid a default on its external debt. Inflation has been above twenty per cent for two years, the rupee has lost a third of its value since 2022, and the electricity sector carries a circular debt that the government has been unable to clear.

Election that produced the parliament was disputed before it took place. Khan's party was barred from using its symbol, its candidates stood as independents, and the caretaker government restricted campaigning. Observers from the Commonwealth and from European embassies said the vote had been marked by interference in the count, and several international organizations called for an investigation. Government says the election was the most transparent in the country's history, and the Election Commission has rejected the allegations.

Relationship with the military is the constraint that every civilian government in Pakistan works within. The army chief, General Asim Munir, has taken a more public role in economic policy than his predecessors, and the government's room for manoeuvre on security and foreign policy is limited. Sharif said in his first speech that he would seek consensus with all institutions, which is the formulation that civilian leaders use when they mean that they do not intend to pick a fight.

Consequence of his return is a government that can pass a budget and negotiate with the fund, and that will be judged on whether it can hold together. Pakistan's coalitions have rarely lasted a full term, and the parties in this one disagree on everything except the wish to keep Khan out of power. What the third of March settled was who would sit in the prime minister's chair. What it left open was whether the arrangement can survive the budget, the fund negotiations and the next round of pressure from the street.
