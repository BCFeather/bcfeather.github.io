On 15 September, a man with a rifle spent nearly twelve hours waiting in the shrubbery along the edge of Donald Trump's golf course in West Palm Beach, Florida, and was discovered, by a Secret Service agent sweeping the course ahead of the former president, at the moment when Trump was a few hundred yards away. Shots came from the agent, the man dropped the rifle, climbed into a car and drove off, and was arrested within the hour on the interstate, where the authorities found the AK-47, the scope, the camera and the handwritten notes that would later frame the charge. Two months after a gunman had come within centimetres of killing Trump at a rally in Pennsylvania, a second attempt had been made, and stopped, by the difference of a glance through a scope.

Suspect, Ryan Routh, 58, is a figure whose biography reads like a catalogue of the causes that have converged on this election year. A former construction worker with a history of arrests and a trail of online activity that drifted from support for the candidates of the left to volunteerism in Ukraine to posts about the 'choice' facing the American republic, he appears to have been improvising his politics as he improvised his attempt. He was charged on 24 September with the federal offences of attempting to assassinate a major presidential candidate and possessing a firearm in a school zone, and the case, like the shooting in July, will test how the legal system treats the political violence of the decade.

Immediate question, the one the campaign is arguing about, is how this was possible. Security around Trump, who still holds public events without the full perimeter of a sitting president, was the subject of a Secret Service review after the July shooting. That review produced resignations and reforms. The September incident, on a golf course the agency had declined to secure like a rally, raised the questions again, and the director, at a hearing within days, called the level of protection 'not where it should be'. Trump responded with his usual mixture of defiance and grievance. He declared himself safe and blamed his opponents' rhetoric, and the campaign's reaction, on both sides, followed the script each party has been reading since July.

Deeper story is the one the arguments keep circling without facing. The attempted violence is a symptom of the structure of this election rather than an aberration. Two candidates describe each other, to audiences of millions, as existential threats to the republic. Platforms are saturated with the same language. A man who believes the election is a last stand can conclude, as two men in two months have concluded, that a rifle is the logical instrument. Authorities stopped both attempts, and the machinery of the state has so far contained the violence the machinery of the campaign has fed. Containment is not prevention, and the season still has seven weeks to run.

What can be said, in the aftermath of the second attempt, is that the country has arrived at a point it has been building toward for years: a presidential campaign in which assassination is no longer unthinkable but a recurring risk to be managed, in which the candidates move through crowds that have been searched and the crowds accept it. It held on 15 September and it held in July, and holding has begun to look less like strength than like routine. Suspect is in custody and the trial will follow, and the campaign will continue. The question the country keeps postponing, of what has made its politics this way, waits for an election that will not answer it.
