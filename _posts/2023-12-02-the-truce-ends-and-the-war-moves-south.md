Fighting resumed in Gaza on the morning of 1 December, an hour before the seven-day truce was due to be extended for a second time. The pause had exchanged 105 hostages held by Hamas and other groups for 240 Palestinian prisoners, most of them women and teenagers, and it had let aid convoys reach the north of the strip at a rate the first weeks of the war never approached. By the time the guns started again, the deal had run out of women and children to trade.

Within two days the centre of the operation had moved south. Leaflets and social media messages told residents of Khan Younis and the surrounding towns to leave for Rafah on the Egyptian border, which meant asking people who had already walked thirty kilometres to walk fifteen more, into a stretch of land that had been a village before October. The United Nations said the designated areas covered about a fifth of the territory and could hold perhaps a third of the population, and that the shelters in Rafah were already at twice their intended capacity.

Aid organizations set out the numbers in the briefings that followed. The strip's population of 2.3 million had been displaced almost entirely, more than half the housing stock had been damaged or destroyed, and the health system was running on generators and donated fuel. Water production stood at a fraction of its pre-war volume, the sewage pumped into the sea for years now ran in the streets of the camps, and the vaccination campaigns that had kept polio out of the strip since the 1980s had stopped in November.

The politics of the pause had been difficult from the start. Qatar and Egypt negotiated each day of it in hours, and the releases twice came close to collapsing over the composition of the lists. Once the fighting resumed, the mediators lost the lever they had used, and the families of the remaining hostages began a campaign that split Israeli opinion into two camps which could not be reconciled: those who wanted the war finished, and those who wanted the hostages first.

Washington and European capitals answered with the formula they had used in November, urging restraint while supplying the munitions that made restraint a matter of discretion. The American secretary of state had arrived in the region on 30 November and spent the following weeks telling reporters that the operation had to protect civilians, a sentence with no mechanism attached, which the Israeli government answered by publishing maps of safe zones.

For the two million people inside the strip, the choice on 1 December was the choice it had been in October. Stay in a building that might be hit, or walk to a place declared safe that might become a target within a week. Most of them walked, because walking was the only decision left to them, and because the alternative involved waiting for a bomb that might arrive in an hour or in a week, in a place that had been described as safe by the army that was dropping it.
