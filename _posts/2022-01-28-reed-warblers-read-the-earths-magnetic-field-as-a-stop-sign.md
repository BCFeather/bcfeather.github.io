---
title: "Reed warblers read the Earth's magnetic field as a stop sign"
date: 2022-01-28 23:55:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "A century of ringing records shows that a small migrant returns to the exact latitude where it hatched, and a new analysis argues that the bird decides when to stop by sensing the field rather than by recognising the place."
---

Ringers have marked migratory birds with numbered metal bands for more than a century, and the records they left behind carry a puzzle. A reed warbler that hatches in a reedbed in southern England will spend the winter in West Africa and, if it survives, come back to the same reedbed, arriving within days of the date its parents did. Birds return with such precision that the operation looks less like navigation than like recognition of a place. Ringing has been in decline for years, and this paper is one of the arguments for keeping it funded.

A study in *Science* this week tested the recognition explanation against nearly a century of records, and it does not survive them. Young birds migrate alone, without a parent to follow, and they arrive at a site they have never seen. Something has to tell them that they are in the right place, and the candidates are the stars, the sun, the coastline and the geomagnetic field, each of which offers a different kind of information. The team also noted that birds raised in captivity and released away from their natal site behave in the same way, which rules out learning.

The new analysis compared nearly a century of ringing recoveries with geomagnetic parameters along the routes the birds fly. Recoveries from breeding sites clustered where the geomagnetic inclination and intensity fell within narrow ranges, and recoveries from stopover sites did not, which suggests that the field matters most at the end of the journey rather than along it. Birds released away from their natal site flew until the field matched what they had experienced as nestlings, and then stopped.

Result turns a compass into a trigger. A bird can use the field to hold a heading with a magnetic compass, and it can also use the field's inclination to know how far north or south it has travelled, which is how a chick that has never been anywhere can tell when it has arrived. The stop sign comes from the field rather than from the landscape; it is a set of numbers that the bird reads with an organ nobody has yet located precisely.

Implications run beyond warblers. Species that return to the same site are common among birds, and if the mechanism is magnetic rather than spatial, then a change in the field over time would shift where a population settles, which is a form of range change that no habitat map would predict. It also suggests that the magnetic sense is fine enough to resolve differences of a few hundred kilometres in inclination.

A century of amateur record-keeping produced the dataset that made the argument possible, which is worth noticing. The finding is about a bird's sense of where home is, and it exists because thousands of people wrote down, in small notebooks, where a bird with a numbered ring was found dead or caught again. The authors also compared birds that stopped short of their natal latitude with those that overshot it, and the field at each stopping point differed by the same amount in the same direction.
