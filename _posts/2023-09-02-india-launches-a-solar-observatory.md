---
title: "India launches a solar observatory"
date: 2023-09-02 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "The Indian Space Research Organization launched Aditya-L1 on 2 September 2023, a spacecraft that will study the sun from a point 1.5 million kilometres from Earth where the view of the solar atmosphere is never interrupted."
---

A Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle lifted off from Sriharikota at about noon on 2 September with Aditya-L1, a spacecraft of about fifteen hundred kilograms carrying seven instruments. The mission is the space agency's first dedicated solar observatory, and it follows the landing of Chandrayaan-3 on the moon a week earlier, which has made this the most crowded month in the agency's history. The launch vehicle placed the spacecraft in a low orbit, and a series of engine burns over the following weeks will raise it towards its destination.

Destination is the first Lagrange point, a gravitational balance about 1.5 million kilometres from Earth on the side facing the sun. A spacecraft there keeps station with the planet and never sees an eclipse, which means its cameras can watch the corona without interruption. NASA's solar observatory has been parked there for years, and the Indian mission joins it with instruments built in India and designed for the same questions: why the corona is hundreds of times hotter than the surface beneath it, and how the solar wind is accelerated.

Science here has practical consequences. Solar storms can damage satellites, disrupt radio and navigation and overload power grids, and the energy that drives them is released in the corona that Aditya-L1 will observe. India operates a large number of satellites for broadcasting, weather and navigation, and its power grid is long and therefore vulnerable to the currents that a geomagnetic storm induces in transmission lines. The mission carries a particle experiment, two X-ray instruments and a coronagraph, and its data will be shared with other agencies. The particle instrument will measure the solar wind arriving at the spacecraft, which is the same wind that reaches Earth a day or two later.

Cost was modest and the aims specific. The spacecraft and its instruments are reported to have cost about a hundred million dollars, far less than the American mission it will work alongside, and the seven payloads were built by six Indian institutes. The launch took place four days after the lunar landing, and the space agency's chairman has described both as the same programme, which is a national capability rather than a single spectacular result.

The politics are domestic and regional. The government has promoted the space programme as a demonstration of self-reliance, and the agency has been told to plan a crewed mission in the coming decade and a Venus orbiter after that. India's neighbours are watching, because the country's space budget is now large enough to support missions that other developing countries cannot afford, and India has been selling those services commercially.

A spacecraft is now climbing towards a point it will hold for years, and it will watch the sun in wavelengths that no Indian instrument has observed before, with data downlinked to a ground station near Bangalore. The data will be public within months, and the storms it helps to predict will keep arriving whatever the budget, which is why the mission was approved in the first place.
