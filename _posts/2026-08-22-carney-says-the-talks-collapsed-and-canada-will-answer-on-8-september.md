title: "Carney says the talks collapsed and Canada will answer on 8 September"
date: 2026-08-22 23:45:00 +0800

description: "In a televised address on 22 August the Canadian prime minister said trade talks with Washington had broken down at the last minute and that matching tariffs on 20 billion dollars of American goods would take effect on 8 September."
---

Mark Carney used a televised address on 22 August to explain why the trade talks with Washington had collapsed, and to announce a date. Matching tariffs on 20 billion dollars of American goods would take effect on 8 September, covering dairy, steel, household appliances, pulp and paper, and electronics. The list was chosen to spread the pain across regions and industries rather than to maximize retaliation on a single sector. Business groups in Toronto and Montreal welcomed the clarity and warned about the price, since the same duties raise costs for Canadian manufacturers that buy American steel.

His account of the breakdown was specific. The two sides had come close to a deal earlier in the week, and then the American side introduced terms at the last minute that would have limited Canada's ability to sign trade agreements with other countries and constrained its ability to protect its language, culture and sovereignty. The automotive provisions, he said, were grossly unfair, and he described the package as uneconomic and damaging.

The prime minister has governed since 2025 on a single economic theme: reduce dependence on the United States and diversify. He has said repeatedly that Canada will accept no deal at any price, and on 22 August he gave the shortest explanation of the retaliation available. Asked why he was imposing it, he said: because we were attacked. He also cited job creation running at four times the American rate and foreign direct investment at a twenty-year high. His government has also signed agreements with Europe and with partners in Asia this year, moves that reduce exposure to a single market and that Washington has watched with irritation.

The American response was theatrical. On 27 August the president announced that Lake Ontario would be renamed American Lake, a gesture with no legal content and considerable political content. It signalled that Washington does not regard Canadian sensibilities as a constraint, and it made a return to the table harder for any Canadian government, because a leader who negotiates after that would look as though the gesture had worked.

Economics will decide the dispute's cost. The two economies share supply chains in vehicles and parts that neither can reroute quickly, and duties on steel and appliances reach consumers directly. American tariffs have already been through the courts, with the Supreme Court striking down parts of the schedule this year, which adds legal uncertainty to the commercial one. Provincial governments have asked for compensation, and the dairy lobby has warned that the dispute will cost farmers a season of exports to their largest customer.

Carney's calculation is that a country of forty million can absorb a tariff fight that a country of three hundred and forty million will notice less. That asymmetry cuts both ways, and 8 September will show which government has the longer patience. A negotiation that resumes after September will start with both sides poorer and angrier, which is exactly the state that a tariff dispute tends to produce before anyone finds a way out.
