The national subsidy for households with young children was announced in the last week of July and begins paying out this month. The amount is three thousand six hundred yuan per child per year up to the age of three, paid to the parent, and the eligibility is broad enough that most households with a young child qualify. It is the first payment of its kind that the central government has made at national scale, and it was announced with a figure and a date rather than with a target.

The arithmetic of raising a child in a Chinese city is well documented and it dwarfs the subsidy. A place at a nursery in a large city can cost more than the subsidy in three months. The cost of housing a family of four rather than three is the largest item and it does not appear in any budget the policy touches. The subsidy covers a fraction of a fraction, and everyone involved in drafting it knew that.

What the transfer does is establish a principle. A payment from the centre to a household for the specific purpose of raising a child asserts that the cost of the next generation is partly public, which is a different claim from the one that has been made for the last forty years. The amount is small and the claim is large, and the claim is what will be argued about in the next budget.

The design has one feature worth noting. It is paid in cash, unconditionally, to whoever is registered as the caregiver, rather than through a tax deduction or a voucher for services. A deduction helps households that pay income tax and does nothing for the ones that do not. A voucher for nursery places helps only where places exist, which in most of the country is not the case. Cash reaches the household that has the child, which is the argument for it, and it also reaches households that would have had the child anyway, which is the argument against.

The interaction with local policy is where the effect will actually be felt. Several provinces and cities already pay their own subsidies, some of them considerably larger, and the national payment is meant to sit on top rather than replace them. Whether it does depends on how the transfers are sequenced, and the sequencing is being decided by finance departments that are already short of revenue.

The demography is the reason the policy exists and it is also the reason it will be judged harshly. Birth numbers have fallen for a decade, and a payment of three thousand yuan has never in any country reversed a decline of that kind. The research on cash transfers and fertility is consistent and modest. The payments that have worked are the ones large enough to change a household's decision at the margin, and the ones that have not are almost all smaller than this.

What the subsidy can do is make the choice less punishing for the households already inclined to have a second child. That is a real effect and a limited one, and the policy is honest about its size in a way that most announcements are not.
