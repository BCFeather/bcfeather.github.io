---
title: "Nearly two hundred Silicon Valley founders ask Washington not to ban Chinese models"
date: 2026-07-22 23:45:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "A letter signed by founders of start-ups that depend on cheap open-weight models argues that a ban would hand the market to the handful of labs that can afford to train at frontier scale."
---

Nearly two hundred founders of American start-ups signed a letter to the president on 22 July asking his administration not to cut off access to Chinese open-weight artificial intelligence models. The signatories argued that their companies were built on models they can download and run themselves, and that a ban would leave them paying prices set by a few American labs. It was the first coordinated intervention by that part of the industry on the administration's most closely watched technology question. The letter circulated for signatures over four days and was published with the names of the companies attached.

Economics behind the letter are easy to state. A start-up that serves a chatbot or an agent to a few thousand customers spends more on inference than on salaries, and open weights let it run the model on its own servers at a marginal cost it controls. The American labs price at levels that reflect their training budgets, and the difference between a few dollars and a few cents per million tokens decides whether a product can be launched at all. A ban would also reach copies that have already been downloaded, which is the part the signatories found unworkable.

Trigger was a set of reports that the administration was considering restrictions on Chinese models, including rules that would treat the weights as an export. A White House spokesperson responded to the letter by saying that the administration was increasing investment in innovation and widening the technological gap between America and the rest of the world. The statement did not address the specific question of whether downloads would be restricted.

Immediate context made the letter timely. Moonshot had released Kimi K3 four days earlier, and the chief executive of NVIDIA had said on 21 July that the Chinese models were excellent. The White House had also accused Moonshot of copying American work, an accusation the company denied, and the combination of praise from a chip maker and suspicion from the administration framed the choice the founders were writing about. Officials in the administration have been divided between a narrow rule and a broad one, and the letter was addressed to the president rather than to the agencies.

Policy problem is that weights are harder to recall than widgets. A model that has been downloaded a million times cannot be recalled by a customs order, and a rule that restricts American companies from using it would bind only the companies that follow the rules. Officials who understand this have argued for disclosure requirements and for safety testing rather than for prohibition, and the letter gave that argument a constituency. The Commerce Department has been drafting rules on model weights for months, and no text has been published.

What the signatories asked for is a narrow thing. They did not defend the Chinese labs or their practices, and they did not argue that open weights carry no risk. They asked that American companies be allowed to compete on cost, which is the request that a government serious about its own start-ups finds hardest to refuse. Whether the letter changes anything will be known when the rules appear.
