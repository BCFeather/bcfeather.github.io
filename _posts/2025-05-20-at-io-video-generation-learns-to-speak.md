---
title: "At I/O, video generation learns to speak"
date: 2025-05-20 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "Google's developer conference on 20 May introduced Veo 3, a video model that generates dialogue, sound effects and ambience alongside the picture, plus Imagen 4 and a faster Gemini. The addition of sound is the change that matters, because it is the step that turns a demo into footage."
---

At its developer conference on 20 May, Google presented a set of upgrades that would have been the headline of any other year and were instead additions to a long list. The one worth spending time on is Veo 3, a video model that now generates audio with the image: dialogue in a character's mouth, sound effects timed to a door closing, ambient noise that matches the scene. Imagen 4 arrived for still images, and a new preview of Gemini 2.5 Flash was announced with the usual claims about speed and cost.

Silent video generation was already impressive, and it was also obviously incomplete. A shot of a person speaking with no sound is a moving photograph, not a scene. Every maker of synthetic video has spent two years working around the absence of audio, either by dubbing afterwards or by keeping the subject's mouth away from the camera. Veo 3 removes the workaround, and in doing so it changes what the output can be used for: a five-second clip with matching sound is a shot, and a sequence of shots with sound is a scene, and scenes are the unit that advertising, film and television actually buy.

The technical difficulty is not the audio itself. Speech synthesis is a solved problem in isolation; the hard part is synchronisation, the requirement that the sound be plausible for the specific space in the frame and arrive at the right moment in the action. A room with a hard floor sounds different from a room with a carpet, and a door that closes at frame ninety has to be heard at frame ninety. Models that get this wrong produce the uncanny effect that everyone recognises immediately and nobody can quite name.

The strategic logic is clear enough. Google is selling a pipeline, and a pipeline that produces finished footage is worth more than one that produces elements that must be assembled. That is also the reason to be cautious about the demonstrations. Generated audio in a trailer is selected from many attempts, and the ratio of attempts to usable output is the number that decides whether a studio can use the tool on a schedule.

For the people who work in this medium, the near-term consequence is a shift in where the labour sits. The camera and the microphone were two separate crafts with two separate budgets; a model that produces both at once collapses them into a prompt and a set of choices. That does not eliminate the jobs; it moves them: the person who decides what a scene should sound like becomes more important than the person who can record it, and the skill of specifying becomes the skill that pays.

Two years ago the interesting question about generative video was whether the motion would hold together. This week the motion holds together and the room has a sound. The question has moved to what the footage is for, and that is a question that models cannot answer, though they can now supply the sound of somebody answering it.
