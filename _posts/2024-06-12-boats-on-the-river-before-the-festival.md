The dragon boat races fall in the first half of June, on the fifth day of the fifth lunar month. A village that keeps a boat takes it out of storage a month before the race. The hull is scraped, the lacquer is renewed, the drum is tuned.

The boat is built by hand. A hull of fir, twenty metres long, narrow enough that a paddler sits with knees against the back of the person in front. The building takes a workshop most of a winter. The drummer sits at the bow facing the crew, the steersman stands at the stern with a long oar.

The crew is a village institution. Twenty-four paddlers, chosen by tryout, mostly men between eighteen and forty. The training runs at dawn for four weeks. A crew that has rowed together for years beats a crew of stronger men, and every village knows which of the two it has.

The race itself is short. Two hundred metres, or five hundred, decided in under two minutes. The start is a gun, the finish is a flag on a line, the result is argued about for an hour afterwards. A crew that loses by a bow length will claim the lane was wide.

The food around the date has its own market. A pyramid of glutinous rice wrapped in reed leaves, boiled for hours, sold in boxes of six or ten. The factories that make them start in April. A household that used to wrap its own now buys them, and the leaves that used to be soaked in a basin are now bought in a packet.

The herb that hangs on the door is sold on the pavement. A bundle of mugwort and calamus, a yuan or two, bought by people who no longer know what it is for. The explanation is offered by the seller, who repeats it several hundred times a day.

The festival is a public holiday of three days this year, and the first of them fell on a Saturday. The roads out of the cities were busy on Friday evening. A race that draws ten thousand spectators fills the hotels in a county town for one night.

A county that hosts a race builds a temporary grandstand for the day. The tickets go to residents first, then to visitors, at twenty or thirty yuan. The food stalls along the bank pay a fee to the organisers. The town fills its guesthouses for one night and empties again on Monday.

The wrapped rice has become an export. A factory in the delta ships boxes to supermarkets in the cities and to overseas Chinese communities. The leaves are now bought in bulk from a supplier rather than gathered. The recipe has not changed and the wrapping has, since a machine can fold a hundred in the time a pair of hands folds twenty.

The boats go back into storage in the afternoon. The hull is washed, dried, covered and hung under a roof that keeps the rain off. The crew drinks together and argues about the start. Next year the same twenty-four men will try out again.
