Dragon boat races fall in the first half of June, on the fifth day of the fifth lunar month. A village that keeps a boat takes it out of storage a month before the race. Hulls are scraped, lacquer is renewed, drums are tuned. Boats are built by hand. A hull of fir, twenty metres long, narrow enough that a paddler sits with knees against the back of the person in front. Building one takes a workshop most of a winter. At the bow sits the drummer, facing the crew. At the stern stands the steersman with a long oar.

A crew is a village institution. Twenty-four paddlers, chosen by tryout, mostly men between eighteen and forty. Training runs at dawn for four weeks. A crew that has rowed together for years beats a crew of stronger men, and every village knows which of the two it has. The race itself is short. Two hundred metres, or five hundred, decided in under two minutes. A gun starts it, a flag on a line finishes it, and the result is argued about for an hour afterwards. A crew that loses by a bow length will claim the lane was wide.

Food around the date has a market of its own. A pyramid of glutinous rice wrapped in reed leaves, boiled for hours, sold in boxes of six or ten. Factories that make them start in April. A household that used to wrap its own now buys them, and the leaves that used to be soaked in a basin are now bought in a packet. Herbs for the door are sold on the pavement. A bundle of mugwort and calamus, a yuan or two, bought by people who no longer know what it is for. An explanation comes from the seller, who repeats it several hundred times a day.

Three days of public holiday came with the festival this year, and the first of them fell on a Saturday. Roads out of the cities were busy on Friday evening. A race that draws ten thousand spectators fills the hotels in a county town for one night. A county that hosts a race builds a temporary grandstand for the day. Tickets go to residents first, then to visitors, at twenty or thirty yuan. Food stalls along the bank pay a fee to the organisers. Guesthouses fill for one night and the town empties again on Monday.

Wrapped rice has become an export. A factory in the delta ships boxes to supermarkets in the cities and to overseas Chinese communities. Leaves are now bought in bulk from a supplier rather than gathered. The recipe has not changed and the wrapping has, since a machine can fold a hundred in the time a pair of hands folds twenty.

By the afternoon the boats are back in storage. A hull is washed, dried, covered and hung under a roof that keeps the rain off. Afterwards the crew drinks together and argues about the start. Next year the same twenty-four men will try out again.
