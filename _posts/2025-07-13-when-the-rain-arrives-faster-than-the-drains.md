A summer thunderstorm in the north of the country can drop in forty minutes what a city's drainage system was designed to carry in a day. The water arrives faster than any pipe can take it, collects in the low places, and produces the scene that appears on the news every July. Cars floating in an underpass, a bus with water at the height of its windows, a man in a suit walking out of a flooded tunnel with his shoes in his hand.

The engineering answer has been available since the nineteenth century. Cities can be built with permeable surfaces, retention ponds, underground storage and pipes sized for the storm rather than for the average. The difficulty is that none of it is visible, and all of it is expensive, and a city that spends its infrastructure budget on a pipe nobody can see is a city whose mayor will be asked at the next election why the money was not spent on a road.

The newer idea in the field is to treat the city as a catchment rather than a set of pipes. Roofs hold water, gardens absorb it, roadside strips slow it down, and a park is designed to flood on purpose so that the street does not. This is cheap compared with engineering and it requires coordination rather than concrete, which is why it advances slowly. It also requires that the public accept a park that is a lake twice a year, and the public accept it only after they have understood why.

The pattern of the damage has changed as the cities have grown. Older districts were built on ground that drained naturally, and the drains were designed for a hundred-year storm on a smaller city. New districts are built on low land that was farmland and is now a grid of towers, and each new tower adds a surface that sends water somewhere else. The flood does not appear where the rain falls. It appears where the water collects, which is usually the district of the people who have least capacity to deal with it.

The forecasts have improved and the warnings now arrive several hours early, which is a genuine achievement given that the storms that cause the damage are the ones that form in the afternoon and are gone by evening. A message that reaches a driver before he enters an underpass is worth more than a pipe he will never see. The remaining gap is behavioural. People drive into flooded roads because the water looks shallower than it is, and no warning has yet solved that.

What the summer tests, in the end, is the honesty of a city's spending. Flooding is not an accident. It is the arithmetic of surfaces and drains, done over decades, and a city that has been built for cars and for growth will flood in the way that its decisions predicted. The storm is merely the auditor.
