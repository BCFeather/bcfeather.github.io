---

The Nikkei 225 fell 12.4 per cent on 5 August, which is its worst single day since the crash of 1987. The Topix index fell further. The fall in both was concentrated in the first two hours of trading.

The trigger was a decision taken in Tokyo on the last day of July. The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to a quarter of a per cent and signalled that it would not stop there. A rate rise of that size is small in absolute terms and large against a market that had priced in nothing.

The amplification came from the currency. Foreign funds had borrowed yen at a low rate and used the money to buy shares, property and emerging market debt. When the yen strengthened, those positions lost money twice, once on the asset and once on the loan. Selling the assets to repay the loan is the mechanism that turns a small rise into a large fall.

The currency moved four per cent in a day against the dollar. An exporter in Osaka earns in dollars and pays in yen, so a stronger yen cuts the profit on every shipment already contracted. The shares of the carmakers fell hardest for that reason.

The circuit breaker was triggered in the afternoon. A futures contract that moves more than a set amount in a session is halted for ten minutes. The halt was followed by another fall when trading resumed.

The Bank of Japan intervened in the currency market the next morning. The intervention was described as a response to disorderly moves, which is the language that central banks use when they want to act without promising a level.

The American market opened weaker and closed almost flat. The contagion that the headline numbers suggest did not arrive, because the fall in Tokyo was a deleveraging rather than a repricing of the world economy.

The margin calls came later in the week. A fund that had borrowed in yen had to post collateral against the loan. The collateral was sold in whatever market was open. The selling hit the smaller markets in Asia before it hit the large ones.

The index had risen by a fifth during the year before the fall. The rise was carried by exporters and by banks. The banks were the shares that fell most, because a higher policy rate makes the bonds they hold worth less.

The retail investor was a small part of the selling. A household that holds shares through a tax-free account does not trade on a Monday morning, and the volume in the first hour was institutional from the first minute.

The rebound came the next day and it was not a recovery. The index rose by ten per cent, which returned it to where it had been two days earlier, and the year to date was still behind the American market.

The episode is a reminder of how a carry trade ends. A trade that earns a small margin for years loses a year of margin in an afternoon, and the exit is crowded because everyone uses the same funding currency.
