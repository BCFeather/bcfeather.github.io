On 5 August the Nikkei 225 fell 12.4 per cent, its worst single day since the crash of 1987. Topix fell further. Both falls were concentrated in the first two hours of trading.

A decision taken in Tokyo on the last day of July set it off. Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to a quarter of a per cent and signalled that it would not stop there. A rate rise of that size is small in absolute terms and large against a market that had priced in nothing. Amplification came from the currency. Foreign funds had borrowed yen at a low rate and used the money to buy shares, property and emerging market debt. When the yen strengthened, those positions lost money twice, once on the asset and once on the loan. Selling the assets to repay the loan is the mechanism that turns a small rise into a large fall.

Against the dollar the currency moved four per cent in a day. An exporter in Osaka earns in dollars and pays in yen, so a stronger yen cuts the profit on every shipment already contracted. Carmakers' shares fell hardest for that reason.

In the afternoon the circuit breaker was triggered. A futures contract that moves more than a set amount in a session is halted for ten minutes. A halt was followed by another fall when trading resumed. The Bank of Japan intervened in the currency market the next morning. Officials described the intervention as a response to disorderly moves, which is the language central banks use when they want to act without promising a level.

New York opened weaker and closed almost flat. Contagion did not arrive, because the fall in Tokyo was a deleveraging rather than a repricing of the world economy. The margin calls came later in the week. A fund that had borrowed in yen had to post collateral against the loan. Collateral was sold in whatever market was open. Selling hit the smaller markets in Asia before it hit the large ones.

During the year before the fall the index had risen by a fifth. Exporters and banks carried the rise. Bank shares fell most, because a higher policy rate makes the bonds they hold worth less. Retail investors were a small part of the selling. A household that holds shares through a tax-free account does not trade on a Monday morning, and the volume in the first hour was institutional from the first minute.

A rebound came the next day, and it was not a recovery. The index rose by ten per cent, which returned it to where it had been two days earlier, and the year to date was still behind the American market. What the episode recalls is how a carry trade ends. A trade that earns a small margin for years loses a year of margin in an afternoon, and the exit is crowded because everyone uses the same funding currency. By the end of the week the margin had been rebuilt, the positions that caused the fall had been closed, and the question of what the episode meant was left to the autumn.
