---
title: "Celsius froze the accounts of its customers"
date: 2022-06-12 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "A lending platform that had promised high yields on crypto deposits suspended withdrawals on a Sunday night, locking up more than eleven billion dollars and turning a price fall into a run on a bank that had no insurance."
---

Celsius Network told its customers on the evening of 12 June that it was suspending withdrawals, swaps and transfers between accounts, citing extreme market conditions. The platform held more than eleven billion dollars of customer assets at the time and served about 1.7 million accounts. Depositors who had treated the app as a savings account discovered that their money was a loan to a company that could stop repaying it. The company's website described the suspension as a pause rather than a failure, and it gave no date for withdrawals to resume.

The business model explains the speed of the run. Celsius paid yields of up to eighteen per cent on deposits of bitcoin and ether, and it funded those yields by lending the assets to trading firms and by making leveraged bets of its own. Customers were promised that their holdings were safe and that the company held enough collateral, and the terms of service, which few read, described deposits as unsecured loans to the platform. Deposits were advertised with a calculator showing how much a holding would grow in a year.

Contagion spread through the market within hours. The price of bitcoin had already fallen from 29,000 dollars to below 20,000 during the previous week, and the freeze pushed it lower still as traders sold whatever they could to cover positions elsewhere. A large crypto hedge fund, Three Arrows Capital, had borrowed heavily across the sector and was reported to be unable to meet margin calls, and several lenders that had taken deposits from retail customers began to restrict withdrawals as well.

Nothing about this was insured. Crypto deposits carry no equivalent of the federal insurance that protects bank accounts in the United States, and the platforms that accept them are regulated, where they are regulated at all, as money transmitters rather than as banks. Customers in line for a payout rank behind the company's other creditors, which is why the freeze was described by lawyers as the beginning of an insolvency rather than as a pause.

State regulators moved within a week. Securities officials in several states opened inquiries into whether the yield accounts were unregistered securities, and the platform hired restructuring advisers. The company's chief executive, Alex Mashinsky, had spent two years telling audiences that traditional finance was the risk and that his platform offered a safer alternative, and his public statements were quoted back to him by customers on every channel the company still operated. Lawyers for depositors said the terms of service left customers with little standing, because the assets had been transferred to the company rather than held in trust.

The episode shows a familiar structure in a new industry. A promise of high yield is a promise of risk that someone else is carrying, and a run on an uninsured lender is fast because depositors have no reason to wait. The crypto market spent the summer learning the lesson that banks learned in the nineteenth century, and the customers of Celsius learned it in a single weekend.
