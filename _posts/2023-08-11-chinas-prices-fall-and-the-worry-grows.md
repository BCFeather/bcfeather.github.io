---
title: "China's prices fall and the worry grows"
date: 2023-08-11 22:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Consumer prices in China fell 0.3 per cent in the year to July 2023, the first decline since 2021, while producer prices fell for a tenth month, adding to concerns that the economy is sliding towards deflation."
---

The statistics office reported on 9 August that consumer prices had fallen 0.3 per cent in the year to July, the first annual decline since February 2021, and that producer prices at the factory gate were 4.4 per cent lower than a year earlier, the tenth consecutive monthly fall. Food prices were the main reason for the headline number, and pork, the item that moves the index more than any other, was cheaper by a quarter. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, was 0.8 per cent, the weakest reading in years.

Deflation is a word economists use carefully and markets use loosely. Falling prices sound pleasant to a shopper, and they are corrosive to a borrower, because a debt fixed in nominal terms grows heavier as the prices around it fall. Japanese experience since the 1990s is the reference point that every analyst in Beijing reaches for, and the comparison is now being made in public by officials who would rather avoid it. The government's own economists have started to talk about the risk of a balance-sheet recession, a phrase borrowed from Tokyo.

The weakness is broader than one month of prices. Exports fell 14.5 per cent in July, the sharpest drop since 2020, imports fell 12.4 per cent, and lending to households and firms slowed. Property investment has been falling for more than a year, and the developers that build it are defaulting one after another. The youth unemployment rate, which the government stopped publishing in June after it reached a record, has become a number that cannot be cited and cannot be ignored.

Policy has been incremental. The central bank cut two policy rates on 15 August, the second reduction in three months, and it has been injecting cash into the banking system every week of the summer, and the government has promised support for small firms, consumer spending and the property market. Officials have described the measures as targeted and have declined to announce a large stimulus, which economists outside the country have been urging for months. The reluctance reflects a belief in Beijing that debt-financed growth creates the problems it is meant to solve.

Prices tell the story that output does not. An economy growing at five per cent with falling prices is an economy where supply exceeds demand, and where factories produce goods that nobody is borrowing to buy. Households have responded to a decade of rising property prices and then falling ones by saving more, and those savings stay in deposit accounts instead of becoming spending. Retail sales grew by 2.5 per cent in July, the weakest pace of the year.

Growth this year is officially targeted at around five per cent, and the second half will have to be stronger than the first to reach it. A deflationary quarter makes that harder, and the tools available are the ones that have been used before with limited effect, which is why several foreign banks have cut their forecasts for the year to below five per cent.
