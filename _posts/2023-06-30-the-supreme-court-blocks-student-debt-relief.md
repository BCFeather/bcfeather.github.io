---
title: "The Supreme Court blocks student debt relief"
date: 2023-06-30 21:00:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Supreme Court ruled on 30 June that the education department had no authority to cancel up to twenty thousand dollars of student debt for tens of millions of borrowers, ending a programme that had been blocked since last year and that would have cost about four hundred billion dollars."
---

In a ruling on 30 June, the Supreme Court held that the Biden administration's programme to cancel up to twenty thousand dollars of student loan debt for borrowers earning below a threshold lacked the legal authority to exist. The decision, by a vote of six to three along the court's usual lines, held that the education secretary could not use a law written after the September 11 attacks to forgive debts on the scale the programme proposed. The programme had been blocked since October, when a district court in Missouri stopped it, and the ruling ends it rather than sending it back.

The numbers behind the case are the reason it mattered. About forty-three million borrowers hold federal student loans, and the programme would have cancelled part of the balance for roughly twenty-six million of them, at an estimated cost of four hundred billion dollars over ten years. The government argued that the 2003 law allowed it to modify or waive the terms of loans during a national emergency, and that the pandemic was such an emergency. The majority held that the law's language about waiver concerned the administration of loans rather than their forgiveness, and that a programme of this size required the consent of Congress.

In dissent, the justice who has been the court's most consistent voice on administrative law argued that the government's reading was plausible and that the court had substituted its own judgment for the secretary's. It also pointed out that the case was brought by states whose own borrowers would have benefited, and that the plaintiffs had asked the court to prevent the federal government from helping people who were not before it. The political argument that followed the ruling was about the same thing, and it will be repeated in the election.

Practically, payments resume in October after a pause of more than three years, and the interest that accrued during the pause will be charged again. The administration has announced a temporary twelve-month on-ramp in which missed payments will not be reported to credit agencies, and it has opened a rule-making process for a separate programme that would reduce payments for low-income borrowers. Those measures are smaller than the programme the court struck down and are likely to face their own challenges.

It also settles how much a president can do without Congress. The programme was designed to be delivered by executive action because the votes did not exist for legislation, and the court has now said that the votes are required. That is a constitutional principle with a political cost, and it will shape what the next administration attempts. For the borrowers who had counted the relief into their household budgets, the ruling arrived on the last day of the month in which the first payments were due to restart.

A narrower programme will now go through the ordinary rule-making process, which will take a year and will be challenged in court. The borrowers who were told the debt would disappear have until October to find the first payment, and the pause that protected them is over.
