---
title: "The court leaves a pill where it was"
date: 2023-04-21 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Supreme Court issued an emergency stay on 21 April 2023 that kept mifepristone available under the rules the drug had operated under for seven years, freezing a lower court order that would have withdrawn its approval nationwide."
---

The Supreme Court acted on 21 April, eight days before a lower court's restrictions on mifepristone were due to take effect, and stayed the order while the appeals proceed. The effect is that the drug remains available under the conditions the Food and Drug Administration set in 2016 and expanded in 2021, including prescription by telemedicine and delivery by mail. Only two justices, Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito, said they would have let the restrictions take effect, and Alito wrote a short opinion explaining why. The order itself ran to a paragraph, was unsigned, and did not say when the court would hear the case.

It began in Amarillo, Texas, where a judge issued a ruling on 7 April that withdrew the approval the agency had granted in 2000 and questioned the safety of the drug. The judge stayed his own order for seven days to allow an appeal, the Fifth Circuit narrowed it the following week, and the government asked the Supreme Court to intervene. The sequence took two weeks and produced three rulings in three courts.

Argument is about the power of a single district judge over a national drug approval. The plaintiffs argued that the agency had exceeded its authority in 2000 and again in 2016 when it relaxed the rules, and that the court could remedy that by suspending the approval. The government argued that no court had ever withdrawn an approval and that allowing one to do so would unsettle every drug on the market. The government's brief described the order below as unprecedented in the history of the agency, and the plaintiffs described it as the correction of an error that had stood for twenty-three years.

The stakes for patients were immediate. Mifepristone is used in about half of the abortions in the United States, and the combination with misoprostol is the standard method in the first ten weeks. Clinics in states where abortion remains legal had begun to prepare for a supply interruption, and several said they would switch to a misoprostol-only regimen that is less effective and more painful. Some clinics said they would send patients to hospitals, and the hospitals said they had no room.

Politics ran in opposite directions. Republican attorneys general in a group of states had asked the court to let the restrictions stand, and Democratic attorneys general in others had stockpiled the drug in anticipation of a ruling. The administration said it would defend the approval, and the manufacturer said it would continue to distribute while the litigation continued, which is what a stay means in practice.

What the order settled was the status quo and nothing else. The appeal will be heard in the Fifth Circuit and probably returned to the Supreme Court, and the merits of the approval will be examined in a case that has no precedent to guide it. A drug that has been on the market for twenty-three years is now the subject of a constitutional argument about who decides what a regulator may permit.
