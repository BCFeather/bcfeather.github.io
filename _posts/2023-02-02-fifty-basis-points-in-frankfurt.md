---
title: "Fifty basis points in Frankfurt, and a promise of another"
date: 2023-02-02 21:40:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "The European Central Bank raised its deposit rate to 3 per cent on 2 February 2023, its highest level since 2008, and said it intended to raise rates by another half point in March."
---

The European Central Bank raised all three of its policy rates by half a point on 2 February and said that it intended to do the same again at its meeting in March. The deposit rate went to 3 per cent, the refinancing rate to 3.5 per cent and the marginal lending rate to 3.75 per cent, levels last seen in 2008. The governing council also repeated that it would stop reinvesting maturing bonds in its asset purchase programme from the beginning of March.

A case for another half point rested on the inflation figures rather than on the vote. Euro area headline inflation had come in at 8.5 per cent in January on the first estimate, and core inflation, which excludes energy and food, had reached 5.2 per cent. Core had been rising for four months while headline fell, a pattern that the bank read as evidence that the energy shock was passing into wages and services.

Lagarde described the decision as a move into a different phase rather than a pause. She said the bank had covered a lot of ground and that there was more to cover, and she repeated that a half-point rise in March had been discussed and intended. She also conceded that the effect of the previous increases was only partly visible, which was the argument that some national central bankers had been making for a slower pace. Lagarde also said that the bank would not stop at a level it had decided in advance, which was a sentence the market chose to hear as a ceiling.

Money markets took the guidance seriously for once. The euro rose briefly, German two-year yields reached their highest since 2008, and the spread between Italian and German ten-year bonds widened to about 190 basis points. The widening was small by the standards of 2011, and the bank's transmission protection tool remained unused, which was the point of having it.

Banks were already adjusting to the other end of the policy. Lenders had repaid most of the four-year loans they had taken under the long-term refinancing operations, and credit growth to households and firms was slowing from the autumn. Energy prices had fallen far enough that the German government was preparing to cap the gas price for households from March, which would take some of the pressure off the index by the summer. Its survey of lenders, published at the end of January, showed credit standards tightening for the first time in seven years.

Sequencing was the problem the bank could not solve. Tightening into a war economy that had already cut real incomes meant that the second round of inflation would be fought at the expense of output, and the winter had been mild enough to postpone that argument. By the summer the argument would be about growth rather than prices, and the bank would be asked to explain why it had promised another half point in February.
