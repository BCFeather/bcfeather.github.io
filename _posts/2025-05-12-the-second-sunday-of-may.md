description: "Mother's Day arrived on the eleventh of May this year, carried by the same florists and the same group chats as every year. A short essay on what a fixed date does to a feeling that is otherwise ungovernable by the calendar."
---

The second Sunday of May arrives with a florist's window display and a group chat full of forwarded blessings, and it does the same thing every year: it turns a private feeling into a public appointment. The date is not Chinese and it is not ancient. It came in through advertising, stayed through convenience, and now it is a fixture, one of the few imported observances that has settled into the domestic calendar without argument.

The oddity is the formality. Nobody needs a fixed day to think about their mother, and the people who remember her every day are not the ones the day is for. The day is for the rest of us, the ones who are busy, who live in another city, who mean to call and do not. A calendar appointment exists precisely because the impulse is unreliable, and its value lies in interrupting the ordinary sequence of weeks in which nothing is said. A date on a calendar is a small mechanical thing, and small mechanical things are what most families actually run on.

The gift economy of the day is well understood and slightly embarrassing. The advertising starts a fortnight early, the price of a rose doubles, and the restaurants print a menu with four dishes and no choices. Flowers are bought at a markup, cards are written in three sentences, and restaurants fill for lunch. There is a version of this that is pure commerce and a version that is a genuine ritual, and they look identical from the outside. What distinguishes them is what happens after the meal, when the phone rings back and the conversation lasts longer than the appointment required.

For students and for anyone living away from home, the day has a particular weight. The distance is the point of the whole arrangement, and it means that the observance has to be conducted remotely: a call, a parcel, a photograph sent at the wrong hour because of the time difference. The people who left are the ones who celebrate the day most carefully, which is a reasonable summary of how families work.

There is also a version of the day that is difficult, and it is worth naming because the advertising never does. Not everyone has a mother to call, and not everyone had a childhood that the cards describe. A fixed date makes absence louder, the way anniversaries do. The florists do not have a window for that, and the group chats do not have a message for it, so it goes unmarked in public and sits quietly in the private calendar.

What the day actually achieves, in the end, is modest and useful. It makes one phone call happen that would otherwise have been postponed, and it gives a name to an obligation that everyone already has. The feeling is not manufactured by the date; the date simply gives it an occasion. That is enough, and it is more than most calendar entries manage. The bar is low, and the day clears it every year.
