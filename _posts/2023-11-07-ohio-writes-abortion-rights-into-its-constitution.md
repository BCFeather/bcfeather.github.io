---
title: "Ohio writes abortion rights into its constitution"
date: 2023-11-07 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Ohio voters approved Issue 1 on 7 November 2023 by 57 per cent to 43, adding a right to reproductive freedom to the state constitution and extending a run of ballot victories for abortion rights since the reversal of Roe v Wade."
---

Ohio voters approved a constitutional amendment protecting abortion and other reproductive care on 7 November, by a margin of about 57 per cent to 43, with more than two million votes in favour. The measure, known as Issue 1, was the only statewide question on the ballot that dealt directly with reproductive rights, and its passage made Ohio the seventh state to have written such a protection into its constitution since the Supreme Court reversed Roe v Wade in 2022.

The amendment's language was drafted to survive a court. It establishes a right for an individual to make decisions about reproductive health, including abortion, contraception and fertility treatment, and it allows the state to regulate abortion after the point of fetal viability, generally around twenty-two to twenty-four weeks, provided the regulation does not burden the right. It also permits limits that serve the health of the patient. Opponents argued that the wording would invalidate parental consent rules, waiting periods and licensing standards, and the state's attorney general campaigned against it.

Money and advertising gave the campaign a national character. Groups on both sides raised tens of millions of dollars, most of it from outside Ohio, and advertisements ran for weeks on television in Columbus, Cleveland and Cincinnati. Turnout was higher than in a typical off-year election, with about 3.9 million votes cast, and the yes vote ran ahead in the cities and in several suburban counties that had voted for Republicans in 2022.

A pattern continued. Since 2022, voters have approved abortion-rights measures in California, Vermont, Michigan, Kansas, Kentucky, and in Ohio itself in August, when they rejected a proposal that would have raised the threshold for constitutional amendments to sixty per cent. That August vote was widely read as a signal about the November question, and the organizers of both campaigns treated it as a rehearsal.

Courts will decide the practical consequences. A six-week ban that had been blocked by a state judge while the amendment campaign ran was still under review, and supporters of the new amendment say it now prevents such a ban from taking effect. Opponents have said they will challenge the amendment's application to specific regulations, and the state legislature has signalled that it may test the boundaries with new rules on clinics and on medication.

For the national picture, Ohio adds a Republican-leaning state to the list of places where direct democracy has protected access, and it gives both parties a template. Democrats will point to the result as evidence that the issue still motivates their voters a year before a presidential election. Republicans will point to the same numbers as evidence that their position needs to change, or that the question should stay out of federal politics, and the two readings will shape the campaigns of 2024. Turnout in the state's largest counties ran well above the 2022 midterm figure, and the share of voters under thirty who cast a ballot rose, a detail that both sides cited afterwards as evidence about an off-year contest that had become a national proxy.
