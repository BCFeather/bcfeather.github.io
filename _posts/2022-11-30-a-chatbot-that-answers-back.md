The announcement was made on the company's blog on 30 November and it was unusually modest in tone. OpenAI described ChatGPT as a sibling model to InstructGPT, trained to follow instructions in a prompt and produce a detailed response. It described the release as a research preview, said that usage would be free during that period, and asked users for feedback on its strengths and weaknesses. There was no launch event, no list of corporate customers and no price. The company had spent the previous two years publishing research on language models, and the blog post linked to a paper rather than to a product page.

What the company had built was a chat interface in front of a language model, with a system that kept track of the conversation. The model had been trained on a very large amount of text, then tuned by people who ranked its answers, so that it learned to reply in the shape of a helpful assistant rather than to continue the internet's sentences. The interface was plain, with a box at the bottom of the screen and a column of messages above it, and a warning that the answers might be wrong.

What drew people in was the way the thing behaved in ordinary conversation. It could draft a letter, explain a piece of code, summarize a long document, argue a case, and produce a plausible poem in a requested form. It could also invent a citation, miss the point of a question, and answer with great confidence about things it did not know. Both behaviours were reported in the first week, often by the same people. The model had no memory beyond the window of the conversation, and it could not consult anything the user had not pasted into it.

By the middle of December the transcripts were everywhere. Teachers were reading essays that had been written in ten seconds, programmers were using it to explain errors in their own code, and journalists were asking it questions about itself. The company's servers were under strain for days, and the queue for a new account grew longer than the queue for a new phone. Universities began to rewrite their rules on essays within weeks, and some of them went back to examinations held in rooms with paper.

Nobody could agree on whether the tool would make students lazier or make the work of writing clearer. Some of those rules were written in a week, and several universities discovered that their examination regulations said nothing about a machine that could write.

The company had described a research preview, and the world outside treated it as a product launch, which is what happens when a demonstration turns out to be useful. What the release demonstrated was something narrower than thought: that a great deal of white-collar work consists of producing a first draft. What it changed first was the price of a first draft, which fell to nothing in a single afternoon.
