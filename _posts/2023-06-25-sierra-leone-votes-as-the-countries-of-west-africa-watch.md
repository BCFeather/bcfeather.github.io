---
title: "Sierra Leone votes as the countries of West Africa watch"
date: 2023-06-25 19:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Sierra Leone held a general election on 24 June with more than three million registered voters, as President Julius Maada Bio sought a second term against Samura Kamara and a region unsettled by recent coups watched the count."
---

Sierra Leone voted on 24 June in a general election that was held alongside parliamentary and local contests, with more than three million people registered to cast a ballot. President Julius Maada Bio, who won in 2018 in a runoff, sought a second term against Samura Kamara of the All People's Congress, the party that governed for most of the country's post-independence history. The campaign was dominated by the cost of living, by youth unemployment and by the question of whether the electoral commission could run a count that the losing side would accept.

Economics set a severe backdrop. Sierra Leone is one of the poorest countries in the world by most measures, and the shock of higher food and fuel prices that followed the war in Ukraine pushed inflation above forty per cent and eroded the incomes of the urban households that decide elections. The government has been negotiating with the International Monetary Fund and has cut fuel subsidies, which made the price of transport a campaign issue. The country's iron ore exports, which had driven a period of growth before 2015, have not recovered, and the economy remains dependent on agriculture and on remittances.

Regional politics shaped the campaign as much as the domestic one. West Africa has seen a series of coups since 2020, in Mali, Guinea, Burkina Faso and Niger, and the leaders of the Economic Community of West African States have responded with sanctions and with threats of intervention. Sierra Leone has a history of military rule, and both parties have an interest in presenting themselves as the guarantee of civilian government. The electoral commission has been criticized for its handling of the register, and the opposition has said it will reject a result that does not match its own tally.

Observers from the African Union, the Commonwealth and the European Union were deployed, and their assessments were expected to be the most detailed account of how the vote was conducted. The turnout and the count were the two things that mattered: a low turnout in the capital would favour the incumbent's rural base, and a slow count would create the space in which disputes grow. The commission said it would publish results within four days, which is fast by regional standards and which puts pressure on the count itself.

Whatever the result, the election is a test of whether a poor country with a recent civil war and a fragile economy can hold a contest that the loser accepts. Sierra Leone's last transfer of power, in 2018, was peaceful, and that is the asset the country has spent twenty years accumulating. The campaign was rough and the rhetoric was heated, and the count will be watched by every government in the region that has an interest in proving that elections still work.

A four-day timetable is the tightest in the region, and both parties have said they will accept the result only if their own agents see the tally sheets. The count will be finished before the weekend, and the country will know by then whether its record of peaceful transfers survives.
