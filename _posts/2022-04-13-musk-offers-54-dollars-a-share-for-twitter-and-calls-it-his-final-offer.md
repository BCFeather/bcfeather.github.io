Elon Musk sent a letter to Twitter's board on 13 April proposing to buy the rest of the company at 54 dollars and 20 cents a share in cash, valuing Twitter at about 43 billion dollars. He made the letter public the next morning, described it as his best and final offer, and said that if it were rejected he would need to reconsider his position as a shareholder.

Terms were unusual. The bid was unsolicited, it assumed no financing partner, and it carried a premium of roughly 38 per cent over the closing price on 1 April, the last trading day before his nine per cent stake became public, but only about eight per cent over the price the shares had already reached after the disclosure. Most of the takeover premium had been paid to the market by the disclosure itself, which is why the board could argue that the offer undervalued a company whose shares had traded well above that level a year earlier.

The letter made a case before it made a number. Musk argued that Twitter was a digital town square, that its moderation rules had undermined the speech it was supposed to protect, and that a private owner could fix the product and the culture. The argument appealed to his own 80 million followers and to a wing of the company's users, and it rested on a view of the platform's public role that his stake made plausible. The price also sat below the ten per cent line at which American disclosure rules tighten around a holder, which is a detail a bidder notices.

Twitter's board responded on 15 April by adopting a shareholder rights plan with a 15 per cent trigger, the defensive measure known as a poison pill. The plan gave other holders the right to buy discounted shares if any investor crossed that threshold without board approval, which made the accumulation of a hostile position much more expensive. The board said its purpose was to preserve the company's ability to make an orderly decision, and its advisers, Goldman Sachs and J.P. Morgan, were retained within the week while the largest outside holders were briefed on the mechanics.

That pill did not end the argument, and it was never meant to. Musk could still negotiate, go hostile at a lower price, or take the dispute to the shareholders, and the filings over the following week showed him lining up commitments and dropping the conditions about financing and due diligence that had accompanied the proposal. The structure came to 21 billion dollars of his own equity, 12.5 billion of margin loans secured against Tesla shares and bank debt, which made the bid personal and leveraged at once.

This episode is best understood as a contest about price rather than about speech. The premium looked large against a share price that had fallen for a year, and small against the one his own disclosure had created. The board's defensive move was the only instrument that could hold the difference in place while it negotiated, and both sides knew it. The gap between the two valuations, some four billion dollars of value created by the disclosure and then offered back as a premium, is the part of the story that shareholders noticed first.
