---
title: "The night shift that saves the afternoon"
date: 2024-07-10 21:00:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "A factory that moves its furnaces to the night pays less for the power and leaves the afternoon capacity to the air conditioners. The saving is shared between the plant and the grid."
---

A factory that moves its furnaces to the night pays less for the power. Bands make up the tariff. Between midnight and eight in the morning the power is the cheapest of the four bands. A plant that can shift its load shifts it. The saving is not small. A furnace that runs for six hours at full power costs a third less at night than in the afternoon. A plant with a hundred workers on the night shift saves enough to pay their allowance twice over.

The saving runs to real money. Air conditioners in the cities set the afternoon peak in July. A factory that removes itself from that peak removes the need for a power station that would run for forty hours a year. Avoided cost is larger than the discount. The workers pay part of the price. A night shift disrupts sleep. A rotating roster disrupts it more. The plants that run a fixed night crew report fewer accidents than the plants that rotate, which is the reason the allowance exists.

Heat is easier at night. A workshop in July is ten degrees cooler after midnight. A furnace that is cooled by air is cheaper to run. Comfort is a second saving that the accounts do not show. The load is metered in quarter hours. A plant that starts its furnace at seven in the evening is recorded as part of the evening peak until eleven, which is why the schedule is set for eleven in the evening rather than seven.

Province publishes the list of plants that have signed the agreement. Several hundred names are on it. Total capacity on it is enough to cover a city of a million people for an hour. The agreement is a contract rather than a rule. A plant that breaks it loses the discount for the month, and a plant that keeps it receives a payment at the end of the year. Enforcement is done by the meter, which is why the scheme works.

Tariffs are published a year ahead so that a plant can plan. A manager who wants to move a furnace buys the schedule and works out the difference for each hour of the day. Simple is the calculation, and the decision is not, because the crew has to agree.

Night shifts carry a social cost that the balance sheet ignores. A worker who starts at eleven in the evening cannot take a child to school in the morning. A plant in a county town recruits from the villages around it, and a village that loses its evenings loses its football team. The pilot scheme pays a plant for a capacity that it does not use, which is a contract that an accountant finds harder to explain than a discount. The demand response has grown every year since the first pilot. The next step is to include the households, which means a smart meter, a signal, and a payment for a thermostat that goes up by a degree. Technology exists and the market does not yet.
