---
title: "Musk discloses a 9.1 per cent stake in Twitter and the shares gain 27 per cent"
date: 2022-04-04 23:45:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "A filing revealed that the world's richest person had quietly bought seventy-three and a half million shares, and the reaction was immediate: the stock closed nearly a third higher than the day before."
---

A regulatory filing on 4 April disclosed that Elon Musk had accumulated 73.5 million shares of Twitter, about 9.1 per cent of the company, worth roughly three billion dollars at the prices he had been paying through the winter. The stock closed at 49.97 dollars, up 27 per cent from 39.31 the previous session, a move that company filings later cited as the market's own assessment of what his involvement might mean.

The buying began in late January and continued in steady blocks through February and March, executed through a trust in his name and routed through a wealth manager who had been told to keep the position below five per cent. The stake was assembled in open-market purchases rather than a block trade, which kept the price from moving until the news broke. The instruction did not survive the spring: at the start of March the manager was authorized to buy past that line, and the threshold was crossed in the middle of the month.

Twitter's board answered by offering him a seat, on the condition that he hold no more than 14.9 per cent of the company. That cap is the standard standstill used to keep a large shareholder from turning into a bidder without warning, and it was announced with the board's blessing on 5 April, which is why the offer looked like a settlement rather than a confrontation. Twitter's shares had traded below 40 dollars for most of the winter, so the position had been cheap to build.

The market read the stake as the opening move of something larger, and the reading was reasonable. Musk had used the platform as his primary megaphone for a decade, had more than 80 million followers, and had spent months criticizing its moderation as an attack on free speech. A holder with nine per cent and a board seat can press for changes in product and policy without making a tender offer, and the price implied that investors expected him to try. Index funds and retail buyers followed the filing, and volume that day ran at several times the average.

Twitter's own position was awkward. The company had argued for years that its rules applied to everyone, and it now had to decide whether its largest shareholder could campaign, on its own platform, for changes at the company that owned the platform. Independent directors began examining what the standstill permitted, and their deliberations were reported within days of the filing. Employees were asking in internal channels what a board seat would mean for moderation policy, and nobody in the building had an answer.

What followed is history. Within ten days he had offered to buy the company outright, and within three weeks the board had accepted, which makes the filing on 4 April the moment when a passive holding became a campaign. Disclosure rules exist to give the market time to absorb news of that weight, and this market absorbed it in a single session, with the closing print almost exactly at the level where the stake became public.
