Museums filled up this summer, and the booking system that most of them adopted has become the thing visitors talk about most. A large provincial museum now releases its free tickets on a fixed day, they are taken within minutes, and the visitors who did not get one arrive at the door anyway and stand in a line that the staff manage with a rope and a sign. The line is not a failure of the museum. It is a description of the demand.

The demand is partly a fashion and partly a change in what a family does with a hot afternoon, and the two are hard to separate. A museum has air conditioning, no admission charge, and a room where a child can be walked for two hours. That combination is close to unbeatable in August, and the institutions that have understood this have organized their summer programming around it, with late openings, guided walks and a café that stays open until nine.

The ticketing is the awkward part. A free ticket that must be reserved in advance is a rationing device, and rationing devices always allocate according to something. In this case it allocates according to information and time, which favours people who know the release date and can sit at a screen at ten in the morning. The visitors who arrive on a train from another city and did not know the rule are the ones left outside, and they are usually the ones for whom the visit was a bigger effort.

Several museums have tried to solve this with a split system. Half the tickets are released in advance, half are held for the door on the day. The split is administratively annoying and it produces the crowd outside, and it also produces the only thing that resembles fairness, since a person who queued at seven in the morning has the same chance as a person who booked from home. The queue is the price of that fairness.

The exhibitions themselves have changed to suit the new audience, and the change is visible in the lighting. A room built for study has a glare that ruins a photograph, and the newer galleries have solved it, at the cost of a room that is easier to photograph than to study in. Shows are designed to be photographed, because a photograph is how the visit is shared, and a show that produces no photograph circulates less. The curators who complain about this are also the ones who use the images to argue for their next budget, and the tension is not resolvable.

What the summer shows is that free admission worked. The institutions are full, the audience is younger than it was a decade ago, and the queue is a problem of success. The next problem is the one that every free public service meets, which is that the demand is larger than the capacity and the capacity is decided by a budget rather than by the number of people who want to come in.
