---
title: "The code the buildings were built to"
date: 2023-02-16 22:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The earthquake in southern Turkey exposed the gap between a modern building code adopted in 2018 and the enforcement of it, which had been weakened by amnesties for unpermitted construction."
---

Turkey had a modern seismic code when the earthquake struck, and the code had been rewritten in 2018 to the standards used in California and Japan. Buildings designed and inspected under it were expected to survive an event of the size that occurred, and a number of them did. The problem was the stock that had been built before 2018 and the proportion of the newer stock that had been signed off by inspectors who were paid by the developer.

Between the code and the site lay the subject of the first engineering reports. Investigators found concrete with too much water and aggregate, reinforcement that had been placed at the wrong depth, and columns that had been shortened by renovations. Those failures are cumulative rather than singular, and they explain why some buildings in the same street collapsed while others stayed upright with damage that could be repaired. The reports found reinforcement laid at the wrong depth and columns shortened by a later renovation, failures that combine rather than stand alone.

Amnesties were the political part of the failure. Successive governments had passed laws allowing owners to register buildings that had been put up without permits, in exchange for a fee, and millions of structures had been legalized that way. The amnesty did not weaken the structures themselves, but it removed them from the inspection system and it created a record in which a building was compliant because it had been registered rather than because it had been checked.

Enforcement was difficult because of how the industry was organized. Most of the housing in the affected provinces had been built by small contractors working on margins of a few per cent, and the inspection firms were licensed by the same municipalities that collected the fees. A developer who wanted a certificate could shop for an inspector, and the penalties for a false certificate were smaller than the savings from cheaper concrete. Inspection firms were licensed by the same municipalities that collected the fees, which is the arrangement that makes a certificate cheap.

Reconstruction within a year was the promise the government made after the quake, alongside a higher standard for the new housing. Independent engineers welcomed the promise and noted that the previous twelve years had produced similar promises, and that the rebuilding would be done by the same firms under the same supervision. Whether Turkey was willing to pay for the checking mattered more than whether it knew how to build safely.

The cost of that gap is now a number that will be counted for years. Official estimates of the damage reached tens of billions of dollars, and the reconstruction programme was financed with borrowing and with a special tax. The engineers who had warned about the amnesties before the quake received no invitation to the meetings where the new programme was designed, and their absence was the most telling detail of the response. That rebuilding was financed with borrowing and with a levy, and the firms that had put up the collapsed blocks were among those that won the contracts.
