---
title: "A pipe with smoke in it lands near the prime minister"
date: 2023-04-16 22:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "A man threw an explosive device at Japan's prime minister on 15 April 2023 at a fishing port in Wakayama, a year after a similar attack killed Shinzo Abe, and the incident produced no deaths and a long argument about the security of election campaigns."
---

Fumio Kishida was about to speak at the Saikazaki fishing port in Wakayama on 15 April when a cylindrical object landed at his feet. It exploded with smoke and a bang, the prime minister was pulled away by his security detail, and police officers tackled a twenty-four-year-old man from Hyogo prefecture who had thrown it. Kishida was unhurt, one officer was slightly injured, and the speech was cancelled.

Ryuji Kimura, twenty-four and from Hyogo prefecture, was arrested at the scene and charged with attempted murder, among other offences. Reports in the days afterwards described a man who had tried to run for the upper house in 2022 and had sued the government over the eligibility rules that excluded him, and who had told investigators that he wanted to draw attention to his case. A court later ordered a psychiatric evaluation, and the case took two years to reach a verdict.

Comparison with Shinzo Abe was made within an hour. Abe was shot in Nara in July 2022 while speaking in the open air, and the review that followed found gaps in the coordination between the national police and the local officers who were closest to him. The device used in Wakayama was cruder and the response faster, and the result was an incident rather than a murder, which is the difference that the reviews could point to.

Campaign security became the subject of the week. Japan was in the middle of by-elections for the lower house, and the parties that had been speaking at train stations and shopping arcades began to use barriers and metal detectors. The chief cabinet secretary said that the police would review arrangements for the summit that Japan was to host in Hiroshima in May, where the leaders of the Group of Seven would appear in public. Officers were issued with hand-held detectors, and parties began checking bags at entrances that had never had a door.

Deeper question was about the space between a politician and a crowd. Japanese election law protects street campaigning, and the tradition of a candidate standing on a box with a microphone at a station exit is one of the few places where voters can question a leader directly. The measures taken after July 2022 had already pushed candidates behind glass and behind rope lines, and Wakayama moved the argument further towards distance, where a voter who once shook a candidate's hand waves from behind a barrier.

For Kishida the incident interrupted a difficult spring. His approval rating had fallen below thirty per cent over a series of scandals involving his party's connections to a religious organization, and the by-elections on 23 April left the governing party holding four of the five seats, which was better than the polls had predicted and worse than the party had expected. The smoke bomb gave him a day of sympathy and no change in the arithmetic, and the photograph of the prime minister being hurried away became the image of a government that was, in several senses, under siege.
