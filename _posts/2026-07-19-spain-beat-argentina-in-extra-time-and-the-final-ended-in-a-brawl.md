Spain won the World Cup for the second time on 19 July, beating Argentina 1-0 after extra time at MetLife Stadium outside New York, and the goal that decided it came from Ferran Torres in the 106th minute. The match had gone goalless through ninety minutes, with Spain holding the ball and Argentina waiting for the counter that had carried it through three knockout rounds. It is Spain's first title since 2010, and the first final to be decided in extra time since 2014. The stadium held more than eighty thousand people, and the crowd was split between the two countries' supporters. The trophy was presented on a pitch that still had stewards on it.

Tactically the game was a contest between two ideas about how to win a tournament. Spain pressed with a high line and kept Argentina's midfield chasing the ball, while Argentina sat deep and tried to release its forwards into the space behind. Neither side created much in the first hour, and the chances that did come fell to Spain, whose midfield controlled the tempo without converting it into shots. A final decided by one goal in extra time tends to be remembered for the goal and for whatever follows it.

Conduct after the whistle is what the tournament will remember. Spanish substitutes ran onto the pitch to celebrate, and Argentina's Molina was seen striking Rodri in the abdomen, which set off a scuffle that spread to both benches. Paredes was then seen grabbing Garcia by the throat and pushing Gavi, and stewards took several minutes to separate the groups. Argentina's captain apologized in a television interview and said the squad had let the occasion carry it away.

FIFA opened a disciplinary case on 21 July and referred the incidents to its disciplinary committee, which said it would examine whether the players had breached the disciplinary code. The case expanded at the end of the month when the committee opened proceedings against the Argentine federation as well as against individuals. Sanctions published in August banned Paredes for ten matches, Molina for seven and Gavi for one. The committee's decisions are subject to appeal, and the players' clubs were told before publication.

Argentine reaction at home was severe. Commentators described the team as having lost the match and the argument, and the federation issued an apology that stopped short of naming the players involved. The coach said the pressure of a final and the disappointment of a defeat had produced a reaction that nobody in the squad was proud of.

Format of this tournament explains part of the intensity. It was the first World Cup with forty-eight teams and a hundred and four matches, and the winner played eight games rather than seven in a month that stretched across three countries. A longer tournament produces more fatigue, more travel and more chances for a match to end in a way that the organizers have to explain afterwards. The next tournament expands again, to forty-eight teams across three continents, which is a calendar question the federation has not settled.
