title: "The tree planting day and the arithmetic of a forest"
date: 2025-03-12 19:20:00 +0800
categories: [environment]
description: "The twelfth of March is the tree planting day, and the country has planted an extraordinary number of trees in the last four decades. The arithmetic of what those trees do is more complicated than the arithmetic of how many were planted."
---

The twelfth of March is the tree planting day, a date chosen because it is the anniversary of the death of Sun Yat-sen, who argued a century ago that a country with a broken landscape could not be a rich one. Schoolchildren plant saplings, companies photograph their staff with spades, and the statistics that accompany the day are always impressive, a few million mu added to the forest area in the year, a percentage of coverage that has risen for forty years.

The coverage figure is real and it is also a definition. A forest in the statistics is an area with a certain density of trees, and the density threshold matters, because a hillside planted with a single species at wide spacing counts as forest in the inventory and does not behave like a forest in a watershed. The distinction between a plantation and a wood is the distinction that foresters argue about, and it is not a quibble, because the two hold water, hold soil and hold species in very different quantities.

What the plantations have done is visible from the air. The shelter belts in the north, planted since the 1970s to slow the dust, have changed the colour of the land in a band several hundred kilometres wide. The sand that used to move has, in many places, stopped moving, and the villages behind the belts have kept their fields. The cost of that achievement is a landscape that depends on a single species in rows, which is vulnerable to a pest or a drought in a way that a mixed wood is not.

The water accounting is the harder part. Trees transpire, and a plantation in a dry region uses water that the river below it also needs. The research on the catchments where large-scale planting was carried out finds reduced streamflow in several of them, and the effect is strongest in the areas where the trees grow fastest. The policy response has been to shift the planting towards native species and towards the places where the water balance is favourable, which is a slower and less photogenic programme.

The carbon accounting is the newest and the most contested, and it is the one that the reporting obligations have made urgent. A plantation accumulates carbon while it grows and releases it when it is harvested or burned, and the net balance over a rotation depends on what the wood becomes, a beam in a building or a fire in a stove. The inventories that the country reports to the climate convention count the stock, and the stock is real, and the flow is what the atmosphere responds to, which is why a forest that is harvested and replanted is not the same as a forest left standing.

What the day is good for is the argument about all of this, which happens in the forestry journals and rarely in the ceremonies. A tree planted is a photograph and a forest established is a thirty-year commitment, and the second is the one that the arithmetic rewards.
