---
title: "The Senate passes the debt-ceiling bill"
date: 2023-06-01 21:20:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Senate voted 63 to 36 on 1 June to suspend the federal debt limit until January 2025, sending to the president the compromise that ended months of brinkmanship and averted a default that the Treasury had dated to 5 June."
---

The Senate voted 63 to 36 on 1 June to suspend the federal debt limit until January 2025, ending a standoff that had occupied Congress since January and had begun to unsettle the Treasury market. The bill, negotiated between the White House and the speaker of the House, suspends the borrowing cap rather than raising it by a fixed amount, and pairs that with two years of discretionary spending caps and a handful of procedural changes to energy permitting and to work requirements for food aid. President Biden said he would sign it before the Treasury's projected exhaustion date of 5 June.

Mechanics mattered as much as politics. A suspension means the government can borrow whatever it needs for the next nineteen months without another vote, and that removes the recurring threat from the calendar until after the next presidential election. The spending caps are modest in the first year and looser in the second, with a side deal that lets Congress waive them under emergency designations, which is how both parties sold the compromise to their own members. The cost of the deal is measured in the spending that was held down rather than in the debt that was authorized.

Both parties split over the vote, which is the ordinary signature of a deal that nobody loves. Twenty-one Republican senators voted against it, along with four Democrats, and the objections came from opposite directions: that the cuts were too small to justify the borrowing, and that the work requirements would take food assistance from people who need it. The House had passed the bill the previous evening, and the speed of the final sequence was deliberate, because the Treasury had begun to run down its cash balance and had already used extraordinary measures to keep paying its bills.

Markets responded with relief rather than enthusiasm. Yields on short-dated bills that had traded at a premium during the worst of the uncertainty fell back towards the rest of the curve, and the rating agencies, which had warned about the standoff, said they would keep watching. The episode did not resolve the underlying arithmetic, in which the deficit is large, the interest bill is rising, and neither party has a plan for the trust funds that are heading towards insolvency in the next decade. What it resolved was the question of whether the United States would pay its existing obligations on time.

That question is the one that matters to everyone else. A default would have been felt first in the repo market and in the money funds that hold Treasury paper as though it were cash, and then in every borrowing cost in the world, because the American bond is the collateral on which the global financial system is built. The bill removes that risk until 2025, which is a long time in politics and a short time in fiscal policy. The next fight is already scheduled, and the only thing this week changed was the date on which it begins.
