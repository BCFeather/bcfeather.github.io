title: "Eighteen hundred variants among half a million people"
date: 2023-01-19 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "Two studies published in Nature on 19 January 2023 reported the analysis of genome and health data from 224,737 Finnish participants, identifying 1,838 genetic variants associated with 681 diseases."
---

Two papers in Nature on 19 January reported the largest genetic association study yet conducted in a single national population, using genome and health data from 224,737 people in Finland. The studies identified 1,838 genetic variants associated with 681 diseases, including 702 associations involving low-frequency variants that the authors described as likely to be new. The project is a partnership between universities, hospitals, the national institute for health and welfare and three pharmaceutical companies, and it began in 2017.

The population is the reason the study was possible. Finland was settled in stages, and the settlers who moved north and east carried a subset of the variation present elsewhere, so that certain variants are far more common there than in the rest of Europe and others are absent. The country also keeps registries of diagnoses, prescriptions and deaths that go back decades, and linking them to a biobank is a matter of law rather than of persuasion. The country's genetic structure is the result of migrations that happened over the last few thousand years, and the variants that arrived with them are still visible in the genomes of people whose ancestors stayed in one region.

FinnGen, which produced the data, had been built for this purpose. Participants gave consent once, and the analysis linked their genotypes to every hospital visit and every reimbursed prescription in the national systems, which turns a country of five and a half million people into an epidemiological cohort with unusually complete follow-up. The consent covers the whole register, which is a legal construction that most countries cannot reproduce because their health records sit with insurers rather than with the state.

The findings included rare variants with large effects on common diseases, which is the class of variant that drug developers most want, and a set of recessive conditions that are easier to detect in an isolated population because the carriers are more numerous. Several of the associations pointed at genes that had no previous link to the disease in question. The papers also describe variants that change the way a protein is made, which is the kind of finding a drug company can act on within a decade.

Both papers stated the limits of the design. A population that is genetically homogeneous makes rare variants easier to find and makes the findings harder to generalize, because a variant that matters in Finland may be absent or common elsewhere. The phenotypes came from registries, which means they are as good as the coding practices of the clinicians who filled them in. The authors also noted that the population is small enough that some of the rarer associations rest on a handful of cases.

A small population with good records is a laboratory that cannot be built. The papers are a demonstration of what a country can learn about itself when it decides, decades earlier, to keep the receipts. The next step is to repeat the analysis in other biobanks, and the first results from Estonia and the United Kingdom suggest that some findings travel and some do not.
