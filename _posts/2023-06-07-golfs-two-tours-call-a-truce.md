---
title: "Golf's two tours call a truce"
date: 2023-06-07 17:50:00 +0800
categories: [sport]
description: "The PGA Tour, the DP World Tour and Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund announced on 6 June that they would merge their commercial interests, ending two years of litigation and a split that had divided the sport."
---

The PGA Tour, the DP World Tour and the Public Investment Fund, the Saudi sovereign wealth fund that bankrolls LIV Golf, announced on 6 June that they would combine their commercial businesses into a single entity. The agreement ends the antitrust litigation that the tour had filed against LIV and the dispute that had split the men's game since the rival circuit began signing its players in 2021. The announcement came with no explanation of how the new entity would be governed, and the commissioner of the PGA Tour, Jay Monahan, said only that the tour would control the board and hold a majority of the voting rights.

Disclosed terms are a framework rather than a contract. The three parties said they would create a for-profit company, with the tour's existing television and sponsorship business inside it, and that the fund would be a minority investor. LIV was given a place on the board and a promise that its events would continue, at least for the remainder of the season. Player contracts, ranking points and the eligibility rules that had been rewritten to cope with the rival circuit were left to be worked out.

For the players who had turned the offer down, the announcement was a shock. Several of those who stayed with the tour had been paid loyalty bonuses and had turned down guaranteed money, and they learned of the deal from a news conference rather than from the tour. The players who had left for LIV had received signing fees reportedly in the hundreds of millions, and they now found themselves inside a structure run by the tour they had left. Neither group was told what the new entity would mean for their future earnings, and both said so loudly within hours.

Strategic logic is easy to follow. The fund had spent more than two billion dollars on a circuit that never made money, and the tour had spent a similar order of magnitude on legal fees, elevated purses and bonuses while its own finances tightened. Both sides had reason to stop, and the Saudi state has been patient about buying influence in sport, moving from football to golf and, more recently, into tennis and into the proposed breakaway football competitions. The deal converts a destructive rivalry into a seat at the table for the fund, which is the outcome it has pursued from the start.

Whether it is good for golf depends on details that do not yet exist. The tour has promised that the new company will run a schedule in which the best players meet more often, and that could produce the stronger regular competition the sport has lacked. It could also produce a closed shop in which the biggest names are compensated by an owner whose interests run beyond the tour. Players on both sides spent the week asking the same question, and until the board is appointed and the contracts are written, the honest answer is that nobody knows what they have agreed to.
