---
title: "A nanoparticle that carries an editor into the lungs"
date: 2023-03-30 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "Researchers reported in Nature Biotechnology on 30 March 2023 that an inhalable nanoparticle could deliver messenger RNA and a CRISPR editor to the lungs of mice, a step towards gene editing that is given as an aerosol rather than as an injection."
---

Gene editing has been limited by delivery as much as by the editing itself. The editor has to reach the right tissue, enter the right cells and do so without provoking an immune response, and the tools that work in a dish often fail in a body. A paper published in Nature Biotechnology on 30 March described a delivery system for the lungs, which is the tissue that an aerosol reaches most directly. The lung is also the tissue where an aerosol deposits most of its dose, which is why inhalers have been the standard treatment for asthma for fifty years.

The system is a polymer nanoparticle that carries messenger RNA encoding a CRISPR enzyme and a guide molecule, and it is formulated so that it can be inhaled as a mist. The team screened a library of polymers for their ability to deliver cargo to lung cells, and the screening was done in a way that allowed them to compare hundreds of candidates at once. The best candidates were then tested in mice, and the editing was measured in the tissue rather than in a surrogate. The polymer was designed to degrade within days, so that the editing machinery would be cleared rather than persist.

Results were reported as editing in the lungs that was specific and lasted long enough to matter for a disease. The team tested the system against a model of a genetic lung disease, and it reported a reduction in the target protein and an improvement in a functional measure. The editors were detected in the lung for a period measured in weeks, and the immune response was described as modest, which is the claim that previous delivery systems had failed to make.

The therapeutic target is the part that the paper is careful about. Most genetic lung diseases are caused by a single mutation and affect a fraction of the cells, and a delivery system that reaches a fraction of the cells might be enough for some of them and would not be for others. The authors also noted that the editing was partial, and that the long-term consequences of editing lung cells are unknown, which is the sentence that any clinical translation would have to test.

Behind the technique lies the third generation of a line of work. Lipid nanoparticles had made the messenger RNA vaccines possible, and the same chemistry had been adapted for editing in the liver, where an infusion reaches the organ directly. The lungs are harder to reach by infusion and easier to reach by inhalation, and the polymer approach was chosen because lipids had not worked well in the lung.

What the paper suggests is a route for diseases that are currently treated by repeated infusions. Cystic fibrosis, surfactant deficiencies and some inherited immune problems in the lung would all be candidates if the delivery could be made reliable in people, and the gap between mice and people is the one that the field has repeatedly failed to close. The result is a demonstration of a delivery route rather than a therapy, and the route is the part that had been missing.
