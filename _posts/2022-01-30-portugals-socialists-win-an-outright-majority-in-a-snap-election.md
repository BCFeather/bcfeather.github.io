title: "Portugal's Socialists win an outright majority in a snap election"
date: 2022-01-30 23:10:00 +0800

description: "Antonio Costa's party took about forty-one per cent of the vote and 117 of 230 seats, ending a decade in which it had governed with the support of parties to its left."
---

Portugal's Socialist Party won an outright majority in the snap election on 30 January, taking about 41 per cent of the vote and 117 of the 230 seats in the assembly, according to the count published that night. The result gives Antonio Costa a second consecutive term as prime minister and removes his dependence on the two far-left parties whose support had kept his minority government standing since 2019. The result ended the era in which the Socialists governed with the support of parties that wanted to spend more than the budget allowed.

Election had been called because that arrangement collapsed. The Communists and the Left Bloc had voted against the budget in the autumn, and the president dissolved the assembly in November. Costa campaigned on the argument that a minority government had become unstable in a pandemic, and the electorate gave him the majority that the constitution had made possible but that his allies had made unlikely.

Turnout was low by Portuguese standards and it favoured the winner. Abstention rose to about 42 per cent, and the parties that lost support were the smaller ones: the Left Bloc and the Communists fell back, the centre-right Social Democrats held second place with about 29 per cent, and the new far-right party, Chega, became the third force with about 7 per cent and a dozen seats. Costa had promised during the campaign to raise the minimum wage and to keep the fiscal deficit inside the euro-area limit, two promises that a majority makes easier to keep and harder to blame on anyone else. The budget that the assembly rejected had included a package of rail investment that his former partners had negotiated and then voted down, which left the projects in limbo until the new session opened.

Majority changes what the government can do. Costa had governed with the support of parties that demanded higher pensions and public wages, and the budget that brought them down had been written to satisfy them. With 117 seats he can legislate alone, and the constraint on spending shifts from coalition arithmetic to the rules of the euro area and to the inflation that had begun to lift prices across the union.

Portugal's election was one of several in Europe that winter in which a governing party did better than the polls had suggested, and the reason was the same in each: voters who had lived through two years of emergency measures rewarded the incumbents who had managed them. That effect would fade as energy prices rose, and it would be tested in France in April. The far-right party's rise had been the story of the campaign, and its dozen seats gave it a platform that the two mainstream parties had spent years denying it.

An absolute majority is a licence to govern alone, and it is also a licence to be blamed alone. Costa now owns the budget, the health service and the railway upgrades that had been negotiated with his former partners, and the parties that used to share the blame have been sent back to the opposition benches.
