---
title: "Blinken and Lavrov meet in Geneva and agree only to keep talking"
date: 2022-01-21 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "A week of talks in Geneva, Brussels and Vienna produced no agreement on Ukraine or on NATO's open door, and Washington promised a written reply to Moscow's demands within days."
---

Antony Blinken and Sergey Lavrov met in Geneva on 21 January for talks that lasted about an hour and a half, and left with no agreement beyond a commitment to continue. The American secretary of state said afterwards that the meeting had been candid and that the United States would put its response to Russia's written demands on paper within the following week, while his Russian counterpart said the talks had been useful and that Moscow would study the reply. By the summer the talks had resumed, and by then the question of the open door had answered itself on the ground.

The demands on the table were substantial. Russia had asked NATO to withdraw the promise, made in 2008, that Ukraine and Georgia would eventually join, to pull back forces and missiles from countries that joined after 1997, and to forgo military cooperation with Ukraine. The alliance had said in a series of meetings that month, in Geneva on 10 January, in Brussels on 12 January and in Vienna on 13 January, that it would not close its door to new members.

Russian build-up continued while the diplomacy ran. Estimates of the troops near Ukraine's borders put the number above a hundred thousand, and Russia announced exercises in Belarus, which put its forces within a short drive of Kyiv. American officials said publicly that an invasion could come within weeks, and they began to move equipment and advisers into Poland and Romania. The deployments were small and were described as rotational, and their political purpose was to reassure allies who doubted that the guarantee extended to them.

Western position rested on two arguments that fit together badly. One was that NATO's membership policy is a matter for the alliance and its applicants, which is a procedural claim. The other was that the deployments were defensive and that Russia had no veto over its neighbours, which is a substantive one. Moscow's answer was that the alliance had expanded by invitation for thirty years and that its assurances about not doing so had been broken.

Written reply, delivered on 26 January, repeated the offer of talks on missile deployments and on military exercises while refusing to discuss the open door. It was a document designed to be published, its substance was written to be quoted, and several sentences read like a press release because Washington wanted Russia to be seen refusing a reasonable offer. The alliance also published a list of the capabilities it would discuss with Moscow, which is the sort of detail that makes a refusal look like a negotiating position rather than a wall.

Geneva produced the last meeting of its kind before the war. What the week established is that the two governments had different questions: Moscow wanted a guarantee about the future of the alliance, and Washington wanted to talk about the troops on the border, and neither was willing to answer the other's question. What followed was a war fought on the ground that the meeting had described, with the alliance that Moscow wanted to roll back expanding instead. The talks resumed in the summer, and by then the agenda had changed to the export of grain.
