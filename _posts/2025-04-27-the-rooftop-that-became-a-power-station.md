A household that puts panels on its roof becomes a small generator, and the terms on which it sells what it generates have been the subject of a policy argument for a decade. The arithmetic is simple at the household level and complicated at the level of the grid, and the complication is why the rules have been revised twice in the last three years.

The household case is this. A roof of a hundred square metres holds perhaps twenty kilowatts, the installation costs a sum that a middle-class family can borrow, and the electricity that the panels produce either reduces the bill or is sold to the grid. In the early years of the programme the sale was at a fixed tariff that the utility was obliged to pay, and the return on the investment was predictable to the year, which is why the installations grew so fast in the provinces with the most sun and the highest retail tariff.

The grid case is different. A distribution network was built to carry power from a plant to a load, and a neighbourhood in which every roof generates reverses the direction of the flow at midday. The transformer that was sized for the load is now fed from the other side, the voltage rises, and the utility has to invest in a network that it did not plan. The cost of that investment is borne by all the customers, including the ones without panels, which is the fairness argument that the regulators use.

The revision that took effect this year moved the price of the electricity towards the market. A new installation is paid the price that the market clears at the time it generates, which is low at midday when the sun is strongest and high in the evening when the panels are idle. The household that invested on the old tariff is grandfathered for a period, and the household that invests now faces a return that depends on when it sells and on what storage it can afford.

What the change produces is a different kind of buyer. A family that can afford a battery can store the midday surplus and sell it in the evening, and a family that cannot is paid the midday price and earns less. The battery is the object that separates the two, and it costs a sum that is close to the panels themselves, so the policy that was meant to be neutral between households is neutral only between the ones with capital.

What the rooftop does, in any case, is change the way a household thinks about electricity. A family with panels watches the weather, schedules the washing machine for the sunny hours and notices the meter in a way that a family without them never does. The behaviour is the part that the energy economists did not forecast and the part that has the longest effect, because a household that has learned to read a meter will read a tariff change too.
