---
title: "The rider in the heat of the afternoon"
date: 2025-07-10 20:40:00 +0800
tags: [labour, delivery, heat, cities]

description: "Delivery platforms pay by the order, and the orders keep arriving when the temperature passes thirty-five. The rules written to protect riders from heat exist on paper in several cities, and the gap between the paper and the app is the subject of this piece."
---

The app does not know what the temperature is. It knows how many orders are waiting, how far away the nearest rider is, and how long the customer has been promised. On an afternoon in July when the thermometer passes thirty-five, the number of orders goes up, because fewer people are willing to go out, and the number of riders willing to work goes down, because the same heat applies to them. The gap between those two curves is filled by people who ride anyway.

Pay for a delivery rider is mostly per order, with bonuses for completing a quota and penalties for lateness. The structure rewards speed and punishes rest, which is fine when the weather is mild and something else entirely when the pavement is at fifty degrees. A rider who stops for twenty minutes in the shade loses the bonus. A rider who does not stop risks heat exhaustion, and heat exhaustion on a motorbike is a specific hazard that the industry has been slow to name.

Several cities have issued rules on this. The measures usually include a requirement to suspend orders above a certain temperature, a mandatory rest period, and a payment for the hours lost. The rules are recent and they are real, and their enforcement depends on a platform's willingness to lose orders, which is a commercial decision taken by a company that is measured on volume. The result is a set of provisions that exist, are cited in the annual reports, and are observed unevenly.

The riders themselves have adapted in ways that are not in any regulation. A frozen bottle of water inside a jacket. A route that uses the shade of a particular row of trees. A shift that starts at six in the morning and ends at eleven, with the afternoon abandoned. The adaptation is skilled and it is individual, which means it benefits the riders who have been doing the job long enough to know the city, and leaves the newest ones exposed.

There is also the question of who is liable when something goes wrong. A rider who collapses in the street is treated as an independent contractor by the platform and as a worker by the hospital, and the paperwork that follows is its own kind of hazard. The customer is part of the system and mostly does not know it. An order placed at two in the afternoon in July is a request for someone to cross the city in the worst hours of the day, and the fee paid does not reflect that. Some platforms have added a small heat surcharge in recent summers, which is a start, and the surcharge has not changed the underlying arithmetic of a job paid per parcel.

What would change it is a rule that the platforms cannot route around, applied across the whole city and enforced by an inspector rather than by an algorithm. The technical design is not complicated. The political design is, because the cost of the rule is visible in the delivery fee and the benefit is visible only to the person who would have collapsed.
