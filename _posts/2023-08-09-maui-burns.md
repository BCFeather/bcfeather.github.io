---
title: "Maui burns"
date: 2023-08-09 23:45:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Wind-driven fires destroyed most of Lahaina on the Hawaiian island of Maui on 8 and 9 August 2023, killing at least 115 people and destroying more than two thousand buildings in the deadliest American wildfire in a century."
---

The fire began on the slopes above Lahaina on the morning of 8 August and reached the town by the afternoon. Winds from a passing hurricane offshore, gusting at times to 100 kilometres an hour, pushed it through wooden houses and along the waterfront until most of the historic town had gone. More than 2,200 buildings were destroyed, at least 115 people were killed, and several hundred remained unaccounted for at the end of the month, which makes this the deadliest wildfire in the United States since 1918 and the worst natural disaster in the state's history.

Warnings failed in ways that will be examined for years. Hawaii maintains a network of sirens designed to alert the population to tsunamis and other hazards, and the sirens stayed silent. Emergency managers said later that they had relied on mobile telephone alerts and on messages broadcast by local stations, and that the sirens would have sent people towards the sea, where the fire was coming from. Power lines knocked down by the wind are being examined as a possible source of ignition, and the utility has acknowledged that its equipment is involved in the investigation.

Response has been slow and controversial. The governor, Josh Green, described the destruction as catastrophic and asked tourists to stay away, and the federal emergency agency sent staff, search teams and dogs. Residents ran their own supply runs with boats and trucks, and volunteers at the harbour distributed water before official convoys arrived. The head of the state's emergency management agency resigned after defending his decision about the sirens, and the attorney general opened an inquiry into the response.

Housing is the second disaster. Lahaina's residents were already short of affordable homes, and the fire removed thousands of units at once. Hotels that normally host tourists have been housing survivors, and the state has been negotiating to keep them there while permanent accommodation is built. The cost has been estimated at more than five billion dollars, and insurance will cover part of it, slowly and with arguments about what a policy actually promised.

The history complicates the grief. Lahaina was the capital of the Hawaiian kingdom before American annexation, and it held sites that mattered to the islands' native population, including a palace and a church built in the 1820s. Fire is a political event in Hawaii, because the islands were once covered in plantations that dried the land and changed the water, and because the town that burned was the one that had kept a record of what came before. Rebuilding will be argued in those terms, with developers, residents and the state each holding a different idea of what the place was.

A town of twelve thousand people was reduced to ash in two days, and the search for the missing went on for weeks, and the state is now arguing about sirens, power lines and who will be allowed to rebuild. The fire itself was weather; the losses that follow it will be policy.
