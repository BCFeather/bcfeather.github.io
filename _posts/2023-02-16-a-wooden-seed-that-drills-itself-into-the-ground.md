---
title: "A wooden seed that drills itself into the ground"
date: 2023-02-16 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "A team writing in Nature on 16 February 2023 described a carrier made of oak veneer that coils and drills itself into the soil when it rains, designed to improve the survival of seeds dropped from aircraft or drones."
---

Aerial seeding is a cheap way to cover a hillside and a poor way to grow anything. Seeds dropped from a plane or a drone mostly land on the surface, where birds eat them and the sun dries them, and the germination rates that follow are low enough that the method is usually a first step rather than a fix. A paper published in Nature on 16 February described a carrier that imitates a seed which has solved the problem already, and reported that the imitation works up to eighty per cent of the time on ground where the natural version often fails entirely.

The seed in question belongs to Erodium, a genus of small plants that grow in dry places around the Mediterranean and in Central Asia. Its seed has a coiled body and a long tail, and the tail responds to humidity by unwinding and rewinding, which turns the coil into a drill that works the seed into the soil. The mechanism is a hygromorph, a structure that changes shape as it takes up water, of the same family as the scales of a pine cone that close when it rains.

A team led by researchers at Carnegie Mellon, with partners at Zhejiang University, the University of Pennsylvania and Syracuse, built the carrier out of white oak veneer rather than out of plastic or metal. Wood is stiff, cheap and biodegradable, and its cells swell differently in different directions, which produces bending when the material is cut at the right angle. The group engineered the veneer so that it kept about a third of its stiffness while reaching a bending curvature forty-five times larger than any wood-based actuator previously reported.

The shape is where the design differs from the plant. The wooden carrier has three tails rather than one, which anchors it better and generates more rotation as the tails unwind, and the body was moulded on a three-dimensional printed form and then dried so that the curvature set. In tests the carrier buried seeds in a range of soils, and in field trials dropped from a drone it performed well enough that the authors described aerial seeding as a realistic use rather than a demonstration.

Measurements in the paper explain why the natural seed is a hard act to copy. Erodium's own burial rate fell to zero on some surfaces, because the tail needs something to push against, while the wooden version managed the same task on compacted ground and on loose soil. The authors attributed the difference to the three tails and to a tip that is sharper than the plant's, and they reported the curvature and stiffness values so that other groups can test the claim.

What the paper does with the imitation is the part that matters outside the laboratory. Aerial seeding is used to restore land after fires, to stabilize slopes above roads and to plant cover on ground that machines cannot reach, and a carrier that raises the success rate changes the arithmetic of all three. The authors also suggested that the same structure could carry sensors or fertilizer rather than seeds, which would make it a delivery system that happens to look like a seed.
