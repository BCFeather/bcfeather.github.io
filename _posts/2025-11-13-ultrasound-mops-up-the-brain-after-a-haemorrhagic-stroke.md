When a blood vessel bursts inside the skull, the damage does not end with the bleeding itself. The blood that pools there becomes neurotoxic debris: red blood cells and their breakdown products linger in the tissue, trigger inflammation, and injure the nerve cells around them. In the decades since doctors learned to stop the bleeding, clearing that debris has remained an unsolved problem. Surgical removal is invasive and demands rapid access to a specialist centre, and no drug has yet been approved for the job. A paper in the 10 November issue of *Nature Biotechnology* describes a different approach: a low-intensity, non-invasive ultrasound treatment that, in mice, mops up more than half of the lingering blood cells and improves every measured outcome of the stroke.

The study, led by Raag Airan and colleagues, tested the ultrasound protocol in two mouse models that together cover the two common forms of haemorrhagic stroke: subarachnoid bleeding on the surface of the brain, and intraparenchymal bleeding inside the tissue itself. In both models, the treated mice cleared more than half of the red blood cells from the brain, and the imaging suggested where the cells were going: they appeared to be shuttled along the body's own drainage route to the deep cervical lymph nodes, the stations that normally carry waste out of the nervous system. The treatment did not break down the debris on the spot. It helped the brain carry it away.

The downstream differences were consistent. In both models, treated mice showed less brain inflammation and less nerve-cell damage than untreated controls. In the intraparenchymal model, the effect reached the outcomes that matter: the treated animals lived longer, had less swelling around the bleed, recovered their body weight faster, and scored higher on corner-turning and grip-strength tests, the standard behavioural readouts of recovery in rodent stroke. The team also compared the ultrasound with an experimental drug previously shown to improve debris clearance in mice, and the ultrasound was both more effective and safer.

The reason the result is being taken seriously is the design discipline behind it. The authors built the protocol to fit the existing safety limits for ultrasound exposure, which means the path to a human trial is shorter than it would be for a device built from scratch. They are now planning exactly such trials. If the work translates, the appeal is obvious: a patient with a haemorrhagic stroke could be treated without surgery and without a new drug, on a machine that already exists in hospitals.

The authors are careful not to overclaim. The work is in mice, the two models are induced rather than naturally occurring, and the leap from rodent recovery to human recovery is the one that has killed many promising stroke therapies before. They also suggest, with appropriate caution, that the same debris-clearing logic could one day apply to other conditions in which waste builds up in the brain: Alzheimer's disease, in which protein aggregates accumulate, and traumatic brain injury. Those are longer bets. The nearer bet is the stroke itself, and the paper makes that case unusually cleanly.
