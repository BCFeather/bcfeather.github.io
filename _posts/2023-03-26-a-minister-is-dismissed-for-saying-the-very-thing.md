---
title: "A minister is dismissed for saying the very thing"
date: 2023-03-26 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Israel's defence minister was dismissed on 26 March 2023 after calling for a pause in the judicial overhaul, and the dismissal brought hundreds of thousands of people into the streets."
---

The defence minister, Yoav Gallant, had used a televised statement the previous evening to ask the government to stop its legislation for a few weeks. His argument was about the army rather than about the law: reservists had begun to say they would not report for duty, and the warning was that the dispute was reaching the units that the country depends on. He was dismissed on Sunday, and the announcement came while the cabinet was meeting. The statement lasted four minutes and was broadcast live, and the minister said the legislation had become a danger to the army's cohesion.

Read as a decision about who inside the government could speak, the dismissal told the remaining doubters what a public objection would cost. Three ministers had expressed doubts about the overhaul, and the two others had been dissenters within the coalition rather than inside the cabinet, and the prime minister had tolerated them. The defence minister was a member of his own party and had served in the same unit, and his removal told the remaining doubters what the cost of a public objection was. He had been the only member of the cabinet from his party who said so, and the prime minister had said nothing in public for a day.

Streets filled that night without an organization to fill them. Crowds gathered in Tel Aviv and in Jerusalem, and the numbers were estimated in the hundreds of thousands, which was larger than any previous demonstration of the year. The police used water cannon, the roads to the airport were blocked, and the universities announced they would strike, and the country's president called on the government to stop. The demonstrators were organized by nobody and had no single demand beyond stopping the legislation, which made them hard to negotiate with and hard to ignore.

For the following morning the labour federation announced a general strike, and the airports, the banks and the hospitals joined it, which was the first time in the country's history that the federation had struck over a political question. The strike was suspended by a court order after a few hours, and the suspension was the beginning of the compromise that arrived the following day.

For months the economic cost of the dispute had been accumulating. The currency had weakened, the technology sector had reported a flight of investment, and the central bank had said in a public statement that the uncertainty was already reducing activity. Those numbers had been published before the dismissal, and the dismissal was the moment when the economic argument and the street argument became the same argument.

The argument that the minister had made was about manpower and the one that the government made in reply was about authority. A country that had spent seventy-five years building an army of reservists had discovered that the reservists were citizens with opinions, and the government had discovered that it could not command them. The dismissal settled nothing and it changed everything, which is how a political crisis usually begins. A government elected on a promise to change the courts had discovered that the courts were defended by the reservists, and the reservists had families in the crowds.
