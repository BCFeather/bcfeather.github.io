The opening ceremony was staged on the river rather than in a stadium. Eighty boats carried the delegations downstream from one bridge to another. The audience stood on the embankments above them. The route ran for six kilometres through the centre of the city.

The seats were sold in tiers. A ticket on the lower quay cost several hundred euros and a ticket on a bridge cost nothing at all, because the bridge was public. An estimated three hundred thousand people watched from the banks. The police counted them by the section.

The weather was the risk that the plan could not control. A forecast of rain held for most of the afternoon. The ceremony started under a sky that had been threatening all day. The boats carried umbrellas for the athletes who wanted them.

The order of the boats followed the alphabet of the host language. A small delegation of a few dozen athletes shared a boat with another. The large ones had one to themselves. The flags were held by the athletes rather than by a hired bearer, which is a change from the stadium era.

The lighting was done from the bridges and from the buildings along the route. A projector on a roof threw an image onto a facade. A drone show ran above the water at the interval. The drones were flown from a barge in the middle of the river.

The torch was carried by a sequence of athletes past the landmarks. The final lighting was a balloon that rose above the city and stayed there for the fortnight. The flame was not a fire in a bowl, which disappointed some of the older commentators.

The transport plan was tested on the night. Six metro lines ran until two in the morning, and the stations closest to the route were closed for security. A spectator who left at eleven walked two kilometres to a station that was open.

The cost was counted in the morning papers. The ceremony was the most expensive item in a budget that had already been revised twice, and the security was the second. The city expects the visitors to pay part of it back in a fortnight.

The ceremony ended after midnight. The boats moored, the crowd walked away along the embankments, and the cleaning crews started on the route before the last train.

The stands on the lower quay were dismantled within a week. A temporary structure of scaffolding and seats costs a fortune to build and nothing to remove, because the parts are rented from a company that supplies festivals.

The delegations that arrived by boat had to be at the pontoon three hours before. A team of two hundred queued in a hall and was fed in shifts. The athletes who walked in the parade instead of sailing it called the evening the longest of their fortnight. The permanent legacy of the evening is a set of pontoons on the river, which the city will keep for the boat traffic that it already had.
