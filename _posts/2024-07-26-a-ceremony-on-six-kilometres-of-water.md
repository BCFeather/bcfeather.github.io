Staged on the river rather than in a stadium, the opening ceremony put six kilometres of water to work. Eighty boats carried the delegations downstream from one bridge to another. Audience stood on the embankments above. Six kilometres of the city centre made the route. Seats were sold in tiers. A ticket on the lower quay cost several hundred euros and a ticket on a bridge cost nothing at all, because the bridge was public. An estimated three hundred thousand people watched from the banks. Police counted them by the section.

Weather was the risk that the plan could not control. A forecast of rain held for most of the afternoon. Under a sky that had been threatening all day the ceremony started. Boats carried umbrellas for the athletes who wanted them.

Boats went out in the alphabetical order of the host language. A small delegation of a few dozen athletes shared a boat with another. The large ones had one to themselves. Athletes held the flags themselves rather than a hired bearer, which is a change from the stadium era. Lighting came from the bridges and from the buildings along the route. A projector on a roof threw an image onto a facade. A drone show ran above the water at the interval. The drones were flown from a barge in the middle of the river.

A sequence of athletes carried the torch past the landmarks. The final lighting was a balloon that rose above the city and stayed there for the fortnight. The flame was not a fire in a bowl, which disappointed some of the older commentators.

Transport was the part that was tested on the night. Six metro lines ran until two in the morning, and the stations closest to the route were closed for security. A spectator who left at eleven walked two kilometres to a station that was open. Cost was counted in the morning papers. Most expensive item in a budget already revised twice, the ceremony cost more than the security, which came second. The city expects the visitors to pay part of it back in a fortnight.

By the morning papers the cost had been counted. A temporary structure of scaffolding and seats costs a fortune to build and nothing to remove, because the parts are rented from a company that supplies festivals. Delegations arriving by boat had to be at the pontoon three hours before. A team of two hundred queued in a hall and was fed in shifts. The athletes who walked in the parade instead of sailing it called the evening the longest of their fortnight. A set of pontoons on the river is the permanent legacy of the evening, which the city will keep for the boat traffic that it already had.

After midnight the ceremony ended, and the stands came down within a week. Boats moored, the crowd walked away along the embankments, and the cleaning crews started on the route before the last train. By the middle of the week the river was carrying barges again, and the six kilometres of water had returned to the traffic that uses it every day.
