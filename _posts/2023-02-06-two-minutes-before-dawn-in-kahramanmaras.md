---
title: "Two minutes before dawn in Kahramanmaras"
date: 2023-02-06 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "A magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck southern Turkey and northern Syria at 04.17 on 6 February 2023, followed nine hours later by a second quake of magnitude 7.5, and the damage spread across ten Turkish provinces."
---

At 04.17 local time the first shock broke, with its epicentre in the Pazarcik district of Kahramanmaras province and its shaking felt from Adana to Diyarbakir. The magnitude was 7.8, and the rupture was shallow, which is the combination that does the most damage to buildings rather than to hillsides. Nine hours later a second quake of magnitude 7.5 broke on a neighbouring fault near Elbistan, and it brought down structures that the first had weakened. Whole apartment blocks came down in a single movement, and the noise of it was heard several kilometres away, which survivors described as the last thing they remembered before the dust.

Ten provinces were declared a disaster zone, and the government imposed a three-month state of emergency. Airports in Hatay and Kahramanmaras were damaged, roads were blocked by collapsed bridges, and the winter weather slowed the arrival of rescue teams in the first two days. Turkey had mobilized tens of thousands of search and rescue personnel, and more than eighty countries offered teams, equipment or money. Help arrived from outside the region more slowly than the government had promised, because the roads that carried it were the same roads that had collapsed.

Deaths climbed faster than the rescuers could work. The disaster agency reported 4,544 deaths in Turkey by the evening of 7 February, with more than 26,000 injured, and the figure doubled again within two days. In Syria the count was slower and less reliable, because the affected area was divided between government-held territory, opposition-held territory and a border that aid had to cross.

The building stock was the reason the toll was so high. The region had grown quickly over three decades, and much of that growth had been permitted under amnesties that legalized structures built without inspection. Engineers who examined the rubble in the first weeks described failures at the joints and in the columns rather than in the walls, which is the signature of concrete that was mixed and placed without adequate supervision.

Rescue became a matter of mathematics after the third day. Survivors were pulled from voids in the rubble for more than a week, and each of those cases was reported as a small exception to the rule that the first seventy-two hours decide most of the outcome. The cold worked against the trapped and against the rescuers, and the shortage of heavy machinery in the first days was the complaint that survivors' families made most often. Families who had lost their homes moved into tents and into student dormitories, and the cold made the tents a second emergency.

Infrastructure in the province depended on a single fault line, which the earthquake made visible. The Ceyhan terminal that loads Azeri and Iraqi crude, the pipelines that cross the province, and the roads that carry trade to the border all sat within reach of the rupture. Most of it restarted within a month, and the fact that it did was treated as luck rather than as planning.
