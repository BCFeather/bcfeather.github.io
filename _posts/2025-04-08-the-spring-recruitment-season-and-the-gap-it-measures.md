The spring hiring season runs through March and April, and in the Chinese labour market it has a name that describes its shape, the third and fourth months, the golden three and the silver four. The autumn season that precedes it is for the large employers and the campus interviews, and the spring season is for the students who did not take a job in the autumn, the employers who did not fill their quotas, and the smaller firms that cannot plan a year ahead.

The numbers behind the season are large and they have been growing. The graduating class of the universities and colleges is above ten million for the third year in a row, and the positions that the state-owned enterprises and the large private firms offer on campus are a fraction of that. The rest of the market is the small and medium firms, the agencies, the platforms, and the provincial civil service examinations, and the season is the period in which those channels are worked.

The mechanics of the season have changed with the technology. The applications are filed through platforms, the first screen is an algorithm that filters on a keyword and a school, and the interview that follows is often a video call with a recorded answer. The effect of the automation is to increase the number of applications a student can send and to decrease the attention each one receives, which is a trade that benefits the student who fits the filter and penalises the one who does not.

The gap that the season measures is more than a number of positions. It is a mismatch of expectations on both sides. A graduate with a master's degree expects a salary and a title that the market for that degree does not offer, and an employer with a small budget expects a candidate who can be productive in a month. The negotiation between those two expectations is what an interview is, and the season compresses several hundred thousand of them into six weeks.

What the policy has done is widen the channels rather than increase the demand. The programmes that place graduates in county-level institutions, the subsidies for firms that hire a first-time jobseeker, the expansion of the professional master's degrees, all of them address the supply side or the matching, and none of them creates a job in a company that has no orders. The distinction matters, because a subsidy that pays part of a salary for a year produces a position for a year.

What the season leaves, for the student, is a decision made under time pressure. The offer that arrives in April expires in a week, the better interview is scheduled for May, and the choice between them is a choice between a certain job and a possible one, made with the clock running. The season is designed to produce that choice, and the students who navigate it well are the ones who understand that the deadline is a negotiating position.
