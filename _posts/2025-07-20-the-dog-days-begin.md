The hot stretch of the year has a name and a start date. It begins in the third week of July and runs for thirty or forty days depending on how the calendar falls, and it is called sanfu, the three periods of the dog days. The naming system is old and slightly baroque, tied to the position of a particular star in the twenty-eight lunar mansions, and it produces a period whose length varies from year to year by ten days.

What the period means in practice is heat that does not relent at night. The daytime maximum is high and the minimum stays above twenty-six, so a building never cools, and a person who has no air conditioning sleeps badly for a month. The hospitals see more cardiovascular admissions. The construction sites shift their hours. The evening streets fill later than usual and empty later than usual, and the city acquires a rhythm that belongs to the temperature rather than to the clock.

The traditions attached to the period are mostly about food, and the food advice has survived longer than any of the medical advice that accompanied it. Eating something hot to drive out the heat is the counter-intuitive one, and it has a real basis in the way sweating and appetite interact. Eating bitter melon and drinking mung bean soup is the more sensible one. The advice columns publish the same list every year, and the list is roughly correct, and nobody follows it for more than a week.

There is an economic layer under the weather. The dog days are when electricity demand peaks, when the price of vegetables climbs because the harvest is stressed, when the air conditioning repair trade cannot keep up with the calls. A restaurant that does not have air conditioning loses customers for a month. A factory that cannot cool its floor loses productivity in a way that shows up in the quarterly numbers and is attributed to something else.

The period is also when the country's attention goes to water. The floods in the south and the drought in the north arrive in the same weeks, and the same weather system that produces one produces the other. The dog days are a name for the temperature, and the temperature is a name for a much larger arrangement of wind and ocean that nobody has been able to schedule.

There is a version of the period that belongs to people who cannot escape it. Roofers, street sweepers, market traders, the crews who repair the roads at night because the asphalt is too hot to touch at noon. For them the season is a month of endurance, and the holiday advice published in the newspapers is written for somebody else. For the individual, the period is a set of small adjustments. A cold shower before bed. A fan pointed at the ceiling. A decision to walk on the shaded side of the street. None of it is significant and all of it is what the season is made of, and in forty days it will end, and the evening will arrive earlier, and everyone will say the summer went quickly.
