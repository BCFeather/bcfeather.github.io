---
title: "A prime minister of thirty-four"
date: 2024-01-10 22:14:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Emmanuel Macron appointed Gabriel Attal, thirty-four, as prime minister on 9 January, making him the youngest head of government in the history of the Fifth Republic and handing him the task of holding a parliament without a majority."
---

On the evening of the ninth of January, the Élysée announced that Gabriel Attal would replace Élisabeth Borne as prime minister, and the number that followed every mention of his name was thirty-four. Attal becomes the youngest prime minister of the Fifth Republic, younger than any of the men and women who have held the post since 1958, and the first who has been open about being gay. He arrives from the education ministry, where he had been in the job for less than a year, and before that from the government's communications operation, where he had been the face of the pandemic response and the spokesman the cameras liked.

Changing the prime minister was a decision about arithmetic and about mood. Borne had served for twenty months and had never been comfortable in the job. She had used article 49.3 of the constitution, which allows a government to pass a bill without a vote, more than twenty times, and each use gave the opposition a scene to perform. Her pension reform in 2023 brought the country to a halt for weeks, and the memory of the rubbish in the streets of Paris lasted longer than the reform's supporters expected. Macron's party does not have a majority in the National Assembly, and every budget since 2022 has had to be assembled from pieces borrowed from the right.

Attal's mandate is therefore narrower than the job usually is. He has been told to work on the supply side of French politics, which means the labour market, the schools, the apprenticeship system, and the argument about whether France works hard enough. He has been given a small group of junior ministers and a promise that the president will keep his distance from the day-to-day running of the government, which is a promise every French president makes and no French president keeps. What he has not been given is a majority, or an election that would produce one, because Macron cannot call parliamentary elections before the summer and would not want to.

Opposition parties have spent the week pointing out that the new prime minister is the same project in a younger body. Marine Le Pen called him a reorganisation of the same team. Jean-Luc Mélenchon said the appointment proved that the president had learned nothing. There is truth in both remarks, and it is not the whole picture. Attal is a different kind of figure from the ones Macron has promoted before. He is direct in a way the French call, without much affection, communication, and he has a habit of answering questions rather than deflecting them. In a government whose central problem is that nobody believes what it says, a spokesman who sounds like he means it is not a small asset.

The deeper question is what happens to the office itself. The Fifth Republic was designed around a strong president and a prime minister who implements, and the last seven years have shown how elastic that arrangement can be. When the president is unpopular and the parliament is divided, the prime minister becomes the lightning rod, absorbing the anger that the president no longer has to take. Borne did that for twenty months and was worn down by it. Attal, who is young, popular in the polls and close to the president, will be asked to do the same job with better tools. Whether he survives the year depends less on his own abilities than on whether the budget he must pass in the autumn can be passed without another round of the same scenes.
