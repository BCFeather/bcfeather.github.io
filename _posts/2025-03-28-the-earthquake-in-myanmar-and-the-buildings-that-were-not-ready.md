A magnitude 7.7 earthquake struck central Myanmar on the twenty-eighth of March, with the epicentre close to Mandalay and the rupture shallow enough that the shaking was severe across a wide area. The reports that arrived in the first day described collapsed houses, damaged monasteries, a bridge that had lost a span, and a hospital that was treating patients in the street. The death toll rose for a week as the villages in the irrigated plain were reached.

The geology is the standard one for the region. The earthquake happened on a strike-slip fault, where two blocks of the crust move past each other horizontally, and the shaking from that kind of fault is strong over a long distance because the rupture propagates along the line rather than spreading from a point. The same fault system runs the length of the country, and the historical record includes events of similar size in the last two centuries, which is why the seismic hazard maps of the region have carried a high rating for decades.

The damage is a function of what was built. A house of timber and bamboo bends and usually survives. A house of unreinforced brick, which is what a household builds when it has a little more money and wants a permanent wall, fails at the corners and comes down on the people inside. A monastery of masonry with a heavy roof does the same. The engineering that prevents this is known, inexpensive relative to the cost of the building, and applied unevenly in a country with a low income and a weak building inspectorate, and the gap between the two is the gap that the photographs of a collapsed school always describe.

The wave reached Bangkok, six hundred kilometres from the epicentre, and a high-rise under construction there collapsed. The building was not finished, the structural frame was in place and the floors were not, and a frame without its floors is a frame without the mass and stiffness that the design assumes. The photographs of the site became the image of the disaster outside the country, and the fact that they came from a capital with a functioning building code made the point about enforcement.

The response is the part that will be measured in the months that follow. The country has a government with limited capacity, several areas are outside its effective control, and the roads and the airports that relief depends on were damaged by the same event. The agencies that responded first were the local ones, the monks and the village committees and the Red Cross society, and the international teams that arrived in the second week found a landscape in which the local organisations had already done the first and most useful work.

What the earthquake shows, again, is that the difference between a hazard and a disaster is the quality of the buildings. The hazard was fixed by the fault. The disaster was decided by the brick, and by the inspection that the brick never received. The rebuilding that follows will decide the next one.
