title: "One hundred and forty-one votes and a word they could not agree on"
date: 2023-02-23 23:40:00 +0800

description: "The General Assembly adopted a resolution on 23 February 2023 demanding that Russia withdraw from Ukraine, with 141 states voting in favour, seven against and thirty-two abstaining."
---

On 23 February the General Assembly adopted a resolution demanding that Russia withdraw its forces from Ukraine and calling for a settlement consistent with the United Nations charter. The vote was 141 in favour, seven against and thirty-two abstaining, a result that was close to the one produced a year earlier when the assembly had first condemned the invasion. The text had been negotiated for a week, and the negotiations were about verbs.

One word caused the difficulty, and it was peace. A group of developing countries wanted the resolution to call for negotiations and to avoid language that prejudged their outcome, and Ukraine's allies wanted the text to state that Russia had to leave before talks could begin. The compromise described a settlement as comprehensive, just and lasting, and left the sequencing to the reader, which was the only formula that could hold the coalition together.

Abstentions were the interesting part of the tally. India, South Africa and a number of states that had voted in favour a year earlier abstained this time, and their diplomats said the text had become a statement of one side's position rather than a call for the charter. China, which had published a twelve-point position paper the previous day, abstained as well, and the paper's emphasis on sovereignty and on the territorial integrity of all states was its own argument about the war. Their diplomats said the text had hardened into a statement of one side's position, and that a call for the charter had become a call for a verdict.

The seven votes against came from Russia, Belarus, North Korea, Syria, Eritrea, Mali and Nicaragua. That list was shorter than the one the previous year, and the difference was made up of countries that had switched to abstention rather than of countries that had switched sides. Assembly resolutions carry no binding force, and the vote's value is in the record it creates rather than in the obligations it imposes.

Another purpose was to answer the argument that the assembly had said nothing since the first year. It had passed three resolutions on the war, and two of them had been about humanitarian access and about the suspension of Russia from the Human Rights Council. A fourth one, on reparations, would come in the autumn, and the pattern was that the assembly could pass anything that did not require enforcement. The assembly had also suspended Russia from the Human Rights Council in April 2022, and the suspension had been the only punitive step it could take.

What the vote measured was the distance between the charter and the coalitions. A majority of the world's states could agree that an invasion was an invasion, and they could not agree on what should happen next, or on who should be the one to say it. The resolution recorded that majority, and it recorded the fact that a majority does not make a policy. A majority could agree that an invasion was an invasion, and the agreement stopped at the sentence where the next step should have been.
