title: "The longest day falls in a loud month"
date: 2022-06-25 23:55:00 +0800
categories: [essay]
description: "At the solstice the light lasts longest and the news arrives fastest, and the two habits of June, staying up and keeping up, are hard to tell apart."
---

The sun reached its highest point on 21 June, and for a week afterwards the evenings in the northern hemisphere stayed light until half past nine. Trees that had finished flowering held their leaves still, and the noise of the day, traffic and drilling and the lift of a hundred conversations, went on longer than it would in any other month because there was no darkness to stop it. Gardeners noticed that the roses had finished a fortnight earlier than usual, and that the grass had begun to brown at the edges.

June was loud in the other sense as well. A war in Europe ground through its fourth month, prices climbed, a court removed a right that had stood for half a century, and a currency fell through a level that had held for five years. Each of those things happened on a different day and each was described as historic, and by the end of the month the word had lost most of its weight.

Reading the news at that pace produces a particular kind of tiredness. A story arrives, is understood in outline, is overtaken by the next story, and the reader retains a mood rather than a set of facts. The mood in June was one of things coming apart faster than they could be explained, which is roughly what the numbers said too. A person who reads four newspapers in a morning retains less than one who reads a single long piece in the evening, which is a fact about attention rather than about journalism.

The solstice offers the opposite instruction. It is a date that changes nothing, arrives on schedule, and can be observed by looking out of a window. Nobody has to be told what it means, and no one can be blamed for it. There is a reason that the festivals invented around this date are about light and fire and staying up late, and that none of them is about urgency. The festivals of the season are all about duration: a fire kept burning, a table kept laid, a conversation kept going until the light goes.

Morning is when the month is quietest. Between five and seven the streets are nearly empty, the light is flat and white, and the day's news has not yet been published. A person who wakes early gets an hour in which nothing is happening, and in a month like June that hour is worth more than it is in December, when the dark does the same work.

What the season suggests is a way of holding the two together. Attention can be given to the loud things without being given to all of them at once, and the longest day is a reminder that the light is a resource rather than a deadline. By the end of the month the evenings had begun to shorten by a minute or two, which nobody notices, and the news had not shortened at all. By August the evenings will be noticeably shorter, and the habit of staying up will have to be revised, which happens every year and catches everyone by surprise.
