---
title: "A warrant for a president"
date: 2023-03-17 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The International Criminal Court issued arrest warrants on 17 March 2023 for President Putin and for Russia's commissioner for children's rights over the deportation of Ukrainian children."
---

The court's pre-trial chamber issued two warrants on 17 March, one for the president of Russia and one for Maria Lvova-Belova, the official responsible for children's rights. The charges concerned the deportation of Ukrainian children from occupied territory, which the court described as a war crime. The chamber said there were reasonable grounds to believe that the two bore individual responsibility for the policy. Both warrants were issued under seal and were made public only because the chamber decided that the interest of the proceedings outweighed the confidentiality that normally attaches to them.

The legal step was unprecedented in one respect and routine in another. The court had issued warrants for heads of state before, including for a sitting president of Sudan, and it had never issued one for the leader of a permanent member of the Security Council. The practical effect was to limit where the president could travel, because the hundred and twenty-three states that had ratified the treaty were obliged to arrest him if he set foot on their territory. The statute gives the court jurisdiction over crimes committed on the territory of a state party, and Ukraine had accepted that jurisdiction in 2015.

Evidence behind the warrants had taken a year to collect. Ukrainian prosecutors and international investigators had documented transfers of children to camps and to foster families in Russia, and the numbers that they published ranged from a few thousand to more than sixteen thousand, depending on the definition. The court's case was built on a smaller set of documented individual transfers, because a warrant needs proof of a chain of command rather than an estimate of scale.

Russia's response was immediate and procedural. The foreign ministry said the warrants had no legal force, the security council opened criminal cases against the court's prosecutor and judges, and the investigative committee issued its own warrants for them. The argument that Russia made was that the court had no jurisdiction because Russia had never ratified the treaty, and the court's answer was that it had jurisdiction over acts committed on the territory of a state that had. The investigation committee in Moscow said the warrants were void, and it opened files of its own against the prosecutor and the judges within a week.

Members of the court were put in an awkward position by the warrant. A summit in South Africa in August would have required the government to arrest the president if he attended, and he did not attend, and the government's explanation was about diplomatic immunity rather than about the warrant. Several other states said they would not act on it, which was a reminder that the court has no police and depends on the willingness of governments.

What the warrants created was a record that could not be erased. A president who is wanted by a court cannot be pardoned by a successor in his own country, and the file remains open for as long as the court exists. That is a different kind of consequence from a sanction, and the people who built the case described it as the point.
