---
title: "Annie Ernaux wins the literature Nobel for a lifetime written out of memory"
date: 2022-10-06 23:20:00 +0800
categories: [culture]
description: "The Swedish Academy rewards a writer who turned her own life into a record of a class, a region and a generation."
---

The Swedish Academy gave the literature prize on Thursday to Annie Ernaux, who is eighty-two and has spent fifty years turning her own life into something that reads like a collective record. The citation praised the courage and the clinical sharpness with which she uncovers the roots, the estrangements and the restraints of personal memory. She is the seventeenth woman to receive the prize, and the first French writer to win it since 2014. The announcement came an hour after the academy had cleared the room of the usual speculation.

Her method is the reason the academy could describe a memoir as a public document. In the book that many readers treat as her masterpiece, published in 2008 and translated as The Years, she writes about herself in the third person and about her generation in the first, so that a private life appears as a sequence of photographs and advertisements. She has called the whole project ethnological rather than autobiographical. Earlier books took single events and worked them until nothing decorative was left: her father's shop, her mother's illness, an illegal abortion in 1963, a marriage that ended.

She was born in 1940 in Lillebonne, in Normandy, and grew up in Yvetot, where her parents ran a grocery and then a small shop. The class she came from is the subject she never stopped writing about, and the shame of leaving it is the emotion that runs through the early books. She studied at Rouen and then at Bordeaux, taught in secondary schools for thirty years and moved to a suburb of Paris. She has said that she began to write because she wanted to avenge her father.

The award is a departure for an institution that has spent a decade being attacked for rewarding the wrong kind of writer. The academy had given the prize to European novelists for most of the previous twenty years, and its members have argued in public about whether the prize should travel further. Ernaux writes short books, and she has never written a novel with a plot that could be summarized. Her election is a vote for the plain sentence and for the idea that a life is worth recording because it is ordinary.

She said in a statement released by her publisher that she was surprised and that she hoped the prize would help the work reach readers who had been told it was too small. The prize is worth ten million kronor, and it will be handed over in Stockholm in December with the others. Her books have sold modestly in France and better abroad, and the translations into English have appeared slowly, one every few years. The academy's citation will send a few of them back into print within the week.

What her work leaves is a question about the form. A writer who spends fifty years on her own experience runs the risk of producing a diary that only she can read, and Ernaux avoided that by treating the self as a specimen of something larger. The shop in Yvetot, the grammar school, the abortion, the cancer, the affair with a younger man: each is described as an instance of a rule that the reader has also lived under. That is what the academy has honoured, and it is the reason her readers tend to say the books describe their own families rather than hers.
