title: "The night the peninsula moved"
date: 2024-01-01 22:41:00 +0800

description: "A magnitude 7.6 earthquake struck the Noto Peninsula of Ishikawa prefecture on the afternoon of 1 January, bringing tsunami warnings to the Japan Sea coast, cutting roads to fishing villages and leaving an unknown number dead in the winter cold."
---

At ten minutes past four on the afternoon of the first of January, the ground beneath the Noto Peninsula began to move, and it did not stop at once. Officials at the Japan Meteorological Agency put the magnitude at 7.6 and the depth at about sixteen kilometres, with the hypocentre a short distance off the coast of Ishikawa prefecture. Shaking reached the top of the Japanese intensity scale in Wajima and Shika, and within minutes the agency issued a major tsunami warning for the northern coast of the Japan Sea, the first of its kind since 2011, telling people to leave the shore at once and to expect waves of up to five metres. Water reached Wajima at about 1.2 metres, high enough to push cars and boats inland and low enough that the worst of the warning did not materialise there. By nightfall the count of the dead had reached several dozen and was still rising, and a fire had broken out among the wooden shops of Wajima's morning market.

Geology explains why this coast is treated differently from the Pacific side of Japan, and why the warning was issued in such urgent language. This coast sits above a different system of faults, one that produces large earthquakes far less often than the Nankai and Japan trenches to the south and east, so the memory of a tsunami is thinner among the people who live there. When such a quake does come, the water arrives quickly, because the source is close to shore, and narrow bays and river mouths amplify whatever height the wave has. The 1983 earthquake off the coast of Akita killed more than a hundred people, most of them on the coast at the moment the wave came in, and the Okushiri tsunami of 1993 killed about two hundred on an island that had prepared for earthquakes but not for water. Each of those events taught the same lesson, that the interval between the shaking and the wave on this coast is minutes rather than an hour.

Within an hour the machinery of the Japanese state was moving. Shinkansen services were halted across a wide area while tracks and viaducts were inspected, expressways closed, and the Shika nuclear plant, the only reactor site close to the epicentre, shut down as a precaution and reported no abnormalities in its cooling systems. Coast guard vessels went out to check the shoreline, the Self-Defence Force began preparing aircraft and trucks, and the prime minister's office set up a disaster response room. What the machinery could not do was open the roads. The Noto Peninsula is a narrow finger of land with a spine of hills down the middle, and most of its fishing villages stand at the end of a single road that runs along the water. Landslides and collapsed surfaces closed those roads early, and by evening several communities were reachable only by sea or by helicopter.

Then there was the season. It came in the coldest week of the Japanese winter, with temperatures near freezing on the peninsula and snow on the higher ground. Homes that had lost walls and roofs could not be kept warm, and the power cuts that spread across Ishikawa left tens of thousands of households without light or heating. Water mains broke under the shifted ground, and the shelters that opened in schools and gymnasiums filled with people who had come in on foot because the buses had stopped. Officials warned that the aftershocks would continue for weeks and asked residents not to return to damaged buildings, which for a fishing family with a boat and a store of nets is an instruction that is easy to give and hard to follow.

What the coming weeks will show is the same pattern that every large Japanese earthquake has shown since Kobe. The engineering will be judged a success in the newer buildings and a failure in the older ones, the warning system will be judged to have worked because the water was smaller than feared, and the reconstruction will take far longer than the first news cycle suggests. The deeper difficulty is demographic. Noto is one of the most aged and most emptied regions in Japan, and villages that were already losing their young people will now have to decide whether to rebuild at all. An earthquake does not create that problem. It makes it visible, on a single afternoon, to everybody watching.
