---
title: "Mariupol fails to hold a ceasefire long enough to evacuate"
date: 2022-03-05 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Buses wait five hours at a departure point and leave half empty, and the siege that follows turns a city of half a million into a name on a map."
---

A ceasefire was announced for the morning of 5 March to let people leave Mariupol, and it lasted less than two hours before the shelling began again. The city administration had agreed with the Russian side on a corridor from 10 in the morning until 3 in the afternoon, and buses waited at a departure point in the south of the city. The International Committee of the Red Cross was to observe the route, and it said its team could not reach it.

Shelling fell on the road and on the departure point, and each side blamed the other. Ukrainian officials said Russian armour had fired on the buses, the Russian ministry said the Ukrainian side had used the pause to move fighters, and neither account could be checked from outside. About 400 people left on foot and by car that afternoon, out of the 200,000 the city said wanted to go.

Mariupol had been without water, gas, electricity or heating for five days. Residents melted snow, took water from a pipe the city opened in one district, and buried neighbours in the courtyards of their buildings because the cemeteries were on the edge of town and the ground between them and the city had become a front line. A maternity hospital and a children's hospital had been hit, and the doctors had moved into a basement, where they treated the wounded by torchlight.

A humanitarian corridor is a promise that two armies have to keep at the same moment. A route is safe only if the unit that controls the ground holds its fire and the unit on the other side does the same, and the two commands have to agree on a time, a road and a mark on the cars. Each of those needs a radio call that is answered on both sides at once, and the calls from Mariupol went to headquarters that did not reply.

The siege continued through March and April. Shelling hit the drama theatre on 16 March, a building in which hundreds of people were sheltering and which had the word for children painted in white on the ground outside. Fighting reached the Azovstal steel works in April, where the last defenders held out for weeks, and the city was emptied of most of its people by the end of May. The mayor's office later put the number of dead above twenty thousand, a figure that the United Nations said was probably an underestimate and that no independent body could check while the fighting lasted.

What the failed corridor showed was the pattern of the war in the south. A siege creates the conditions that make a corridor necessary, and the same fight that produces the siege makes the corridor impossible to verify. The buses waited five hours and left half empty, the drivers refused to make a second run, and the war went on for another eleven weeks in a city of half a million people, a third of whom stayed behind.
