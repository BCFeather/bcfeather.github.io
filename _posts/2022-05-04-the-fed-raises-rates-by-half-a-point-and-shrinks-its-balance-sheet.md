---
title: "The Fed raises rates by half a point and shrinks its balance sheet"
date: 2022-05-04 23:55:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "The Federal Reserve lifted its benchmark range to between 0.75 and 1 per cent on 4 May, its largest single increase since 2000, and said the run-off of the assets it bought during the pandemic would begin on 1 June."
---

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate by half a percentage point on 4 May, lifting the target range to between 0.75 and 1 per cent. It was the first increase of that size since May 2000 and the second rise of the year, after the quarter-point move in March, and the vote was unanimous. The statement that accompanied it cited inflation running well above the two per cent objective, a labour market that it called tight, and the disruption caused by the war in Ukraine and by lockdowns in China.

The committee also set out how it intends to shrink the balance sheet it built during the pandemic, a stock of assets that had reached nearly nine trillion dollars. Run-off begins on 1 June with a cap of forty-seven and a half billion dollars a month, split between thirty billion of Treasury securities and seventeen and a half billion of mortgage-backed securities. After three months the caps rise to sixty billion and thirty-five billion respectively, which would remove roughly a trillion dollars of support over the following year.

At the press conference that followed, Jerome Powell tried to describe a path to lower inflation without a recession and admitted that the path had become narrower. He said a rise of three quarters of a point had no place in the committee's thinking, a sentence that markets read as a promise of gradualism, and he used the phrase softish landing to describe the outcome the Fed is attempting. Unemployment stood at 3.6 per cent, vacancies at around eleven and a half million, and wages were climbing at the fastest rate in decades.

Markets took the decision as a relief. The S&P 500 rose nearly three per cent on the day, its best session since 2020, on the reasoning that the Fed would not accelerate beyond the pace it had just set. The relief lasted about a week. By the middle of the month the same index had given up the gain and more, mortgage rates had climbed above five per cent for the first time since 2010, and the ten-year Treasury yield had pushed towards three per cent.

Arithmetic makes the task awkward, because the Fed is tightening into a slowdown rather than ahead of one. Growth contracted in the first quarter, consumer sentiment had fallen to its lowest level in a decade, and the stimulus that carried the economy through 2021 was being withdrawn while prices were still rising. Powell's argument is that demand, not supply, has to give way, and that a labour market this tight can absorb higher borrowing costs without shedding jobs.

What happens next depends on numbers the committee will see before its June meeting: another inflation reading, another payrolls report, another quarter of wage data. The rate path it published in March implied further half-point moves, and several officials had begun to say in public that they would go as high as the data demanded. The experiment now under way is whether the fastest tightening cycle in two decades can cool prices without putting the same two decades of job gains into reverse.
