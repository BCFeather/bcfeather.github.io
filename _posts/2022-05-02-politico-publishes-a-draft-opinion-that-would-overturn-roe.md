date: 2022-05-02 23:10:00 +0800

description: "A ninety-eight page draft written by Justice Samuel Alito and circulated in February reached a newsroom on the evening of 2 May, and by the next afternoon the chief justice had confirmed it was real and ordered an investigation into who let it out."
---

Politico published a draft majority opinion on the evening of 2 May that would strike down Roe v. Wade, the 1973 decision that established a federal constitutional right to abortion. The document runs to ninety-eight pages, was written by Justice Samuel Alito, and had been circulating inside the court since 10 February according to the newsroom that obtained it. Its language leaves little room for interpretation: the majority calls the earlier ruling egregiously wrong from the start and says the constitution offers no right to abortion at all.

By the following afternoon Chief Justice John Roberts had confirmed that the draft was authentic, and he said he had directed the marshal of the court to investigate the leak. He described the incident as a singular and egregious breach of trust and insisted that the work of the court would not be affected, though the institution he leads had never before had a pending opinion published in full while the case was still being decided.

The substance of the draft returns the question to the states, and the states had been preparing for years. Thirteen of them passed trigger laws designed to take effect the moment Roe fell, several still carry bans written before 1973 that were never repealed, and clinics in Texas, Louisiana and Kentucky had already drawn up plans for the morning after, down to the wording of the notice they would post on the door. A ruling along these lines would split the country into places where abortion remains available and places where a journey of hundreds of miles becomes the price of the procedure.

The leak changed the calendar as well as the argument. A decision had been drafted for the last week of June, when the term ends and the justices leave Washington, and instead the country spent May arguing over a text that the court had not finished voting on. Politico reported that four other conservative justices, Clarence Thomas, Neil Gorsuch, Brett Kavanaugh and Amy Coney Barrett, had voted with Alito in the private conference, which left the chief justice in a position where his own vote and his management of the institution both came under scrutiny.

Reaction outside the court followed the lines already drawn. Demonstrators gathered outside the building within hours, senators introduced a bill to codify the right and lost a vote on it in the middle of the month, and Republican candidates in primary contests discovered that a question they had answered cautiously for years now demanded a straight answer. The opinion itself may still change: drafts circulate, sentences are traded, and a vote taken in February can move before June.

What arrived in May, then, was a preview of a ruling rather than the ruling. The court will publish its decision when the term ends, and the consequence will fall on clinics, on patients who live in the wrong state, and on a bench whose internal deliberations are now a public document. Roberts wanted an investigation into the source; the country wanted to know what the final text would say.
