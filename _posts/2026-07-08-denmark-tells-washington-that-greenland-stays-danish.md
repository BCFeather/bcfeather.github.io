---
title: "Denmark tells Washington that Greenland stays Danish"
date: 2026-07-08 23:45:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The prime minister answered a fresh round of American interest with a flat refusal on 8 July, and her government spent the week explaining that sovereignty over the island is not a commodity that a richer partner can buy."
---

Denmark's prime minister, Mette Frederiksen, said on 8 July that Greenland would remain Danish, that her government would not discuss a sale, and that the island's status was a matter of sovereignty rather than of price. Her statement came after a fresh round of American interest in the territory, including public comments from Washington about its strategic value and about the minerals under its ice. Frederiksen said Denmark would defend its territory and that everyone involved should respect Danish sovereignty and territorial integrity.

Wording of the refusal mattered as much as the refusal. Danish officials avoided the transactional language that American commentators had used, and they described the question as one that a small ally answers by restating a legal position rather than by negotiating. The foreign ministry briefed journalists that any discussion of the island's future would have to involve Greenland's own government, which holds home rule and has its own view about independence.

American interest has a material basis. Greenland holds rare earth deposits, a nickel and cobalt potential that has barely been mapped, and a position that controls the approaches to the Arctic sea routes that are opening as the ice retreats. The island also hosts a American radar station under a 1951 agreement, and the American military has been examining whether it needs more infrastructure there for early warning and for satellite tracking. The ice sheet also holds the cores that climate scientists use to model sea level, which gives the island a weight beyond its minerals.

Greenland's own politics complicate every answer. Its government has argued for years that the island should move towards independence, and its parties disagree about whether that should come with a continued Danish subsidy or with a new arrangement that includes foreign investment. A sale to another country has no constituency in Nuuk, and a lease of land for a base is a different question that the island's politicians have been willing to discuss. Nuuk's coalition has said it wants a referendum on independence within a decade, a timetable Copenhagen has neither endorsed nor rejected.

European reaction was quick because the precedent is larger than the island. Denmark's partners in the Nordic council and in Brussels said in private that a member's territory had become a subject of another power's domestic politics, and that the alliance's northern flank would be weaker if the question stayed open. They also noted that Denmark had increased its own Arctic spending after the previous round of American interest in 2019.

A refusal of this kind is cheap to make and expensive to sustain. Denmark can say that the island stays Danish and cannot control the investment that flows in, the ships that pass, or the satellites that look down. What it can control is the legal position, and restating it clearly is the best available answer to a question that Washington keeps asking and that Copenhagen keeps declining. American interest is likely to return, because the Arctic is opening and because the minerals are needed.
