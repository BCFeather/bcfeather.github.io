---
title: "The Senate votes 100 to 0 to end normal trade relations with Russia"
date: 2022-04-07 20:05:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Two bills passed without a single dissent, one stripping Russia and Belarus of permanent normal trade treatment and one banning imports of Russian oil, gas and coal, which turned a partisan chamber into a unanimous one for an afternoon."
---

The Senate passed two bills on 7 April by identical votes of 100 to 0, one suspending permanent normal trade relations with Russia and Belarus and one banning imports of Russian oil, gas and coal. The unanimous tallies were the day's political news in themselves, since a chamber split fifty-fifty on almost everything found no senator willing to be recorded against either measure.

Trade legislation does what its title says and a little more. Withdrawal of normal trade relations means Russian goods lose the low tariffs that membership of the World Trade Organization had secured, and the legislation gives the president authority to raise duties in a way that the tariff schedule otherwise forbids, subject to a congressional review. It applies to Belarus as well, which matters because Belarusian potash and fertiliser have kept flowing into world markets. The suspension also ends the low-duty treatment that had let Russian aluminium, steel and fertiliser into the American market.

Energy legislation is narrower and more symbolic. Imports of Russian crude and refined products had already been stopped by executive order in March, and the legislation writes that ban into statute so that a future administration cannot lift it quietly. American refiners had taken only a small share of their crude from Russia, but the products market is integrated, and the ban touched imports of unfinished blending components as well, which refiners on the Gulf coast had used to stretch domestic crude.

Neither bill had been held up by disagreement about the goal. The delay came from procedure: the House had passed its versions weeks earlier, the Senate wanted amendments on trade remedies and on human rights provisions attached to the trade measure, and the two chambers had to reconcile them without losing votes. Leadership scheduled the votes for the same afternoon once the texts cleared, and senators who had wanted a price cap on Russian oil settled for a study of the idea, the usual compromise when a chamber wants to signal resolve without writing a hard rule.

What the unanimity means in practice is a delegation of the decision to the executive. Congress has now endorsed the direction and handed the mechanics of sanctions relief, tariff suspension and reauthorization to the Treasury and the trade representative, which is where the hard choices about exemptions, secondary sanctions and allied carve-outs will actually be made, over banks in third countries and over whether European allies accept the same tariff treatment.

Two hundred members of one chamber agreeing is rare enough to be worth noting, and the reason has nothing to do with the policy being modest. What explains it is that the cost falls on future trade talks rather than on any current industry, which lets every senator vote yes while leaving the difficult negotiation to officials who will have to persuade allies to accept the same rules. Whether those officials can hold the line on secondary sanctions will be tested by the next round of talks, and nobody in the chamber expects that test to be easy.
