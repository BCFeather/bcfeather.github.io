Six hundred thousand chips is not a number that fits easily in the imagination, so it helps to translate it into space. Reuters reported on 5 November, citing two people familiar with the matter, that roughly 600,000 of NVIDIA's H200 accelerators are stored in Hong Kong, waiting to be shipped to the mainland. The size of the stockpile had not previously been reported. Neither the Chinese government nor the American one nor NVIDIA has confirmed it, and China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology and the National Development and Reform Commission did not respond to requests for comment. Alibaba, ByteDance, DeepSeek and Tencent did not either.

The H200 is not NVIDIA's newest part, which is the first thing to understand about why it is sitting there. It is a Hopper-generation device, with 141 gigabytes of HBM3e memory at 4.8 terabytes per second, roughly twice the memory of the H100 and 1.4 times its bandwidth. Blackwell is newer and faster. But the H200 still has capabilities that Chinese alternatives have not matched, particularly when paired with NVIDIA's software stack, and it remains one of the strongest parts available for training large models. The chips are not obsolete. They are wanted, and they are stuck.

Two gates control the route, and they belong to different governments. Washington licenses the export: the Trump administration gave NVIDIA permission late last year to sell to approved Chinese customers after trade negotiations, and on 13 January 2026 the Bureau of Industry and Security replaced presumption of denial with case-by-case review for the H200 and comparable parts, subject to conditions on global capacity, on the buyer's compliance procedures and on third-party testing inside the United States. Beijing controls the import: large-volume shipments require approval, and that approval has not come, reportedly out of concern about dependence on American technology.

What does move is small. Three people told *Reuters* that China has allowed some H200s in over recent months, and one said several major Chinese internet companies had each obtained about 10,000 units. Three people described shipments through intermediaries and indirect routes, without saying how many. In July, *The Information* reported that officials had told Alibaba, ByteDance and DeepSeek they could receive permission for limited quantities for training, that total approvals might amount to fewer than 200,000 chips, and that Beijing wanted firms to use domestic processors for inference, the less demanding work of running a trained model.

The price of the gap is visible. An eight-card H200 server was offered in the mainland for more than 6 million yuan, about $900,000, by the end of October, roughly three times what it cost a year earlier. Servers built on Blackwell, which remains under tighter American controls, were offered at more than 20 million yuan apiece. That premium is not a market inefficiency. It is the price of a product that is legal to export and legal to buy but not legal to import at volume, and it is being paid by whoever needs the compute most.

Meanwhile the demand has not gone away, it has changed shape. *Reuters* reported in October that ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent had ordered more than 3 million RTX PRO 5500 cards, a weaker part that falls outside the controls, with a combined sticker price above $35 billion, bought mainly for inference. NVIDIA has told customers it cannot guarantee fulfilment at those volumes. Companies that cannot get the part they want buy the part they can, and the shortfall moves down the product line rather than disappearing.

The stockpile in Hong Kong is the physical form of a policy that neither capital has settled. Six hundred thousand accelerators in a warehouse are not computing capacity; they are inventory, depreciating, insured, and unusable until someone signs. Both governments can describe their position as prudent. Neither can describe the result as efficient, and the goods do not care which of them moves first.
