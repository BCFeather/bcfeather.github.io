The admission notices go out in the second half of July, and the arrival is tracked like a parcel. The courier calls ahead, the family gathers, the envelope is opened on camera or at least photographed on a table with the house key placed beside it, and the picture is posted before the contents have been read carefully. The university has designed the envelope for exactly this moment, which is why it is thick, printed in two colours and sealed with something that looks like a wax stamp.

The design competition is real, and it has a budget behind it that most academic departments would recognise as large. Universities commission illustrators, emboss the school gate, include a cloth-bound booklet, sometimes a small object that can be kept. The exercise is expensive and its purpose is marketing rather than administration, since the information inside is also available in a text message. A school that produces a memorable envelope gets a photograph in a family album and a post that circulates for a week, and the cost is charged to a recruitment budget that is judged on exactly that.

The delivery has its own logistics, and a small industry of couriers, photographers and printers has grown around it. Notices go out by courier to addresses across the country, and the addresses are not always the ones the student lives at. Many are sent to a grandparent's house in a smaller town, or to a relative who works at a place with a reception desk, because that is where someone will be present to receive them. The mismatch produces a few days of uncertainty in which the family knows the result but does not yet have the object.

The photograph that follows is now part of the ritual. The student holds the envelope, the parents stand behind, the light is adjusted, and the picture is taken on a phone that has been wiped clean for the purpose. It is a formal portrait of a transaction, and it is the last image of the student as a schoolchild. Beneath the ceremony there is a straightforward administrative fact, which is that the envelope is the only document in the whole process that the family keeps. The next photographs will be taken on a campus in September, in different clothes, with different people.

What the envelope does not contain is the thing the family has been arguing about since June. The decision was made in five days at a table, and the envelope confirms it, and confirmation is not the same as approval. There is a version of July in which the envelope arrives and the household is quietly disappointed, and the photograph is still taken, and nobody says so for four years.

The notices are getting thinner in some places and heavier in others, which is a fair summary of the sector. The universities that need the marketing spend the money, and the ones that do not need it send a plain envelope with a printed sheet inside. Both arrive in July, and both are opened with the same expression.
