---
title: "Two trains collide in Odisha and kill nearly three hundred"
date: 2023-06-02 18:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "A passenger train ran into a derailed coaches near Bahanaga Bazar in Odisha on 2 June, and the state government's count reached 296 dead and more than a thousand injured, the worst railway accident in India in more than two decades."
---

Shortly after seven on the evening of 2 June, the Coromandel Express, running from Kolkata towards Chennai, struck coaches that had already fallen onto its track near Bahanaga Bazar station in Balasore district, Odisha. A second train, travelling in the opposite direction, then hit the same wreckage. By the following morning the state government's count had reached 296 dead and more than a thousand injured, making it the worst railway accident in India since 1995 and the deadliest in the career of a network that carries more than twenty million people a day.

The immediate cause appears to have been a signalling failure. Preliminary investigations pointed to a route-setting error in the interlocking equipment that governs the station's loop lines, which sent the Coromandel Express onto a track where a goods train was standing rather than onto the through line. The collision derailed the passenger train's coaches into the path of the second service. India's railway board has said the electronic interlocking system was replaced at the station in recent years, and that the accident occurred during maintenance work.

What made the toll so high was the hour and the loading. The trains were full, as they always are on that corridor, and the coaches that took the impact were general compartments with unreserved passengers standing in the aisles. Rescue work continued through the night with cutting equipment, and the injured were distributed across hospitals in Balasore, Bhadrak and Cuttack. The state declared a day of mourning and the railway ministry announced an inquiry, compensation for the families of the dead and free treatment for the injured.

This accident has revived a familiar argument about the safety of the Indian network. The railways have invested heavily in track renewal and in the Kavach automatic train protection system, which is designed to brake a train before it passes a signal at danger, and the corridor where the accident happened had not yet been equipped with it. Deploying the system across the network is a programme measured in years and in tens of thousands of kilometres, and every serious accident since it began has been followed by the same question about how quickly it can be finished.

That question has an honest answer that the ministry does not like to give. India's railways are safer than they were a decade ago, and the number of accidents has fallen, and the network still runs on a mixture of modern signalling and equipment that predates the mobile phone. The gap has never been a secret; it is a budget line. What the families in Balasore will want to know, and what the inquiry will have to say, is whether the money that was spent on the corridor in the past five years went to the equipment that would have prevented this, and if it did not, why not.

An inquiry will take months, and its conclusions will be technical. What the state can do before then is publish the sections of the network that still lack automatic protection, and let passengers see the list for themselves.
