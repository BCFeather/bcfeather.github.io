title: "A secretary of state removes a name"
date: 2023-12-28 23:20:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Maine's secretary of state ruled on 28 December 2023 that Donald Trump is barred from the state's Republican primary by the insurrection clause, becoming the second state official to reach that conclusion and extending a legal argument from Colorado to the rest of the country."
---

Maine's secretary of state ruled on 28 December that Donald Trump could not appear on the state's Republican primary ballot, applying the third section of the Fourteenth Amendment. Shenna Bellows, a Democrat elected by the legislature, wrote a 34-page decision that followed the reasoning of the Colorado Supreme Court nine days earlier and added findings of her own about the events of 6 January 2021. She stayed her decision until the federal Supreme Court rules on the Colorado appeal, which means the practical effect was deferred rather than delivered.

The office is the difference from Colorado. In that state, a group of voters sued and a court decided; in Maine, the secretary of state is the official who qualifies candidates, and she acted on challenges filed by two former candidates. Her decision is therefore administrative, appealable to a state court, and taken by a person who is a Democrat in a state that has voted Democratic in every presidential election since 1992, which her critics said was the only relevant fact about her.

Her ruling repeated the evidence assembled in Colorado: the rally on the ellipse, the march to the Capitol, the slates of electors organized in seven states, the pressure on the vice president and on state officials, and the candidate's own statements in the hours afterwards. Bellows concluded that the events amounted to an insurrection and that the former president had engaged in it, and she rejected the argument that the clause applies only to officers of the United States and not to the presidency, the same argument the Colorado majority had dismissed.

Republican reaction was immediate and hostile. The state party asked a court to stay the decision, the campaign called the secretary an activist who had been elected by a legislature dominated by her own party, and the speakers in the House began to describe the rulings as an attempt to decide the election in the courts. The Supreme Court had already agreed on 22 December to hear the Colorado case on an expedited schedule, with oral argument in February and a decision expected before Super Tuesday in March.

The legal question that the nine justices will settle was put plainly by the Colorado dissent. Section three, they wrote, requires a determination of insurrection by somebody, and the Constitution gives that determination to Congress, which has never made it against this candidate, and to the courts only through legislation that has never been passed. The majority's answer was that the clause has been applied by courts and by officials since the 1860s without legislation, and that the text's silence is a grant of jurisdiction rather than a gap in it.

Whatever the Supreme Court decides, the pattern of December is worth noting. Two states reached the same conclusion by two different routes, a legal question that had been a footnote in constitutional law became the central issue of a presidential campaign, and the country's election machinery found itself arguing about a clause that had been written to keep Confederates out of office. The ruling will be technical; the reception of it will be anything but.
