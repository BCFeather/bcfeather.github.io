title: "Twitter's board accepts Musk's offer of 54 dollars and 20 cents a share"
date: 2022-04-25 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "The company agreed to a cash deal worth about forty-four billion dollars, with no financing condition and a break fee, hours after the buyer dropped his due diligence demands and the board concluded the price was fair."
---

Twitter's board accepted Elon Musk's offer on 25 April, agreeing to sell the company for 54 dollars and 20 cents a share in cash, about 44 billion dollars, and to take it private. The board said it had voted unanimously, after its advisers delivered fairness opinions, and that the price represented a premium of roughly 38 per cent over the closing level on 1 April, the last trading day before his stake became public.

The deal that was signed had changed shape in ten days. When Musk made the offer on 13 April, it carried conditions about financing and due diligence. By 20 April he had lined up commitments of about 46.5 billion dollars, including 21 billion of his own equity, 12.5 billion of margin loans secured against Tesla shares and bank debt from Morgan Stanley and other lenders, and he told the board in a filing that those conditions were gone. The diligence condition that would have let him walk away if the numbers disappointed him went with them.

Absence of a financing condition mattered to the seller. A cash offer that depends on the buyer raising money can fail at closing, and Twitter's advisers insisted on covenants requiring Musk to use his best efforts to obtain the financing, along with a right to be kept informed of progress. The agreement also carried a break fee of a billion dollars, payable by either side if the other walked away under specified conditions. The reverse fee applied if the buyer failed to close for reasons within his control, which is the clause that would matter in the autumn.

The price was the argument. Twitter's shares had closed at 39.31 dollars on 1 April, before the stake was disclosed, and had risen to about 50 after it, so most of the premium Musk advertised had already been delivered by the disclosure itself. Against the 70 dollars the stock had touched a year earlier, the offer looked cheap, and against the price of the preceding month it looked generous, which is the sort of gap that boards settle with fairness opinions rather than with conviction.

Musk's stated reasons were about speech and about spam. He said he wanted to make the platform a better home for free expression, to publish its ranking algorithms, and to defeat the automated accounts that inflate engagement, and in the announcement he described Twitter as the digital town square where matters vital to the future of humanity are debated. Shareholders were told the deal would close during 2022, subject to a vote and to regulators. That promise ran into the reality of a private company's trade secrets.

Completion was never the likeliest outcome, though the contract was written as if it were. The agreement bound Musk to a specific price, gave Twitter the right to sue for specific performance, and removed the financing escape that buyers usually keep. Three months later he would argue that the company had misled him about bots, and the case would spend the autumn in a Delaware court before he bought the company anyway.
