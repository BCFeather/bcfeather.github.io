---
title: "Six and a half metres of gold: Science names the Webb telescope its breakthrough"
date: 2022-12-15 18:00:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "Science names the James Webb Space Telescope its breakthrough of 2022, after a first year of images and disputed atmosphere results."
---
Announcement came from the journal on 15 December, and the choice was the least surprising of its recent decisions. The telescope had been launched on Christmas Day in 2021, and its first full-colour images had been published in July. Between those two dates the instrument had unfolded in space, cooled to its operating temperature and begun to work. Each of those steps had been designed to be performed without a human hand nearby, and any of them could have ended the mission.

The mirror is the size of the claim. Eighteen hexagonal segments, gold-coated, combine into a surface six and a half metres across, and they have to hold their shape to a fraction of the wavelength of the light they collect. Engineers adjusted them in space with motors that move in steps of a few nanometres, and the alignment was completed in weeks rather than in the months that had been allowed. The surface has to stay in shape while the telescope turns and while the temperature of the structure changes. One segment had to be driven through more than a hundred steps before the figure was right.

What the telescope sees follows from that precision. Its infrared instruments can detect light that has travelled for more than thirteen billion years, and the first deep field image contained galaxies whose light left when the universe was a few hundred million years old. The same instruments read the atmospheres of planets around other stars, because a molecule leaves a signature in the spectrum of the light that passes through it. Water, carbon dioxide and clouds have all been reported in the first year, and each report was argued over within days.

The first year of results was crowded. Astronomers reported water in the atmosphere of a planet a thousand light years away, clouds on another, and a cluster of galaxies whose age forced a revision of the models. Some of those results were contested within weeks, which is how a healthy field behaves. The journal's news staff noted that the telescope had produced papers at a rate that no other facility had matched in its first months.

Scientists who had spent their careers on the project described the year in interviews as the payoff for two decades of work. The telescope had survived budget overruns, a redesign, a launch delay and a pandemic, and the instrument that reached the launch pad had been assembled from parts made in a dozen countries. Its sunshield, the size of a tennis court, was folded for launch and opened in space without a tear, a manoeuvre that had been rehearsed on the ground more than a hundred times. The programme had come close to cancellation twice, and the people who saved it were, for the most part, not astronomers.

Runners-up included the Artemis mission, a vaccine against respiratory syncytial virus and the progress in protein structure prediction. None of them had produced pictures that a newspaper could print on its front page, and pictures are part of what a breakthrough means now.
