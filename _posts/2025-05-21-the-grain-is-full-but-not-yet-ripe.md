Around 21 May each year comes Xiaoman, the eighth of the twenty-four solar terms, and the only one whose name describes something unfinished. The classical gloss is that at this point the grain is small and full: the wheat has filled out its kernels, the ears are heavy, and none of it is ready to eat. The name is a description of a stage, and the stage is the point.

The twenty-four terms are a farming calendar dressed as poetry, and most of them are unambiguous. Grain in ear, white dew, frost descent, the great cold: each names a condition the land has reached. Xiaoman names a condition the land has reached and cannot leave. For about two weeks the wheat is at its maximum weight and its minimum usefulness. It is full, and it is not ripe, and there is nothing anyone can do about either fact.

That interval has a moral attached to it, and the moral is old. In Chinese letters the term is read as a lesson about sufficiency: small fullness is enough, great fullness invites loss, and the pursuit of the second is where people go wrong. The reading is a commonplace, repeated in seasonal essays and in the sort of calligraphy that hangs in offices. It is not wrong, and it is also not what the wheat is doing. The wheat is simply in the middle of a process that has a schedule it did not choose.

The more interesting reading is about patience as a technical matter. A farmer watching a field of full, unripe wheat is watching a quantity that will not improve by harvesting early and will be ruined by harvesting late. The two weeks of Xiaoman are the two weeks in which the correct action is to do nothing, and doing nothing on a farm is harder than it sounds, because there is always a task that looks urgent and a neighbour who has started.

The term has outlived its agriculture. Very few of the people who use the word now have any relationship to a wheat field, and the calendar survives because it gives the year texture, a set of names for the passing of time that the ordinary week does not provide. In that role Xiaoman does something the other terms do not. It marks the moment when the year is at its fullest and its results are still unknown: the spring has been planted, the summer has not yet been harvested, and everything that will be decided later is still undecided.

That is a reasonable thing to have a name for. The other terms describe weather. This one describes a state of affairs that applies equally to a field, a term of study, a project, and a life: the point at which the work is done and the outcome has not arrived. The wheat will be ripe soon enough. For two weeks, it is full, and that is all it is. The wheat does not know it has become a metaphor, which may be the reason the metaphor survives.
