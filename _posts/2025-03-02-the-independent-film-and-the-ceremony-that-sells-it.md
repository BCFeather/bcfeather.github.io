The ninety-seventh Academy Awards were handed out on the second of March, and the best picture prize went to a film that was shot on a budget reported at around six million dollars, financed partly by a French sales company and released by a distributor with a staff small enough to fit in one room. The ceremony that celebrated it is the most expensive marketing event in the film industry, and the thing it marketed this year was the case for the small film.

The economics of that case are worth spelling out, because the awards are usually discussed as art and they are also a business. A studio film costs a hundred million dollars or more and must earn several times that to repay the investment. A film at six million can break even on a modest release and a streaming sale, and it can afford to be strange, because its audience does not have to be everybody. The prize does not change those numbers. It changes the odds that the next strange film gets financed.

The campaign that precedes the ceremony is a months-long operation and it is expensive in a way that the ceremony hides. Screeners are sent to several thousand voters, parties are held, interviews are arranged, and a distributor that intends to compete spends a sum that can exceed the budget of the film it is promoting. The films that cannot afford the campaign are the ones that the ceremony rarely reaches, which is a structural bias that no amount of good intentions removes.

What the evening itself does is convert attention into tickets. The winners add screens in the weeks that follow, the streaming platforms feature the titles, and the foreign markets that waited for a verdict now buy. What the prize is worth, then, is the several million dollars of free advertising that arrives with it, and the statuette is the receipt. A distributor calculates that value precisely before deciding how much to spend on the campaign.

The ceremony has also become a television product with its own problems. The broadcast lost its host for two years and then hired one back, the running time is negotiated with the network, and the speeches are timed by a clock that the winners can see. The audience for the broadcast has declined for a decade in the way that every live award show has declined, and the industry keeps the show because the advertising that surrounds it is worth more than the audience rating suggests.

What the night leaves is a list, and the list is the thing that survives. In a year the winners will be a line in a database, the speeches will be clips, and the argument about whether the right film won will be settled by nobody. What will remain useful is the record of what a small film with no stars could do in a market that keeps saying such films are impossible, and that record is the reason the ceremony still matters to the people who make them.
