---
title: "A fusion reaction produces more energy than the lasers delivered to it"
date: 2022-12-05 21:00:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "The National Ignition Facility produces more energy from a fusion reaction than the laser delivered to the target, a result announced a week later."
---
An experiment on 5 December at a facility built to study nuclear weapons without exploding them produced the result. A target the size of a peppercorn, filled with deuterium and tritium, was placed at the centre of a chamber the size of a sports hall. One hundred and ninety-two laser beams converged on it, and for a few billionths of a second the fuel compressed and burned. The energy released by the fusion reaction was greater than the energy that the lasers had delivered to the target. The facility had been chasing that result for twelve years, and the shot that produced it was one of a series designed to test a new target design.

The numbers were small and historic at the same time. The lasers gave about two megajoules to the capsule, and the reaction produced about three point one five. A gain of roughly one and a half had been predicted for years by the computer codes, and the codes had been wrong before. This shot was the first in which the prediction came true, and officials described it afterwards as the first controlled fusion experiment to reach scientific energy breakeven.

Qualification matters here, and the laboratory was careful to make it. Two hundred megajoules of electricity went into the lasers to produce the two megajoules that arrived at the target, so the facility as a whole consumed far more than it produced. A power station would need a hundred times the gain achieved here before the electricity it sold covered the electricity it used. Nobody in the building pretended that a grid connection was imminent.

The announcement came at a press conference in Washington on 13 December, where the energy secretary called it one of the most impressive scientific feats of the century. Reporters asked about weapons, because the facility exists to study them, and about climate, because fusion produces no long-lived waste. The director answered both questions and repeated that the result was a step rather than a solution. The energy department's own laboratories had published the result in a paper before the announcement, and the reviewers had asked for the laser energy to be stated in the same sentence as the fusion yield.

Fuel supply is the next constraint, and it is easier to state than to solve. Tritium is rare, it decays, and it is produced in reactors that are themselves expensive. The targets have to be manufactured to a tolerance measured in microns, and each shot destroys one of them. A power plant would need a target every few seconds, and the present facility fires a few times a year. The laboratory has since said that it will need several hundred targets a year to keep the programme running, and that the factory that makes them is being expanded.

Engineering has a longer road than physics, and both are long. What the shot establishes is that the physics works, that the codes can be trusted, and that the energy released by fusion can exceed the energy delivered to the fuel. Everything after that is a question of machines, money and time.
