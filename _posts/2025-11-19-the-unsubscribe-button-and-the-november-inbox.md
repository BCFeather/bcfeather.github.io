By the third week of November the inbox has quietly become a different place. The ordinary mail from people who know you is still there, but it is buried under a second layer of mail from companies that do not. Each message carries a subject line engineered for the moment: a countdown, a threshold, a price that is about to change, a reminder that the cart has been waiting. They arrive in waves, timed to the retail calendar, and the small link at the bottom of each one that says unsubscribe is doing more work than it was designed for. It has become the last quiet protest left to the reader.

The mechanics of the thing are well understood by now. A year ago you bought a jacket, or you entered an email to read an article, or you abandoned a cart at two in the morning, and on the basis of that single transaction you were added to a list. The list has grown, because the industry that sends this mail measures engagement in fractions of a second and treats every address as a resource to be mined. The mail is cheap to send, which means the marginal message costs almost nothing, which means the marginal message is sent regardless of whether you ever open it. The unsubscribe button is not a courtesy the sender offers. It is a legal minimum, and it is placed where it is placed precisely because most people will not use it.

What is interesting is what the button actually costs. To unsubscribe you must click through to a page, wait for it to load, confirm that you really mean it, and sometimes answer a survey about why you are leaving. The design is friction by intent: every extra step is a chance to talk you out of going, a chance to show you one last offer, a chance to re-earn your address. By the time you reach the confirmation screen, the five seconds it takes have already told the sender something about you, and the address is sometimes simply labelled suppressed rather than removed. The button exists, in other words, and yet it does not quite exist.

The honest response is not to blame the retailers. The calendar of sales is the calendar of revenue, and a company that stops sending mail in November will be the company whose competitors do not. The pressure that produces the flood is upstream, in the expectations placed on quarterly results and in the low cost of sending another message. What is worth noticing is the reader's side of it: the small, repeated decision to open a message, the split second spent deciding whether to delete or unsubscribe, the slow accumulation of a habit of ignoring anything that looks promotional. The inbox, once a place where things arrived to be read, has become a place where things arrive to be dismissed.

There is a larger point hiding inside the small inconvenience. Attention is the resource that the retail calendar is now explicitly competing for, and the competition has expanded to fill every channel that has a slot. The inbox is only the oldest of them; the social feeds are worse, and the push notifications are worse still. The unsubscribe button matters because it is one of the few places left where the reader can opt out in writing, without an argument, without a phone call. It is small, and it is deliberately hard to find, and yet, used often enough, it still works. The November inbox will not stop flooding this year, or next, but every unsubscribe click is a sentence the reader gets to write back.
