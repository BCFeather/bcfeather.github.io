---
title: "Spain starts checking arrivals from Italy as Ceuta fills with migrants"
date: 2026-08-08 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "About 80,000 people crossed from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta after a court ruling was misread online as an amnesty. Italy suspended Schengen with Spain, and from 8 August Madrid imposed its own checks on travellers arriving from Italy."
---

Roughly 80,000 people crossed from Morocco into Ceuta in a few weeks, and the Spanish enclave on the North African coast has become the centre of a European argument about borders. The immediate cause was a misreading. In July the Supreme Court ruled that migrants who reach Ceuta and Melilla by sea cannot be returned immediately, and social media turned that judgment into a promise of eventual legal status. By the time officials tried to correct the record, the traffic had already started.

Italy responded by suspending the Schengen arrangement with Spain and restoring checks at its borders, a step that Finland and Denmark said they supported. France tightened its own controls, and several governments asked Madrid to defend what the union calls its external border. Spain then announced that from 8 August to 7 September it would check travellers arriving from Italy at airports and ports, and Rome said its own restrictions would run at least to the middle of the month.

The legal point at issue has been buried under the political one. The court did not grant an amnesty; it held that summary returns from the enclaves lack a sufficient legal basis, which leaves Spanish authorities with procedures that take longer than the arrivals. Lawyers have pointed out that the ruling applies to individual cases and creates no collective right to stay. A rumour travelled faster than the judgment, and thousands of people acted on the rumour before any government could correct it. Spanish officials have since said that reception centres in the enclave were full within a week and that the police were processing a few hundred cases a day against arrivals in the thousands.

The numbers behind the crisis are unglamorous. Irregular crossings at the union's external borders fell sharply in recent years but still reached about 178,000 in 2025, and the migration and asylum pact became fully applicable only in June. Border procedures, reception capacity and return agreements all depend on member states that have not finished implementing them, and a pact that exists on paper cannot process people who arrive at a fence.

Domestic politics does the rest. Parties of the far right in Italy, Finland and elsewhere have built their support on exactly this issue, and every new arrival at an enclave becomes an argument in a national election somewhere else. Spain, which has taken a comparatively open line, now finds itself accused of exporting disorder to its neighbours, and a government that prides itself on solidarity has spent the summer explaining why it is checking Italians at its ports. Madrid has also asked the commission to treat the enclaves as a European border rather than a Spanish one, a request that would put the cost and the legal burden on the union as a whole.

The damage to Schengen may outlast the queue at the fence. A union that suspends free movement between two of its members over a misread court ruling has demonstrated how thin that freedom's political foundations are, and the next crisis will start from a lower base of trust.
