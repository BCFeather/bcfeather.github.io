---
title: "Europe's economy stalls as industry shrinks"
date: 2023-08-17 22:45:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Euro zone output grew just 0.3 per cent in the second quarter of 2023 and German industrial production fell again in June, leaving the currency area close to stagnation while the European Central Bank weighs another rate rise."
---

Eurostat confirmed on 16 August that output in the euro area grew by 0.3 per cent between April and June, a figure that looks adequate until it is set beside the first quarter's fall and the flat reading in Germany, the bloc's largest economy. Industrial production fell 1.5 per cent in June across the currency area, and in Germany it fell for a second month as orders from abroad dried up. France grew, Spain grew faster, and Italy shrank. The composite purchasing managers' index, published earlier in the month, pointed to a private sector that has stopped growing.

The causes are structural and cyclical at once. China's slowdown has reduced demand for German machinery and cars, energy prices remain far above their pre-war level, and the European Central Bank has raised rates nine times in a year. Construction has been hit hardest, because mortgages in much of the bloc are priced off short-term rates, and housing permits in Germany have fallen to their lowest level in a decade. Retail sales have been flat since the spring.

Inflation explains why the bank has not stopped. Consumer prices rose 5.3 per cent in the year to July, down from the peak but still far above the two per cent target, and services inflation has proved stickier than goods inflation. Wage settlements in Germany and France have been generous by the standards of the past decade, and the bank's council has said it is watching unit labour costs. Its deposit rate stands at 3.75 per cent, the highest since the currency was created. Markets expect a tenth increase in September or an explicit promise to hold rates at their present level for a long time.

Fiscal policy has moved in the opposite direction. Germany's coalition agreed a budget for next year that cuts spending and borrows less, in keeping with the debt brake written into its constitution, and Italy has been slower to spend the money it was allocated from the European recovery fund. The result is an economy where the central bank is tightening and the governments are consolidating, which is the combination that turns a slowdown into a recession. Italy has spent barely a third of the recovery money it was allocated, and Germany has been arguing about whether to loosen its own borrowing rule.

The labour market is the counter-argument. Unemployment across the bloc is at 6.4 per cent, its lowest on record, and firms in Germany report shortages of skilled workers even as order books shrink. That is why forecasters expect a shallow downturn rather than a deep one, and why the bank can keep raising rates without fearing an immediate collapse in demand.

A currency area of three hundred and fifty million people has produced almost no growth for six months, and its industrial core is shrinking. The bank will decide in September whether the fight against inflation still requires higher rates, and the answer will depend on data that has so far refused to settle.
