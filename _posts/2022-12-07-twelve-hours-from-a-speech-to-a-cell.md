The crisis began shortly before noon on 7 December, when the president appeared on television and announced that he was dissolving congress. He said he would rule by decree until a new constitution could be written, and he called on the armed forces to support him. The broadcast lasted a few minutes, and by the time it ended the country's institutions had begun to move against him. The speech began at half past eleven in the morning and lasted fewer than ten minutes.

Congress met within the hour in a building a few blocks away, and it voted to remove him for moral incapacity, the same ground it had used twice before. The vote was swift and large enough to be called unanimous by the television channels, and the resolution was published while the president was still in the palace. Ministers resigned in a stream of statements, and the ones who did not resign were sacked or simply stopped answering their telephones. The constitutional court rejected the dissolution within hours, and the congress had already scheduled a session for the afternoon.

Police arrested him that afternoon on his way to a diplomatic mission where he hoped to claim asylum. He was taken to a police station and then to a prison in Lima, and prosecutors said he would face charges of rebellion and of violating the constitution. His family left the palace in a convoy of cars, and the television pictures showed the gates being closed behind them. Dina Boluarte, the vice president, was sworn in at four in the afternoon in a session held in the congress building. She was the sixth president in six years, and the first woman to hold the office. In her first speech she said that she would serve until the term ended in 2026, that she would form a government of national unity, and that she would call elections only if the congress agreed. Police units surrounded the palace, and prosecutors announced that the former president would be held while they prepared charges.

The country that woke up on 8 December was governed by a president nobody had voted for, with a congress that had just demonstrated how easily it could remove one. Peru had been through five presidencies in six years, and the pattern had a shape: a president elected with a narrow margin, a congress determined to obstruct, and a crisis resolved by impeachment rather than by negotiation. The currency opened lower and recovered by the afternoon, and the stock exchange closed almost flat.

Protests began the same week in the southern provinces where Castillo had won his largest majorities. Roads were blocked, airports closed, and the government declared a state of emergency in parts of the country. By the end of the month people had died in clashes with the police, and the question of when to hold the next election had become the central argument in Lima.
