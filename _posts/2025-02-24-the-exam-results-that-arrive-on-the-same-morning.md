The results of the postgraduate entrance examination were released from the twenty-fourth of February, and the morning is a national event with a number attached to it. The candidates log in to a provincial portal at the hour that the province announced, the page that loads shows a set of scores and a rank, and the number that follows the candidate for the next month is decided in that minute.

The examination is the largest of its kind in the country, and the number of candidates registered for this year's sitting was about three point nine million, a fall from the year before. The fall is the first in nearly a decade, and the reasons that the analysts give are the labour market and the cost of a further degree rather than a decline in the appetite for study.

The score is not the only number that matters. The candidate also needs the national line that the ministry publishes in March, and the line is a threshold that decides whether the application can proceed at all. The universities then set their own thresholds above it, and a candidate who clears the national line can still fail a departmental one.

The interview follows the score. A candidate who reaches the second stage is examined by a panel, and the panel weighs the written test, the interview and the undergraduate record. The weightings differ by department and they are published in a document that the candidates read carefully and often misunderstand.

The preparation industry is built around the uncertainty. A tutoring course sells a schedule, a set of practice papers and a forecast of the line, and the forecast is a service that the candidates pay for because the alternative is to wait for March. The industry is large, and it is regulated unevenly.
 
The morning itself has a sociology. The screenshot of a score is sent to a family group within minutes, the forum that hosts the discussion fills with the numbers, and the candidates who did badly go quiet. The public ritual of the result is a part of the examination that the designers did not intend and that the culture supplies.

What the day shows is that a single examination organizes a year of a young person's life. The preparation begins in the spring, the sitting is in December, the result is in February and the admission is in April, and the calendar is the same for three million people. The number that appears on the portal is the pivot of that calendar, and the rest of the year follows from it.

The universities also publish the number of places that they will fill, and the places have not grown as fast as the candidates. The competition is therefore concentrated in a small group of institutions, and the candidates who apply to the second tier are making a calculation about the return on a degree in a labour market that has more graduates than it had a decade ago.
