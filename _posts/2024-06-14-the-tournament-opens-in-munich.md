The European championship opened in Munich this evening. Twenty-four teams qualified, drawn into six groups of four. Fifty-one matches will be played in ten German cities over a month. The first of them ended five-one.

The format has grown since the tournament was sixteen teams. A group stage of three matches per team, then a round of sixteen. The expansion was designed to bring more countries into the competition and it has also brought more matches of low consequence. A team can lose two games and still advance.

The host cities are spread across the country. Munich, Berlin, Dortmund, Gelsenkirchen, Frankfurt, Hamburg, Stuttgart, Düsseldorf, Cologne, Leipzig. Each has a stadium of at least forty thousand seats. The travel between them is by train, and the railway has promised extra services that it will struggle to provide.

The tickets were sold in phases through a portal. The cheapest group-stage seat cost about thirty euros, the final cost several hundred. A fan who bought in the first phase paid less than a fan who bought in the last one, and the resale market did the rest.

The fan zones are the part that costs the cities money. A square closed for a month, a screen, a stage, a set of toilets, a security contract. Munich expects a million visitors in the zone over the tournament. The city earns it back in beer and hotel tax, or it does not.

The squads are young this year. Several of the favourites brought players who are twenty-one. A tournament played at the end of a long club season rewards a squad with fresh legs, and the coaches have noticed.

The football itself has become more controlled. A team that wins the ball high up the pitch scores within eight seconds or gives it back. The statistics that the broadcasters show have changed to match, and the number of passes in the final third is now printed beside the score.

The first match set a tone that the tournament will struggle to repeat. The host scored in the first ten minutes, the crowd sang for an hour, and the opposition was beaten by four goals. The second match is tomorrow afternoon.

The broadcast is the largest part of the tournament's economy. The rights were sold to a handful of networks for a sum that dwarfs the ticket revenue. A match kicks off at nine in the evening so that it lands in prime time across the continent. A viewer in Asia watches at three in the morning, and pays for the privilege through a subscription.

The security bill is carried by the cities. A match day brings eighty thousand people to a stadium and twice that to the surrounding streets. The police draft officers from other regions, close the approach roads four hours before kick-off. Bags are searched at the gates by a contractor. The cost is counted afterwards, in a report that nobody reads. A city that hosts four matches spends more on policing than it earns on the tickets it was allotted.
