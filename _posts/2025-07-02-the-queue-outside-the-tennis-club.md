title: "The queue outside the tennis club"
date: 2025-07-02 21:15:00 +0800
categories: [sport]
description: "The oldest tennis tournament in the world keeps a queue instead of a ticket ballot for its standing places. People camp overnight on a field in south-west London for the chance to watch an afternoon of grass-court tennis, and the queue has become as much of an institution as the tournament itself."
---

The tournament in south-west London began in the last week of June and will end in the middle of July, and for those two weeks a field beside the club becomes a small town. People arrive with tents and sleeping bags and join a line that moves a few hundred places each morning. The line is the point. The tournament sells most of its seats in advance through a ballot, and it holds a few hundred places each day for anyone willing to stand in a queue overnight, first come, first served.

The arrangement is old and deliberately inefficient. A modern ticketing system would sell those places in the time it takes to load a page, and would also, within about four seconds, hand all of them to software. The queue prevents that. A person has to be physically present in a camping chair at six in the morning, which is a filter that no bot can pass, and the filter is why the queue survives in an era when everything else has been digitised.

What the queue produces is a population that the tournament could not otherwise assemble. Students, retired couples, people who have flown in from another continent with a tent in a suitcase, and a steady supply of first-timers who have been told by someone that this is the way to see the place. The line has its own etiquette, its own stewards, its own lost property, and a printed card that a person can hold up to establish their place while they go and find coffee.

The days are long and mostly uneventful. The queue is not a party. It is a wait, conducted in a field, in weather that ranges from pleasant to absurd, and the people in it spend most of their time reading, sleeping badly, and talking to the strangers in the next tent because there is nothing else to do. That last part is the reason the institution has defenders. A person who has shared a queue for twenty hours with a stranger from Osaka has had a conversation they would not otherwise have had.

The economics of it are worth noting. A ground pass bought in the queue costs less than a single afternoon at any other major tournament, and the cheapness is part of the design. The club has decided that access should be rationed by patience rather than by price, and it has maintained that decision for decades while the rest of the sport moved in the other direction.

Whether the queue can survive is a separate question. It requires land, staff and the tolerance of a local authority, and all three are under pressure in a city where a field beside a tennis club is worth more as flats. For now it survives, and on a warm evening in early July the line is longer than it was last year, which is either a sign that the institution is beloved or a sign that the alternative has become too expensive.
