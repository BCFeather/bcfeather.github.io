---
title: "The first morning of the dragon year"
date: 2024-02-11 20:48:00 +0800
categories: [essay]
description: "Notes on the morning after the Spring Festival in a household where the firecrackers are over, the red paper is swept up, and the day has nothing to do but be quiet."
---

New Year's morning arrives without an alarm and without anyone deciding when to get up. The firecrackers have been going since midnight and stopped sometime after one, and the street outside is covered in a layer of red paper that will be swept into the corner of the yard before breakfast. In the house the heating is on, the television is on with the volume low, and the adults are asleep in chairs. The first morning of the dragon year is the only morning of the year when nobody is late for anything.

The night before is the loud one. There is the reunion dinner, which in this house means a table that has been extended with a board so that eleven people can sit at it, and a menu that has not changed in any important way in forty years. There is a whole fish, which is not eaten entirely because the word for the leftover carries the meaning of surplus, and there are dumplings folded by three generations at the same table, each with a different opinion about how much filling is correct. There is a red envelope for every child, handed over with a number that is chosen for its sound rather than its size.

By ten in the morning the house has started to move. Someone puts on water for tea, someone else opens the back door to let the smoke out, and the cousins go into the yard to see what the firecrackers did to the paving. The paper is thick on the ground and the smell of it is the smell of the whole holiday. In past years the debris would have been left until the second day, because sweeping on the first day was said to sweep the luck out of the house. That rule has been quietly abandoned, along with most of them, and someone sweeps.

The visiting starts after eleven. In a small city the first day is for the families who live within a few streets, and the order is fixed by custom and by who has the better tea. People arrive with fruit and leave with fruit, and the conversation is almost entirely about who has married, who has moved, and which of the children has grown. Nobody says anything about work. It is understood that the first day is not for that, and the questions about salary and school places begin on the second day, when the obligation of politeness has been discharged.

In the afternoon the house empties out and the television becomes the only sound. The gala is repeated, the heating ticks, and the light through the window is the flat white light of a February afternoon in the Yangtze valley. Someone is asleep on the sofa with a phone face down on their chest. The year has begun, and it began quietly, after a night that was loud enough to make the quiet feel earned. Nobody in the house would describe the feeling as happiness. It is closer to relief, and it lasts about a day and a half.
