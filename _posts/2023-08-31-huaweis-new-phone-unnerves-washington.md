---
title: "Huawei's new phone unnerves Washington"
date: 2023-08-31 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "Huawei began selling the Mate 60 Pro in China on 29 August 2023 with a chip made by the domestic foundry SMIC, a device that appeared to offer 5G speeds without the American components that export rules were meant to deny it."
---

Huawei put the Mate 60 Pro on sale in China on 29 August without a launch event, and the first teardowns followed within days. The phone contains a processor labelled Kirin 9000s, made by Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation, the mainland foundry, on a process that analysts describe as a seven-nanometre equivalent. Tests published by users showed download speeds in the range that only a 5G connection delivers. Huawei has not said how it made the chip, and the company's representatives have declined to discuss the supply chain.

Timing made the point. Gina Raimondo, the American commerce secretary, was in Beijing for meetings that week, and she had said before travelling that restrictions on the sale of advanced chips to China would remain. The phone appeared while she was in the country, and Chinese state media covered it as an answer to the export controls that Washington has been tightening since 2019, when Huawei was placed on a list that barred it from buying American technology.

Controls were designed to stop exactly this. American rules restrict the sale of chips and of the equipment and software needed to make them, and they reach beyond American companies to foreign firms whose products contain American technology. SMIC has been on the American list since 2020, and it has been able to buy some tools and not others. Analysts who have examined the die say it shows the marks of a manufacturing process that has been pushed beyond its intended limits, which is a technical achievement and an expensive one.

Washington's response has been cautious. The commerce department said it would gather information and would not hesitate to act, and officials have noted that the chip's yield and cost remain unknown. American chipmakers have argued for years that controls that are too broad push Chinese firms to build their own capacity, and the Mate 60 has given that argument a photograph. NVIDIA, which sells the accelerators that artificial intelligence work depends on, has been designing cut-down versions of its products for the Chinese market to stay inside the rules.

For consumers the phone matters for other reasons. It is a flagship with a satellite call feature, a camera system built with a German optics partner, and a screen made in China, and it sold out in several cities within days. Huawei's share of the Chinese smartphone market had fallen after the sanctions, and the Mate 60 has been presented at home as proof that the company can compete without American parts. Pre-orders in the first week were reported by Chinese retailers to have passed a million units, and shops in Shenzhen ran out of stock before the weekend.

A handset that nobody announced has become the most consequential piece of technology politics of the year. The chip inside it may be costly and may be hard to make in volume, and the message it carries is the one that export controls were written to prevent, which is why the device is being taken apart in laboratories in three countries.
