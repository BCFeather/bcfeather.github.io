description: "Father's Day arrived with a smaller shop window than Mother's Day and the same forwarded messages. A short piece on a holiday that nobody quite knows how to spend, and on the gifts that get bought because the alternative is worse."

The third Sunday of June arrives quietly. The flower shops do not build a display for it. The restaurants run a promotion but they do not print a special menu. The greeting is forwarded in the chat groups in the same way it is forwarded in May, with the same clip art and the same number of exclamation marks, and the reply is usually a thumbs-up.

The commercial asymmetry is old and easy to explain. Flowers have a purchase attached to them, and the purchase is cheap, repeatable and photogenic. A gift for a father has none of those properties. The obvious objects are razors, belts, wallets and bottles, and all four are things a man buys for himself when he needs one. There is no threshold at which a father becomes a person who requires a second wallet.

So the day gets spent on food instead. A restaurant booked for lunch, a table of six, a photograph taken by the waiter who does not know how to work the phone. The meal is the safest possible gift, because it is also a gift to everyone else at the table, and nobody has to pretend to like it.

There is a father's version of the day that is worth naming. The people who are not being celebrated. The men whose children are far away and whose calls are shorter than they would like. The men who are stepfathers and are never quite sure whether the day belongs to them. The men for whom the whole apparatus of family photographs is something to be endured rather than enjoyed. The day has no ceremony for any of that. The cards are designed for fathers who are present, available and pleased to be photographed, and the catalogue of greetings contains nothing adequate for the other cases.

What the day does well is smaller than what May does. It provides a date on which a phone call is expected, which removes the awkwardness of making one for no reason. For a lot of men of a certain generation, this is the whole value of the holiday. The call is short, the topics are the weather and the price of vegetables, and both parties hang up satisfied, having said nothing important and having said the thing that mattered.

The gifts will still be bought. A pair of shoes in the right size, a tool that will be used twice, a bottle that will be saved for a visitor. They are not good gifts and they are not the point. The shop assistant who sells them knows this. Nobody has ever been enthusiastic about a wallet on the day it was given, and the wallet is still, twenty years later, the object that gets kept. The point is the walk to the shop, the wrapping paper, and the moment when the object is handed over and the recipient says something dismissive about the expense while meaning the opposite. The holiday is built out of that exchange, and it works, which is more than most holidays manage.
