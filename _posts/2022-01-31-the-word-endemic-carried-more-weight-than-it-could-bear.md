---
title: "The word endemic carried more weight than it could bear this month"
date: 2022-01-31 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [essay]
description: "Politicians and newspapers spent January using a term from epidemiology as a synonym for over, and the term has never meant that."
---

January was the month in which the word endemic did most of its work. Denmark lifted nearly all restrictions, Britain prepared to, Spain's government argued that the disease should be treated like flu, and each announcement carried the same word somewhere in the coverage, as though a classification had produced a policy rather than the other way round. The word was doing the job of a conclusion. The better question is what level of illness a health system can absorb, and that is a number that depends on beds, staff and money rather than on a word.

Endemic means that a disease circulates at a predictable level in a population. It says nothing about how high that level is, and it says nothing about whether the level is acceptable. Malaria is endemic in parts of Africa and kills hundreds of thousands of children a year. Tuberculosis is endemic almost everywhere and remains one of the leading causes of death. A disease can be endemic and still be the worst thing that happens to a country in a decade.

Confusion comes from the way the word is used in ordinary speech. People say that something has become endemic when they mean that it has become ordinary, and ordinary in a conversation about a virus sounds like a prelude to normality. Epidemiologists use it to mean stable, and stability is a property of the transmission, not a verdict about the cost. Several countries that lifted restrictions in January reimposed some of them in the spring, which is what a predictable disease does when a new variant arrives.

Politics of January needed that ambiguity. Governments that had imposed restrictions for two years wanted a word that sounded like expertise and meant relief, and the word obliged. Omicron was moving fast through populations with some immunity, hospitalisations were lower relative to cases than they had been in earlier waves, and the charts that had terrified everyone for two years were beginning to bend in a direction that permitted optimism. That is a question about capacity and about money, and it is the one the word was being used to avoid.

The charts, though, leave much out. A variant that infects more people can produce more hospitalisations even at a lower rate, and the immunity that makes the wave look mild is an immunity acquired at the cost of the previous waves. The countries that declared an end in January were in most cases the ones with the highest vaccination coverage and the best surveillance, which is a description of a lucky position and not a description of an endpoint.

Language is where policy gets decided in advance, and this month argued it well. A word that means predictable was used to mean finished, and the people who used it were hoping rather than lying, which is the more common case. It would be worth remembering that a disease becomes ordinary only in the sense that we have stopped counting, and stopping is a choice we make rather than a fact we discover.
