title: "The year the streets were quiet"
date: 2023-01-21 23:30:00 +0800

description: "A note on the last day of the lunar year, on a fifteenth birthday, and on a city that had stopped letting off fireworks."
---

The last day of the lunar year fell on a Saturday, and it was also my birthday. I turned fifteen in the afternoon, in the kitchen, while my mother was rolling out the dough for the dumplings and my father was upstairs looking for the tape for the couplets. Nobody sang anything. There was a bowl of filling on the table and a bowl of flour on the counter, and that was the ceremony. The couplets were red with gold characters, and the one my father chose said something about peace, and he read it aloud before he taped it up.

The street outside was quieter than it had been in my childhood. The city had restricted fireworks for several years by then, and the restriction had done what restrictions do: it had moved the noise to the edges of the town and left the centre to the television. At midnight I could hear the neighbours' set through the wall, and beyond that a few crackers going off somewhere near the river. The restriction had been introduced for the air and for the fires, and it had also taken away the one night of the year when the street belonged to the children.

We ate at seven, and the table was the same table it had always been, with one more chair than we needed because the chair had been kept for a cousin who did not come. My mother put the fish in the middle and said the sentence about the surplus that she said every year, and my father laughed at it in the way that he laughed at it every year. The cousin's chair was pushed against the wall, nobody said anything about it, and the fish was the largest thing on the table.

I did homework after dinner, which is the thing that marks the last year of middle school more than any other. There was a set of physics problems to finish and a passage to translate, and I did them at the desk by the window while the gala on the television went through its songs. The exercises were about resistance and current, and the answers were in the back of the book, and I checked them anyway.

Fifteen is the age at which the counting changes. There were a hundred and forty-four days until the examinations in June, and the number was written on the board at school in a hand that changed every week. At home nobody wrote it anywhere, but I knew it the way you know the weight of a bag you carry every day. I had written the number on the inside cover of my physics book in pencil, and I erased it and wrote it again every Monday.

New year came without noise, and I counted the days anyway. That is what the last year of a school does to a person: it turns a festival into a number. At fifteen the future had a date on it, and the date was the fourteenth of June.
