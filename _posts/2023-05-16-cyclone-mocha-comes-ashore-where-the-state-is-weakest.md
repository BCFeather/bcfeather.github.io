---
title: "Cyclone Mocha comes ashore where the state is weakest"
date: 2023-05-16 21:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Cyclone Mocha made landfall on 14 May across the coast of Rakhine state and the refugee camps of Coxs Bazar, bringing winds of over two hundred kilometres an hour to the most exposed and least governed stretch of the Bay of Bengal."
---

Cyclone Mocha came ashore on the morning of 14 May near Sittwe, the capital of Rakhine state in western Myanmar, with sustained winds reported at around two hundred and fifty kilometres an hour, making it one of the strongest storms recorded in the Bay of Bengal. It then moved north-east across the delta and into Bangladesh, weakening as it went. The damage was concentrated in a strip of coast where several hundred thousand people live in camps and villages that had already been displaced by conflict before the storm arrived.

Rakhine has been the site of a war since the army seized power in February 2021. The state's Rohingya population, most of whom were driven out in 2017 and live in camps in Bangladesh or in apartheid-like conditions at home, were the most exposed. Aid agencies had pre-positioned supplies and evacuated staff, and the junta, which restricts access to the state and has been accused of blocking humanitarian deliveries, announced that it would accept help. Whether the help will be allowed to move is the question that the next weeks will answer.

In Bangladesh, the storm skirted the camps at Cox's Bazar, where about a million Rohingya refugees live in shelters of bamboo and tarpaulin on hillsides that had been stripped of trees. The cyclone passed close enough to destroy thousands of shelters and to flood the low ground, and the government had moved people to evacuation centres before it arrived. The camps have been described for years as a disaster waiting for a storm, and the storm came close enough to test the description without quite proving it.

Its wider cost will be counted in agriculture and in infrastructure. The coast of Rakhine depends on fishing and on rice, and the storm surge pushed salt water into fields that will take several seasons to recover. Bridges and embankments that connect Sittwe to the rest of the country were built with international money and have been damaged repeatedly in the past decade; the storm took out sections of the road that the army uses to supply its positions. Communications were cut across much of the state for days.

Politics of the disaster are as old as the Bay of Bengal. Cyclone Nargis killed more than a hundred and thirty thousand people in the Irrawaddy delta in 2008, and the junta then refused most foreign aid for weeks, which turned a weather event into a political one. The current government has said it will accept assistance, and the United Nations and the Red Cross have asked for unimpeded access. In a country where the army is fighting for control of the very state that the storm hit, access is a military question as much as a humanitarian one.

The storm season is only beginning. Meteorologists had expected a strong cyclone in the pre-monsoon period because the Bay has been unusually warm, and the same warmth will feed the storms of the autumn. For the people in the camps, the calculation has not changed: they live on land they do not own, in shelters that cannot resist wind, in a place where the state is either absent or hostile. Mocha was a disaster that arrived on schedule, in a place where everything else had already gone wrong.
