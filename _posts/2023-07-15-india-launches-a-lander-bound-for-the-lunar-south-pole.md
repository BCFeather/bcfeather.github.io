---
title: "India launches a lander bound for the lunar south pole"
date: 2023-07-15 21:40:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "Chandrayaan-3, carrying a lander and a rover towards the lunar south pole, lifted off from Sriharikota on 14 July on India's heaviest rocket, four years after the Chandrayaan-2 lander crashed."
---

The LVM3 rocket lifted off from Satish Dhawan Space Centre at Sriharikota at 14:35 local time on 14 July, carrying 3,900 kilograms of spacecraft and a national point to prove. Chandrayaan-3 is India's third lunar mission and its second attempt to put a lander down gently on the surface. The first, in September 2019, ended in silence eleven kilometres above the moon when the braking thrusters fired too hard, and the failure was broadcast live to a country that had gathered to watch.

The mission is built around a lesson learned. The lander, named Vikram after the physicist who founded the Indian space programme, carries four engines with wider throttling ranges, more propellant, stronger legs and a laser doppler velocimeter to measure its speed towards the ground. Its target is a patch of high latitude near the south pole, chosen because craters there hold water ice in permanently shadowed floors. The rover Pragyan, small and solar powered, will spend a lunar day examining soil and rocks in a region no lander has reached.

India's space programme is cheap and ambitious in the way that poorer countries learn to be. The whole mission cost about six hundred crore rupees, roughly seventy-five million dollars, a fraction of what a European or American planetary mission spends, and it was assembled by a workforce paid civil-service salaries. The same agency launched a record 104 satellites on a single rocket in 2017, put a probe into orbit around Mars on its first attempt in 2013, and has a human spaceflight programme waiting for money.

A soft landing would make India the fourth country to manage one, after the Soviet Union, the United States and China, and the first to reach the southern polar region. The achievement matters beyond prestige, because the ice at the poles is the foundation of any long-term lunar presence, and the countries that can find and use it will set the terms for everyone else. India's lander also carries a small American retroreflector, one of several instruments that keep the two programmes talking. China's programme has its own sample return planned, and NASA's Artemis missions are aiming at the same terrain from the other direction.

Engineering risk stays high until the last burn. Controllers will raise the orbit in stages over the coming weeks, then send the lander down in the third week of August, when the sun is at the right angle over the landing site. The descent takes about fifteen minutes, and the agency has said it will not repeat the mistake of treating a test as a formality.

Four years ago the crash left a scar on the programme and a determination to try again. The rocket has done its part, and the hard part is still a month away, in the silence between the last engine cut-off and the first picture from the surface. The agency's chairman has said the mission will be judged by the landing, which is an honest way to describe a test that has already cost one lander.
