---
title: "Boris Johnson leaves parliament"
date: 2023-06-10 12:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Boris Johnson announced on 9 June that he would give up his seat in the Commons rather than face the findings of a privileges committee inquiry into whether he misled parliament over lockdown parties, and seven of his allies resigned the same evening."
---

Boris Johnson announced on 9 June that he would resign as the member of parliament for Uxbridge and South Ruislip, rather than wait for a report by the Commons privileges committee into whether he had deliberately misled the House about parties in Downing Street during the pandemic. The committee had been investigating his statements for more than a year and had been expected to publish its findings within weeks. In a statement that ran to several pages, Johnson described the process as a kangaroo court and accused his colleagues of driving him out, and he left office with the grievance intact.

The timing was characteristic. He used the disclosure of the committee's draft findings, which he said he had been shown, to announce a by-election he would not contest, and within hours seven of his allies in the Commons said they would follow him out. The resignations included several who had been given honours in his resignation list, which was published the same weekend and which the committee had also examined. The effect was to turn a disciplinary process into a purge, and to leave the government of Rishi Sunak with a series of by-elections it would rather not fight.

Narrow in substance, the inquiry has wide implications. The committee was asked to decide whether Johnson's assurances that the rules had been followed amounted to contempt of parliament, and its draft reportedly found that he had repeatedly misled the House and had impugned the committee itself. The sanction it can recommend runs from suspension to expulsion, and the spectacle of a former prime minister being suspended from the chamber would have been unprecedented. By resigning first, Johnson avoided the vote and turned the verdict into a matter of his own narrative.

For Sunak, the practical consequences are smaller than the noise suggests. The Conservative Party holds a working majority, and the by-elections will be held in seats that it was defending with large margins that have since narrowed. The deeper difficulty is that Johnson retains a substantial following among party members and in the press, and every resignation he prompts reminds those followers that the man they backed was removed by people they distrust. A leader who cannot control his predecessor's exit cannot easily set his own agenda.

For the institution, the episode is one more test of whether parliament can police its own members. The privileges committee is a committee of peers and members chaired by a senior opposition figure, and its authority rests on the assumption that findings are accepted even when they are unwelcome. Johnson's response was to reject that assumption in advance, and the precedents he set will be cited by the next member who decides that answering a committee is optional. The by-election will settle the seat; it will not settle whether the process was fair, and the argument over that will outlast the campaign.

By-elections will be held in July, and the results will be read as a verdict on the government as much as on the men who resigned. The committee will publish its report anyway, because a process this far advanced cannot now be withdrawn.
