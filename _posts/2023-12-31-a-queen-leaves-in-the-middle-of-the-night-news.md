title: "A queen leaves in a new year address"
date: 2023-12-31 23:55:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Queen Margrethe II of Denmark announced in her New Year address on 31 December 2023 that she would abdicate on 14 January after 52 years on the throne, the first Danish monarch to give up the crown voluntarily in more than 500 years."
---

Queen Margrethe II of Denmark used her New Year address on 31 December to announce that she would abdicate on 14 January, the anniversary of her accession in 1972. The speech was twelve minutes long and the announcement came in the last two, after a paragraph about the length of her reign and the state of her back. She said the time had come to hand the responsibility to the next generation, and she thanked the country for the trust it had given her for fifty-two years.

The decision was hers alone, which is the part that made it news. Danish monarchs have reigned until death since the fifteenth century, and the last one to leave the throne did so under pressure in 1848. Margrethe had said in interviews that she regarded abdication as something other monarchies did, and she changed her mind at 83 after a back operation in February that kept her out of public life for months. Her prime minister was informed a few days earlier, and the court kept the secret through the Christmas holidays.

The succession is immediate and orderly. Crown Prince Frederik becomes Frederik X on 14 January, with his wife Mary as queen, and the ceremony was scheduled as a proclamation in the courtyard of Christiansborg rather than a coronation, in keeping with a monarchy that has no crown to place on a head. The couple's eldest son becomes crown prince at 18, and the family that had spent the autumn defending the decision to strip titles from the children of the queen's younger son now prepares to be the face of the institution.

Her reign covered a transformation of the job. Denmark joined the European Communities a year after she took the throne, the country voted against the Maastricht treaty in 1992 and then approved it with opt-outs, and the monarchy moved from an institution that was covered by protocol to one that is covered by television. Margrethe illustrated her own books under a pseudonym, designed costumes for ballet and film, and gave interviews that were frank enough to make the court nervous, which is how a constitutional monarch in a small country stays relevant.

Republicanism in Denmark is a small movement with no parliamentary representation, and the polls that followed the announcement put support for the monarchy above 70 per cent and for Frederik above his mother. The change of reign leaves the system unchanged, and the commentators who spent the week writing about the end of an era were mostly writing about a person they had known for half a century. The queen's own framing was more modest: she said she had been lucky, and that the country had been kind.

Abdication has become the ordinary way for European monarchies to change hands. Belgium, Spain, the Netherlands and now Denmark have done it within a decade, and each time the argument was the same: an institution that cannot renew itself while a single person holds it for life will eventually be judged for that person rather than for itself. Margrethe spent fifty-two years making that argument unnecessary, and then, in a paragraph at the end of a New Year speech, made the institution's next move on her own terms.
