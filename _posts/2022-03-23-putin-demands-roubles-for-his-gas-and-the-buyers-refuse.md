---
title: "Putin demands roubles for his gas and the buyers refuse"
date: 2022-03-23 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "A currency manoeuvre announced on television, a payment mechanism invented by a state bank, and seven economies that say the contract still says euros."
---

President Putin said on television on 23 March that countries he called unfriendly would have to pay for Russian gas in roubles, and he gave the central bank and the state energy company a week to arrange it. The measure was presented as a defence of sovereignty, and it was read by the buyers as an attempt to force them to deal with a bank that the sanctions had singled out. The order covered the pipeline gas that Europe buys under long-term contracts, about a third of its consumption.

A hybrid mechanism emerged. A buyer would open two accounts at Gazprombank, pay in the currency named in the contract, and the bank would convert the money into roubles and transfer it to the seller. The buyer could therefore say that it had paid in euros and the seller could say that it had been paid in roubles, and both could keep the contract that the lawyers had drafted in 2018. Germany's government described the arrangement as a breach and then opened the accounts anyway.

The Group of Seven rejected the demand within a week. The energy ministers said that the contracts specified the currency, that a unilateral change was a breach, and that companies should refuse to sign new agreements that named the rouble. The European Commission said that paying in roubles through the designated bank would give Russia a way around the sanctions, and it advised governments and companies to keep paying in the contracted currency into accounts that did not belong to the sanctioned entity.

Legality mattered more than economics. A contract for gas is a contract for molecules, and the currency is a term that the parties can vary by agreement. A state that changes the term unilaterally is in breach, and a buyer that accepts the change has amended the contract, which is what the lawyers on both sides understood. The Russian side then produced a decree that treated a payment in the old currency as not having been made, which turned a commercial dispute into a question about whether the lights would go out.

Nothing was cut off in March. The flows through the pipelines continued, the buyers that refused found ways to pay, and the price of gas in Europe fell from the peak of the month as the winter ended. The cuts came later and selectively, to Poland and Bulgaria in April, to Finland in May, and to the companies that had refused the mechanism, and each was announced as a consequence of a payment that the seller said had not arrived.

What the manoeuvre achieved was a demonstration. A country that exports a commodity can rewrite the terms of payment if its customers need the commodity more than they need the rule, and the customers can comply while insisting that they have not. The rouble recovered from the panic of March partly on the strength of the demand, and the European Union spent the summer trying to write a rule that would let its companies pay and still be able to say that they had not.
