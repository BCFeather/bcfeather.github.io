---
title: "Fifteen thousand cancellations"
date: 2022-12-26 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "Southwest cancels more than fifteen thousand flights in a week as its crew scheduling system fails under the volume of a winter storm."
---
By the morning of 26 December the storm had moved east and the airports in the Midwest were open again. Southwest had cancelled more than two thirds of its flights that day, and the count kept rising through the week. Other carriers had recovered within a day of the weather clearing. The airline that had built its reputation on point-to-point flying was the one that could not put its schedule back together. Passengers who had been rebooked twice found that the second cancellation arrived by text message at four in the morning.

Weather started the problem, and the storm was severe enough to close airports in several states for days. Crews were stranded where they had diverted, and aircraft were parked where they could not be used. Every airline suffered cancellations on the worst day of the storm. What separated Southwest was what happened afterwards, when the weather had gone and the schedule still could not be flown. Airlines that run through hubs cancelled a few hundred flights each and resumed within thirty-six hours, while Southwest cancelled flights in cities that had not seen snow, because crews and aircraft stood nowhere near where the schedule put them.

Its own explanation centred on the crew scheduling system. That software had been written years earlier and could handle a normal week of disruption, and it could not handle a week in which thousands of crew members were out of position at once. Reassigning a crew requires a sequence of steps that the system processed slowly, and the queue of reassignments grew faster than it could be cleared. Executives admitted afterwards that the system had failed at scale. Its chief executive appeared in a video message and said that the company would fix the system, and that he was sorry for the disruption.

Network structure made the failure worse. Southwest flies point to point rather than through hubs, and its planes and crews move from city to city through the day. A hub carrier can reset at a single airport where spare aircraft and staff are concentrated, and a point-to-point carrier has to reset everywhere at once. The efficiency that keeps costs low in normal weeks is the thing that has no slack in a bad one.

Passengers described airports filled with bags and with queues that ran the length of the terminal. Some of them drove to other cities to find a flight, and some gave up and stayed where the storm had left them. The airline offered refunds and, after several days, a gesture of frequent-flyer points. The transport department said it would look into whether the airline had scheduled more than it could operate in winter conditions.

By the end of the week the airline had cancelled more than fifteen thousand flights, and its operations were running again at a reduced level. The episode cost the company a large sum that it would report in January, and it cost the industry some of the goodwill it had earned during the pandemic. The storm lasted two days, and the recovery lasted nine. The transport secretary said in a letter that the cancellations had gone beyond what weather could explain.
