---
title: "Two molecules found in a bacterium became a pair of scissors"
date: 2022-03-26 21:20:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "Eight authors, one protein and a guide made of RNA, in a paper that turned a system bacteria use against viruses into a tool that can cut any sequence for about twenty dollars."
---

Bacteria have been fighting viruses for billions of years, and the record of that fight is written in their genomes. A stretch of DNA called an array holds short fragments of the sequences of viruses that the lineage has survived, and the cell transcribes those fragments into RNA and uses them as a memory. When the same virus returns, the RNA guides a protein to the matching sequence and cuts it. The system was known by 2007, and the mechanism by which it found the target was understood by 2010.

The paper that made the system a tool appeared in Science in June 2012, with eight authors and two of them, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier, as the laboratories that had done the work. It showed that one protein, Cas9, was sufficient for the cut, and that the two RNAs that the bacterium uses could be fused into a single guide. The fusion was the engineering step: a molecule of about a hundred bases could be synthesized to match any sequence, and the protein would do the rest.

Their experiment used purified components in a test tube. They mixed Cas9 with a guide RNA and a piece of DNA that carried the target, and they found that the protein cut both strands at a position three bases upstream of a short motif that the target had to contain. The motif, which the field calls a PAM, is a three-letter sequence that the protein recognizes before it unwinds the DNA. The cut was clean and it happened at the place the guide specified, which is the property that made the system programmable.

What the paper did not show was the part that mattered for medicine. It demonstrated cleavage in a test tube rather than in a living cell, and the second step, editing a genome in a human cell, was published by other groups in January 2013. The patents became a dispute between the University of California and the Broad Institute, which had filed on the eukaryotic use, and the litigation went on for years in two jurisdictions.

The prize came in 2020, to Doudna and Charpentier, and the citation named the programmable scissors rather than the bacterial immune system. The tool had by then been used in every organism that has a genome, in crops, in laboratory mice and in the first clinical trials for sickle cell disease. The same year, the scientist who had edited human embryos in 2018 was released from prison, and the field had to explain why the scissors were safe in a body and dangerous in an embryo.

Reading the 2012 paper now is an exercise in watching a mechanism be reduced to a procedure. The guide, the PAM and the blunt cut are all in the figures, and the discussion is about what the system might be used for rather than about what it is. The authors did not claim to have edited a genome, and the paper is best read beside the 2013 papers that did, because the twenty dollars that a guide costs is the number that changed the world.
