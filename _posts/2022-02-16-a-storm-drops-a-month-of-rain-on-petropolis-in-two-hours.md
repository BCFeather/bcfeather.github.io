---
title: "A storm drops a month of rain on Petrópolis in two hours"
date: 2022-02-16 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The mountain city above Rio records its heaviest rain since 1932, and the landslides that follow kill at least ninety-four people in a single afternoon."
---

Rain began in Petrópolis in the middle of the afternoon on 15 February and fell at a rate that the gauges had not recorded since 1932: 240 millimetres in two hours, against an average of 238 for the whole of the month. Water came down the streets of the historic centre as a brown river, carrying cars and shop fittings, and the hillsides above the centre gave way in at least twenty-six places. By the evening of the next day the state civil defence office had confirmed 94 deaths.

The worst slide came at Morro da Oficina, in the district of Alto da Serra, where a slope above a row of houses collapsed and took about eighty homes with it. Firefighters from three states worked in the mud for a week, and 372 people were registered as homeless or displaced and were housed in twenty-five schools. The mayor declared a state of public calamity and three days of mourning, and the governor said the sirens installed above the risk areas had given people time to leave.

Geology and building set the terms of the disaster, and so did the weather of the weeks before it. Petrópolis sits in a valley below granite slopes that are steep, weathered and covered with vegetation that logging and construction have thinned. The city grew along the roads that follow the rivers, and the poorest households built on the ground that nobody else wanted, which is the ground that water reaches first. The rain of 15 February fell on soil that was already wet from an earlier storm, so the water ran off rather than into the ground.

The comparison that every resident made was with 2011. That January, rain in the same mountain region killed 918 people, most of them in Nova Friburgo and Teresopolis, and it led to a federal programme of slope stabilization and resettlement that proceeded in some places and stalled in others. In 1988 another storm killed 134 in Petrópolis, and the pattern of settlement that produced those deaths has not changed much since.

By the end of February the count had passed two hundred, and it would settle at 233 after the last of the missing were found. Reconstruction costs were estimated in the hundreds of millions of reais, and the state and federal governments argued about who would pay. Volunteers from neighbouring towns joined the search with dogs, picks and shovels, and the state forensic institute worked for three weeks to name the dead. The rains continued into March, and more slides followed in the same districts, and the sirens sounded again.

What the city received in February was an emergency and a promise. What it needs is a programme that moves people off the slopes it cannot make safe, and that programme has been discussed after every disaster of the past forty years, and funded in the months when the memory was fresh. The city's population has doubled since 1988, and the slopes have been built on with the permission of the same councils that now count the dead.
