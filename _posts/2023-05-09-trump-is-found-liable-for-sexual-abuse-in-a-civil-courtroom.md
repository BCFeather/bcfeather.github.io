---

A federal jury in Manhattan found Donald Trump liable on 9 May for sexually abusing the writer E. Jean Carroll in a department store dressing room in the mid-1990s, and liable for defamation over the statements in which he denied it and called her account a lie. The jury awarded her five million dollars in damages, roughly two million of which were for the abuse itself, and rejected her claim of rape in the narrow sense that the civil law of New York required. Trump was absent from the courtroom. He was in New York for other reasons, and he gave a statement within the hour.

The case was built on a law that had been revived for the purpose. New York's Adult Survivors Act, passed in 2022, opened a one-year window in which survivors of sexual assault could bring civil claims long after the statute of limitations had expired. Carroll's suit was filed on the first day the window opened, and it depended upon testimony rather than upon physical evidence: a handful of friends to whom she had told the story at the time, two other women who described similar encounters, and the Access Hollywood tape in which Trump was heard describing his approach to women in terms the jury was allowed to hear.

This verdict is a civil finding, and the distinction matters. A civil case requires a preponderance of the evidence, not the proof beyond a reasonable doubt that a criminal case would demand, and the penalties are money rather than liberty. In the American system, a finding of liability for sexual abuse carries no register, no restriction and no criminal record. What it carries is a judgement, and a phrase that will be attached to the man's name for the rest of his public life, in every search result and every paragraph of opposition research.

Trump's response followed the pattern of his previous legal difficulties. He called the verdict a disgrace and a continuation of a political witch hunt, said he would appeal, and framed the case as an attack on him by a judge and a legal system that he described as rigged. His campaign apparatus reported an increase in small donations within a day, which has been the pattern after each indictment. The appeal will be heard while he is campaigning, which is the condition in which he now spends most of his time.

What the case changes is narrower than the headlines suggest and wider than his defenders admit. It does not affect his candidacy, because the constitution sets no such bar and his party has shown no appetite to create one. It does not produce a criminal consequence, because a separate criminal case about payments to a woman is being tried in a different court and is a different matter. It does reduce, a little, the availability of the word hoax, which was the defence he had used for years.

The more durable effect is the precedent registered in the law. A sitting and former president has been found liable by a jury of ordinary citizens for conduct he denied, in a case he could not stop and a court he could not control. That verdict now sits in the record alongside the ones that are still to come. The damages will be paid, the appeal will be argued, the campaign will continue, and the sentence written on 9 May will stay attached to all of it.
