title: "Putin lands on Iturup and calls the Kuril question settled in 1945"
date: 2026-08-13 23:35:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "On 13 August the Russian president visited Iturup in the Kuril Islands for the first time, toured a fish-processing plant and said the islands' status was fixed by the postwar settlement. Tokyo, which claims the four southern islands, called the visit unacceptable."
---

Vladimir Putin landed on Iturup on 13 August, the first visit by a Russian president to the Kuril island that Japan calls Etorofu, and spent part of the afternoon at a fish-processing plant. The Kremlin published photographs of him with the plant's workers and of a plate of local caviar, and the imagery did as much work as the statements that followed. A president standing on a disputed island two days before the anniversary of Japan's surrender is a message that needs no translation.

Those statements were unusually direct. Putin said Japan's new defence white paper lists Russia among its main threats while Russia threatens nobody, and that the status of the southern Kurils was confirmed by the documents signed at the end of the Second World War. He added that Moscow is ready to look for a settlement and to conclude a peace treaty with Japan, a formula that has been repeated for decades without result. He also linked the dispute to economic cooperation, saying that Russian and Japanese firms could do more business on the islands if the political weather improved.

The treaty has been missing since 1945. Japan claims the four southern islands, which Soviet forces took in the closing weeks of the war, and the dispute has kept the two countries from signing the instrument that would formally end hostilities. Neither side has found a formula that satisfies its domestic opinion, and every attempt has run into the same obstacle: a settlement requires one government to accept a loss that its voters have been told never happened.

Tokyo's response was swift and predictable, calling the visit unacceptable and summoning the Russian ambassador. Russian officials had already complained about the Takaichi government's defence posture, and the deputy foreign minister said in August that dialogue could resume only when Japan abandons its hostile line. Trade between the two countries has shrunk under sanctions, and the fishing agreement that once sustained the island economy has been suspended. The islands themselves hold little industry apart from fishing and some mineral deposits, and their value is strategic as much as economic.

Beijing, which has no direct stake in the islands, used the occasion to restate a larger principle. The foreign ministry said on 14 August that the outcomes of the war against fascism should be respected and that Tokyo should abide by the Cairo and Potsdam declarations. That framing ties the Kurils to Taiwan and to the wider argument about the postwar order, and it gives Chinese diplomats a useful paragraph for every regional statement this month.

The timing carried its own message. Two days before the anniversary of Japan's surrender, a Russian president stood on an island that Soviet troops took in that surrender, and said the question had been settled then. For a Japanese government already arguing about rearmament, the photograph was worth a thousand words of protest. Japanese officials have said the visit will not change their position, and they have pointed out that no Russian leader has accepted the 1956 declaration that once offered two of the islands back.
