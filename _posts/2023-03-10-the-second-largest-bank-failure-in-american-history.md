---
title: "The second largest bank failure in American history"
date: 2023-03-10 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "California's regulator closed Silicon Valley Bank on 10 March 2023 and handed it to the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, which made it the second largest bank failure in the country's history by assets."
---

The California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation closed the bank on the morning of 10 March, and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation was appointed as receiver. The bank had 209 billion dollars in assets at the end of 2022, which made it the sixteenth largest in the country, and the failure was the largest since Washington Mutual in 2008. It was the second largest in the history of the insurance fund, and it took less than forty-eight hours from the announcement to the closure.

No run in the system's records had been faster. On 9 March depositors tried to withdraw more than forty billion dollars, which was about a quarter of the bank's deposits, and the bank's own projection for the following day was larger. The withdrawals were made by companies moving payroll and reserves, and they were executed through the same electronic rails that had always existed, which meant that the constraint on a run was no longer the number of branches or the speed of a queue.

Coverage ran to 250,000 dollars per depositor, and most of the bank's deposits sat above that limit. The uninsured share was the reason the closure left the story unfinished, because the companies that banked there needed their money to pay staff, and the receivership's rules would have paid them a fraction after a delay measured in months. The fund began selling the bank's assets and looking for a buyer over the weekend, and the auction produced no whole-bank bid, which was itself a signal.

On Sunday evening the regulators announced their next step, which was a decision about a category rather than about a bank. The boards of the insurance fund, the Federal Reserve and the Treasury invoked the systemic risk exception, which allows the fund to protect uninsured deposits when a failure would pose a risk to the financial system, and they applied it to this bank and to another that had failed the same weekend. The exception had been written into the law in 1991 and had been used sparingly, and its use here was the moment the crisis became general.

Within a day the bank's British subsidiary was sold to HSBC for a pound, and its branches reopened on Monday under a new owner, which was the contrast that the British authorities pointed to. The American resolution was slower and more expensive, and it left the insurance fund with a cost that would be estimated later and charged to the industry through a special assessment.

The question that the failure raised was about the supervision that had preceded it. The bank had grown faster than its risk management, its regulator had flagged the concentration of its deposit base, and the rules that applied to it had been relaxed in 2018 when the threshold for enhanced supervision was raised. Those facts were assembled by the Fed's own review within a month, and the review described a supervisor that had identified problems and had not been able to make the bank fix them.
