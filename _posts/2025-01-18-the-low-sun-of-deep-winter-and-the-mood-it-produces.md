In the second half of January the sun rises after seven and sets before five in the northern cities, and the daylight that a person receives in a working day is the daylight of a commute. The short day changes the way a body works, and the change is large enough to have a clinical name.

The mechanism is the circadian system. The body sets its internal clock by light, and the light that matters most is the blue end of the spectrum, which arrives in the morning and tells the brain that the day has begun. A person who leaves home in the dark and returns in the dark receives almost no morning light in the winter, and the clock drifts later, which produces the difficulty of getting up that the season is known for.

The clinical form of the problem is the seasonal affective disorder, a pattern of low mood and low energy that arrives in the late autumn and lifts in the spring. The diagnosis is made by the timing of the symptoms rather than by their severity, and the prevalence rises with the latitude, which is why the condition is common in the northern countries and rare near the equator.

The treatment that the trials support is bright light therapy. A lamp that produces a bright white light is placed on a table and used for half an hour in the morning, and the effect on the seasonal pattern is comparable to that of an antidepressant in the studies that have compared them. The lamp has to be bright, the exposure has to be in the morning, and the dose is a research question that the clinical guidelines have settled at a level that a household can buy.

The cheaper interventions are also effective and less discussed. A walk at midday, a window opened on a bright morning, a lunch eaten outside, a holiday taken in February. The light is free and the exposure is a habit, and the habit is the part that the medicine cannot prescribe.

The season also affects the vitamin D that the skin makes. The ultraviolet that produces it is weak at the latitude in the winter, and the diet is a poor substitute for the sun. The supplement is cheap and the evidence for it in a healthy adult is modest, and the advice that the guidelines give is to take it in the winter rather than to expect a benefit that the trials have not shown.

What the short day shows is that a mood is a biological event with an environmental cause, and the cause can be measured in lux. The northern winter is a season that a body can adapt to and cannot avoid, and the adaptation is made of light, sleep and a walk at the right hour. The lamp on the table is the medical version of the same advice, and the advice is worth taking seriously in a month when the sun sets before the office closes.
