On the first of March the calendar announces spring and the thermometer ignores the announcement. In the north the heating season runs until the middle of the month, and the radiators in a flat are still warm at night while the trees outside are bare and the wind has the edge that only March wind has. The season that the date promises arrives about three weeks later than the date says it will.

The three calendars that a Chinese city keeps are not in agreement about this. The meteorological convention in the northern hemisphere begins spring on the first of March, because it divides the year into four blocks of three whole months and needs a date rather than an event. The astronomical calendar waits for the equinox, which falls around the twentieth, and treats that moment as the boundary. The solar terms, which have been the working calendar of farming for two thousand years, place the beginning of spring much earlier, at 立春 in early February, and put 惊蛰 a month later, when the insects are supposed to wake.

Three calendars, three answers, and the weather does what it does. The practical consequence is that people dress for the wrong season for a fortnight every year. The coat comes off in the first warm afternoon, the temperature falls ten degrees in the night, and the outpatient clinic fills with the coughs that follow. Nothing about this is new and nothing about it is preventable, and it happens in every city at the same latitude.

What March does have, reliably, is dust. The soil of the northern plateau is dry and loose after the winter, the winds are strong and the vegetation that holds the surface has not yet grown back, and the result is the yellow haze that turns the sky the colour of weak tea for a few days. The forecasting of it has improved a great deal, from the satellite images that show the source region to the models that predict the transport, and the warnings now arrive a day ahead rather than an hour. The dust itself has not changed, and the habit of closing the windows on the right day is the only adaptation a household has.

The month also carries the last of the winter vegetables and the first of the greenhouse ones, and the difference between them is visible in the price. A tomato in March is either a storage tomato from the autumn crop or a greenhouse tomato grown at a cost that the label does not show, and the two taste so different that a shopper who buys without thinking will conclude that tomatoes have become worse. They have not. They are simply out of season.

What the first of March teaches is the difference between a calendar and a climate. A calendar is a decision that a society makes about how to name a period, and a climate is a set of conditions that arrives when it arrives. The naming is useful for taxes, school terms and the heating subsidy, and it is unreliable for the coat.
