---
title: "A plane left Sanaa with nine hundred prisoners"
date: 2023-04-14 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Yemen's warring parties began a three-day prisoner exchange on 14 April 2023 that was expected to return about nine hundred detainees, the largest such release since the conflict began and the one piece of the war that both sides could agree to finish."
---

Aircraft began moving prisoners between Sanaa and Marib on 14 April in the first stage of an exchange that both sides had agreed to months earlier. The International Committee of the Red Cross supervised the transfers, and the total was expected to reach about nine hundred detainees, including people held since the beginning of the war in 2015. The operation was scheduled to take three days and to use aircraft from both sides. Red Crescent buses carried some of the released between the airports, and families waited outside the gates with lists of names.

The lists had taken a year to agree. A first exchange in 2020 returned 1,056 people, and the negotiations that followed stalled over the numbers and over the categories, with each side accused of holding people who had never fought. The agreement reached in March 2023 covered the remaining names on both lists and left the question of the missing unresolved. Both delegations brought files of names that the other side said it had never held, and the files were exchanged along with the prisoners.

Above everything else stood the exchange, the most visible result of a rapprochement that had been building since the spring and that had been arranged through Oman. Saudi Arabia and the Houthi movement had been talking through Omani intermediaries since late 2022, and the talks had produced a truce that expired in October and was never renewed formally. The prisoner release was the first measure that required the two sides to trust each other for three consecutive days.

The war itself had reached a stalemate that neither side could break. The front lines in Marib and in the south had barely moved since 2021, the Houthis had attacked shipping and Saudi infrastructure with drones, and the Saudi-led coalition had bombed the ports and the capital. The United Nations described the humanitarian situation as the worst in the world, with about 17 million people dependent on food assistance. The appeal for funds had been cut twice for lack of money, and the agencies running the clinics had begun to ration the fuel for the generators.

Diplomacy around the exchange was regional and transactional. Saudi Arabia wanted an exit from a war that had cost it money and reputation, the Houthis wanted the blockade lifted and salaries paid, and the internationally recognized government wanted to be included in a process that was being conducted without it. The exchange showed that the first two parties could deliver on a promise, which is what a settlement would require.

Peace did not follow. The truce never came back, the fighting continued in the same places at a lower intensity, and the talks in Riyadh and Sanaa produced statements rather than a timetable. The prisoners who flew home in April were the only people the war had released in years, and the exchange is remembered for that rather than for what it changed. A year later the fighting has spread to new fronts, and the talks have moved to Riyadh and to Muscat without producing a text that either side will sign.
