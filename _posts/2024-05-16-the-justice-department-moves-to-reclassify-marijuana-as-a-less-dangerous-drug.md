---
title: "The Justice Department moves to reclassify marijuana as a less dangerous drug"
date: 2024-05-16 22:50:00 +0800
categories: [society]
description: "The Justice Department proposed on 16 May to move marijuana from schedule one to schedule three of the Controlled Substances Act, a step that would recognize its medical use and change the federal tax treatment of the cannabis industry."
---

Justice Department proposed on the sixteenth of May to move marijuana from schedule one to schedule three of the Controlled Substances Act, a change that would place it in the same category as prescription drugs such as ketamine and anabolic steroids. Announcement followed a review ordered by President Joe Biden in 2022, and it was made by the attorney general after the Department of Health and Human Services concluded that cannabis has a currently accepted medical use and a lower potential for abuse than drugs in the stricter schedules.

Schedule one is reserved for substances with no accepted medical use and a high potential for abuse, and it includes heroin and LSD. Marijuana has been listed there since 1970, alongside other drugs that the Nixon administration placed in the same category. Move to schedule three would not legalize cannabis at the federal level, and possession outside a state programme would remain an offence, but it would change the terms on which the industry operates.

Practical effect would be largest in taxation. Businesses that sell cannabis have been barred from deducting ordinary expenses under a provision of the tax code that applies to traffickers in controlled substances, which has left them paying tax on gross income rather than profit. Rescheduling would remove that restriction, and analysts estimated that it could increase the after-tax earnings of large producers by a third or more. It would also ease some research restrictions and change the rules for prescribing.

Procedure is long and not guaranteed. Proposal was sent to the White House budget office for review and then to a public comment period administered by the Drug Enforcement Administration, and a final rule would follow a hearing that is likely to be contested. Several states that have legalized cannabis for adult use welcomed the step, and opponents, including some medical associations and law enforcement groups, said it understated the risks of the drug, particularly for young people.

Politics of the announcement was visible. Move came in an election year, and the administration has been seeking to appeal to younger voters who favour legalization. Campaign of the former president said he would go further and support federal legalization, and advocates noted that rescheduling leaves the industry in a strange position, legal in most states and still criminal under federal law.

Justice Department said the review had been conducted on scientific and medical grounds, and it published the health agency's findings alongside the proposal, which is unusual for a rescheduling decision and was read as an attempt to insulate it from political challenge.

Meaning of the change depends on what happens after it. If the rule is finalized, cannabis businesses will pay less tax, banks will be more willing to lend to them, and research on medical uses will become easier. Federal prohibition will remain, and the patchwork of state rules will continue to produce conflicts over banking, interstate transport and employment. What the sixteenth of May did was to move the federal government off a position it had held for fifty years without deciding where it should stand instead.
