---
title: "A letter from doctors that changed what patients believed"
date: 2022-06-16 23:15:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "Ninety per cent of Czech doctors trusted the COVID vaccines, yet most people guessed fifty. Correcting that guess raised uptake and kept it raised for months, according to a study in *Nature*."
---

Nine thousand six hundred and fifty doctors in the Czech Republic were asked whether they trusted the COVID-19 vaccines, and ninety per cent said they did. A nationally representative sample of two thousand and one hundred adults was then asked to guess the same number, and ninety per cent of them underestimated it. The most common guess was fifty per cent, half the true figure, and that gap is the subject of a paper published in *Nature* on 16 June.

The guess matters because it is the kind of fact people act on. Someone who believes that half of all doctors distrust a vaccine has a reasonable excuse for waiting, and someone who believes that nine in ten trust it has a reason to go. Public health messaging usually tries to persuade, with arguments about efficacy and safety, and this study did something simpler: it told people what doctors actually thought and then watched what happened. The same survey found that the misperception was strongest among younger respondents and among those who used social media daily.

Design of the experiment was built for persistence. Participants were enrolled in a panel that records their vaccination status through the national health system, and the information about the doctors' survey was delivered at random to part of the sample and withheld from the rest. The panel was followed for nine months, which is long enough to see whether an effect decays and long enough to catch the booster campaigns that ran during the period.

Results showed a recalibration of beliefs and a rise in uptake that did not fade. Treated participants moved their estimate of doctors' trust upward, and their vaccination rate stayed above the control group at every later check rather than spiking and settling back. Because the outcome came from administrative records rather than self-reports, the result does not depend on people remembering accurately or wanting to please the researchers.

The mechanism at work is social rather than clinical. Doctors are a trusted group in most countries, and a statement about what that group believes is a statement about a norm, which is a cheaper thing to communicate than an argument about risk. The authors point out that medical associations already survey their members and could publish the results at almost no cost, which makes the intervention unusually scalable for a behavioural study.

Limits are the ones that field experiments always have. The setting was one country with a specific history of vaccine hesitancy and a specific health system, and the doctors' survey depended on a high response rate that other associations might not achieve. Trust in the profession was high in the Czech Republic, and a similar message in a country where it has collapsed would carry less weight. Read as a methods paper, this is an argument about what a public health message should contain. Facts about what a trusted group thinks travel further than explanations of why they think it, and the effect lasts because it changes an estimate rather than winning an argument.
