The spring marathons arrive at the end of April, and for one morning a city closes the roads it spent the winter repairing. Forty thousand people start, a few hundred finish under two and a half hours, and the rest finish somewhere between three and six, which is the part of the race that the coverage does not show.

The organisation of it is the part that the runners never see. The course has to be measured to the metre, the water stations have to be placed where the runners need them rather than where they are convenient, the medical tents have to be staffed for the whole morning, and the volunteers who hand out the bottles have to be recruited, briefed and fed. A race of that size uses several thousand volunteers, and the volunteers are the reason it is affordable, since a race that paid the market rate for the labour would cost a great deal more than the entry fees bring in.

The economics are unusual for a sporting event. The entry fee of a large marathon is a few hundred yuan, the cost per runner is lower than that, and the surplus is spent on the elite field, the timing equipment and the permits. The city that hosts it spends money on the road closures and the policing, and it does so because the race brings visitors who stay for a night and eat for two days, and the visitors are the part of the balance sheet that the race can claim.

The public health argument is the one that the organisers make most often and can prove least. A marathon does not make a city fit, because the people who run it were already running. What the race does is put a running habit into the public calendar, which is a different thing, and the shops that sell shoes report a spike in the weeks after the entries open rather than after the race.

The runners themselves have their own economy. A place in a popular race is allocated by a ballot, the ballot is oversubscribed, and the charity places that come with a fundraising commitment are the way in for the ones who lose it. The training takes four months, the shoes take two pairs, and the physiotherapy takes a visit that the runner pays for out of a budget that is not discussed at the finish line.

What the morning produces, beyond the times, is a piece of civic theatre. A city that normally moves its people in cars moves them on foot for six hours, the streets that are usually loud are quiet, and the people who live along the route come out with a chair and a sign. The race is the one day on which the city's geography is something that a person can walk through rather than drive around, and that is the part that the photographs do not capture, because a photograph of a street can show the crowd and it cannot show the silence behind it.
