---
title: "A country that changed its money"
date: 2023-01-01 20:10:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Croatia adopted the euro on 1 January 2023 at a fixed rate of 7.5345 kuna and joined the Schengen area on the same day, becoming the twentieth member of the euro area."
---

Croatia adopted the euro at midnight, and the kuna, which had been the country's currency since 1994, stopped being legal tender after a fortnight of dual circulation. The fixed rate was 7.5345 kuna to the euro. Croatia became the twentieth member of the euro area, and on the same morning the checks at its land borders with Slovenia and Hungary came down as it entered the Schengen area. Notes had been printed in the autumn, and the coins were struck in two metals that the national bank had stockpiled for a year.

For a small economy that lives on tourism, the change removed a tax that visitors pay twice. Arrivals from Germany, Austria and Italy had previously changed money at kiosks and lost a margin on each transaction, and prices were now quoted in the currency that most of them already used. The finance ministry put the gain from lower conversion costs in the hundreds of millions of kuna a year. Hotels had quoted prices in euros for a decade anyway, which made the change feel to many Croatians like a formality that had finally been signed.

The costs were domestic and immediate. Every price in every shop had to be displayed in both currencies for twelve months, and the enforcement of that rule became the year's small national drama. Cafes and bakeries were accused of rounding upwards, inspectors issued fines, and consumer groups published lists of outlets that had raised prices while the conversion was in progress. A carton of milk and a tram ticket became the units in which the rounding was measured, and both were found in the first week to have risen by a few per cent.

Joining the euro area also meant giving up the ability to set interest rates, and Croatia entered at a moment when the European Central Bank was raising them quickly to fight inflation. Critics on the right argued that the country had surrendered a tool it would need in the next downturn, and the government replied that the tool had been borrowed money in a foreign currency all along.

The Schengen decision was the older of the two projects. Croatia had applied in 2015, and its admission had been held up for years over border management and over a dispute with Slovenia about a stretch of the Adriatic coast. The removal of internal checks left the country with a longer external border to police, along Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro and Serbia. Police numbers on the borders with Serbia and Bosnia were increased, and the interior ministry asked Brussels to pay for the extra patrols.

Money is the part of sovereignty that is easiest to trade and hardest to explain. Croatia traded it for the certainty that its savings, its mortgages and its tourist receipts would be denominated in the currency of its largest trading partners, and the trade was made in a year when certainty was scarce. The kuna had been introduced in 1994 to end a period of hyperinflation, and its retirement closed a chapter that most Croatians know from their parents rather than from their own wallets.
