---
title: "Cocoa at a record price and the bar that got smaller"
date: 2025-02-14 21:05:00 +0800
tags: [commodities, food, industry, africa]
categories: [business]
description: "The price of cocoa has more than doubled in two years, and the chocolate industry has responded by shrinking the bar rather than raising the price on the shelf."
---

The price of cocoa has more than doubled in two years, and the chocolate industry has responded in the way that food companies respond to a cost shock, which is to change the product rather than the number on the shelf. The bar is thinner, the filling is more of the bar than it was, and the weight printed on the wrapper is smaller than the weight that a customer remembers.

The cause of the price is a supply problem in West Africa, where most of the world's cocoa is grown. The trees in Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire are old, the yields have fallen, and the disease that the plantations suffer has spread with the weather. The two governments set a farmgate price each season, and the gap between that price and the world price has been a political question for a decade.

The market's behaviour has been extreme. The price passed ten thousand dollars a tonne in the winter, a level that had not been seen in the modern record, and the futures market became volatile in a way that pushed the smaller traders out. The grinders who turn the beans into butter and powder have cut their volumes because the working capital needed to buy a tonne has doubled.

The industrial response is a reformulation. A manufacturer can substitute the cocoa butter with a vegetable fat in some products, cut the cocoa content in a filled bar, and reduce the size of the portion. The first is regulated by the labelling rules in most markets, the second is legal if the label is honest, and the third requires no disclosure at all.

The retail price has risen anyway in most markets, and the increase has been larger in the countries where the currency has weakened against the dollar. The confectionery aisle is a small part of a household's budget, which is why the inflation there is noticed less than the inflation in the staples and recorded more precisely by the statisticians.

The farmers are the part of the chain that the price has not reached. The farmgate price in West Africa is set by a national board, and the board has raised it, and the raise has been smaller than the rise in the world price. The difference is the margin that the exporters and the treasury take, and the argument about it is the oldest argument in the trade.

What the episode shows is how a commodity shock travels through a consumer product. The price of a bean in a West African village reaches a wrapper in a supermarket two years later, and the route is a set of contracts and labels that the shopper never sees. The smaller bar is the end of that route, and it is the part that the shopper can weigh. The rest of it is in the futures curve, the farmgate price and the label, and none of those three is visible from the aisle where the decision is made.
