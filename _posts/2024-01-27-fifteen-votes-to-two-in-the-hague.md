title: "Fifteen votes to two in The Hague"

description: "The International Court of Justice ordered provisional measures against Israel on 26 January in the genocide case brought by South Africa, by fifteen votes to two, without ordering a halt to the military campaign in Gaza."
---

Judges at the International Court of Justice delivered their order on the afternoon of the twenty-sixth of January, in a case that South Africa had brought a month earlier, and the votes told most of the story. Fifteen of the seventeen judges found that the circumstances were sufficient to order provisional measures, and two dissented. Those measures require Israel to take steps to prevent acts that could fall within the scope of the genocide convention, to prevent and punish incitement to such acts, to allow humanitarian access to Gaza, and to preserve evidence of what has happened there. Israel must report back to the court within a month. Nothing in the order requires a halt to the fighting.

At issue is a legal threshold that is lower than the one most people assume. South Africa did not have to prove genocide at this stage, only that the claim was plausible and that the rights at issue faced irreparable harm. The court found both, in language that was careful and, for the applicant, remarkably favourable. It noted the scale of the casualties reported in Gaza, the destruction of civilian infrastructure, the statements of some Israeli officials about the population of the territory, and the conditions in which humanitarian aid was arriving. It did not find that genocide had been committed, and it did not say that the claim would succeed at trial, which will take years.

Two dissents are worth naming. Judge Aharon Barak, the ad hoc judge Israel appointed to the panel, voted against several of the measures and wrote that the court had not given sufficient weight to the threat Israel faced after the attacks of the seventh of October. Judge Julia Sebutinde of Uganda voted against all of them, a position that surprised observers because she was expected to support the applicant. Neither dissent changes the outcome, and both will be cited for years by governments that want to argue that the order was politically rather than legally arrived at.

Reaction followed the lines the case had already drawn. South Africa called the order a victory and pointed out that the court had accepted the plausibility of its claim. Israel's government said the measures were consistent with what it was already doing, noted that the court had not ordered a ceasefire, and described the case as an abuse of the convention. The Palestinian Authority welcomed the decision. Washington, which had called the case meritless before the hearing, said the order was consistent with its own position that Israel should do more to protect civilians, which is a sentence that can be read in two directions.

Practical effect is small and the legal effect is not. It has no police, and enforcement depends on the Security Council, where the United States has a veto. But an order from the principal judicial organ of the United Nations establishes a fact that governments, companies and courts in third countries will use. Arms export licences, investment decisions, the wording of statements by foreign ministries, the arguments of lawyers in national courts, all of them will now refer to a document that says a plausible case of genocide exists. That is what South Africa asked for, and it is what South Africa got. Whether it changes anything in Gaza depends on governments that were not in the courtroom.
