A night market in summer begins when the heat leaves the pavement. The first stalls set up at half past seven and the street is full by nine. By ten the queue at the grill is twenty deep and the road is closed to cars.

The stalls pay a fee to a management company that runs the street. The fee covers the cleaning, the electricity, the bins and the security guard at each end. A vendor who sells grilled skewers at three yuan a stick needs to sell two hundred to cover the night.

The neighbours have an interest in the noise. A household on the second floor hears the music until midnight and the cleaning until one. The management company pays a compensation that is agreed with the residents' committee. The amount is renegotiated each summer.

The street earns more after dark than it does by day. A shop that sells clothing to office workers closes at seven and rents its frontage to a food stall for the evening. The same square metre produces two rents.

The food is chosen for speed. A dish that takes ten minutes to cook cannot be sold from a stall with a queue. The menu is short everywhere. The short menu is what makes the street walkable in an hour.

The inspections happen at the start of the season. A health officer checks the cold storage and the water supply of every stall. A fire officer checks the gas cylinders. A stall that fails is closed until it passes again. The pass is displayed on the counter.

The waste is the part that the street does not advertise. A thousand skewers produce a thousand sticks. The bins at the ends of the street are emptied twice a night. A vendor who leaves a bag of rubbish behind is fined by the management company.

The market is also a stage. A singer with a guitar plays for tips at the corner, a magician works a small circle of children, a painter draws portraits for thirty yuan. The performances are licensed by the same company that runs the stalls.

The permit is issued for the season rather than for the year. A street that hosts a market from June to September applies again in the spring, and the application includes a plan for the waste and a list of the vendors with their licences.

The vendors are often the same people each summer. A couple who grill skewers in the market have done it for nine years and have a stall that their children will take over. The knowledge of where to stand, when to restock and which customer to greet is the asset that the licence does not capture. A visitor who arrives at eleven finds the best of the night already sold, which the regulars know and the guidebooks do not.

The season ends in September. The stalls pack up, the road reopens to cars, and the residents' committee collects the last payment. The street returns to being a street, and the queue moves somewhere else.
