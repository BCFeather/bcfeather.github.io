---
title: "Dutch election: a populist surge and an arithmetic problem"
date: 2023-11-22 23:35:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Geert Wilders's Party for Freedom won 37 of 150 seats in the Dutch election on 22 November 2023, a surprise victory that ended Mark Rutte's era but left the far-right leader needing partners who had campaigned against him."
---

Geert Wilders's Party for Freedom won 37 of the 150 seats in the Dutch House of Representatives on 22 November, more than double its previous tally and the largest result of any party in the election. The Labour and Green alliance led by Frans Timmermans took 25 seats, and the outgoing prime minister's party, the People's Party for Freedom and Democracy, took 24, its worst result in decades. Turnout was about 78 per cent, higher than in the previous election.

It was a shock to the Dutch political establishment and to the pollsters. Wilders had spent two decades in parliament, mostly in opposition, and his programme of closing the borders, banning new mosques and holding a referendum on leaving the European Union had been treated as a permanent protest vote. The campaign's final week, in which he softened his language and put housing and health care at the centre of his message, appears to have moved voters who had previously stayed home.

Arithmetic now constrains him. A majority requires 76 seats, and the parties that could plausibly join a government with him, the VVD and the New Social Contract of Pieter Omtzigt, campaigned explicitly against cooperating with a party whose leader has been convicted of insulting a group and whose manifesto would test the constitution. Omtzigt's party took 20 seats and made its conditions public within hours, including a demand that no policy be adopted that violates the rule of law.

The formation process that follows is the longest in Europe. Dutch cabinets are built through months of negotiations over detailed coalition agreements, and the last one took 299 days. With four or five parties needed to reach a majority, and with the Senate elections in May 2024 shaping what a government can actually pass, the talks are likely to run into the spring. Wilders has said he is willing to compromise, and his opponents have said they will not govern with him.

Economics explains the swing. The Netherlands has a housing shortage of roughly 400,000 homes, and rents in the largest cities have risen faster than wages for a decade. Immigration has increased, and the asylum system has been unable to process applications within the legal deadline, which forced the previous government to consider measures that its own coalition partners rejected. The cost of living rose sharply in 2022 and 2023, and the parties that had run the country carried the blame.

For Europe, the Dutch result adds to a pattern that the continent has been tracing since 2015. Populist parties of the right have entered or led governments in Italy, Hungary, Poland and Slovakia, and they have grown in France, Germany and Sweden. The Dutch case is distinctive because the system's fragmentation makes it hard for any single party to govern alone, which turns every election into a negotiation about what the mainstream is prepared to accept. Formation talks began the following week with a scout appointed by the speaker of the house, and the first rounds of interviews produced no agreement on even the shape of a cabinet.
