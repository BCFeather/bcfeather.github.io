---
title: "Odysseus lands near the lunar south pole and tips over"
date: 2024-02-23 21:15:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Intuitive Machines lander touched down on 22 February near Malapert A, the first commercial spacecraft and the first American vehicle to land softly on the Moon since 1972, then came to rest on its side."
---

Lander built by Intuitive Machines touched down on the Moon at twenty-three minutes to midnight universal time on the twenty-second of February, near a crater called Malapert A about three hundred kilometres from the south pole. It was the first soft landing by a commercial spacecraft and the first by an American vehicle since Apollo 17 in December 1972. Company confirmed the landing about ten minutes later than expected, after a period in which the signal was intermittent. By the following morning it had established that Odysseus was resting on its side at an angle of about thirty degrees, and that its solar panels were still generating power.

Descent had been in difficulty before it began. Laser rangefinders were not working, because a safety switch that arms them had not been flipped before launch, and the navigation system was therefore flying without its principal altitude sensor. Engineers at the company's control centre in Houston patched the lander's software in the final hours to feed it data from an experimental NASA instrument on board, which measures velocity and altitude by laser. Patch worked, and the landing happened despite it. Company later confirmed that the failure was caused by a procedural omission rather than by the hardware.

Site is the farthest south any spacecraft has set down, in terrain of craters and permanently shadowed ground where water ice is thought to be present in the soil. That is why NASA paid for the delivery: the agency's commercial lunar payload programme buys rides for instruments rather than building landers itself. Odysseus carried six NASA payloads and six commercial ones, including a camera experiment and a radio beacon, and it was expected to operate for about a week until the Sun set at the site and the solar panels stopped producing power.

Tilt limited what the mission could do. Some antennas were pointed at the ground, the data rate was lower than planned, and at least one camera was unable to deploy. Company said the science instruments were mostly functioning and that images had been returned, including one taken during the descent. Mission control declared the landing a success on the second attempt at communication and published the first picture the next day, and the spacecraft continued to transmit until the end of February, when it was put to sleep ahead of the lunar night.

Programme has had a difficult year. The previous American commercial lander, built by Astrobotic, failed in January because of a propellant leak and never reached the Moon. Japan's SLIM landed in January and ended up face down. Odysseus reached the surface and stayed in contact for a week, which is enough to count as a success, and the fact that it did so after a software patch written in the last hours is the part engineers will study.

Second mission is already in preparation. Intuitive Machines has said it will fly again later in the year, with a lander designed to test the same navigation hardware and to carry a drill for the south polar soil. NASA's Artemis programme is still aiming to return astronauts to the surface before the end of the decade, and the commercial deliveries are meant to be the rehearsal: the instruments, the landing software and the communications links are all being proven on machines that carry no crew. Whether the timetable holds is a question about money as much as about engineering, and the answer will come from Congress rather than from Houston.
