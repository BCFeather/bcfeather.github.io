---
title: "The Fed lifts rates again and hints that the end is near"
date: 2023-05-03 21:15:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "On 3 May the Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate by a quarter point to 5.25 per cent, the tenth increase in fourteen months, and changed the language of its statement in a way that markets read as a signal to stop."
---

On 3 May the Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate by a quarter of a percentage point, lifting the federal funds target to a range of 5 to 5.25 per cent, the highest level since 2007. It was the tenth consecutive increase since March 2022, and the smallest of the cycle, after four straight moves of three quarters of a point. What changed the mood was the language rather than the number, which was fully expected: a single sentence in the statement that accompanied it.

One sentence said that the committee would take into account the cumulative tightening of policy, the lags with which it affects activity, and the economic and financial developments to come. In the previous statement, the same clause had said that some additional policy firming may be appropriate. Removing those words, in the coded language of central banking, is as close as the institution gets to announcing that the end of the cycle is in sight without promising it.

The case for stopping rests on a slowdown that has been gradual and uneven. Consumer price inflation peaked above nine per cent last summer and had fallen to 4.9 per cent in April, still more than twice the two per cent target. Core prices, which exclude food and energy, have been the stubborn part, held up by rents and by services where wages are the main cost. Unemployment, at 3.4 per cent, is at its lowest in more than half a century, and the labour market has kept adding jobs even as the rate rises.

Caution comes from the banking system. The collapse of Silicon Valley Bank in March and the seizure of First Republic at the start of May by JPMorgan, the second largest bank failure in American history, have made lenders more careful with the loans they write. A credit squeeze does the work of a rate rise without the committee having to vote on one, and the chairman made the point plainly, saying that the tighter conditions would weigh on activity and that the committee would be watching.

Markets heard the pause and started pricing cuts. Futures contracts moved within an hour to imply reductions before the end of the year, and the two-year Treasury yield fell below four per cent for the first time since the previous autumn. That reaction is a bet on a central bank that has broken something, and the chairman has spent months saying that no cuts are coming in 2023. The gap between those two expectations is the space in which the next few months will be argued.

For the rest of the world, the effect runs through the dollar and through debt. A pause in American rates tends to weaken the currency, which eases the import bills and the dollar-denominated loans of poorer countries that spent 2022 watching their own reserves fall. It also removes, at least for now, the pressure that had pushed the euro and the yen to multi-decade lows. A rate peak is a relief for the borrowers at the edge of the system, and the question that remains is how long the peak lasts.
