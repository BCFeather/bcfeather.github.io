---
title: "The Fed raised rates by three quarters of a point"
date: 2022-06-15 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "The Federal Reserve lifted its benchmark range to between one and a half and one and three quarters per cent, the largest single increase since 1994, and signaled that another of the same size was possible in July."
---

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate by three quarters of a percentage point on 15 June, to a range of between one and a half and one and three quarters per cent. It was the largest single increase since November 1994, and it came a week after the May inflation report had shown prices rising faster than at any point in forty years. The committee had been expected to move by half a point until the report landed. One official preferred a smaller increase, according to the minutes published three weeks later.

Fourteen of the eighteen officials who set policy supported the larger move, and the projections published with the decision showed the median participant expecting the rate to reach about three and a quarter per cent by the end of the year. That implies further increases at every meeting in 2022, and the chair, Jerome Powell, told reporters that another three-quarter-point rise was on the table for July.

Language in the statement changed in one important way. Officials dropped the sentence that had promised to keep inflation averaging two per cent over time and replaced it with a commitment to returning it to that target, which is a stronger promise and one that constrains future easing. They also acknowledged that the war in Ukraine and lockdowns in China were contributing to supply problems that monetary policy cannot fix. The projections also showed unemployment rising to three point nine per cent by the end of 2023, which is consistent with a slowdown rather than a recession.

Markets had already priced much of the move. Mortgage rates had reached six per cent for a thirty-year fixed loan, the highest since 2008, and the dollar was trading near a twenty-year high against a basket of currencies. Stocks rose on the day of the decision and fell the next, which is the pattern that has followed every meeting this year, because investors keep hoping for a pause and officials keep declining to offer one. Applications for new mortgages had fallen to the lowest level in two decades as the rate rise priced buyers out.

Global consequences arrive through the dollar. Countries that borrow in American currency face higher servicing costs and weaker local currencies, and several emerging markets had raised rates earlier in the year to defend their exchange rates. The European Central Bank, which had been slower to move, called an unscheduled meeting the same week to discuss the widening spread between German and Italian bonds. The yen fell to its weakest level against the dollar since 1998 in the same week.

The precedents being invoked are uncomfortable ones. The last tightening cycle of comparable speed, in 1994, was followed by a bond-market sell-off and a recession in Mexico, and the cycle that began in 2004 ended with the financial crisis. Powell has argued that the labour market is strong enough to absorb higher rates, and the unemployment rate, at three and six-tenths per cent, supports the argument for now. He has also said that the aim is to bring inflation down without a deep recession, a combination achieved twice in the past fifty years.
