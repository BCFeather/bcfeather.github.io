---
title: "Russia says Mariupol is cleared except for the Azovstal works"
date: 2022-04-16 23:20:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "After seven weeks of fighting that destroyed most of the city, Russian forces claimed the urban area and left a steel plant holding the last defenders and a large number of civilians in its bunkers."
---

Russian forces claimed on 16 April that they had cleared Mariupol of Ukrainian resistance except for the Azovstal steel works, the sprawling Soviet-era plant on the eastern edge of the city where the remaining defenders had withdrawn. Ukrainian officials confirmed that the urban area had largely fallen, while the plant, with its tunnels, blast furnaces and underground shelters, held out.

Seven weeks of fighting destroyed most of the city. Mariupol had about 430,000 residents before the war, and satellite images by the middle of April showed residential districts reduced to rubble, with the theatre, the hospital and the port damaged or destroyed. The city's maternity hospital was struck in March, and the number of civilians killed there is still being counted by international organizations. Municipal workers began counting the dead in the yards behind the buildings that still stood, and the mayor's office said the true figure would take months to establish. The city had been without running water since early March, and residents had melted snow and collected rain from the roofs.

Civilians inside the plant became the centre of the story. Several thousand people, including children, had taken shelter in the plant's bunkers and underground passages, and Ukrainian officials and the Red Cross negotiated corridors to bring them out. Evacuation convoys that had been agreed in principle repeatedly failed to run, and those who did leave described weeks without food, water or medicine. A first convoy of about a hundred people reached Zaporizhzhia at the start of May, and the rest waited inside the plant while negotiations continued.

Grinding down Mariupol was the first case in this war where a city was taken by siege rather than by assault. Russian forces cut water, gas and electricity in early March, then pounded the residential districts with artillery and air strikes, then moved infantry through the ruins block by block, a method that cost them heavily and left nothing standing for the people who remained.

On 21 April, Vladimir Putin told a meeting in Moscow that Mariupol had been liberated and ordered that the plant be sealed rather than stormed, so that, in his words, not even a fly could pass through. The order was a recognition that a direct assault on the underground works would be expensive, and it converted a military problem into a siege that could last for weeks. Ukrainian commanders said their orders were to hold, and their appeal for a military extraction never produced the force they asked for.

The fall of the city gave Russia a land bridge from the Donbas to Crimea and a port that had been closed since 2014, and it cost Ukraine an industrial base that employed tens of thousands. Whether that exchange was worth the destruction is a question for the reconstruction budget, and the first estimate of what it will cost to rebuild Mariupol arrived before the fighting there had ended. Estimates for rebuilding the city ran into the billions before the last district had been surveyed. The port had handled a fifth of Ukraine's steel exports, and the mills that fed it will need years to restart.
