---
title: "Musk buys Twitter and tells half the staff their jobs are over"
date: 2022-11-04 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "Elon Musk completes his purchase of Twitter and, within a week, tells roughly half the staff that their jobs are over."
---

The takeover closed on 27 October, at forty-four billion dollars, and Elon Musk walked into the company's headquarters carrying a sink. Photographs of that arrival circulated for days, and they set the tone for everything that followed. He had spent months arguing in public about the number of fake accounts on the platform, and that argument had ended in a lawsuit which he lost. Then the deal closed anyway, and the company he had described as badly run became his to run, with the debt he had taken on to buy it.

On the evening of 4 November, about half of the company's seven thousand five hundred employees received an email telling them that their positions had been eliminated. The messages arrived in batches, some of them to people who had spent the afternoon in meetings about the transition. Access to corporate systems was cut within hours, and the badges that opened the doors stopped working before the end of the night. Some of those who remained slept in the office, because there was nobody left to hand over the passwords to.

Twitter had never made cuts of that kind, and the company had been losing money for years before the takeover. Its new owner had borrowed heavily to buy it, so the interest payments alone were a burden the old company never carried, and the debt was secured against the business. Musk argued in public that the site was spending far more than it earned, and that the payroll had to shrink before anything else could be repaired. He also said that a smaller team of people willing to work long hours would move faster than a larger one.

Departures ran through every department. Engineers who maintained the servers, moderators who handled reports of abuse, staff who dealt with advertisers, and people whose job was to keep the service standing during a large news event all left in the same wave. Contractors were dismissed by a separate message, and some of them learned of it from colleagues rather than from the company. The teams that ran moderation systems in languages other than English were reduced to almost nothing, and the people who remained were told to expect longer hours.

Advertisers watched with care. Several large brands paused their spending, uneasy about the direction of the platform and about the teams that had been asked to leave. Civil rights groups warned that the people who policed hate speech had been thinned out just as a series of elections was about to bring a flood of posts. The company's own trust and safety staff said in public that they had lost the tools they needed to do the work they were hired for.

By the weekend the site still worked, and the argument about what had happened had already begun. Supporters described a bloated company being forced to face its costs, and pointed at the years of losses and the size of the payroll. Critics pointed at the systems now being run by fewer hands than ever, and asked what would happen the first time something broke at three in the morning.
