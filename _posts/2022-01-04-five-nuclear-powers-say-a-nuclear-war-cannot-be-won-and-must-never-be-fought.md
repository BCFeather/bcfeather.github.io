---
title: "Five nuclear powers say a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought"
date: 2022-01-04 20:15:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "A joint statement from the foreign ministries of Britain, China, France, Russia and the United States repeated a line from 1985 at a moment when the two largest arsenals were arguing about Ukraine."
---

The five permanent members of the Security Council issued a joint statement on 3 January affirming that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought, and that their arsenals exist for defensive purposes. The text was released simultaneously by the foreign ministries of Britain, China, France, Russia and the United States, and it repeated language that Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev had used in Geneva in 1985.

Its text was thin on commitments and notable for the moment it chose. Russia had massed troops near Ukraine's borders, the United States and NATO were preparing a written reply to Russian demands about alliance expansion, and the two countries had suspended most of the arms control architecture that had kept their arsenals predictable. A sentence both governments could sign in January was one both could cite in February. The document ran to a few hundred words and appeared without a communiqué, which is the format governments use when they want the text and not the argument.

Its substance was the part that arms control specialists read most closely. The five affirmed that none of their weapons were targeted at each other and that they would continue to seek bilateral and multilateral arrangements to prevent war. They said nothing about the New START treaty, which was due to expire in 2026, nothing about the INF treaty that had collapsed in 2019, and nothing about the tactical warheads that Russia had deployed in Kaliningrad and that NATO planners worried about most. Two of the five had deployed new warheads in the previous year, and a third had tested a hypersonic glide vehicle, so the sentence about defensive purposes described an intention rather than a posture.

Timing had a bureaucratic explanation as well. France held the rotating presidency of the Council of the European Union in the first half of 2022 and had been assembling the statement since the autumn, and China's foreign ministry had been looking for a text that demonstrated great-power responsibility ahead of the Beijing Winter Olympics. Each capital could present the document at home as evidence of restraint by the others.

What the statement does not do is constrain behaviour. Declaratory policy of this kind is useful mainly as a signal that communications channels remain open, and the test of that signal arrived within weeks, when Russia put its deterrent forces on a higher state of alert and when the two sides stopped pretending that the other's assurances were worth much. Whether the sentence survives the war that began seven weeks later depends on whether either government wants to be reminded of it. By then the document had been overtaken by events that neither capital described in the same words, and the sentence survived mainly as a reference point for commentators.

Diplomacy in January still had room for sentences that everybody could sign. The value of such sentences is that they establish a floor below which nobody wants to be seen to fall, and that floor is worth having even when both sides are already climbing down from it.
