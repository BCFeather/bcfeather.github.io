title: "Haley loses New Hampshire by eleven points and stays in"
date: 2024-01-24 22:31:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Donald Trump beat Nikki Haley by about eleven points in New Hampshire on 23 January, a narrower margin than the polls had suggested in the state where she was expected to do best, and Haley said she would continue to South Carolina."
---

New Hampshire voted on the twenty-third of January and the result was the one the polls had predicted, with a margin nobody in either campaign had predicted two months earlier. Donald Trump won the Republican primary with about 54 per cent to Nikki Haley's 43, a lead of roughly eleven points and more than a hundred and seventy thousand votes to her hundred and forty thousand. Turnout was high for a New England primary, above three hundred thousand, and included a substantial number of independents who registered on the day and voted in the Republican contest, which had been the basis of Haley's hope in the state.

The shape of the result is what matters. Haley had spent the previous two months arguing that New Hampshire was the place where the party's moderate wing could be measured, and she ran a campaign built on college-educated voters, suburban women and the independents who make up a third of the state's registered electorate. That coalition showed up. Trump's vote was bigger, and it came from the same places it has come from since 2016, with a strong share of voters without a college degree, of rural towns, and of people who told exit pollsters that the economy or immigration was their first concern. Among voters who said they would be dissatisfied if Trump were the nominee, Haley won overwhelmingly. There simply were not enough of them.

Its closing week was ugly in a way that suggests how the rest of the year will go. Trump spent it attacking Haley by name, questioning her birthright as the daughter of immigrants, and warning that any donor who gave to her would be excluded from his operation for good. His campaign and the state party pressed her to withdraw before the vote, on the argument that a divided party helps the general election opponent. Haley refused, said the race was not over, and pointed out that she had won more votes in New Hampshire than any Republican woman had before. She has since said she will go on to South Carolina, the state she governed for six years, where the polling is worse for her than it was in New Hampshire.

On the Democratic side, the ballot produced its own signal. President Biden was not on it, having declined to appear because the state's primary date broke the party's calendar rules, and he won anyway as a write-in candidate by a margin that surprised the state's Democrats. That result was organised in a few weeks by volunteers and by the state party, and it is the kind of thing that a campaign remembers. It also made the contest that the party had tried to avoid, and it left the national committee with an argument about whether the calendar it designed was worth the trouble it caused.

Two primaries have now been held and the race is effectively settled. What is left is the question of how long the losing side keeps its case open. Haley's argument, that the party should think about the voters it needs in November, is correct on the arithmetic and has no traction among the voters who decide the nomination. Her candidacy will be remembered for one thing above others, which is that she was the last candidate standing between the party and the renomination of a man who had already lost a general election. That is a place in the record, and it is not a victory.
