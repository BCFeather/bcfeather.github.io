The festival dinner is increasingly assembled from prepared dishes that arrive frozen in a box. A braised pork, a steamed fish, a soup, a dessert, each sealed in a tray with a heating instruction printed on the lid, and the box is sold in a supermarket or ordered through an app in the fortnight before the holiday.

The industry behind the box has grown fast and it is now a substantial part of the food manufacturing sector. The companies that make the dishes run central kitchens that cook at a scale a restaurant cannot, they buy the raw material on contract, and they sell through the retail chains and the delivery platforms. The margin is thin and the volume is large, and the competition has pushed the price of a dish down to a level that a household can compare with the cost of the ingredients, which is a comparison that the industry would rather the household did not make.

The argument for the box is time. A festival dinner of eight dishes takes a household two days of shopping, preparing and cooking, and the days before the holiday are the days on which the household is also travelling, cleaning and receiving. The prepared dish buys back the two days, and the two days are what the buyer is paying for.

The argument against it is the taste and the texture. A dish that is cooked, frozen and reheated loses the crispness that a stir-fry depends on, and the sauces are built to survive the process rather than to be subtle. The result is food that is recognizably the dish and noticeably a copy of it, and the guests at the table can usually tell which plates came from the box.

The industry's own solution is a division of the menu. The dishes that survive freezing are the braised and the stewed, the dishes that do not are the fried and the steamed, and a sensible box contains the first and leaves the second to the kitchen. The households that use the box well are the ones that use it for the slow dishes and cook the quick ones themselves.

What the box changes is the labour that the festival used to require. The dinner was a demonstration of a household's skill as much as a meal, and the dish that took three hours was a gift of three hours. A household that buys the slow dishes is trading that gift for time, and the trade is honest even if the gift is smaller.

For the cook who wants to keep the tradition, the box is a tool rather than a replacement. The soup from the box and the vegetable from the wok make a dinner that is half assembled and half cooked, and the household that can tell the difference is the household that still knows how the dish should taste. That knowledge is the thing the box cannot deliver, and it is also the thing that a guest remembers a week after the meal.
