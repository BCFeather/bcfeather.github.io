date: 2022-04-10 23:55:00 +0800

description: "The incumbent took 27.85 per cent and the National Rally candidate 23.15, but a third of the electorate backed the left-wing candidate who finished two points behind her, which redrew the map for the final round."
---

Emmanuel Macron finished first in the opening round of the French presidential election on 10 April with 27.85 per cent, and Marine Le Pen advanced with 23.15, setting up a runoff that looked much closer than the same pairing five years earlier. The count from the interior ministry, certified later by the Constitutional Council, put Macron at 9.78 million votes and Le Pen at 8.13 million. Turnout was 73.69 per cent, so abstention reached 26.31, higher than in 2017 and concentrated among the young.

The result that changed the campaign was third place. Jean-Luc Melenchon, standing for a coalition of the left, took 21.95 per cent, about 7.7 million votes, and finished within two points of the runoff. His campaign had peaked late, drawing on younger voters and on a promise of a minimum wage increase and a lower retirement age, and his supporters became the most contested bloc in the two weeks that followed. He had asked voters for a third place strong enough to force the others to negotiate with him, and he came close to getting it.

The rest of the field collapsed. Eric Zemmour, standing for the far right, took 7.07 per cent, less than half of what polls had suggested in the winter. Valerie Pecresse of the Republicans took 4.78, the worst result for that party in its history, and the Socialist candidate, Anne Hidalgo, took 1.74, finishing within the noise of the fringe candidates. The Republicans had campaigned on tax cuts and never found an audience, and their result was the worst in the party's history.

Abstention shaped the arithmetic. Turnout was 73.69 per cent, so more than a quarter of the electorate stayed away, and the share of voters who chose a candidate outside the two main parties came to about half of those who voted. In a system built for two rounds, that fragmentation moves the decisive argument from the first round to the transfers of the second, and it makes the abstention rate as important as any candidate's total.

Macron's first-round coalition was narrower than it looked. He won comfortably among older voters and the wealthy, lost ground among the young and among workers to Melenchon, and faced a challenger whose campaign had spent months on purchasing power, pensions and the cost of fuel, which happen to be the issues the government had handled worst in the preceding year. His score came in slightly below the final polls, a reminder that an incumbent's support is softer than a headline suggests.

Le Pen's position was stronger than her percentage suggests, because she had spent five years softening her party's image and because the runoff gave her a pool of Melenchon voters who disliked the president more than they feared her. Whether they would abstain, vote for her or hold their noses for Macron was the only question that mattered between the two ballots. She had also avoided the confrontations of 2017, when a televised debate damaged her, and a rival on the far right had split the harder vote.
