---
title: "Floods in Emilia-Romagna put climate adaptation back on the agenda"
date: 2023-05-17 22:20:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Two rounds of rain in nine days turned the rivers of Italys richest agricultural region into torrents, killing fifteen people and displacing tens of thousands. The floods followed a drought that had left the same ground too hard to absorb water."
---

Torrential rain fell across Emilia-Romagna on 16 and 17 May, and by the second day more than twenty rivers in the region had broken their banks. The floods killed fifteen people, displaced tens of thousands, cut the main road and rail links between Bologna and the Adriatic coast, and submerged farmland that produces a large share of Italy's fruit and grain. Some towns recorded rainfall that in thirty-six hours exceeded their average for an entire spring.

Emilia-Romagna has seen this before. The region has been flooded four times in a decade, and the local authorities have an engineering system of embankments, basins and pumping stations that was designed in the 1970s and reinforced after each event. What defeated it this month was a combination of scale and saturation: the ground was still recovering from a drought that had baked it hard, so the first rain ran off rather than infiltrating, and the second storm found every channel already full.

Drought connects this flood to the wider argument. Italy's longest river, the Po, which runs across the north and through the region, fell to a third of its usual flow last summer, and the salt water of the Adriatic advanced forty kilometres up its channel. Farmers planted less, the hydroelectric reservoirs ran low, and the government declared a state of emergency in five regions. The same soil that could not absorb water in May was the soil that had baked in July.

The political response moved faster than the physical one. The prime minister cut short a trip to the G7 summit in Japan and returned to oversee the rescue, the cabinet released a first tranche of emergency funds, and the region's president asked for a national plan for land management rather than a series of emergency decrees. The argument that the country needs a single authority for river basins, which has been made by engineers for twenty years, has been revived by a disaster that no one can call unusual any more.

The economic bill will be counted in the autumn. Emilia-Romagna is one of the wealthiest regions in Europe, with an export economy built on machinery, food processing and ceramics, and the industrial districts around Bologna and Ravenna have been damaged in ways that insurance will not fully cover. The region's orchards were in fruit when the water came, and the harvest that was expected in June will be a fraction of it. Small firms that supply components to German factories will find their orders delayed by a month or more.

What the floods did to the national conversation is the durable effect. A country that has spent a decade arguing about whether to spend money on prevention has been given an answer by the water, and the answer is that the cost of not spending it is paid in lives and in reconstruction. Italy has a civil protection system that is admired abroad and a land-management budget that has been cut for years. The rain of May arrived in the gap between the two, and the gap will be filled or it will rain again.
