---
title: "Forty-two days without a case"
date: 2023-01-11 22:15:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Uganda declared the end of its outbreak of Sudan ebolavirus on 11 January 2023, forty-two days after the last confirmed case, with 164 cases and 77 deaths recorded across nine districts."
---

Uganda declared on 11 January that its outbreak of Ebola disease caused by the Sudan ebolavirus was over, forty-two days after the last confirmed case was buried. The outbreak had been declared on 20 September, when a twenty-four-year-old man in Mubende district was confirmed to have the virus after days of fever, convulsions and blood-stained vomiting. The man had been treated at a hospital in Mubende and then at a referral hospital in Kampala, and the confirmation came after his death.

The final count was 164 cases, of which 142 were confirmed in a laboratory and 22 were classified as probable, and 77 deaths, of which 55 were among the confirmed cases. The case fatality rate among confirmed patients was about 39 per cent. Nine districts reported cases, and two of them, Mubende and Kassanda, were placed under restrictions on movement for two months. The two restricted districts were released in December, after two incubation periods had passed without a case.

The virus is one of the four ebolaviruses that cause disease in humans, and it is the one for which no vaccine and no approved treatment existed. The vaccines that were used in the outbreaks in west Africa and in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo are built against the Zaire strain, and they do not cross-protect. Three candidate vaccines against the Sudan strain were rushed into a ring-vaccination trial during the outbreak, and the trial was still recruiting when the last case appeared. The ring trial was designed to vaccinate the contacts of every new case, which is the method used for Ebola in the west African outbreak in 2015.

Contact tracing, isolation wards and burial teams were what ended the outbreak. More than a thousand contacts were followed, some of them in Kampala, where the virus arrived in October and where a single case in a densely populated district could have restarted the epidemic. The ministry and the World Health Organization credited the speed of the tracing and the willingness of families to report symptoms.

Uganda had handled four previous Ebola outbreaks, three of them of the Sudan strain, and the experience showed in the way the districts organized themselves. Health workers were vaccinated against the Zaire strain as a precaution that had no basis in immunology, and the ritual of the response, which includes the careful burial of the dead, was performed by teams that had done it before. The health ministry's budget had been cut over the previous decade, and the response was paid for largely by donors.

An outbreak ends when the last contact is traced, and tracing is unglamorous work done by people whose names do not appear in the reports. The declaration on 11 January was a statement about that work, and about how quickly a country can do it when it has done it before. The clock started on 29 November, when the last confirmed patient was buried, and the forty-two days that followed were the incubation period observed twice over.
