---
title: "The Bank of England cuts rates to 4.75 per cent"
date: 2024-11-08 21:15:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "The Bank of England lowered its benchmark rate by a quarter point on 7 November to 4.75 per cent, its second cut of the year, and said the budget would add to inflation over the next two years."
---

Bank of England cut its benchmark interest rate by a quarter of a percentage point on the seventh of November, taking it to 4.75 per cent. Decision was the second reduction of the year, after a cut in August, and it was supported by eight of the nine members of the monetary policy committee, with one member voting to hold. Statement that accompanied it said inflation had fallen further than expected and that the committee would take a gradual approach to further reductions.

Inflation background made the cut easy to justify. Consumer price inflation fell below the two per cent target in September, to 1.7 per cent, and services inflation, which the committee watches closely, eased more than its forecasts had assumed. Wage growth remains above the level consistent with the target, and the committee said it needed to see that measure continue to fall before it could be confident the job was done. Unemployment has risen to 4.3 per cent from 3.8 per cent a year earlier, which is a loosening of the labour market that supports the case for lower rates.

Budget complicated the outlook and the committee said so plainly. Measures announced by the chancellor at the end of October, including higher employer national insurance contributions, higher minimum wages and extra spending on public services, will add to costs for businesses and to household incomes. Bank's revised forecast puts the effect at about half a percentage point on inflation at the peak, which is why it expects inflation to return to target more slowly than it did in August, and why it described the path of future cuts as gradual.

Market reaction was muted. Sterling was little changed, and gilt yields fell slightly as investors read the decision as confirmation that rates will reach about 3.75 per cent by the end of next year. Mortgage lenders had already priced in the cut, and the immediate effect on household budgets is small, because most fixed-rate mortgages in Britain are set against swap rates rather than the policy rate. Variable-rate borrowers will see the benefit within weeks.

Argument about the bank's independence runs alongside every decision. Government has promised to raise growth, and the budget shifted spending towards investment and capital projects, which the bank said would support demand over several years. Governor has been careful not to comment on fiscal choices, and the committee's minutes note that the fiscal loosening is one reason it cannot cut as quickly as the European Central Bank, which faces a weaker economy and has moved faster.

Balance of risks is now tilted towards holding rather than cutting. Committee said a sudden rise in oil prices or a further depreciation of sterling could push inflation back above target, and that the labour market remains tight by historical standards. On the other side, the economy has grown barely at all in the past six months, and business surveys have weakened since the budget. Both directions are plausible, which is why the bank has stopped giving forward guidance and is deciding one meeting at a time.
