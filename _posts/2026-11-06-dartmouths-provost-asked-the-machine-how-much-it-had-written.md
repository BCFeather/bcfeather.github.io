The most quoted detail of the Dartmouth affair is the question. According to the inquiry report released on 27 October, the provost, Santiago Schnell, once asked Claude whether an article he was preparing carried a lot of signs of AI writing. A strange sentence to read in a formal document: a senior academic checking with the machine itself about how much of the text the machine had produced. A day later, on 28 October, the college announced that he had stepped down and left the university.

The sequence is what makes it a story. On 9 August, Schnell published an article in *The Washington Post* arguing that universities were cracking down on AI cheating but that the problem went further: institutions needed to distinguish a student's independent thinking from work completed with AI assistance. On 26 August, reporters ran the article through AI-detection tools and reported that it read as fully machine-written. Schnell said the argument was entirely his own and that he had used ChatGPT to polish passages and check grammar, and that the piece had gone through several rounds of newspaper editing.

The inquiry, commissioned on 25 September, did not use detection software. It read the chat records. Reviewing his papers, his correspondence and the Claude logs the college obtained, the committee found that he had used Claude to substantially revise a research paper, to debug code and to produce figures, without disclosing it as the journal required; on 6 October he told the journal about ChatGPT but not about Claude. In another piece in preparation, he had asked the model how much AI writing it showed. He later told editors he had used AI to polish the text, which the committee judged an understatement of how much the tool had actually done. Even on his own account, the *Post* piece breached the paper's submission rules.

Two things are worth separating. The first is the hypocrisy, which the coverage has rightly noted: the person warning that universities were not going far enough was himself the case the warning described. The second is more uncomfortable, because it does not go away when the individual does. The rules he broke were remarkably vague. Journals ask authors to disclose AI use; few specify what counts, and fewer check. Newspapers have submission policies rarely stated in public and inconsistently enforced. Universities have academic integrity codes written for students, in language that assumes the line between assistance and authorship is obvious, which it is not.

What the inquiry actually did was reconstruct a workflow from logs. That is the part of the episode the sector should keep. Disclosure regimes in which the author describes their own conduct are, by construction, unable to catch the cases that matter, because the cases that matter are the ones where the author's account of their conduct is wrong. Logs, version histories and the plain record of what was pasted into a chat window are a different kind of evidence. The committee was careful to say it had not relied on detection tools, and it was right to be careful: those tools are unreliable, and the public argument over the *Post* piece turned on a measure that no serious person should treat as definitive.

One reading of the affair is that senior people should be held to the same standard as students. That is true, but it is the smaller point. The larger one is that the standard itself has not been written down with enough precision to be applied. A provost who asked a chatbot whether his article sounded like a chatbot cut a strange figure. He was also someone who had no other instrument available, because the institution he led had not yet built one. That is the gap the next few years will close, and it will be closed by records rather than by rhetoric.
