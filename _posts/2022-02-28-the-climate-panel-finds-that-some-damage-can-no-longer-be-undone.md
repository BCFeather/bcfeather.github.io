---
title: "The climate panel finds that some damage can no longer be undone"
date: 2022-02-28 23:45:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "A report approved by 195 governments says billions of people are exposed to hazards they cannot adapt to, and that money for adaptation is far below what is needed."
---

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change published the second part of its sixth assessment on 28 February, after its member governments approved the summary line by line in a meeting that ran into two extra days. The report deals with impacts, adaptation and vulnerability, and it is the work of 270 authors from 67 countries reading more than 34,000 studies. Its central finding is that warming of about 1.1 degrees since the industrial era has already put a large share of humanity in places where the next half degree will do serious damage.

Numbers give the scale. About 40 per cent of the world's population, some three billion people, live in contexts that the panel classifies as highly vulnerable, and most of them are in Africa, Asia and small island states. Deaths from floods, storms and droughts in those regions are fifteen times higher than in places with the least exposure, and the gap has widened since the last report in 2014. Heat, the panel says, has become the leading cause of weather-related death in every region where it measured the effect.

Some of the losses will outlast any policy. Coral reefs that warm past a threshold of about 1.5 degrees will lose most of their cover, and the species that depend on them will go with it. Mountain glaciers in the Andes, the Alps and the Hindu Kush will lose a large part of their mass this century whatever happens to emissions, and the rivers that they feed will change. Low-lying coasts face a rise in sea level that is now locked in for centuries, and the panel notes that the areas exposed will grow even if the rise stops.

Adaptation works, and the report is careful to say so. Early warning systems, mangrove belts, drought-resistant seed, urban drainage and heat plans have reduced deaths in places that adopted them, and the cost of these measures is usually a fraction of the damage they prevent. The limit comes when the hazard exceeds what a household or a city can pay for, and the panel identifies a growing number of places where that limit has been reached, which it calls the point at which adaptation fails.

Money is the constraint that the report returns to. Flows of finance for adaptation are five to ten times below the needs it estimates, and most of the money goes to the countries that have the least exposure and the most capacity to absorb it. Africa, which has contributed least to the accumulation of greenhouse gases, receives a small fraction of the total. Private finance has been reluctant, because the returns on a sea wall or a drainage scheme accrue to the public and cannot be captured by an investor.

The report was published on the same day that the war in Ukraine began to dominate the news, and the attention it received was smaller than the subject warrants. Its authors had spent seven years assembling evidence and its summary was negotiated by the governments that would have to act on it, which is the peculiar strength of the institution: every sentence in the summary has been agreed by a delegation that includes the largest emitters. The next part, on mitigation, appeared in April, and the synthesis that combined them was ready in time for the conference in Egypt in November. What the panel established in February was a frame for the argument of the following years: some damage can be prevented, some can be adapted to, and some is now unavoidable and will have to be paid for in the places that can least afford it. The third category is the one that the poorest countries have been describing in the negotiations since 1991, and the fund agreed at Sharm el-Sheikh in November was the first formal recognition that they had been right.
