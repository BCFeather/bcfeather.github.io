---
title: "An earthquake in West Java kills more than three hundred people in a district of small buildings"
date: 2022-11-21 23:55:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "A shallow shock of magnitude five point six brings down houses and schools in Cianjur, and the rescue runs through the night."
---

The ground shook for about twenty seconds just after one in the afternoon, and by midnight the district of Cianjur in West Java had counted more than three hundred dead and thousands injured. The magnitude was five point six, which would be modest in a place built for earthquakes. The depth was ten kilometres, which is shallow enough to be felt as a jolt rather than a sway. The damage was concentrated in a district of one-storey houses, schools and small workshops.

A strike-slip fault runs through the Cimandiri valley, and the energy was released close to the surface. Seismologists said the area sits on soft soil that amplifies the shaking, and that the buildings had not been designed for a lateral load. Most of the dead were inside structures that collapsed within seconds, and the survivors described the sound of roofs coming down before anybody could reach the door. A landslide blocked the road to one village for most of the evening.

Schools were among the buildings that failed. Officials said more than sixty had been damaged, and many of the children who died were in classrooms when the walls came down. The district's hospital treated patients in the car park because the building had lost its ceilings, and the injured were laid on tarpaulins under the trees. Relatives searched the rubble of their own houses with their hands, and the heavy equipment did not reach the most remote villages until the next day.

Volunteers ran the rescue for the first hours. Police, soldiers and the search teams from the capital arrived through the evening, and the priority was to reach people trapped in the debris before the night got cold. The health ministry set up field stations, and the local government asked for tents, water and generators. Power and telephone lines were cut, which meant that the first reports came from people who had walked to the nearest town.

President Joko Widodo visited the district the following morning and promised that the government would pay for the reconstruction of damaged houses and schools. The national disaster agency said it would need several weeks to complete the survey, and that the toll would rise as the teams reached the villages that had been cut off. The finance ministry released an initial sum for emergency spending, and the provincial governor asked for national help. The army sent a field hospital, and the search for survivors continued into a third day with dogs and thermal cameras.

Indonesia sits on three tectonic plates and has had this kind of disaster before, and the pattern of the casualties repeats with each one. Earthquakes cannot be stopped, and buildings can be built to survive them, and the enforcement of that standard is the part that fails. The district had grown quickly in the previous decade, and much of the new construction had been put up without a permit or an engineer. The dead were killed by the buildings rather than by the ground, which is the sentence that every seismologist in the country writes after a week like this.
