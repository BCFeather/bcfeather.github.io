---
title: "Jackson begins the hearings that will put her on the court"
date: 2022-03-21 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Four days of questions about sentencing, about race and about a career spent on the other side of the bench, and a vote that follows the party lines."
---

Judge Ketanji Brown Jackson appeared before the Senate Judiciary Committee on 21 March for four days of hearings on her nomination to the Supreme Court, and the first day was given to introductions and to the photographs of her family. She had been a judge on the court of appeals in Washington for less than a year and had spent eight years before that as a district judge, and she had been confirmed by the Senate three times, twice with Republican votes. The nomination had been announced in February, after Justice Stephen Breyer said he would retire at the end of the term.

Her career had a shape that the committee had not seen before. She had been a public defender in Washington, a lawyer in private practice, an attorney for the sentencing commission, and a clerk for Justice Breyer himself. She spoke about her parents, who had grown up under segregation in Miami, and about the schools that she had attended, and she described the work of a trial judge as the work of applying rules that other people had written to facts that both parties would dispute.

The Republican questioning concentrated on her record on child exploitation cases, on the sentences she had imposed, and on the material that conservative groups had published about her time on a board of a school. Several senators asked whether she had been lenient in the cases involving images of children, and she answered with the guidelines, the plea agreements and the ranges that the law had required in each case, and she said that no judge should be judged by a single file.

Critical race theory came up in almost every session. She said that she had not studied the theory, that it had not been part of her teaching, and that her approach to the bench was to apply the law and to explain it. The question had been used against nominees in several states and in school board elections, and the senators who asked it were less interested in her answer than in the record of the vote that followed.

Democrats used their time to establish a contrast with the previous three nominations, which had been confirmed by narrow margins and had produced a court that moved further to the right than the country had expected. They asked about the cases that might come before her, about abortion, about voting rights and about the powers of the administrative state, and she declined to answer most of them, on the ground that a nominee should not prejudge a case that she might hear.

The committee deadlocked at 11 to 11, which sent the nomination to the full Senate without a recommendation, and the Senate confirmed her on 7 April by 57 votes to 47. Three Republicans voted with the Democrats, and the vote was the closest for a justice since the confirmation of Clarence Thomas in 1991. She took the two oaths in the following week and became the first Black woman and the first former public defender to sit on the court.
