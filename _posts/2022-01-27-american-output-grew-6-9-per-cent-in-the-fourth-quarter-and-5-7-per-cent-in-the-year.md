---
title: "American output grew 6.9 per cent in the fourth quarter and 5.7 per cent in the year"
date: 2022-01-27 22:50:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "A quarter driven by inventories closed the fastest calendar year since 1984, and the composition of the figure warned that the momentum had come from stock building rather than from sales."
---

The Commerce Department reported on 27 January that American output grew at an annual rate of 6.9 per cent in the fourth quarter, and that growth over the whole of 2021 came to 5.7 per cent, the fastest calendar year since 1984. Economists had expected about 5.5 per cent for the quarter, and the surprise was supplied by businesses adding to their inventories at the fastest pace in a year. Economists also noted that the quarter's strength had been concentrated in October and November, and that the Omicron wave had slowed activity in December. Inventory building had added about five percentage points to the quarter, which is a contribution that cannot repeat without sales growing at the same pace.

Annual figure tells a story about the year as a whole. Output had fallen at an annual rate of about 19 per cent in the second quarter of 2020, and it recovered in stages: a rebound in the second half of that year, a first half of 2021 driven by stimulus cheques and by the reopening of services, and a second half that slowed as the same stock-building and imports that filled shelves began to reverse. The price index for gross domestic purchases rose at an annual rate of 6.9 per cent, which explains why the central bank moved as it did.

Quarterly detail showed where the strength came from. Consumer spending on goods stayed high while spending on services recovered, and business investment in equipment rose. Government spending fell as pandemic-era programmes expired, and the trade deficit widened, subtracting from the total. The arithmetic of those components left growth dependent on inventory accumulation, and inventories can reverse quickly.

A number that matters for policy sits where the report puts little emphasis. Economists calculated that final sales to private domestic purchasers, a measure that strips out inventories and trade, grew at about 1.6 per cent, well below the headline. A quarter that looks strong in aggregate and soft underneath is what a late-cycle economy looks like when firms stock up ahead of expected price increases.

Timing is awkward for the politics of the year. The report landed the week before the Federal Reserve's January meeting, and it allowed both camps to claim what they wanted: the administration pointed to the fastest annual growth in decades, and its critics pointed to the inventory contribution and to the fact that the fourth quarter's momentum had already faded by January. Revisions published over the following weeks moved the quarterly figure, and the annual rate for 2021 stayed at 5.7 per cent.

A year of growth at 5.7 per cent ended with inflation at 7 per cent and with a central bank about to tighten. The two numbers describe a run of demand that outran supply, and the report's composition says that some of the demand was firms buying ahead of anticipated shortages, which is the kind of spending that stops when the shortages are over. The report also showed that the personal saving rate had fallen to 7.9 per cent, down from 13.7 per cent a year earlier.
