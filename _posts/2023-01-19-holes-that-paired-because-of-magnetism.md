---
title: "Holes that paired because of magnetism"
date: 2023-01-19 23:55:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "A paper in *Nature* on 19 January 2023 reported the observation of magnetically mediated pairing between holes in an ultracold gas of fermionic atoms, a simulation of a mechanism proposed for unconventional superconductors."
---

A paper in *Nature* on 19 January reported that holes moving through an ultracold gas of fermionic atoms paired with one another, and that the pairing was produced by magnetic correlations rather than by the vibration of a lattice. The experiment was done with atoms held in an optical lattice at temperatures of a few billionths of a kelvin above absolute zero, where quantum effects dominate and where the gas can be tuned with magnetic fields. The lattice is made of laser light, and the atoms sit in it the way electrons sit in a crystal, which is why the system is called a quantum simulator.

Superconductivity requires electrons to pair, and electrons repel each other, so the pairing has to be mediated by something else. In conventional superconductors the mediator is the vibration of the crystal lattice, and the theory that describes it won a Nobel prize in 1972. In the copper-oxide superconductors, which were discovered in 1986 and still resist a complete explanation, the candidate mediator is the magnetic interaction between the spins of the electrons. The theory of the copper oxides has been argued over since 1986, and the argument is about which interaction does the pairing rather than about whether pairing happens.

The experiment simulated the second case. The researchers prepared a gas with two kinds of fermionic atoms, loaded it into a lattice, and removed some of the atoms to create holes, which behave like positively charged carriers in a solid. They then measured the correlations between holes and found that pairs formed even though the holes repelled each other in the absence of the surrounding spins. The measurement compared the number of pairs at two different strengths of the magnetic interaction, and the difference was the signature the theory predicted.

What the result amounts to is an analogue rather than a demonstration. The gas simulates a model of a superconductor rather than superconducts, because the atoms are neutral and there is no electrical current to measure. What the experiment establishes is that the magnetic correlations in the model are strong enough to overcome the repulsion, which is the part of the theory that had been argued about for three decades.

The limits are the ones that cold-atom work always has. The systems are small, they last for milliseconds, and the temperatures are achievable only in a laboratory with lasers and vacuum chambers that fill a room. The pairing observed in the gas also has no long-range order yet, which is what a real superconductor has and what the next generation of experiments will try to produce. The group's next experiment is designed to look for the long-range order that would make the gas a superfluid, and it needs a larger lattice and a colder gas.

What a simulator offers is a place to test the arithmetic of a theory. The arithmetic of the copper oxides has been checked for thirty-seven years in crystals and in cuprates, and it is now being checked in a gas that has never been a metal.
