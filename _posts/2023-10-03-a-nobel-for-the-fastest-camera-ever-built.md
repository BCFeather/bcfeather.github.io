title: "A Nobel for the fastest camera ever built"
date: 2023-10-03 20:15:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "Pierre Agostini, Ferenc Krausz and Anne L'Huillier shared the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physics, announced on 3 October 2023, for methods that generate attosecond pulses of light and make electron motion observable."
---

Electrons move on the scale of attoseconds, which is a billionth of a billionth of a second, and for most of the history of physics that motion could only be inferred from the energy it left behind. The 2023 prize in physics went to three researchers who built the tools to watch it happen, and the academy described their contribution as a way of producing light pulses short enough to resolve the steps of a chemical reaction. The unit is hard to conceive: a second contains as many attoseconds as there are seconds in about thirty billion years.

Anne L'Huillier found in 1987 that a laser fired at a gas could produce overtones far above the frequency of the driving light, a phenomenon called high harmonic generation. Her work showed that the overtones interfered with each other in a way that produced a train of extremely short pulses, and she spent the following decade turning the observation into a usable source. Pierre Agostini's group in France then measured a pulse lasting about 250 attoseconds in 2001, and Ferenc Krausz's group in Vienna isolated a single pulse of similar length in the same year. Both experiments rested on the same insight, that the overtones stay in phase for only a few cycles of the driving laser.

Applications sit mostly in chemistry and materials. A pulse that short can track an electron leaving a molecule, which is the first step in every reaction, and the technique lets researchers watch charge move through a semiconductor or a protein instead of guessing at the path from the products. Krausz has argued that the method could eventually allow medical diagnostics based on molecular fingerprints, because different molecules respond to the pulses in distinguishable ways.

L'Huillier is the fifth woman to receive the physics prize, and she told the press conference that she was teaching when the call came and had to be persuaded to pick up. She has worked at Lund University for most of her career, and the citation credits her with the foundational discovery rather than the instrumentation alone. The three will share eleven million Swedish kronor.

Recent prizes follow a pattern in which the academy honours measurement rather than theory. Recent awards have gone to optical tweezers, to gravitational wave detection and to the physics of climate modelling, all of which changed what the field could observe. Attosecond metrology belongs in that line, and it has already produced a generation of experiments that no earlier instrument could attempt.

What remains difficult is the gap between the laboratory and the applications promised for it. Attosecond sources are delicate, they need vacuum and precise alignment, and the experiments run on samples measured in the thousands rather than in the trillions. The tools honoured this week have changed what can be seen, and the question of what can be done with the sight is still open. A field that measures in attoseconds has to build its own instruments, and the groups doing that work are small.
