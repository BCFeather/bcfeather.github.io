---
title: "Sri Lanka declares a state of emergency as fuel queues turn into protests"
date: 2022-04-01 22:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Queues outside filling stations had been lengthening for a month and the power cuts deepening, and when the crowd at Colombo's seafront refused to disperse the president invoked emergency powers and sent in the police."
---

Gotabaya Rajapaksa declared a state of emergency on 1 April and imposed a curfew across the island, after protests at Galle Face Green in Colombo grew through the week and police moved in with tear gas and water cannon. Dozens of officers were injured in the clashes, according to police statements, and more than fifty people were arrested. What had begun as a few hundred people sitting on the grass by the sea had turned into a permanent camp with tents, kitchens and a slogan that shortened the president's name into an insult.

Fuel explains the camp. Queues outside filling stations had stretched for kilometres since February, cooking gas had disappeared from shops, and the state utility had begun power cuts of up to thirteen hours a day because it could not buy diesel for its turbines. Hospitals reported shortages of medicines and surgeons worked by torchlight, schools sent children home, and families that had once employed drivers and cooks queued alongside them for a few litres in a can. Three-wheelers parked overnight outside the depots, and drivers slept in their cabs to hold a place in the line.

The balance of payments explains the queue. Tourism, which supplied about five per cent of output before the pandemic, collapsed in 2020, and remittances fell as the currency slid. The government cut taxes in 2019 just as revenue was needed, kept the rupee pegged long after the reserves ran down, and serviced dollar debt while printing rupees at home, so that by the spring the central bank could not pay for cargoes of fuel that had already arrived in port and were waiting offshore on demurrage.

Emergency powers under the public security ordinance let a president authorize detention, restrict movement and deploy the army, and the government used them against a movement that had no leader, no party and no organization. That mismatch made the crackdown counterproductive: the crowd grew after the arrests, and the demand widened from cheaper fuel to the departure of a family that has dominated politics since 2005, with the president's brothers holding the premiership and then the finance ministry. Ministers stayed in office through the first week of the emergency, which told the crowd that nothing had changed.

The economics leave little room to manoeuvre. Talks with the International Monetary Fund had opened in March, and any programme would require a restructuring of external debt and an end to subsidies that the budget cannot carry. The government suspended payments on some foreign debt in April, which bought time but closed the door to new borrowing, and both steps are politically expensive in a year when it must also persuade the public that the shortage will end.

Emergency rule lasted only days and changed nothing structural. Sri Lanka's crisis shows how quickly a small open economy can run out of the things it imports, long before it runs out of the money it prints, and that the political cost of a shortage is paid in the street rather than in the budget.
