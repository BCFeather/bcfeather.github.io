---
title: "Apple ships macOS Ventura and iPadOS 16, three weeks behind the iPhone"
date: 2022-10-24 23:20:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "Stage Manager arrives on both platforms, Continuity Camera turns an iPhone into a webcam, and the settings panel is rebuilt to match iOS."
---

Apple released macOS Ventura and iPadOS 16 on Monday, and both arrive later in the year than the phones that were meant to introduce the season's software. The Mac update had been promised for October, and the tablet version had been held back from September because its flagship feature still needed work. Owners of the newest tablets get the full set of tools, and owners of older ones get a reduced version. The two releases make up the last piece of a software year that began in June.

Ventura's changes are mostly about how the Mac talks to the phone and the tablet. Continuity Camera turns an iPhone into a webcam, with the phone mounted behind the screen, and Handoff now passes a video call from one device to another in the middle of a conversation. The settings window has been rebuilt to look like the one on iOS, which is a change that long-time users have opinions about. Stage Manager puts the windows of an app into a strip on the left and lets them be grouped, and it arrived after a summer of argument about whether a desktop needs it.

On the tablet the same window manager comes with support for an external display, which is the feature that the testers complained about most during the beta. It allows four apps on the screen and four more on a monitor, though the windows still snap to a grid rather than float freely. The Weather app finally reaches the tablet, and Messages can edit and unsend a message within a time limit. Freeform, the whiteboard app that the company showed in June, was announced for later in the year and shipped with a later update.

Apple announced the hardware that runs this software on 18 October, and it went on sale on Wednesday. The iPad Pro moved to the M2 chip and gained a hover feature for the pencil, and the entry-level iPad lost its home button and gained a larger screen and a USB-C port. Prices rose in Britain and Europe, by about twenty per cent in some configurations, which the company attributed to the strength of the dollar. The pound's fall against the dollar made the increase larger in London than in New York.

The strategy behind the releases is visible in the software. The same window manager, the same settings layout and the same widgets now appear on both platforms, and the difference between them narrows to the input device. Apple has argued for a decade that a tablet is a computer for people who would rather not use a computer, and this year's release pushes the two closer together than any previous one. Whether that serves either platform is the question the reviewers have taken up.

The first reviews were warm about the hardware and cooler about the software. Testers found the new window manager hard to learn and slower than the split view it replaces, and several reported that the external display support had slipped to a later update. Apple has promised fixes in the next release and has said the feature is aimed at a professional audience that will use it in a fixed setup. The update costs nothing, and the machines that make the most of it are the ones that cost the most.
