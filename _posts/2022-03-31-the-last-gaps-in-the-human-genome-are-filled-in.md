---
title: "The last gaps in the human genome are filled in"
date: 2022-03-31 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "Six papers, one cell line and about two hundred million bases that twenty years of sequencing could not reach, including the repeats at the centre of every chromosome."
---

Twenty-one years after the first draft, the human genome got a complete sequence. Six papers published in Science on 31 March described a genome of 3.05 billion base pairs assembled from end to end, with no gaps, by a consortium of about a hundred researchers led by Karen Miga and Adam Phillippy. The sequence came from a single cell line, code-named CHM13, that had been derived from a hydatidiform mole, a tumour that carries only the paternal copy of the genome and is therefore homozygous at every position.

About 8 per cent of the genome, roughly 200 million bases, had resisted every method that the Human Genome Project had used. The missing regions followed a pattern: they were the centromeres, the telomeres, the arrays of ribosomal DNA and the segmental duplications, which are the places where the sequence repeats itself with small variations. A short read that comes from a repeat cannot be placed, because the same string occurs a thousand times, and the assemblers of 2001 left those places blank.

The technology that filled them reads longer. The PacBio instrument produces reads of tens of thousands of bases with an accuracy above 99 per cent, and the Oxford Nanopore instrument produces reads that can exceed a hundred thousand bases at lower accuracy, and the two together covered regions that a short read could never span. Optical maps gave the order of the large repeats, and the assembly was checked against the sequence of a second cell line. The consortium published the sequence with the annotations that had been rebuilt from scratch.

The centromeres turned out to be more variable and more structured than the drafts had suggested. Each one is an array of a repeated unit of about 171 bases, organized into higher-order repeats that differ between chromosomes, and the arrays vary in length between people by a factor of ten. The telomeres, which shorten with age, were resolved to the last base, and the ribosomal DNA arrays, which code for the machinery that makes proteins, were found to vary between chromosomes in ways that the older maps had flattened.

Medical consequences were immediate and modest. About a hundred new protein-coding genes were predicted in the regions that had been missing, some of them expressed in the brain and in the immune system, and the variant calling in the medically important genes improved because the reference no longer collapsed a gene into a single copy. The consortium argued that the next step was a pangenome, a graph that holds the variation of many individuals rather than the sequence of one, and that project began the following year.

What "complete" means here is a statement about the reference rather than about a person. The genome that was finished belongs to one cell line, and every other genome differs from it at millions of positions, so the value of the sequence is the coordinate system that it provides. The repeats that were left out for twenty years were the parts that vary most between people, and the paper that closed the gaps opened the question of how to represent the variation that lives in them.
