The twenty-third day of the twelfth lunar month is the little new year in the north of China, and the twenty-fourth in much of the south. The day is given to two tasks that have survived the beliefs that produced them, the sweeping of the house and the sending off of the kitchen god, and the two are connected by the idea that a household should be clean before the year turns.

The kitchen god is the small paper image that hangs above the stove in the old households. His job was to report on the family to the higher authorities at the end of the year, and the offering that was made to him on the little new year was a sweet that would stick his mouth shut or sweeten the report. The image was burned and a new one was pasted up, and the transaction was a mixture of the domestic and the bureaucratic.

The sweet is the part of the day that the shops still sell. A candy made of malt sugar is formed into a stick or a melon, it is hard and it softens in the mouth, and the box is bought as a gift for a child as much as an offering for a god. The sweet is the trace that the ritual leaves in the market, and the trace is the reason the day is still visible in a city where nobody keeps a paper god above the stove.

The cleaning is the part of the day that has no theology left and no less force. The house is swept from the top down, the curtains are washed, the bedding is aired, the cupboards are emptied and the things that have not been used for a year are carried out. The cleaning is a physical audit of the household, and the audit is what the new year requires.

The timing of it explains the urgency. The days between the little new year and the eve are the days in which the food is cooked and the relatives arrive, and there is no time left for the curtains after the twenty-eighth. The twenty-third is the last day on which the household can work on itself rather than on the festival.

What the day shows is that a ritual can lose its explanation and keep its schedule. The kitchen god has gone and the sweet is still sold, the paper image has gone and the house is still swept, and the two survivals are what the calendar does to a practice. The date remains and the reason changes, and the household that cleans on the twenty-third is keeping a promise it cannot quite explain.

For the family that has moved to a city, the day is also a link to a village. The cleaning in a rented flat is smaller than the cleaning in a farmhouse, and the sweet is bought in a supermarket rather than made at home. The scale has changed and the sequence has not, and the sequence is what the day is for.
