title: "The actors' strike ends with a deal on pay and machines"

description: "SAG-AFTRA reached a tentative agreement with the studios on 8 November 2023, ending a 118-day strike that had shut down American film and television production, with gains in minimum pay, streaming residuals and rules on artificial intelligence."
---

SAG-AFTRA reached a tentative deal with the major studios on 8 November, ending a strike that had lasted one hundred and eighteen days and had closed down most scripted film and television production in the United States. The agreement, announced by SAG-AFTRA and the Alliance of Motion Picture and Television Producers, still needed approval by the membership, and the union said voting would begin within days.

Its terms covered the three areas that had kept the two sides apart. Minimum pay rises of seven per cent in the first year, four in the second and three and a half in the third, a structure the studios had offered to writers a month earlier. A new residual for actors in streamed shows, paid on the basis of views, which the union had described as the central demand. And a set of rules for artificial intelligence, including notice when a digital replica of a performer is created, consent for its use, and payment when the replica is used.

The AI clause is the part that will be cited for years. Performers had gone on strike in July with the argument that a studio could scan an actor once and then use the copy forever, and the agreement requires a bargain in each case rather than a blanket licence. It also requires the studios to tell the union which productions are using the technology. Enforcement depends on the union's ability to audit, and the contract gives it the right to request the records.

Economics sat behind the strike, and the argument was about the shape of the business. Studios had spent a decade spending on streaming to win subscribers, and the correction that began in 2022 meant fewer orders and shorter seasons. Actors argued that their share of the value had fallen even as the work became more demanding, and that auditions, publicity and travel were being asked for without pay. The strike followed a writers' strike that ran for nearly five months and ended in late September.

The cost ran into the billions. Production halted across Los Angeles, New York and Atlanta, and the shutdown reached catering crews, drivers, set builders and the small businesses that serve them. Economists at the time estimated the loss to the Californian economy in the range of several billion dollars, and the state's film office counted hundreds of stalled projects. The settlement came after the studios returned with a revised offer that included the streaming residual and a higher cap on contributions to the health plan.

What follows is a compressed season. Writers' rooms reopened in October, actors returned to sets within days of the announcement, and the industry tried to salvage a television season that had already lost its autumn. Several films moved their release dates, and the awards calendar, which depends on actors to promote their work, began to reorganise around a shorter campaign. The union's president called the deal a new baseline, and the studios called it a fair resolution, and both were describing the same thing: a business that has to pay more for the labour it still needs.
