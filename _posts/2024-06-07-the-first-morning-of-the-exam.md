---
title: "The first morning of the exam"
date: 2024-06-07 21:10:00 +0800
tags: [education, china, cities]
categories: [society]
description: "Thirteen million candidates sat the first paper of the university entrance exam this morning. The day is run like a transport operation, on a timetable that has not changed in decades."
---

At half past eight the gates open and the candidates walk in. Thirteen million people registered for the exam this year. They sit in more than seven thousand centres, each of which holds a few hundred desks.

The morning is a transport problem before it is an academic one. Police close the approach roads. The metro adds trains on the lines that serve the large schools. A bus company runs a shuttle from the outer districts to the centre. A candidate who lives an hour away is expected at the gate by half past eight.

The paper arrives under guard. The sheets are printed in a secure plant, packed in sealed bags, delivered to a district office the night before. Two staff sign for each bag. The seal is cut in the exam room at five minutes to nine, in front of the candidates. The hour is written down.

The gate has rules of its own. A teacher with a hand-held detector checks every candidate at the entrance. Phones are collected and stored in a box by the door. Anyone who arrives more than fifteen minutes late is refused entry, whatever the reason, because the rule is printed on the admission ticket.

Parents wait outside in a pattern that repeats across the country. Some wear a red shirt, some a qipao, both of them a joke about a good result. A few bring a folding stool and sit against the wall of the school for two and a half hours. The pavement in front of a centre is crowded at nine and empty at ten.

Inside, the first paper is Chinese language. A composition worth sixty marks closes it. The prompt is published within an hour of the exam starting, and by noon the internet has opinions about it. The candidates are still writing.

The invigilators work a long shift for a small fee. A teacher on duty arrives at seven, stays until half past eleven, cannot use a phone in the room and cannot leave it. The duty is assigned by rotation. The teachers who are assigned do not decline it.

Marking begins that evening. Every paper is scanned in a provincial centre and cut into blocks by question. Each block goes to two markers who never see the name on the page, and a block that is more than a set margin apart goes to a third reader.

The results are released in the last week of June. A candidate logs in with a code and sees a number for each subject, then a line that ranks the total against the province. That ranking is what the universities use, and the filling of preferences follows within a few days.

The day ends with a crowd at the gate. The candidates come out into a street that has been quiet for three hours. Their parents find them, hand over a bottle of water, ask nothing about the paper. The next one starts tomorrow at nine, in the same room, with the same rules.
