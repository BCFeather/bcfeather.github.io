---
title: "The IMF trims world growth to three per cent and lifts China to 4.6"
date: 2026-07-08 23:10:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "The July update cut the global forecast for the second time this year, blamed trade barriers for the cut, and raised its projection for China on the strength of exports and of a consumer recovery that had been slower than expected."
---

The International Monetary Fund cut its forecast for world growth this year to 3 per cent in the July update of its World Economic Outlook, 0.1 percentage point below its April number and the second downward revision of the year. The fund raised its projection for China to 4.6 per cent, and its economists attributed the difference to Chinese exports and to a services recovery that had run ahead of the rest of the world. Most other large economies were left where they had been in April or marked slightly lower.

Trade policy supplied the reason for the cut. The update was published four days before a new American tariff schedule took effect against sixty trading partners, and the fund's economists wrote that rising barriers had begun to show in the data rather than only in the models. Freight volumes between China and the United States had flattened in the second quarter, and companies that had front-loaded orders in the spring had stopped doing so.

China's upgrade rests on two components. Exports grew faster than the fund had assumed, helped by a weaker currency and by demand for machinery and electric vehicles, and consumption recovered as the property sector stopped subtracting from growth. The fund noted that the improvement had come without a large new stimulus package, which made it more durable in its view and less likely to be reversed when the credit impulse faded. The fund also noted that Chinese imports had grown, which matters for the exporters that supply it.

Price of money shapes the rest of the forecast. The fund assumed that central banks in the advanced economies would hold rates where they were through the year unless inflation surprised them, and it flagged the risk that a tariff-driven increase in goods prices would arrive at the same moment as wage growth in services. That combination would leave policymakers with a choice between credibility and employment, which is the position few of them expected to be in three years after the pandemic.

Risks the fund listed read like a map of the year's arguments. A wider conflict in the Gulf would raise energy prices and hit importers in Asia, a debt distress episode in a large emerging market would tighten financing for its neighbours, and a slowdown in artificial intelligence investment would remove the single largest contributor to American growth. Each of those is a low-probability event on its own, and the fund's point is that they have become correlated.

Forecasts of this kind are a way of writing down assumptions where governments can see them. The fund's numbers say that the world economy is growing at roughly its trend rate while trade is growing more slowly than output, which is the signature of a fragmenting system. That is the sentence officials will quote when the next tariff round is announced, and it is the reason the update matters more than its tenth of a point. It put growth in the advanced economies at 1.5 per cent, which is close to what their labour forces can support.
