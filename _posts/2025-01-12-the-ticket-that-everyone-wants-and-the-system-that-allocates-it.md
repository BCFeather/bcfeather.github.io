The spring travel rush begins on the fourteenth of January, and the tickets for the first days of it are released two weeks earlier and sold within minutes. The booking system is a website and an app, the demand in the first hour is measured in billions of requests, and the result that a user sees is either a seat or a message that says the train is full.

The allocation is the interesting part. A railway has a fixed number of seats on a fixed timetable, and the demand on the busiest routes is several times larger than the supply. The system that sells the seats has to decide how to distribute them, and the rule it uses is a queue with a lottery for the surplus and a waiting list for the cancellations.

The waiting list is the invention that changed the experience. A passenger who joins it is told the position and the probability, and the system fills the seat from the list when a booking is cancelled. The effect is that the anxiety of the traveller is moved from the refresh button to a number, and the number is usually accurate.

The railway also adds capacity for the season. The extra trains are scheduled in the timetable that the operator publishes in December, and the carriages that are normally kept in reserve are put into service. The capacity is real and it is still short on the routes that connect the provinces that send the most workers to the provinces that employ them.

The other channels matter as much as the railway. A long-distance coach carries a share of the travellers, the flights that are scheduled for the holiday carry another, and the share that is now taken by a car is the one that has grown fastest. The highway that fills with cars in the last week of January is a highway whose users have decided that the ticket is not worth the wait.

What the rush does to a city is a change in its population for a fortnight. The factories in the coastal provinces empty, the restaurants in the inland counties fill, and the towns that the workers left in November have their busiest month in January. The movement is a seasonal migration with a fixed calendar, and the transport system is the machinery that makes it possible.

For the traveller, the useful rules are the ones the system publishes and the ones the experienced traveller knows. A booking that is made on the day of release is more likely to succeed than one made later, a route that changes train at an intermediate city is often available when the direct train is full, and a seat that is bought for a longer journey can be used for a shorter one. The system is rigid and the people who use it have learned its corners.

What the fortnight shows is a country moving on a schedule. The holiday is fixed, the jobs are in one place and the families in another, and the transport is the bridge that the year is built around. The ticket is a small object and the demand for it is the largest annual event in the passenger railway.
