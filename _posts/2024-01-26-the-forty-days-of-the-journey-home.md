description: "A quiet essay about the first days of the spring travel season in China, when the stations fill with people carrying the year's wages in plastic bags and the country remembers how to move several hundred million of its citizens at once."
---

Travel season began on the twenty-sixth of January, and the first thing to say about it is the size. Transport officials expect about nine billion passenger journeys over the forty days, most of them by road, some by rail, a smaller number by air and by water. Nine billion is a number that the mind cannot hold, and the people in the stations do not try. They hold what they can, which is a ticket, a bag, a child's hand, and the phone that will tell them when the train is boarding.

Busiest stations have been preparing for weeks. Extra trains have been scheduled, temporary waiting areas opened, and the announcements recorded in more languages than usual for the stations near the borders. What is new this year is the technology. Identity checks are done by machine at more gates, tickets are digital by default, and the queues that used to form at the counters have moved to the app, where a different kind of queue waits. Some of the older passengers still prefer the counter, and the volunteers in red vests have been told to look out for them.

People who do this journey every year have a way of talking about it that sounds almost routine. They know which train to take, which car is less crowded, which station has the better food, how long the walk is from the platform to the bus station in their hometown. They also know what it costs, in money and in sleep, and they have decided every year that it is worth it. A man from a village in Anhui, interviewed at a station in Shanghai by a television crew, said he goes home for six days and spends four of them travelling. He said this as a fact rather than a complaint.

Their hometowns have their own preparations. Buses that run from the county station to the villages are full, the drivers work double shifts, and the roads that were quiet in December are now carrying more traffic than they were built for. In our own town the station square has been repaved, and the lights along the road to the highway have been fixed, and none of it is a coincidence. Local government knows that this is the one month in which everyone who left will come back and see what the place looks like.

There is a version of this season that appears in the newspapers every year, in which the journey home is a story about hardship and the distance between the city and the village. There is another version, which is the one the people travelling would tell, in which it is a story about a year's work finished, a family that has not been seen since last winter, and a table that will be set in a few days. Both are true and only one of them is interesting to the people doing it. The trains will run all night, the stations will not close, and in a few days the year's biggest movement of people will be over until next January.
