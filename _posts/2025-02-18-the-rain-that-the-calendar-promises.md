The solar term that the almanac calls the rainwater falls on the eighteenth of February, and the name describes a hope rather than a forecast. The term marks the point at which the winter's dry cold gives way to a warmer and wetter air, and the rain that it promises is the rain that the spring planting depends on.

The meteorology behind it is a shift in the circulation. The cold air that sat over the continent retreats to the north, the moist air from the sea moves inland, and the two meet in a band of cloud that crosses the country from the south-west. The rain that results is light and persistent in the south and rare in the north, and the term arrives in both places on the same day.

The farming calendar that follows the term is a set of windows rather than a set of dates. The wheat that was sown in the autumn begins to grow, the fields that will carry the rice are prepared, and the orchards are pruned. The window is about three weeks, and the farmer who works inside it has a better chance than the one who does not.

The irrigation is the part that the term cannot supply. The northern plain receives less rain in February than the crop needs, and the water comes from the wells, the canals and the reservoirs that were filled in the summer. The term is a reminder that the season has turned, and the water is what makes the turn productive.

The dry years are the ones the farmers remember. A February without rain delays the sowing, the soil that is too dry does not accept the seed, and the harvest that follows is smaller. The records that the counties keep of the rainfall in February are used to plan the year, and the plans are revised when the forecast disagrees.

The term also marks a change in the household. The heating that ran all winter is turned down, the winter clothes are washed and put away, the wet markets begin to sell the first spring vegetables, and the cold that the almanac promised in January is finally over. The change is gradual and the date is a way of naming it.

What the term shows is that an agricultural calendar is a piece of accumulated observation. The date was set by watching the sky for centuries, the twenty-four terms are the record of that watching, and the record is still accurate enough to organize a season. The rain may or may not fall on the eighteenth, and the term is the expectation against which the weather is measured.

The term is also one of the few dates in the agricultural year that a city household still notices. The restaurants begin to sell the dishes of the season, the parks fill on the first warm afternoon, and the calendar on the kitchen wall carries the name of the term in a small square. The observation that produced the date was made by farmers, and the date has outlived the farms that made it.
