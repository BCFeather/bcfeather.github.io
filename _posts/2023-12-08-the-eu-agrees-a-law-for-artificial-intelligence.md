---
title: "The EU agrees a law for artificial intelligence"
date: 2023-12-08 23:55:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "After a negotiation that ran through two nights, the European Union reached a political agreement on 8 December 2023 on the Artificial Intelligence Act, the first comprehensive statute for the technology, with risk tiers, a list of banned uses and fines of up to seven per cent of global turnover."
---

European institutions reached a political agreement on the Artificial Intelligence Act in the early hours of 8 December, after a negotiation that had run through two nights and had twice been declared close to collapse. The law, first proposed in April 2021, is the first attempt by a large jurisdiction to regulate the technology as a category rather than as a series of sectoral problems. It still has to pass a technical drafting stage and a vote in the parliament and the council, which is expected before the European elections in June.

The structure is a pyramid of obligations. Applications that the text calls unacceptable are banned outright: social scoring by governments, systems that exploit the vulnerabilities of children, untargeted scraping of faces from the internet, and most biometric categorization in public spaces. Systems used in hiring, education, credit and policing are classified as high risk and must be registered, tested and supervised by a human being; the rest face transparency duties, mainly the obligation to say that a machine is talking.

General-purpose models, the category that includes the large language models released in the past two years, were the last item to settle. The final text requires providers to publish summaries of training data, to follow copyright rules and to report serious incidents, with heavier duties for the largest systems and a lighter regime for open-source models. Compliance for the biggest providers will run through a code of practice negotiated with the commission rather than through detailed rules written into the statute.

Fines reach thirty-five million euros or seven per cent of global turnover, whichever is higher, for the banned practices, and fifteen million or three per cent for the rest. Enforcement will be national, through authorities that most member states have not yet created, coordinated by a new office in the commission. The obligations for the highest-risk systems apply from 2026, the bans from early 2025, which gives companies a window in which the rules exist but nothing can be enforced.

The lobbying was intense and is visible in the final text. France argued for exemptions that would let domestic providers train on data without full disclosure, Germany wanted the duties on general-purpose models kept light, and Poland and Hungary joined them in a blocking minority at one point. Civil society groups complained that the ban on real-time biometric identification in public spaces had been narrowed to a list of specific crimes, and that the provisions on emotion recognition in workplaces survived only in part.

Europe's claim is that rules create a market, because a single set of requirements is cheaper for a company than twenty-seven, and because a product certified in one member state can be sold in all of them. The counter-claim, made by the American platforms and by parts of European industry, is that the law will slow down exactly the companies it hopes to attract. Both will be tested in 2026, when the first high-risk systems have to be registered before they can be sold.
