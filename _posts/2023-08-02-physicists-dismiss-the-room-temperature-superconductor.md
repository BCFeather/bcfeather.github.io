---
title: "Physicists dismiss the room-temperature superconductor"
date: 2023-08-02 22:20:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "Claims by a South Korean team in late July 2023 that a copper-doped lead apatite called LK-99 conducted electricity without resistance at room temperature collapsed within days as laboratories worldwide failed to reproduce the effect."
---

A preprint posted on 22 July spread faster than any physics result in recent memory. A team from a small company in Seoul reported that a compound of lead, copper, phosphorus and oxygen, which they named LK-99, became a superconductor at ordinary pressure and temperatures up to 127 degrees Celsius. Films of the grey material appeared to levitate over a magnet in videos posted with the paper, and social media drew the conclusion that the century-long search for a room-temperature superconductor was over.

Reproduction began within hours and failed within days. Groups at Beihang University and the Chinese Academy of Sciences prepared the material, measured its resistance and found a drop at around 110 kelvin rather than room temperature, with a residual resistance that stayed finite. A laboratory in India reported a similar result. A true superconductor must show two things, zero resistance and the expulsion of magnetic fields, and nobody measured the second. The levitation in the videos, physicists noted, is what a piece of graphite or a partly magnetic sample does in a strong field.

Independent groups were blunt. The Korean Society of Superconductivity and Cryogenics asked for the original data and said the published material lacked the evidence a claim of that size requires, and it set up a committee. One of the six authors said the paper had been submitted without the consent of the others, and the company's own patents told a different story from the preprint. Nature reported in mid-August that the consensus among specialists had settled: LK-99 behaves as an insulator with impurities, and the resistance drop comes from copper sulphide in the sample, a material whose behaviour at those temperatures is well documented.

The episode says something about how physics is now announced, and about how quickly the field can now check itself. A preprint is a claim, a result is a claim that has survived replication, and the culture that treats the two as the same has grown with the platforms that carry both. Journals have learned to publish replication failures quickly, and the arXiv has carried dozens of papers on this compound, most of them negative. Several groups published their measurements within a week of each other, which is unusually fast for a field that normally moves in months. The material may yet be interesting for other reasons; copper-doped lead apatite is cheap, easy to make and pleasant to study, and several groups have said they will keep working on it.

Ambition explains the appetite of the audience. Superconductors that work at room temperature would change power transmission, medical imaging and computing, and the last decade of hype around them has taught the public to expect breakthroughs. Chinese and American laboratories have pursued high-pressure hydrides that superconduct at 15 degrees but only under pressures found deep inside the earth, and the field has learned to distrust its own headlines.

The result is a correction, and corrections are how the discipline works. What the episode shows is that the distance between a spectacular claim and a fact is now measured in days, which is progress of a kind.
