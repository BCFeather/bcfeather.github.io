---
title: "The founder of Patagonia gives the company away to fight climate change"
date: 2022-09-14 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "Yvon Chouinard transfers the shares to a trust and a fund, and the profits will go to conservation rather than to a buyer or an heir."
---

Yvon Chouinard announced on Wednesday that he had given away the company he founded in 1973, and the structure he chose is the interesting part. Two per cent of the shares, which carry the votes, went to a trust whose purpose is to keep the company's values in place. The remaining ninety-eight per cent, which carry no votes, went to a fund that will give about a hundred million dollars a year to conservation. The company is worth about three billion dollars, and none of it went to a buyer or to his children.

The alternative he described was either to sell the company and give the money away or to take it public, and he rejected both. A sale would have put the company in the hands of a buyer who would want a return, and the founder said the mission would have been the first thing to go. A listing would have exposed the management to a quarterly argument about margins, which is the argument that has already changed the company's products twice. The trust and the fund keep the ownership in a form that cannot be traded.

Patagonia has been a laboratory for this kind of decision for thirty years. The company gives one per cent of its sales to environmental groups, it has used recycled polyester since the 1990s, and it repairs clothes rather than replacing them. It has also grown at a rate that a clothing business envies, with revenue above a billion dollars and stores on four continents. A business that sells jackets can be a vehicle for a political argument without being a charity, which is the claim the founder has been making since the 1980s.

Legal form is unusual and its durability is untested. The trust has to appoint the board, and the board has to balance the fund's demand for distributions with the company's need for investment. A structure of that kind can be changed by the people who control it, and the people who control it will change. Lawyers who work on perpetual-purpose trusts have said the main risk is a future board that decides the dividend matters more than the mission, and there is no mechanism in the deed that can stop that.

The announcement landed in a week when the rest of the industry was reporting bad numbers. Clothing sales have slowed with the cost of living, and the brands that grew fastest during the pandemic have issued warnings. A company that gives its profits away cannot raise money from investors on the same terms, and it cannot be bought. The founder's argument is that this is the point: a business owned by a purpose can plan in decades, and the businesses owned by shareholders cannot.

What the decision leaves is a model that others will test. The founder is eighty-three, and the questions about succession are the ones every founder has to answer, and his answer was to remove the question. Whether the model survives him will depend on the people who run the trust and on whether the customers keep buying jackets. The company's advertising has always sold a version of that argument, and for once the corporate structure matches the advertisement.
