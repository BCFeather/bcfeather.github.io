---
title: "A bacterium the size of an eyelash"
date: 2022-06-24 23:35:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "A single cell nearly a centimetre long, found on mangrove leaves in Guadeloupe, carries its DNA inside membrane-bound compartments, and the paper describing it in *Science* reopens a boundary biologists had treated as settled."
---

A single bacterial cell measuring nearly a centimetre in length, found on rotting mangrove leaves in Guadeloupe, is described in *Science* on 24 June by a team led by Jean-Marie Volland of the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and Olivier Gros of the University of the French Antilles. The organism, named Thiomargarita magnifica, is visible to the naked eye and is roughly fifty times longer than the largest bacteria previously known. It was discovered in a mangrove channel near Petit-Canal, where it grows in chains on the surface of leaf litter.

Beyond the size lies the more interesting finding. The cell contains its DNA in compartments enclosed by membranes, which is the arrangement that textbooks reserve for the cells of animals, plants and fungi. Bacteria normally keep their chromosome loose in the cytoplasm, and the compartments in this organism are bounded by a layer of peptidoglycan, the polymer that gives bacterial walls their shape, which suggests they are an internal extension of the cell envelope rather than a new invention.

The genome is correspondingly large. The organism carries several thousand genes more than a typical bacterium, and the DNA is concentrated in the compartments rather than spread through the cell, which changes how the cell manages the chemistry of a very long body. A cell of that length faces a problem of diffusion: molecules made at one end take a long time to reach the other, and compartmentalization is one way to solve it.

Discovery began with a shrimp. Gros, who studies organisms that live on the surfaces of marine animals and plants, noticed filaments attached to mangrove leaves in 2009 and spent years working out what they were, with microscopy that showed sulphur granules inside and a vacuole that occupied most of the cell volume. Genetic sequencing later placed the organism among the gammaproteobacteria, related to the sulphur-oxidizing bacteria that live in sediments.

Skepticism about such claims is healthy and was applied here. The authors sequenced DNA from individual filaments rather than from a pooled sample, which rules out contamination from other organisms, and they used imaging that resolves the membranes around the DNA. The organism has not been grown in culture, which limits what can be measured about its metabolism, and the reported division mechanism is inferred rather than observed.

What the paper disturbs is a boundary that biology has used for a century. The division between prokaryotes and eukaryotes rests on the nucleus and on membrane-bound organelles, and this bacterium has something close to both while remaining unmistakably bacterial in its wall and its genes. Classification still works, and the categories now need an asterisk. The wider point concerns how much of the microbial world remains unexamined. The organism lives in a habitat that is easy to visit and was found by a researcher looking at leaves, and the techniques that identified it have only recently become cheap enough for this kind of work; the largest bacterium in the world was sitting in a mangrove swamp that had been studied for decades.
