The solstice falls on the twenty-first of June this year. On that day the sun reaches its highest point in the northern sky and the daylight lasts longest. The date is fixed by the tilt of the axis rather than by the calendar, which is why it drifts by a day or two between years.

The extra daylight is distributed unevenly. Beijing receives about fifteen hours of it. Guangzhou receives about thirteen and a half. A town in the far north east receives more than seventeen. The sky there never goes properly dark, even at midnight.

The buildings that face south were designed around this. A residential block built in the last century has a balcony with a concrete slab above it, and the slab is angled so that it shades the window in summer. The angle was calculated by an architect with a table and a ruler, and it still works.

The solar panels produce their peak at a different hour. A panel on a roof reaches its rated output around noon, then loses it as the sun moves west. The curve is a hill with a flat top. That plateau is the part a grid operator has to absorb.

The street lights are switched by a clock rather than by a sensor in most cities. The clock is set twice a year, at the two solstices. The result is a fortnight in June when the lamps come on while the sky is still bright and the electricity is spent on nothing.

The solar term has an agricultural meaning that survives in the calendar. The six weeks after the solstice are the hottest of the year. In the south the rainy season follows the heat by a fortnight. A farmer who plants a second crop counts the days from this date.

Indoor air conditioning has its own rule. A public building is asked to keep its rooms at twenty-six degrees in summer, which is a compromise between comfort and the load on the grid. A room set at twenty-two degrees uses noticeably more power for a difference that most people cannot feel.

The working day has not moved. A noon break of two hours survives in many offices. The break makes more sense in a place where the sun stands high at one o'clock. The habit outlived the reason and the air conditioner made it comfortable again.

The longest day is not the hottest one. The ground and the sea take weeks to absorb the heat, which is why the peak of summer arrives in July. The daylight is already getting shorter when the temperature reaches its highest point.

The shade in a courtyard moves with the season. In June the shadow of a wall reaches the middle of the yard at noon. In December the shadow covers the whole yard and the sun never touches the ground. A tree at the edge is planted for the shade it gives in one month and the light it lets through in another.

The long evening is not used in the way an outsider expects. The streets are busy until nine and quiet after ten, because the school term has not ended and the first bell is at seven.
