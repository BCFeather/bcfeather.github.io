---
title: "Tunisia votes on a new constitution and only three in ten turn out"
date: 2022-07-26 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The president's draft concentrates power in his office, and the turnout is the lowest of any vote since the revolution."
---

Tunisia held a referendum on a new constitution on Monday, and the preliminary turnout was about thirty per cent, which is the lowest of any national vote since the revolution in 2011. The draft was written by a committee that the president appointed and it gives the head of state the power to dismiss the government and to appoint the judges. The opposition boycotted the vote and said the result would be illegitimate. The electoral commission said the count would be published in the week.

Nothing about the vote can be read without the year that came before it. The president dismissed the prime minister and froze the assembly in July 2021, and he has governed by decree since. The judges were dismissed and the electoral commission was replaced, and the parties that had dominated the parliament were banned from the vote. The president has said that the old system was corrupt and that the new one will be cleaner. The opponents say that the old system was democratic and that the new one does not deserve the name.

The campaign lasted three weeks and was held in the heat of summer. The polling stations opened at seven in the morning and the voters who came were mostly older, and the queues were short. The state television broadcast the president's speeches and the private stations gave little time to the opposition. The monitors from the African Union and from the Arab league were invited, and the ones from the European Union stayed away.

Economics explains why the politics has moved as it has, and why the campaign was fought on prices rather than on the constitution. The country has been negotiating with the International Monetary Fund since last year, and the agreement has been delayed by the political uncertainty. The price of bread and of fuel has risen and the public sector wage bill has grown, and the subsidies that keep the peace cost more than the budget can carry. The unions have said they will not accept the cuts that the fund has asked for.

Shorter than the text it replaces, the draft leaves the details to ordinary law. It creates a two-chamber parliament with a weak upper house, and it gives the president the right to run foreign policy and defence without the approval of the assembly. It also removes the constitutional court that had been planned, and it replaces it with a body that the president appoints. The lawyers who have read it say it concentrates more power in one office than any previous text.

The president now has a mandate, and he intends to use it to rewrite the rules of the system. The turnout is low enough for the opponents to say the mandate is thin, and high enough for the president to say it is real. The fund will watch the result and the unions will watch the budget, and the year will end with a parliament that has not been elected. The next election is promised for December, and the calendar has been moved twice already.
