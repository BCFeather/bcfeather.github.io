---
title: "White clover lost its poison in the middle of a hundred and sixty cities"
date: 2022-03-22 22:45:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "A defence against slugs, carried by two genes, came out thinner at the centre of cities than at their edges, and the exceptions appeared where the ground was dry."
---

White clover grows in lawns, verges and pastures on every inhabited continent, and almost every stand of it is mixed for a chemical defence. Two unlinked genes, *Ac* and *Li*, have to be working for the plant to release hydrogen cyanide when its tissue is damaged, which deters slugs and caterpillars and costs the plant something in drought and frost. Deletions at either locus are common in wild populations, so the trait can be switched off by selection without waiting for a new mutation.

Between 2017 and 2021 the Global Urban Evolution Project collected plants along transects that ran from the centre of a city to farmland beyond its edge. The final dataset covered 160 cities in 26 countries, 6,169 sampling sites and 110,019 plants, each of them assayed for cyanide with the same paper test. Each city was sampled by a local team working from a shared protocol, and the transects were fixed before any plant was assayed, so a cline could not be manufactured after the result was known. Sampling on that scale had never been attempted for a trait whose genetic basis was this well known.

Distance from the centre predicted the trait. Across all the cities, the chance that a plant produced cyanide rose by about 44 per cent from the middle of the urban area to the furthest rural site, and 47 per cent of cities showed a cline that was statistically significant. Most of those, 62 of the 160, ran in the expected direction, with less cyanide in town, while 13 ran the other way and the rest showed no clear pattern.

Genomes settled the question of mechanism. The team sequenced 2,074 plants from 26 cities and found neutral diversity to be indistinguishable between urban and rural habitats, with differentiation very low and an FST of about 0.012, which implies that pollen and seed move freely across the gradient. Drift would have pushed the trait down almost everywhere, and the clines instead followed selection. Two genes with a known chemistry make a cleaner test than a trait scored only as a shape or a size.

Which selection, though, depended on the city. Changes in potential evapotranspiration and in vegetation cover explained about 11 per cent of the variation in cline strength. Where rural sites were drier, cyanide was more common outside town, which fits the cost of the defence under water stress. Where the whole transect stayed green, herbivores were the likelier agent, because clover in dense vegetation meets more slugs and the centre of a city meets fewer. Neither variable accounted for the cities where the pattern ran backwards, which the paper puts down to seed sown from agricultural varieties.

The result is a partial form of parallel evolution, and the partiality is what makes it readable. Cities converge on heat, concrete and mown grass, and the plant answers in the same direction often enough to form a pattern and rarely enough to keep the exceptions interesting. The 160 transects amount to an experiment repeated on six continents without a control, and the breakdowns say which of the many things a city changes actually presses on a plant.
