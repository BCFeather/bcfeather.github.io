---
title: "Evergrande's founder faces an investigation"
date: 2023-09-28 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "China Evergrande said on 28 September 2023 that its founder, Hui Ka Yan, was under investigation and that trading in its shares had been suspended, weeks after the company sought American bankruptcy protection for its offshore debt."
---

The company told the Hong Kong exchange on the evening of 28 September that its founder and chairman, Hui Ka Yan, had been notified that he was subject to an investigation, and that trading in its shares would stop until further notice. It did not say what the investigation concerned. Chinese media reported that he had been taken away by police in Shenzhen earlier in the week, at a time when the company's staff had been told to expect a restructuring vote, and the company's other listed units also suspended trading. The announcement came a month before the offshore restructuring plan was due to be voted on.

Timing carries its own message. Evergrande has been trying to restructure more than three hundred billion dollars of liabilities, and its offshore creditors were due to vote on a plan that would have given them a share of two subsidiaries in exchange for releasing their claims. The plan was already in doubt after the company cancelled a meeting in August, and the detention of the man who signed the guarantees makes the plan harder to sell. The company's advisers have said they are reviewing the situation.

Investigations form part of a wider campaign. Chinese authorities have been examining the founders of several financial groups, including the shadow banking company Zhongzhi, whose chairman was detained in the same month, and the wealth management arms that sold products to retail investors. The pattern is that the state investigates the people who ran the schemes rather than the schemes themselves, and that it does so after the losses have been taken by households, which is a decision about who bears the cost and when they hear about it.

Evergrande's collapse has been slow and public. The company defaulted on its dollar bonds in 2021, its shares fell by more than ninety per cent from their peak, and it has been selling assets and negotiating with creditors ever since. Its unfinished projects have left buyers paying mortgages for flats that do not exist, and local governments have been taking over the sites to finish them with money borrowed from state banks, at a pace that has left many buyers still waiting.

Property market that produced the company has not recovered. New home sales fell again in September, investment in the sector has contracted for two years, and the developers that remain are mostly state-owned. The government has eased mortgage rules in some cities and has cut the rate on existing loans, and it has been reluctant to guarantee the debts of private builders, which is the position that turned a liquidity problem into a solvency one.

A founder who built the largest developer in the country has been taken away for questioning, and the creditors who were about to vote on his plan now have a different counterparty. The restructuring will happen or it will not, and the losses will fall where the courts in Hong Kong, the Cayman Islands and New York and the government in Beijing decide they should, in that order.
