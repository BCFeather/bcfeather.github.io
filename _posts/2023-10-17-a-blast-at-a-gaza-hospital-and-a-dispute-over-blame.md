---
title: "A blast at a Gaza hospital and a dispute over blame"
date: 2023-10-17 22:55:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "A blast at the Ahli Arab hospital in Gaza City on 17 October 2023 killed several hundred people according to the Hamas-run health ministry, and Israel and Palestinian officials gave conflicting accounts of what caused it."
---

A blast at the Ahli Arab hospital in Gaza City on the evening of 17 October killed several hundred people, according to the health ministry run by Hamas, which put the figure at 471. The hospital was treating wounded patients and sheltering displaced families, and the damage was visible in photographs taken the following morning. The death toll has been disputed since, and the site has not been independently examined by outside investigators. The hospital is one of the oldest in the city, run by the Anglican diocese, and it had treated patients from the surrounding district for more than a century.

The two accounts diverged within hours. Palestinian officials blamed an Israeli air strike. The Israeli military said its investigation found that a misfired rocket launched from inside Gaza by Islamic Jihad had hit the hospital car park, and it released radar and intercept data that it said supported that conclusion. American intelligence assessments, briefed to Congress, put the casualty figure far lower than the ministry's number and attributed the blast to a failed rocket launch, and independent analysts examining crater patterns and structural damage reached mixed conclusions.

The dispute matters beyond the specific event. Casualty figures in Gaza are produced by a ministry under the control of Hamas, and international agencies have historically treated its counts as broadly credible while noting that they do not distinguish combatants from civilians and that verification during a bombardment is impossible. Both sides have an interest in the number, and the argument about attribution has become a proxy for the wider argument about the conduct of the war. Israel has published its own accounting of the dead, and the United Nations has said the figures need independent verification before any court uses them.

Diplomatic fallout was immediate. A planned summit in Amman with the American president and Arab leaders was cancelled, and protests broke out in cities across the region, including outside embassies in Beirut and Amman. The secretary-general of the United Nations called for an independent investigation, and the Security Council remained unable to agree a statement, having failed twice in two days to adopt a resolution.

Hospital infrastructure had already been under pressure. Fuel for generators was running out, and the World Health Organization said several facilities in the north had stopped functioning. The blast turned a humanitarian argument into a legal one: hospitals have protected status under the laws of war, and determining whether the protection was violated requires facts that neither side has allowed investigators to establish.

What the episode shows is how quickly a contested fact becomes a political weapon in a war fought simultaneously on the ground and in the information space. The crater, the fragments and the flight path are knowable in principle. In practice the evidence sits in a war zone, and the conclusions drawn this week rest on claims that will be tested long after the fighting stops. Both sides have released material that supports their version, and neither has let inspectors reach the site.
