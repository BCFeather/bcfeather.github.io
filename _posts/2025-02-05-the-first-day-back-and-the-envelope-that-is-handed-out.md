The offices reopen on the fifth of February, and the morning is marked by a small ceremony that the Chinese workplace has kept through every reorganization. The manager stands at the door with a stack of red envelopes, the staff arrive in ones and twos, and the greeting that is exchanged is the one that is used once a year.

The envelope is a token and the amount is usually small. It is the return-to-work gift that an employer gives rather than a bonus, and the bonus was decided in January and paid before the holiday. The envelope is the part of the compensation that is not compensation, and its function is to mark the first day as a beginning.

The first meeting of the year is usually about the year that has ended. The numbers that were closed in January are presented to the staff in February, because the audit takes the intervening weeks, and the presentation is a mixture of a review and a thank-you. The companies that do it well present the numbers plainly and name the people who made them.

The week that follows is the one in which the year's plan becomes a set of tasks. The targets that were set in December are broken into quarters, the projects that were approved in the autumn are given a start date, and the calendar fills in the way a calendar does after a holiday. The plan is a document and the tasks are the working version of it.

The mood of the first week is the part that the managers notice. A staff that has had eight days of rest arrives with a different pace, and the work that was urgent in January is seen with a clearer eye in February. The distance is useful and it is brief, and the offices that schedule the hard decisions for the second week are the ones that use it.

The factories have their own version of the day. The production line restarts when the parts arrive, and the parts arrive when the suppliers reopen, and the first week is a week of catching up on the orders that were placed before the holiday. The workers who returned on the fourth are on the line on the fifth, and the line runs at a lower rate until the supply settles.

What the day shows is that a workplace has a calendar of its own. The fiscal year, the holiday, the audit and the plan are four schedules that only partly overlap, and the first week of February is where they meet. The envelope at the door is the smallest part of that and it is the part that people remember. A year is a long document and the first morning of February is the cover of it, and the offices that get the morning right are the ones where the rest of the document is read as intended rather than as a chore that arrived with the post.
