---
title: "The debt bill clears the House with the votes of both parties"
date: 2023-05-31 23:15:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "The House passed the debt ceiling agreement on 31 May by 314 votes to 117, with a majority of Republicans and a large bloc of Democrats carrying it. The Senate had four days to act before the treasury ran out of money."
---

The House of Representatives passed the bill that suspends the federal debt limit on the evening of 31 May, by 314 votes to 117, after a day of whipping that exposed how weak the majorities are inside both parties. A hundred and forty-nine Democrats and a hundred and sixty-five Republicans voted for the agreement that the president and the speaker had reached four days earlier. Ninety-three Republicans and seventy-one Democrats voted against it, which is a fair map of where the parties' activist wings sit.

For the speaker, the problem was the arithmetic of his own conference. The deal was negotiated under rules that he had accepted in January, when he needed fifteen ballots to be elected, and those rules allow a single member to move to vacate the chair. The hard-line faction that extracted those rules had promised to use them against any agreement that did not cut spending by the trillions it had demanded, and the vote on 31 May gave it a list of names and a grievance. Its threats were loud enough that the bill needed Democratic votes to pass, which the speaker had said he would never need.

Substance is modest next to the noise around the bill. Domestic discretionary spending is capped for two years, with a small increase for defence, and the caps are the same ones that the two parties have been arguing about since the 2011 fight, which produced a similar deal and a similar set of threats. The work requirements for the food assistance programme are expanded for people between fifty and fifty-four, and a fund for the Internal Revenue Service that had been appropriated in 2022 is partly clawed back. The debt limit is suspended until January 2025.

The Senate had four days. The treasury's cash balance was reported to be below thirty billion dollars, which is a rounding error for a government that spends that much in a week, and the deadline set by the secretary was 5 June. The chamber's rules allow a single senator to slow a bill for days, and several had said they would, which is why the leadership scheduled the vote for the evening of 1 June and why the treasury's announcement of the deadline had a floor under it.

Economically, the settlement is a postponement. The country's debt will keep rising, because the programmes that drive it, health care for the elderly and pensions, are untouched, and the caps apply to the part of the budget that is annually appropriated, which is about a quarter of the total. The rating agency that put the country on a watch list in May said that the agreement removed the immediate risk and did nothing about the deficit, which is the accurate summary of a deal that both sides needed and neither wanted.

Politically, it is a preview. A speaker who depends on the votes of the other party to pass a budget, a president who gives away spending to protect a credit rating, and a faction that would rather default than compromise are the ingredients of every fiscal argument until the next deadline. The suspension lasts until January 2025, which places the next fight after an election and in the middle of a presidential term. The bill that passed on 31 May did the minimum that the system required, and the system will require it again.
