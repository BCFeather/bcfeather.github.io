---
title: "Microsoft agrees to buy Activision Blizzard for 68.7 billion dollars"
date: 2022-01-18 22:30:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "The all-cash deal valued the game publisher at 95 dollars a share and became the largest acquisition in the industry's history, arriving as the target was still dealing with lawsuits over its workplace."
---

Microsoft agreed on 18 January to buy Activision Blizzard for 68.7 billion dollars in cash, or 95 dollars a share, the largest acquisition in the history of the games industry. The price was about 45 per cent above where the publisher's shares had closed the previous Friday, and the companies said the deal would close in the financial year ending in June 2023, subject to approval in Washington, Brussels and London.

The strategic logic is about content and about a subscription business. Microsoft's console business had always been the weaker of the two large platforms, and its Game Pass service needed a pipeline of new releases to keep subscribers paying. Activision owned Call of Duty, one of the best-selling franchises in the medium, along with Candy Crush and a catalogue that could be pushed into a subscription on day one. The company also owned mobile studios whose output was cheap to produce and could be spread across devices.

Target's own position was difficult. Activision had spent a year under investigations over allegations of harassment and discrimination, its chief executive had been sued by the state of California, and its share price had fallen by a third from its 2021 high. Staff had staged walkouts, and the studio that made the biggest franchise had lost senior developers.

Competition authorities were always going to look at this one. A deal that removes a major independent publisher from the market gives the buyer control over whether its games appear on rival consoles, and the question regulators ask is whether the buyer has the incentive to withhold them. Microsoft's public answer was that it would keep Call of Duty on other platforms, an undertaking that would be tested in writing over the following eighteen months. The buyer offered a set of commitments about platforms and about access to game code, and regulators asked for evidence that the commitments would bind after the contract expired.

Size of the deal also signals what the industry believes about the next decade. Games are the largest entertainment category by revenue, subscriptions are the business model that platforms prefer, and the companies that own the franchises can negotiate from strength. Microsoft was buying a catalogue at a moment when content owners were being priced as infrastructure. Renting a catalogue means accepting terms its owner sets at each renewal, and that arithmetic explains the premium, since a buyer that waits for the share price to fall further risks losing the franchises altogether.

A 69 billion dollar cheque does not settle the case that prompted the discount. The acquisition gives the publisher new ownership, and the employees whose complaints drove the share price down will now work for a company with its own human resources department and its own reputation to protect. Its first public commitment was to keep the publisher's studios intact, which is what buyers say when the asset is the people, and the new owner inherited both the staff and the lawsuits the old one had failed to settle.
