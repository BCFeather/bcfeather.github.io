---
title: "The cartel cuts again and prices jump"
date: 2023-04-03 22:40:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "OPEC and its partners announced voluntary production cuts of about 1.66 million barrels a day on 2 April 2023, effective from May, and Brent crude jumped six per cent on the first trading day after the announcement."
---

Seven producers announced voluntary cuts on 2 April, a Sunday, when the market was closed and the news had two days to settle. Saudi Arabia would take half a million barrels a day, Iraq 211,000, the United Arab Emirates 144,000, Kuwait 128,000, Kazakhstan 78,000, Algeria 48,000 and Oman 40,000, with Russia extending an existing reduction of 500,000. The total came to about 1.66 million barrels a day, and the measure was to run from May to the end of the year.

Brent opened on the Monday above 84 dollars, up six per cent, and settled at 84.93, having touched 86.44 during the session. West Texas Intermediate went from 75.67 to 80.42, a rise of 6.28 per cent that ran within a few cents of Brent's. The move reversed two weeks of decline that had followed the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank, when the price had fallen to 73 dollars on fears that a credit squeeze would cut demand.

Timing was the surprise rather than the size. The group had agreed to hold production steady at its meeting in early March, and ministers had said publicly that no change was needed. A cut of this size three weeks later, announced by individual capitals rather than by the secretariat in Vienna, was read in Washington as a decision to support the price against the interest of importing countries. The White House said the move was ill-advised and that it would work with producers on prices.

Behind the announcement sits a balance that had been tightening quietly. Global demand had been revised upward through the winter as China reopened, and the group's own monthly report put the call on its crude above 29 million barrels a day in the second half of the year. Spare capacity sits almost entirely in three Gulf producers, which means that a decision to withhold a million barrels is a decision that only they can make and that only they can reverse, and the rest of the group has learnt to live with that arithmetic.

Consumers absorb the cost through fuel and through freight. Diesel, which had been the tightest part of the barrel complex all winter, rose faster than crude in the days afterwards, and European gas prices rose for reasons of their own. For central banks fighting inflation, a six per cent move in the price of the world's benchmark commodity arrives at the worst possible moment, three weeks after a banking crisis had persuaded them that the worst was over.

Politics will be worked out at the pump. The American administration had spent a year releasing crude from its strategic reserve to keep prices down, and its reserve is now at the lowest level since 1983. It has threatened legislation that would allow it to sue members of the group for collusion, which no Congress has passed, and it has fewer tools than it had in 2022. The producers know this, which is why the cut came when it did.
