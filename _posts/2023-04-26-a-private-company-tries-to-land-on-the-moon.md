---
title: "A private company tries to land on the moon"
date: 2023-04-26 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "The Japanese company ispace attempted the first landing on the Moon by a privately built spacecraft on 26 April 2023, lost contact during the descent and later confirmed that the lander had crashed near its target in Atlas crater."
---

The lander, named Hakuto-R, began its descent from about 100 kilometres above the Moon at 00.40 Japan time on 26 April, and the company's control room in Tokyo lost contact with it shortly afterwards. The spacecraft had been in lunar orbit since March, after a journey that began with a launch from Cape Canaveral in December 2022 on a Falcon 9. It carried a small rover built by the United Arab Emirates and a payload from Japan's space agency.

Descent was designed to be slow. The lander would fire its main engine to reduce speed, coast over the surface at an altitude of a few kilometres, and then drop the last stretch in a sequence that the company had rehearsed in simulations. Engineers said afterwards that the vehicle had reached the point of the final braking manoeuvre and that the data suggested it was still descending when the signal stopped. Engineers had built a series of checkpoints into the sequence, and the telemetry showed that the vehicle passed the first two before the link failed.

The company waited a day before describing the outcome. A statement on 26 April said that communication had been lost and that the lander's condition could not be confirmed, and the analysis published in May concluded that the cause was a software problem in the altitude measurement. The vehicle had used a sensor that misread the height above the surface as it passed over the rim of a crater, and the engine fired until the propellant ran out.

Failure of this kind had happened before, three years earlier. The Israeli non-profit SpaceIL crashed a lander in 2019 for a similar reason, and the pattern in both cases was a small team attempting a complex manoeuvre without the redundancy that a national programme would build in. The company said that the data from the attempt would be used for a second mission and that its contract with NASA for a future lunar delivery remained in place. The company had raised about 200 million dollars from Japanese investors and had a second lander under construction when the first one was lost.

Business model is the part that the crash tested. ispace sells payload space to governments and companies that want to reach the surface, and its prices are a fraction of what an agency mission costs. The customers in this case had accepted the risk explicitly, and the company's shares fell by a fifth on the day after the failure and recovered as the contracts were confirmed. Its customers are agencies and universities that cannot afford a national mission, and the price of a delivery is why they accept the risk.

Crowded is what the Moon has become, for the first time since 1972. Three missions were launched towards it within a year, two of them by companies, and NASA's commercial programme has paid for several more. Landing remains the hardest part, and the fact that a company can now attempt it at all is the change that the failure did not reverse.
