Every June a large number of students who have never worked in an office begin a two-month internship in one. They arrive with a laptop, a lanyard and a set of expectations built from films and from the career advice they have been given since the second year of university. The first fortnight usually removes most of them, and what replaces them is a working knowledge of things that no course teaches.

The first thing to learn is the geography of time. A student timetable is dense and legible. An office timetable is mostly waiting punctuated by twenty minutes of urgency, and the internship's main lesson is that being available is a skill distinct from being productive. Interns who understand this early get given real work. Interns who treat every idle hour as a waste get given filing, and the filing is, in fairness, also a lesson about how an organization keeps its records.

The second thing to learn is that most of an office's information travels through channels the company has not documented. A decision about a product feature arrived in a chat message, was confirmed in a corridor, and appears in a document only weeks later. An intern who waits for the document will discover it is already obsolete. An intern who asks a colleague directly will be given the version that matters, and will also learn who to ask next time.

The third thing is the etiquette of the small ask. A senior person's time is the office's scarcest resource, and the skill of asking a question briefly, at the right moment, with the specific thing needed already identified, is what distinguishes a useful intern from an exhausting one. This skill is invisible on a transcript and decisive in a reference letter.

The money is also part of the lesson. A stipend that covers a month's rent and a metro card is not a salary, and the discovery that two months of full-time work buys less than a semester's allowance is a small shock that most students absorb without saying so. It teaches the difference between the value of an hour and the price of one, which is the single most useful thing an internship can transmit. What the summer does to the student's sense of a career is less discussed and more important. A student who spends two months doing something routine and well now knows something about their tolerance for routine. A student who spends two months in a job they find alienating has learned that just as clearly, and has learned it earlier and more cheaply than they would have by graduating into it.

Then August arrives, the placement ends, the desk is cleared, and the student returns to a campus that feels smaller. The certificate of internship is issued, the reference is written in four sentences, and the value of the whole thing depends on whether the person can now do something they could not do in June. For the ones who can, the summer was worth more than any two courses of the year, and for the rest it was at least a paid introduction to the fact that work is a place as much as a task.
