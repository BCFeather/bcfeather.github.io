---
title: "An earthquake in Afghanistan killed a thousand people"
date: 2022-06-22 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "A magnitude six quake struck Paktika province before dawn, flattening mud-brick houses in two districts and leaving a government with almost no cash to organize the response."
---

A magnitude six earthquake struck southeastern Afghanistan at about half past one in the morning on 22 June, with its centre in the mountains of Paktika province near the border with Pakistan. The districts worst affected, Gayan and Barmal, are clusters of villages built of mud brick on steep slopes, and the shaking brought most of the houses down on the families sleeping inside. The death toll passed a thousand within two days, with about sixteen hundred people injured. The nearest hospital with surgical capacity lay more than two hours by road from the affected valleys. Aid convoys reached only the district centres on the first day.

Construction explains much of the mortality. Homes in the region are built from sun-dried brick and timber, with heavy roofs and no reinforcement, and they collapse quickly when the ground moves. The quake was shallow, which increased the intensity of shaking at the surface, and it struck at night when everyone was indoors. Aid agencies said that survivors were digging with their hands and with farm tools for the first day. Aftershocks continued for days, and families slept outdoors in temperatures that fell close to freezing at night.

Response fell to a government with few resources. The Taliban administration, in power since August 2021, sent helicopters and ambulances and appealed for international help, and its supreme leader asked for aid without conditions. Hospitals in the region were short of beds, supplies and fuel, and the weather turned to rain, which slowed the arrival of trucks on roads that were already poor. Several villages lost a quarter of their residents.

International assistance arrived with complications. Several governments and United Nations agencies sent supplies, and the United Nations flew in medical teams, but the banking system remained frozen by sanctions and by the suspension of development funding that had paid for much of the country's health service. Agencies said they could deliver goods but could not pay staff, and the country's largest humanitarian appeal was still less than half funded. The United Nations had asked for four and a half billion dollars for the year and had received only a fraction of it, and food rations for eight million people were cut in the spring.

Underlying vulnerability had been building for years. Drought had pushed millions of people into debt and displacement before the earthquake, the economy contracted by about twenty per cent after the takeover, and most rural households had no savings to rebuild with. Aid groups had warned that a single hazard would tip districts that were already dependent on food assistance into a crisis.

After the rescue comes the harder phase. Rebuilding a thousand houses in mountain valleys requires money, engineers and a building code that the region has never had, and the agencies that would normally provide all three are operating under restrictions that make each of them slower. The earthquake will be remembered as a natural event, and its cost will be decided by the politics that surround the reconstruction. Winter arrives early at that altitude, which gives the reconstruction a deadline of about three months.
