---
title: "Deutsche Bank and the question of contagion"
date: 2023-03-24 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "Shares in Deutsche Bank fell by fourteen per cent at one point on 24 March 2023, and the cost of insuring its debt rose sharply, without a specific trigger that anyone could name."
---

The bank's shares fell through the morning of 24 March, and by the afternoon they were down fourteen per cent, which was the largest one-day fall since the first weeks of the pandemic. The cost of credit default swaps on its senior debt rose to the highest level since 2018, and the move spread to other European banks. There was no announcement, no downgrade and no rating action behind the fall, and the absence of a cause was the point. The shares had fallen by a fifth over the month, and the insurance that protects a lender against its own default had widened every day since the Swiss rescue.

Behind the fall lay a week in which two German banks had become the subject of the same question. One of them, a lender in the public sector, had seen its shares fall after it postponed a bond sale, and the government in Berlin had made a statement about its stability. The other was the largest bank in the country, and its balance sheet had been the subject of restructuring for four years, and the market had not forgotten.

For analysts who looked at the numbers, the bank described a profitable year and a capital ratio above the level the regulator requires. It had been profitable in the previous year, its capital ratio was above the level the regulator requires, and its deposit base was more diversified than the American banks that had failed. What it also had was a large derivatives book, a history of losses and a restructuring that had cost tens of thousands of jobs, and those facts were the raw material that a nervous market could work with.

Response from the authorities came within hours. The chancellor said there was no reason for concern, the regulator said the European banking system was stable, and the bank's chief executive wrote a letter to staff that was reported as an attempt to calm them. The statements worked for a day, and the shares recovered part of the fall, and the insurance premium stayed high, which is where anxiety usually settles. Its chief executive wrote to staff on the Friday evening, and the letter was published by a newspaper before most of them had read it.

Reputation can become a liability, and the episode demonstrated how. A lender that had been through a decade of scandals was treated as suspect by a market that had just watched two other banks fail within a week, and the treatment owed nothing to its balance sheet. The information that would have settled the question was public and the market discounted it, which is what a loss of confidence means when the question is confidence rather than solvency.

The month ended with the bank's shares lower than they had begun it and with no supervisory action taken. The European authorities spent the following weeks arguing that the banking system was in better shape than in 2008 and that the failures had been American rather than European, and the argument was accurate and did not stop the next bank's shares from falling. Supervisors had spent a decade telling the market that the bank was safe, and the market had spent the same decade remembering why it had doubted them.
