---
title: "Stubb and Haavisto go to a runoff in Finland"
date: 2024-01-28 21:05:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Finland's presidential election on 28 January sent Alexander Stubb and Pekka Haavisto to a second round on 11 February, after a campaign dominated by the country's new membership in NATO and its long border with Russia."
---

Finland voted for a new president on the twenty-eighth of January, and the count sent the two favourites to a runoff. Alexander Stubb of the National Coalition Party took about 27.2 per cent, Pekka Haavisto, a former foreign minister running as an independent green, took 25.8, and Jussi Halla-aho of the Finns Party took about 19. The remaining votes were spread across six other candidates. No one came close to the fifty per cent needed to win outright, so the election goes to a second round on the eleventh of February, and the country will have a new head of state for the first time in twelve years.

The office matters more in Finland than the powers attached to it would suggest. The president is elected directly for six years, conducts foreign and security policy in cooperation with the government, and is the person the public turns to when something goes wrong outside the country's borders. Since Finland joined NATO in April 2023, and since the closure of the border crossings with Russia began in November, that role has become the most important in the political system. Candidates spent the campaign talking about security, about the eastern border, about what Finland should say to Moscow and to Washington, and very little about the domestic questions that normally decide elections.

Stubb is the candidate of continuity and of Europe. He has been a foreign minister, a finance minister, a prime minister briefly, and a member of the European Parliament, and he speaks four languages with the ease of someone who has used them in negotiations. His campaign argued that Finland's new position in NATO needs a president who is comfortable in the rooms where the alliance makes decisions, and that his years in Brussels and in the finance ministry are the relevant experience. His opponents described him as a technocrat, which in Finnish politics is not as much of an insult as it is elsewhere.

Haavisto is the outsider with the longest record. He has run for president twice before, has served as foreign minister and as a minister for development, and is the only candidate who has negotiated directly with the Russian foreign ministry in the past decade, having worked on the Finnish-Russian border arrangements. He ran without a party label and with a campaign built on volunteers, and his second place is a remarkable result for a candidate who was polling in single digits in the autumn. His argument, that the president should be a diplomat rather than a partisan, appeals to voters who are uneasy about the country's militarization.

Halla-aho's third place is the number that will shape the runoff. His party's supporters are the most sceptical of immigration and of the European Union, and their votes are now the ones both remaining candidates need. Stubb will have difficulty attracting them, because he represents everything the Finns Party opposes, and Haavisto will have difficulty for a different reason, because his background in the Green League and his work on asylum policy are the parts of his record the party dislikes most. The two weeks before the second round will be spent on that question, and the arithmetic is close enough that it will decide the outcome.

The result also says something about how quickly a country's politics can be reorganized by events. Finland spent decades building a foreign policy around the management of a difficult neighbour, and the arguments of that period are no longer the arguments being made. Both remaining candidates accept NATO membership and both accept the closure of the border. The difference between them is temperament and emphasis, not strategy. A runoff between two men who agree on the main question is a sign that the question has been settled, and that the voters are now choosing a face for the answer.
