The environment day in June is given to plastic this year, and the treaty meant to control it is stuck. A committee has met four times since 2022 to draft an agreement. The delegations agree about the problem and disagree about the scope. The disagreement turns on one word.

The problem is measurable. The world makes several hundred million tonnes of plastic each year and recycles less than a tenth of it. The rest is burned, buried or lost. The lost part reaches the ocean through a small number of rivers, mostly in Asia, which is why a cleanup on one coastline changes very little.

The negotiations have two camps. One group wants an agreement that limits the production of virgin polymer under a global rule. The other wants an agreement that improves waste systems and leaves production to each country. The first group contains the countries that import waste. The second contains the countries that make polymer.

Recycling is the claim both camps make. A bottle that is collected is sorted, washed and turned into a pellet. The pellet is worth less than virgin material for most uses. That gap explains why the collection rate has climbed while the recycling rate has stayed low.

Substitutes are a market of their own. A fibre from a plant, a film grown by a bacterium, a cup made of paper with a lining. Each has a cost that a buyer compares with the plastic it would replace. The comparison usually ends with plastic winning on price.

Chinese policy has moved on a separate track. A ban on thin bags in supermarkets. A deposit scheme for bottles in some cities. A rule requiring a share of recycled content in packaging. These are national measures carried out by municipalities, which is why the results differ from one city to the next.

The science has moved faster than the drafting. Researchers now find polymer fragments in soil, in rain and in the blood of large animals, at concentrations that the toxicology cannot yet interpret. A negotiator asked to limit production is therefore asked to act on evidence that is incomplete. The delegates have agreed to meet again before the year ends, in a city that has a port and a plastics industry of its own.

The date has an institutional history. It was set by a conference in 1972, and the agencies have used it ever since to publish a report. This year the report is about the treaty. The treaty is unfinished.

Plastic shows how a shared problem becomes a negotiation about cost. Every delegation accepts that the ocean is filling. No delegation wants to accept the bill for the polymer. The talks will continue. The waste will keep arriving. The distance between the two measures the difficulty, and it has been the same distance for three years.
