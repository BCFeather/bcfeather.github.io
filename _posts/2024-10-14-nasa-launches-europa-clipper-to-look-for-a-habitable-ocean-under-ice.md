---
title: "NASA launches Europa Clipper to look for a habitable ocean under ice"
date: 2024-10-14 22:15:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "NASA's Europa Clipper lifted off on a Falcon Heavy on 14 October, beginning a five-and-a-half year journey to Jupiter, where it will make about fifty close passes over the moon's ice-covered ocean."
---

Europa Clipper, the largest planetary spacecraft NASA has ever built, lifted off from the Kennedy Space Center in Florida at six minutes past noon on the fourteenth of October, carried by a SpaceX Falcon Heavy. Launch had been delayed by Hurricane Milton a week earlier, and the rocket flew on the first attempt after the storm passed. Spacecraft separated from the upper stage within an hour and deployed its solar arrays, which together span more than thirty metres, roughly the length of a basketball court.

Mission was designed around a single question, which is whether the ocean beneath the moon's ice could support life. Europa is slightly smaller than the Earth's moon and is covered by a shell of water ice perhaps fifteen kilometres thick, below which lies a salty ocean that may hold twice the water of all the oceans on Earth. Clipper carries radar that can penetrate the ice, cameras and spectrometers that will map the surface chemistry, and instruments that will measure the ocean's depth and salinity indirectly.

Journey is long and indirect. Spacecraft will fly past Mars in February 2025 to gain speed, then past the Earth in December 2026, and arrive at Jupiter in April 2030. It will then spend four years in orbit around the planet, making about fifty close passes over Europa, some of them within twenty-five kilometres of the surface, and swinging out to distances that limit the radiation dose on its electronics. Total cost of the mission is about five billion dollars.

Radiation is the constraint that shaped the design. Jupiter's magnetic field accelerates particles to energies that would destroy ordinary electronics, and the spacecraft carries a vault of aluminium and tantalum around its computer. Components were tested to a level of radiation three times what the European Space Agency's Jupiter mission was built for, and the team accepted a shorter lifetime in exchange for proximity to the moon.

Science that the mission can do is limited and specific. It cannot detect life directly and has no instrument that could sample the ocean, since the ice shell is far thicker than any drill that could be flown. What it can do is establish whether the ocean has the chemistry that life needs, whether there is energy at the sea floor, and whether the ice is thin enough in places for material from below to reach the surface. Those answers would change the estimate of habitability without proving anything about whether anything lives there.

Place of the mission in the programme is unusual and exposed. Europa Clipper survived a decade of budget pressure and a dispute over whether its rocket needed a nuclear power source, and it is one of the last large science missions built on the assumption of a stable budget. Planetary science has been squeezed by the cost of Mars sample return, which is overrunning and under review, and the next decadal survey will be written in that shadow. Launch of the fourteenth of October therefore marks the end of a long argument and the start of one about what comes after it.
