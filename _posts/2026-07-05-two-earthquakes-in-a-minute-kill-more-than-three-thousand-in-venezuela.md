---
title: "Two earthquakes in a minute kill more than three thousand in Venezuela"
date: 2026-07-05 23:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The shocks of 24 June were the strongest in a century, and by early July the official toll had reached 3,342 dead with seventeen thousand people still unaccounted for, most of them along the coast of La Guaira."
---

Two earthquakes struck north-central Venezuela within about a minute on the evening of 24 June, the first of magnitude 7.2 and the second of 7.5, and by 5 July the government's count had reached 3,342 dead and 16,740 injured. The assembly's president, Jorge Rodriguez, published the figures on social media, and the authorities said 6,462 people had been rescued while 17,345 were still unaccounted for. Seismologists described the pair as the strongest shocks to hit the country in more than a century.

Geography concentrated the damage. La Guaira, the coastal state that holds the country's main airport and the road down from Caracas, reported the heaviest losses, and seven states in all were affected. Apartment blocks built on filled ground near the shore collapsed in the first shock, and the second one brought down structures that the first had left standing but weakened. More than a thousand aftershocks followed over the next ten days, which kept rescue teams out of buildings they had just reached.

Rescue depended on equipment that the country had little of. Venezuela's civil defence and fire services worked with excavators borrowed from state oil companies, and volunteers from neighbourhood committees dug with their hands in the first two days. Hospitals in Caracas took the injured, and the health ministry asked for blood and for dialysis machines, because crush injuries damage kidneys and the machines were already scarce. Shelters were opened in schools and in the baseball stadium in Caracas, and officials said the number of people in them would rise while aftershocks continued.

International aid arrived slowly and unevenly. China's first consignment of relief supplies landed at Maiquetia airport on 6 July, and the Pan American Health Organization published daily situation reports that put the number of people in temporary shelters above six thousand. Sanctions on the state oil company complicated payments for imported equipment, and several aid agencies said they had routed purchases through third countries to avoid banks that would decline the transactions.

Economic stakes are larger than a normal disaster. The affected coast holds refineries, port terminals and the export pipeline that carries most of the country's crude, and the government said some loading berths were damaged. Oil exports had been recovering through the spring, and a shutdown at the terminals would remove the foreign currency that pays for food and medicine, which is the reason reconstruction will be slow even if the money appears. Shipping agents said tankers waiting to load had been moved offshore while the berths were inspected for cracks.

A country that has spent a decade watching its buildings go up without inspections has now been shown what that costs. The shocks were extreme and the toll was made by construction practice, by the absence of enforcement and by a health system that had lost most of its spare capacity before the ground moved. Aid will rebuild some of the housing, and the next earthquake will test whether anything was learned from this one. Reconstruction will need building codes that are enforced, and the country has had no effective inspection system for a generation.
