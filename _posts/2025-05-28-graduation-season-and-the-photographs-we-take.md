---
title: "Graduation season and the photographs we take"
date: 2025-05-28 21:50:00 +0800
categories: [essay]
description: "From the middle of May the campus belongs to the people who are leaving. Gowns, group photographs, and the ritual of posing in front of buildings that will shortly stop being yours: a short essay on how a university says goodbye."
---

The last week of May is when it starts. The gowns come out of their bags in the afternoons, the lawns fill with people arranged in rows, and every building on campus acquires a queue of graduates waiting for their turn in front of it. The photographs will be taken in the same places, in the same order, by the same hired photographer, for the hundredth year running, and each cohort believes it is doing something new.

It is new, to them, which is the whole mechanism. A university is a place a person inhabits for four years and then abruptly does not, and the abruptness is what the photographs are for. Everything else about graduation is procedural: the forms, the signatures, the thesis defence, the queue at the office that issues the certificate. The photographs are the only part that is purely symbolic, and they carry the entire emotional weight of the event.

The pictures themselves are strange objects. They are taken on a bright afternoon in a place that is about to stop being familiar, by a person who is about to stop being available, and they are kept for forty years. In them everyone is twenty-two, standing straight, wearing a gown that fits badly, and squinting. The building behind is recognizable to anyone who has ever been to the university and meaningless to everyone else, which is a fair description of the experience being recorded.

There is a ritual order to the poses. The single portrait, formal, for the family. The group of four or five, arms around shoulders, for the friends. The jump, sometimes, for the ones who want a picture that looks like motion. And the one with the whole class, taken from a distance, in which no individual face is legible and the point is the number. Each of these is a different claim about what the four years were.

The campus, meanwhile, is doing what it always does in late May. The trees are at full leaf, the exams are ending, the weather has turned hot enough that the gowns are unbearable by mid-afternoon. The undergraduates in their second year walk past the queues with a mild contempt for the theatre of it, and they will be in the same queues in two years, and they know it.

Then the term ends, the boxes are packed, the keys are handed back, and the class disperses across the country in the space of a week. The photographs go into a drawer or onto a wall, and the building behind them carries on being a building. What the photographs actually record is not the place. It is the last moment at which everyone was in the same place at the same time, which is a thing that will never be true again for that group of people, and which nobody understands at the time. Anyone who has kept one of these photographs will recognise the effect: the day is remembered in the vocabulary of the picture rather than the other way round, and the afternoon heat is part of the memory.
