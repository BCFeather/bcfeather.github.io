---
title: "Hungary ratifies Sweden's NATO bid and clears the last hurdle"
date: 2024-02-26 21:50:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Hungary's parliament approved Sweden's accession to NATO on 26 February, ending nineteen months in which a single member state held up the application, and Sweden is expected to join as the alliance's thirty-second member within days."
---

Hungary's parliament ratified Sweden's application to join NATO on the twenty-sixth of February, removing the last obstacle to an accession that had been pending for nineteen months. Vote was carried by a large majority, after a session in which the opposition demanded that the government explain what it had obtained in return for dropping its objections. Prime minister of Sweden said the ratification completed a process his country had begun in May 2022, and the government in Stockholm said formal accession would follow within days. Sweden will become the alliance's thirty-second member and the second new one since the invasion of Ukraine.

Delay was almost entirely Hungarian. Sweden and Finland applied together after the invasion, and Finland joined in April 2023 after Turkey dropped its objections. Sweden was held up first by Turkey, which demanded action against Kurdish groups it regards as terrorist organisations, and then by Hungary, which linked the ratification to its own disputes with Sweden and with the European Union. Governing party had accused Swedish politicians of spreading falsehoods about the state of Hungarian democracy, and had made the ratification conditional on concessions that were never specified in public.

Argument at the end was about what Orban had gained. Hungarian officials had spent months saying the ratification would come once Sweden's government had addressed its complaints, and Swedish officials had spent the same months saying there was nothing to address. In the last week the two governments announced a deal under which Sweden would sell Hungary four Gripen fighter aircraft and extend an existing lease arrangement. Whether that constitutes a price or a face-saving formula is a matter of interpretation, and the Hungarian opposition called it a price.

For Sweden, the change is constitutional as much as military. Country had been non-aligned since the Napoleonic wars, and its decision to apply was the most significant shift in its foreign policy in two centuries. Membership brings the alliance's mutual defence guarantee and a say in NATO's councils, and it also brings obligations: a defence budget target, forces assigned to allied planning, and the acceptance of nuclear weapons on alliance territory, which Sweden has said it does not want. Swedish public is divided, and the division has narrowed rather than closed since the application.

For the alliance, the number matters less than the geography. Swedish territory covers the Baltic Sea's northern approaches and the airspace over the Gulf of Bothnia, and its submarines and its fighter squadrons add capabilities that NATO has been short of. Baltic Sea is now surrounded by members on every coast except the Russian and the Kaliningrad exclaves, which changes the strategic geometry of the region. Accession also closes a chapter in which two Nordic countries applied together and were admitted separately.

Russian response has been rhetorical. Officials in Moscow had said for two years that Swedish membership would be answered with unspecified military measures, and the measures announced by the end of the month were the re-establishment of the Leningrad and Moscow military districts and a promise of more forces near the Finnish border. Analysts in Stockholm and in Brussels said the practical effect would be small in the short term, because Russia's forces are committed in Ukraine. Whether that changes over the next five years is the question the Nordic governments will plan around, and their planning has already begun.
