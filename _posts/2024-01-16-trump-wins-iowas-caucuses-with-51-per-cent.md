date: 2024-01-16 22:07:00 +0800

description: "Iowa's Republican caucuses on 15 January gave Donald Trump 51.1 per cent of the vote in wind chills near minus thirty, with Ron DeSantis second on 21.2 and Nikki Haley third on 19.1, and a turnout of about 110,000."
---

On the evening of the fifteenth of January, Republicans across Iowa gathered in school gymnasiums, church halls and living rooms to do the first thing the American presidential calendar asks of them, and by midnight the answer was in. Donald Trump took 51.1 per cent of the vote, Ron DeSantis took 21.2, Nikki Haley took 19.1, and Vivek Ramaswamy, who had spent a year running as the candidate of the party's younger wing, finished with under eight per cent and withdrew the same night. Trump's margin was the largest any Republican has had in a contested Iowa caucus, and it came in weather that would have stopped a lesser campaign.

The weather was the story of the week, and it was not a small one. Wind chills in parts of the state reached minus thirty or lower on the day of the vote, and the party had to cancel or move events, knock on doors in pairs, and remind people that attendance was not compulsory. Turnout came in at about a hundred and ten thousand, which is lower than 2016 and consistent with a state where the caucus format asks people to leave their houses at night in January and stand in a room for an hour. The campaigns that did best had spent two years building lists of committed supporters who would come out in any weather, and the ones that had not were left explaining that their voters had been busy.

DeSantis's second place is the result that keeps a campaign alive and does not make it viable. He had staked everything on Iowa, visited all ninety-nine counties, and moved his operation and much of his money into the state, and the number he got was roughly what the polls had been showing for weeks. His argument now is that he is the only alternative with an organization in the states that follow, and that Haley's support is concentrated among college-educated suburbanites who will not turn out in the South. That argument will be tested within a week, in New Hampshire, where the electorate is exactly those suburbanites and where Haley has been rising.

Haley's third place is a disappointment measured against where she was a month ago and a success measured against where she was in the autumn. She has spent the campaign arguing that the party needs a candidate who can win a general election, and the Iowa result gives her both a reason to continue and a reason to worry, because the voters who agree with her are not the voters who decide Republican primaries. Her campaign has said it will go on to New Hampshire and then to her home state of South Carolina, which is the sequence every losing campaign announces before it loses.

What the result says about the party is more interesting than what it says about the candidates. Around sixty per cent of caucus-goers described themselves in entrance polls as supporters of the movement rather than of a particular candidate, and Trump's share among first-time attendees was as strong as among veterans. The institutional party, the governors and senators and donors who spent 2016 learning to stop him, has largely made its peace. The question that Iowa answered was not whether Trump would win the nomination but whether there was any organized force inside the party that could prevent it, and on a cold Monday night in January the answer was no. New Hampshire votes on the twenty-third, and it will decide how long the contest continues rather than who wins it.
