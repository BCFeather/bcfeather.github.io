title: "Charlie Munger, the second half of Berkshire, dies at ninety-nine"
date: 2023-11-28 23:20:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "Charlie Munger, who died on 28 November 2023 a month short of his hundredth birthday, spent four decades as Warren Buffett's partner at Berkshire Hathaway and supplied the discipline that turned a textile mill into a conglomerate."
---

Charlie Munger died on 28 November at a hospital in California, a month short of his hundredth birthday. He had been Warren Buffett's partner at Berkshire Hathaway since 1978, its vice chairman since 1978 and the company's most quoted voice for the last two decades of his life. Berkshire announced the death in a statement, and Buffett said the company had been built on his ideas.

It began in Omaha, where both men grew up and where Munger's father practised law. Munger ran an investment partnership that outperformed the market for more than a decade, and Buffett has said repeatedly that Munger moved him from buying cheap, mediocre businesses to buying good businesses at fair prices. That shift is the foundation of Berkshire's method: a company with a durable advantage, a management that allocates capital well, and a price that leaves room for error.

His influence extended beyond the portfolio. He designed the company's structure, including the decentralised arrangement in which subsidiary managers run their own businesses with almost no interference from Omaha. He argued for concentration in a handful of positions rather than diversification for its own sake, and he was sceptical of most of what the finance industry sold, from derivatives to forecasts.

He was also a public teacher. His speeches, collected in Poor Charlie's Almanack, set out a method of thinking in multiple disciplines: economics, psychology, biology, mathematics. He warned about the incentive structures that make intelligent people do stupid things, and he applied the idea to corporate boards, investment banks and his own partners. He taught law students in Los Angeles for a term, and he said the classroom was the only place where an argument could be tested without a balance sheet attached. Generations of investors read the book as a manual, and its most quoted line, about needing to invert a problem, is repeated in business schools that never taught his name.

Berkshire's returns were only one measure of the man. He was chairman of Wesco Financial and a director of Costco, and he gave away much of his fortune to universities, including a law school building at Michigan and a graduate housing complex at Stanford. He was blunt in interviews, and he said what he thought about crypto, about accounting standards, and about the quality of American politics, without an editor's help.

Berkshire now faces the question that has hung over it for a decade. Buffett is ninety-three, and the succession plan puts Greg Abel in charge of operations and Ajit Jain in charge of insurance. Munger's role was never written into that plan, because it could not be, and the company will have to manage without the partner whose judgement was the counterweight to the chairman's optimism. He had given the bulk of his Berkshire shares to charity in stages, and his estate was reported to be a fraction of the billions his name had come to represent, a decision he explained by saying that he had never needed the money and that the institutions did.
