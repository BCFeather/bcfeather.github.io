In the first week of June, cities begin publishing notices that read like the rules for a small emergency. Construction sites within a certain distance of an examination centre are told to stop. Pile drivers are banned. Shops are asked to take the loudspeakers off their front steps, and the streets around a handful of school gates are closed to anything with an engine for the duration. The occasion is the national college entrance examination, and the restrictions last four days.

The rules are local and specific, which is part of their interest. One city bans night work within a radius of a school. Another closes a lane on a road where the traffic noise reaches the examination hall. A third sends inspectors to check that the air conditioning in each room is working. Nobody coordinates these measures nationally. They emerge from the same instinct at the same time of year, in hundreds of places, and they converge on an identical shape.

The instinct is about fairness. The examination is a ranking, and a ranking is only worth anything if the conditions are roughly equal. A candidate who sleeps beside a construction site is not competing with a candidate who sleeps in a quiet hotel, and the whole apparatus of admission rests on the assumption that the difference should be as small as the city can make it. Noise is the most visible part of that difference, which is why it attracts the most rules.

Households arrange themselves around the same logic. Hotels near the examination centres fill weeks in advance, at prices that would be refused in any other season. Some families rent a room for three nights simply to shorten the morning journey. Traffic police are stationed at the gates, and a candidate who arrives late because a bus broke down will usually be driven the last kilometre by an officer on a motorbike. None of this is in any regulation. It is what the city does because the city has decided this matters.

The cost of the arrangement is real, though it is rarely weighed. Four days of stopped construction, closed lanes and silenced shopfronts fall on people who are not taking an examination and who did not ask for the interruption. Delivery drivers lose hours. Builders lose a week of a schedule that is already tight. There is a case for all of it, and the case is made every year without being examined.

What the notices reveal, in the end, is the reach of a single test. A modern city is a machine of continuous noise and continuous motion, and once a year it agrees to be quiet around a few thousand teenagers holding pens. That agreement is not written down anywhere, and it is renewed annually by nobody in particular. It is the most concrete evidence available that the examination still sits at the centre of how this country arranges its year, and that the arrangement is maintained by consent rather than by decree.
