---
title: "Fifty years of theory met a bump at 125 gigaelectronvolts"
date: 2022-03-12 21:30:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "A boson that six thousand physicists agreed on, found in a machine built to miss it, and the question of what a completed model actually completes."
---

A paper published in 1964 by Peter Higgs ran to two pages and proposed a field that filled the universe and gave mass to the particles that moved through it. Five other physicists published similar ideas in the same year, three of them in the same journal and one of them, by Englert and Brout, a few weeks earlier. The mechanism solved a problem that had stopped the theory of the weak force: its carriers had to be massless by symmetry, and the experiments said they were heavy.

The prediction that followed was precise and awkward. The field would leave behind a particle, a quantum of itself, with a mass that the theory could not fix and with couplings to other particles that it could. Every fermion would couple in proportion to its mass, every boson in proportion to the square of its mass, and the Higgs would decay in a pattern that could be calculated once its mass was known. For decades the searches consisted of ruling out ranges: LEP excluded the region below 114 gigaelectronvolts, the Tevatron narrowed the window above it, and the American machine was shut down in 2011 before it could close it.

The Large Hadron Collider was built for this and for other things. On 4 July 2012, in a seminar that was broadcast from CERN, the ATLAS and CMS collaborations reported a resonance at about 125 gigaelectronvolts with a significance above five sigma for the first time. Each experiment had seen it in the same decay channels, the two photons and the pairs of Z bosons, and each had analysed the data without the other's knowledge. Higgs, in the audience, wiped his eyes and said that it was extraordinary that the thing had been found in his lifetime.

What the discovery confirmed was the mechanism rather than the theory's completion. The couplings to the W and Z bosons were measured and matched the prediction within errors; the couplings to fermions took longer and came in one at a time, and the tau and the top quark were established by 2014. The mass of 125 gigaelectronvolts turned out to be the value that makes the vacuum of the universe metastable rather than stable, a coincidence that nobody has explained and that the theory permits.

What it left open is the reason the mass has the value it does. The Higgs mass receives quantum corrections from every particle that couples to it, and those corrections should push it towards the Planck scale unless something cancels them. Supersymmetry was the favourite cancellation and the LHC has not found it; the alternative is an anthropic argument that many physicists dislike and cannot test. The measured mass also sits in a range where the vacuum would decay in a time longer than the age of the universe, which is a statement about stability rather than about danger.

Reading the discovery papers now is an exercise in watching a field close a chapter and open another. The measurements of the Higgs self-coupling, which the collider began to constrain in the 2020s, decide whether the potential has the shape that the simplest model gives. The papers of 2012 answered the question that the 1964 papers asked, and the question of why the answer is 125 has outlasted both.
