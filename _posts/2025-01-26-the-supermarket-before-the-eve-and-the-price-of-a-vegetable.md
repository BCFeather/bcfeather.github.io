The two days before the festival eve are the busiest of the year in a supermarket, and the queue at the checkout in the evening is longer than the queue at any other time. The trolleys are full of the things that cannot be bought earlier, the fresh vegetables, the fish, the fruit, and the aisles that hold the dry goods are comparatively empty because those were bought in the first fortnight of the month.

The price of a leaf vegetable is the number that the newspapers quote every year, and the number is a small index of a large system. A vegetable that is grown in a hothouse a thousand kilometres away is priced by the fuel, the labour and the road, and a vegetable that is grown in a field outside the city is priced by the weather of the preceding month. In January the second is scarce and the first is expensive, and the price of a bunch of greens in the last week of the lunar year is usually the highest of the year.

The supply chain that meets the demand is a schedule that the wholesalers set in December. The trucks leave the producing regions on a timetable that is designed to arrive at the wholesale market on the three days before the eve, and the market opens at three in the morning and closes at nine. The retail buyers who are short of stock at seven in the morning pay the price that the market sets at seven, and the price is not the same as the price at four.

The household's strategy is a set of small rules that the experienced shopper follows without stating them. Buy the dry goods early, buy the frozen goods early, buy the vegetables late, and buy the fish on the morning of the eve. The rules are about the storage, and the storage is what the household has less of than the shop.

The festival also changes what a supermarket stocks. The shelves that hold the gift boxes appear in the first week of the month and are dismantled on the day after the holiday, the counter that sells the cured meat is moved to the front of the store, and the freezer that holds the dumplings is restocked twice a day. The store is a different building for three weeks, and the difference is the inventory.

What the two days show is a system that is stretched to its annual limit and holds. The trucks arrive, the shelves are filled overnight, the staff work a double shift, and the household that queues for twenty minutes gets the dinner it planned. The price of a vegetable is the visible edge of that system, and the edge is where the weather and the road meet.

For the household that cooks, the last lesson of the month is about the menu. A dinner of eight dishes that are all fresh is a dinner that requires a kitchen the size of a restaurant, and the households that manage it are the ones that cook half of the dishes in advance and buy the other half ready.
