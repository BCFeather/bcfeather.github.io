July is the month when the western Pacific begins producing tropical storms at a rate the forecasting offices can barely keep up with. A depression forms east of the Philippines, intensifies, takes a name, and starts moving west or north-west. Within five days it is either a typhoon approaching a coast or a system that has curved away, and in either case a set of warnings has been issued, revised and issued again.

A warning is not a forecast of damage. It is a decision about how many people to move, taken under uncertainty, and the decision has a cost whichever way it goes. Evacuate a coastal district and the storm turns north, and the fishermen lose a week's income for nothing. Fail to evacuate and the storm arrives, and the cost is counted in lives. The forecast office is therefore always wrong in one direction, and the skill of the institution is in choosing which direction it can defend.

The physics has improved considerably in twenty years, and the improvement is easiest to see in the five-day track, which used to be a cone of possibility wide enough to cover half the coastline. Satellite coverage is continuous, aircraft reconnaissance fills in the structure, and the models that predict track have cut their error by more than half. Intensity remains harder. A storm's maximum wind is a small-scale feature that a global model cannot resolve, and the rapid intensification events that cause the worst damage are exactly the ones the models miss.

The Chinese forecasting service runs a four-level warning system that was designed after a series of storms in the 2000s exposed how slowly instructions travelled from a provincial office to a village, and each level carries its own instructions for schools, ports, construction sites and public transport. The system is a piece of administrative engineering as much as a meteorological one. It works because the instructions at each level are specific enough that a county official can act on them without waiting for a phone call, and because the public has learned what the levels mean.

The harder problem is the coast itself. Land reclamation, aquaculture ponds and the subsidence that comes with groundwater extraction have lowered parts of the eastern seaboard by enough that a storm surge of the same height now reaches further inland than it did in 1990. The warning has not changed. The ground underneath it has. A levee built for a 1990 storm now protects a district that has subsided by half a metre, and the safety margin was spent without anyone voting for it.

What July tests every year is whether the apparatus holds at the point where an individual decides to stay or go. The models will get better, the colour codes will be refined, and the decision will still be made by a person standing in a doorway listening for the wind. All the forecasting in the world produces a number. Somebody has to read it and act, and that somebody is not the institution.
