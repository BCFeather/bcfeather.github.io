The Formula One season opened in Melbourne on the sixteenth of March, and the race was decided by a margin of less than a second after fifty-seven laps, with Lando Norris in a McLaren ahead of Max Verstappen in a Red Bull and George Russell in a Mercedes close enough to matter. The closeness was not an accident. It is the intended output of a set of rules that have been in place since 2021 and that most of the teams opposed when they were written.

The cost cap limits what a team may spend on the performance of its car to a figure that is adjusted each year and was set at about a hundred and forty million dollars for the current season. It excludes the drivers' salaries, the marketing, the cost of the engines for the teams that buy them, and it includes everything else that makes a car faster, the wind tunnel, the simulation, the factory, the engineers. A large team that used to spend three hundred million now spends the cap and must choose where.

The effect on the field is the one that the rule was designed to produce. The gap between the fastest and the slowest car has narrowed every year since the cap arrived, and the midfield is now separated by tenths rather than seconds. The design of the cars has converged, the strategies have converged, and the races are decided by the small differences that remain, a pit stop of two and a half seconds, a tyre that holds for four laps longer than expected, a driver who brakes ten metres later into one corner.

The economics of the sport have moved with it. A team that is valued at a billion dollars was worth a fraction of that a decade ago, because the cap turned an owner's liability into a business with a predictable cost base and a share of a revenue that has grown with the American audience. The entry fee for a new team is now several hundred million dollars and the queue of applicants is real.

The cost cap has also created a new kind of policing. The accounts of every team are audited, the audits produced a penalty in 2022 for a team that overspent, and the argument about what counts as a performance cost has become a permanent negotiation. A catering bill and a wind tunnel run are separated by a rule that someone has to interpret, and the interpretation is the part of the cap that is least visible and most contested.

What the opener showed is a sport that has become closer and more expensive to enter, and both of those are consequences of one decision. The race was good, and the goodness was manufactured by a rule that nobody in the paddock asked for, which is an unusual thing in a sport that usually writes its rules around its strongest competitors, and the teams that voted against the cap now defend it in their results.
