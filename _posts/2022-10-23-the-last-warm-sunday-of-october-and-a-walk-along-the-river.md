title: "The last warm Sunday of October, and a walk along the river"

description: "A clear Sunday in late October, a folding table of mahjong players, and the light that goes an hour before the day does."

The rain stopped on Saturday night and Sunday came in clear, and by ten in the morning the river walk was full of people who had been indoors all week. Plane trees line the path, and their leaves turn a colour closer to brass than to gold before they fall. Two men had brought a folding table and were playing mahjong in the sun, and the sound of the tiles carried further than the traffic on the road above them. A dog had been left tied to a railing beside a bicycle, and it watched the game with the patience of an animal that had been brought there before.

In this city the seasons arrive quietly and leave the same way. October is the month when the air loses its weight, when the evenings are cool enough to need a jacket and the afternoons are still warm enough to sit outside. The tea houses fill up on a Sunday, and the people in them are mostly old men who have kept the same table for twenty years. They order the same tea, they argue about the same matches and they leave at the same hour, and the staff know all of it without being told.

There is a particular light in the last weeks of the year, low and slightly yellow, that makes the city look older than it is. It falls on the white walls of the old lanes and on the tiles of the roofs, and it lasts about an hour before the buildings take it back. Photographers arrange their afternoons around that hour, and the rest of us notice it only when it is gone. By November the angle has changed and the effect with it, which is why the same street looks different in two photographs taken a month apart.

I spent the afternoon at a desk with a book open, which is what most of the term looks like from the inside. The weeks have a rhythm that becomes visible only when it is broken: the reading, the exercises, the walk to the canteen, the walk back, the hour before dinner when nobody expects anything. A holiday breaks it, and for a day the rhythm looks artificial; then the term resumes and it looks inevitable again. Somewhere in October the pattern stops feeling like something imposed and starts feeling like something chosen, and that shift is the whole of what a student gains in a term.

The World Cup begins in three weeks in a country where the temperature in November is still above thirty degrees. The competition has been moved to winter for the first time, and the European leagues have rearranged their calendars around it, which means the season will pause in the middle and start again in December. In the tea houses the argument has already moved to which team will be embarrassed and which will surprise, and the answers are confident in a way that the answers to most questions rarely are. Nobody has asked what the workers who built the stadiums were paid, and somebody should.

By five the light had left the river and the benches were emptying. A woman was selling roasted sweet potatoes from a drum on the corner, and the smoke went straight up in the still air. Somebody's phone played a song through a speaker, and nobody asked him to stop. Autumn is short here, and it has begun.
