---
title: "Japan's governing coalition loses its majority in the lower house"
date: 2024-10-27 23:00:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Liberal Democratic Party fell to 191 seats and its coalition to 215 in the election of 27 October, below the 233 needed for a majority, leaving Prime Minister Shigeru Ishiba to govern with the support of smaller parties."
---

Japan's governing coalition lost its majority in the lower house in the election of the twenty-seventh of October, the first time since 2009 that the Liberal Democratic Party and its partner Komeito have fallen short of the 233 seats needed to control the chamber. Liberal Democrats took 191 seats, down from 247, and Komeito 24, which left the coalition with 215. Main opposition Constitutional Democratic Party rose to 148 seats from 98, and the Democratic Party for the People took 28.

Result was a verdict on the party rather than on the new prime minister alone. Shigeru Ishiba had taken office at the start of October after winning a leadership contest, and he called the election within days, which gave his party little time to separate itself from the record of the government it had run for most of the past decade. Discontent centred on inflation, on a series of funding scandals in which factions failed to declare donations, and on the party's habit of replacing leaders without changing anything else.

Scandal was the dominant issue and it was local as well as national. Prosecutors investigated slush funds that had been hidden from the party's accounts, and several senior figures were questioned or charged. Party expelled a handful of members and declined to endorse others as candidates, which cost it seats in districts where its organization had expected to win. Voters in those districts told interviewers that they were voting against the party rather than for anything the opposition offered.

Arithmetic of the new chamber leaves the government dependent on small parties. Democratic Party for the People, which campaigns on cutting taxes and on raising the threshold at which income tax starts, has said it will negotiate policy by policy rather than join the coalition. Ishiba said he would continue as prime minister and would seek partners, and he ruled out a grand coalition with the Constitutional Democrats. Minority government is unusual in Japan, and the last one, in the 1990s, lasted less than a year.

Policy consequence is that the government's programme is now hostage to the budget. Spending plans for the coming year require the support of the lower house, and the opposition has said it will demand higher spending on households and a reversal of the increase in the price of fuel. Nuclear restarts, which the government wants to accelerate, are opposed by the Democratic Party for the People. Defence spending, which had been rising under a plan agreed in 2022, may be slowed, and the tax increase that was meant to pay for it has become harder to pass.

Wider meaning concerns the stability of a system that had produced a series of long-lived governments. Japan has had a dominant party for most of the post-war period, and its ability to renew itself has been the basis of that dominance. Election of the twenty-seventh of October shows that the mechanism has stopped working, at least for now, and that voters are willing to divide the parliament in a way they have not since the 1990s. What comes next depends on whether the Liberal Democrats can rebuild from opposition, and whether the parties that gained seats can turn a protest vote into a programme.
