---
title: "Tucker Carlson publishes a two-hour interview with Putin"
date: 2024-02-10 20:35:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The former Fox News host released a long interview with Vladimir Putin on 8 February in which the Russian president spent the first half hour on history and repeated his case for the war, and the Kremlin used the platform to reach an American audience."
---

Former Fox News host Tucker Carlson released a two-hour interview with Vladimir Putin on the eighth of February, the first sit-down the Russian president had given to a Western journalist since the invasion of Ukraine two years earlier. Roughly half an hour of Putin's account of Russian history from the ninth century opened the conversation, delivered at length and without interruption, before it reached the war. Carlson did not challenge the historical claims, and he did not ask about war crimes or about the Russian opposition. He asked once why Putin had not simply explained his case to the West, and Putin said that the West did not listen.

Choice of interviewer was the point of the exercise. Carlson had spent the previous two years arguing that Western coverage of the war was propaganda, and he had built an audience of millions on the claim that the mainstream press would not talk to the other side. Kremlin's calculation was that an American with that audience was worth more than the access given to a news organization with editorial oversight. Publication was on Carlson's website and on X, and the Kremlin reposted it. Within a day it had been viewed tens of millions of times across the two platforms.

Answers followed a script familiar to Russian television viewers. Putin argued that Ukraine was historically Russian territory, that the 2014 revolution was a Western coup, that NATO had broken its promises on expansion and that Russia had been left no choice. He said he was ready to negotiate and blamed the Ukrainian government for refusing. Asked about Evan Gershkovich, the Wall Street Journal reporter held in Russia, he said the case was about espionage rather than journalism.

Reaction outside Russia was about the terms of the interview rather than its content. Russian journalists noted that a Western figure had been given access they had sought for years, and Ukrainian officials called it a platform for propaganda. Several American commentators argued that the interview had been edited to serve its subject, and Carlson's team said nothing had been cut. Broadcast is what it was. Its significance was that a large American audience heard the Russian case in the Kremlin's own words, without a filter, for the first time in two years.

What the interview did to the debate is the part that lasts. Putin had spent two years speaking to Russians and to the global south, and the American public had heard him largely through summaries and quotations. Two hours of unedited conversation put the full argument in front of them, in a form that was easy to watch and hard to check. Whether that changes opinion is unproven, and whether the access was worth it is a question the journalists who had been refused it answered immediately and in the negative.

Coverage of the interview itself became an argument about the terms of public debate. Carlson was received in Moscow as a visiting celebrity, given a tour of the metro and a meeting at the Kremlin, and the programme was promoted for weeks before release. Critics pointed out that he had not asked about the Russian army's conduct in Bucha or Mariupol, and that he had accepted the framing of the conversation from the first minute. Supporters said the interview was a chance for Americans to hear the other side, and that refusing the access produced silence rather than scrutiny. Neither camp changed many minds, and the interview was still being quoted a year later.
