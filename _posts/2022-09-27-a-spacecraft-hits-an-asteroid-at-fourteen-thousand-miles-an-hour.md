---
title: "A spacecraft hits an asteroid at fourteen thousand miles an hour"
date: 2022-09-27 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "DART was the first attempt to move a celestial body, and the camera that watched it was a cube the size of a shoebox."
---

The spacecraft called Dart struck a small asteroid on Monday night, and the last image it sent before the signal stopped was a wall of grey rock filling the frame. The target was Dimorphos, a body about a hundred and sixty metres across that circles a larger asteroid called Didymos, and the pair are eleven million kilometres from Earth. The probe weighed about six hundred kilograms and hit at roughly fourteen thousand miles an hour. The mission cost three hundred and twenty-five million dollars and was designed in part as an exercise.

A second camera recorded the impact. A small satellite the size of a shoebox had been released eleven days earlier, and it flew past the pair two minutes after the collision to photograph the cloud of debris from a safe distance. The images were relayed through the main spacecraft's antenna before it broke up, and they arrived at the control room at Johns Hopkins University about four hours later. Engineers described the sequence as nominal, which in their language means nothing went wrong.

Its purpose is to test whether a spacecraft can change the orbit of an asteroid. Didymos and Dimorphos pose no threat to the Earth, and the experiment is a rehearsal for a future object that might. The theory is that a kinetic impact on a small body, delivered early enough, will shift its path by a margin that grows over years. What matters is the change in the time Dimorphos takes to go round Didymos rather than the size of the crater, and that period was about eleven hours and fifty-five minutes.

Astronomers will now observe the pair with telescopes for months to measure that change. The telescopes in Chile and in Hawaii had been taking images for weeks to fix the orbit before the impact, and the same instruments will repeat the measurement afterwards. The expectation in the team's models was a change of about ten minutes in the orbital period, and the final number will decide whether the models were right. A mission that moved the moonlet by the predicted amount would be evidence that the technique works.

Navigation was the engineering problem that the mission exposed. The probe had to hit a body that had never been photographed closely, and its camera could not resolve the shape until the last hour. The final approach was autonomous, with the spacecraft choosing its own target within a small box, and the images it sent showed a surface of loose rock rather than the smooth sphere of the artist's impressions. A body made of rubble absorbs an impact differently from a solid one, which is why the measurement matters more than the crater.

What the mission leaves is a technique with a known cost and an unknown reliability. Deflecting an asteroid with a spacecraft is possible in principle for an object of that size and impossible for a much larger one, and the warning time is the variable that decides which case applies. The result will be published in the next few months, and the next mission is a European probe that will arrive to survey the crater in four years. For now the achievement is that a machine built by people hit a rock eleven million kilometres away within a minute of the plan.
