The last three weeks of August are when a city's uniform shops do most of their year's business. A shop near a primary school opens at eight and has a queue by half past, and the queue is mostly parents with a child in tow and a list on a phone that was issued by the school in July and has been lost twice since. The list specifies the colour of the trousers, the number of shirts, the kind of shoes and the sports kit that is needed for one term and will be outgrown before the second.

The economics of school uniform are stranger than they look, and the strangeness is concentrated in a single month. The garment is a commodity made in enormous quantities to a standard pattern, and it retails at a price that reflects the shop's rent and its season rather than the cost of the cloth. A shop that sells three thousand shirts in three weeks and nothing for forty weeks has to recover its year in that window, and it does, which is why the same shirt costs more in August than in October.

The schools that specify a unique design make the problem worse. A uniform that only one school uses cannot be bought anywhere else, and the shop that holds the contract has a local monopoly for as long as the contract lasts. Some cities have tried to break this by requiring a standard design that any supplier can produce, and the results have been mixed. The price falls, and the parents who wanted the distinctive blazer complain.

There is a secondhand market, and it has grown for reasons that have nothing to do with the environment. Parents' groups run exchanges, some schools hold a sale in the first week of term, and the online platforms carry listings for a blazer worn for one year. The secondhand trade is efficient and informal, and it saves a household perhaps a third of the cost, which for a family with two children is the difference between buying new shoes and not.

The queue itself is a piece of social infrastructure, and it is one of the few that a parent joins voluntarily and leaves with a phone number. It is one of the few places in the city where a parent stands next to another parent from a different school and talks about the same thing. The conversation is about sizes, teachers, the canteen and the bus route, and it is the beginning of a network that will be useful for the next six years.

What the shop sells, in the end, is cloth and an assurance, and the assurance is the part that cannot be delivered by post. It is the certainty that the child will look like everyone else on the first morning, which is a service that no online retailer has managed to deliver at any price, and which is worth standing in a queue for in the last week of the holiday.
