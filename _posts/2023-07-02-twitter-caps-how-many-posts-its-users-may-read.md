---
title: "Twitter caps how many posts its users may read"
date: 2023-07-02 18:10:00 +0800
categories: [technology]
description: "On 1 July 2023 Twitter limited reading to 10,000 posts a day for verified accounts and 1,000 for unverified ones, a step its owner described as a response to data scraping, and one that broke third-party clients and much of the site for a weekend."
---

Twitter began the weekend of 1 July by refusing to display posts to most of the people who asked for them, and the outage rolled across time zones as each region woke up. Users who opened the site met a message saying that the rate limit had been exceeded, and the limit turned out to be a daily cap on how many posts an account could read: 10,000 for verified accounts, 1,000 for unverified ones and 500 for new unverified accounts. The owner, Elon Musk, described the measure as a response to organizations scraping the platform's data.

The numbers were revised twice within a day, upward each time, which suggested the first figures had been chosen without much testing. Even the revised caps broke ordinary use: a reader who follows a few hundred active accounts can exhaust a thousand posts in an afternoon, and journalists, researchers and moderators reported losing access to threads they were working on. The company also acknowledged that its own staff could not always reach the site, an admission that the limit had gone past the point of control.

The stated justification concerned data extraction. Several AI developers had been pulling public posts to build training sets, and the company said the scraping was degrading service for everyone else. Critics replied that the same goal could have been met with rate limits on application interfaces rather than on human readers, and that the timing had more to do with advertising revenue than with engineering. Advertisers had been leaving since the previous autumn, and a smaller audience is easier to explain to them than a cheaper one.

Third-party clients stopped working altogether. Applications built on the old public interface had already been cut off earlier in the year after an announcement that the free access tier would end, and the July limit appeared to remove the remaining routes into the site. Posting through some scheduling tools also failed, which hurt accounts that run on automation rather than on a person at a keyboard. Emergency services and weather agencies that publish warnings through automated accounts were among the affected users.

Competitors treated the disruption as a marketing opportunity. Bluesky, still invitation-only, reported record sign-ups, and Meta confirmed within days that it was preparing its own text network, which arrived as Threads on 5 July and enrolled tens of millions of accounts in its first week. Substack and Mastodon also reported surges that proved short-lived. The episode handed every rival an argument they did not have to make themselves.

Whether the cap achieved anything is unclear. Scraping did not stop, the platform's advertising business continued to shrink, and the weekend left users with a clear demonstration that access to the site depended on a decision taken without notice. Engineers outside the company noted that a read limit is a blunt instrument against crawlers, which distribute their requests and change addresses. For a service whose value rests on being read, that demonstration was expensive. A limit aimed at machines was felt almost entirely by people.
