---
title: "The Kakhovka dam breaks and floods the Dnipro"
date: 2023-06-06 19:15:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The Nova Kakhovka dam on the Dnipro river was breached in the early hours of 6 June, sending a reservoir's worth of water into towns and farmland downstream and forcing thousands to leave their homes."
---

The Nova Kakhovka dam, one of the largest structures on the Dnipro river, was breached in the early hours of 6 June, and the reservoir behind it emptied through the gap into the towns and farmland downstream. Water reached Kherson within hours, flooded streets in dozens of settlements, and forced the evacuation of thousands of people from both banks. Satellite images showed the dam's roadway broken into sections and the water level in the reservoir falling metres in a single day.

Control of the dam was Russian, and the two governments have accused each other of destroying it. Ukrainian officials said the breach was caused by explosives placed inside the structure, and published footage of what they described as a vehicle carrying charges on the dam in late May. Russian officials said the damage resulted from Ukrainian shelling of the hydroelectric station, which had been hit repeatedly in the preceding months. Neither account has been independently established, and the physical evidence sits in a war zone that investigators cannot reach.

Immediate consequences are hydrological and humanitarian, and they arrived within hours. The reservoir supplied cooling water to the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant, which sits upstream of the breach, and the International Atomic Energy Agency said the plant's ponds held enough water for its needs, though the long-term supply now depends on a different source. Drinking water, irrigation and the canal system that feeds Crimea all depend on the reservoir, and the flood has taken the region's largest store of fresh water with it. Mines that had been laid on the riverbanks were washed downstream, which will make the floodplain dangerous for years.

Longer consequences are agricultural. The area below the dam is some of the most productive farmland in Ukraine, and the irrigation network that serves it was fed by the reservoir. Analysts expect the loss of the water supply to cut yields in the coming season and to make the land dependent on pumps and wells that do not exist. The flood also destroyed the topsoil on the floodplain and, in some places, moved it entirely, which is a change measured in decades rather than in seasons.

There is a legal dimension that will outlast the water. The destruction of a dam is a matter for the Geneva conventions and for the International Criminal Court, and both sides have said they will pursue it. Establishing what happened depends on access that the war denies, and a large civilian structure destroyed in the middle of a campaign, with no agreed account of who did it, sets no comfortable precedent. The flood will recede within weeks; the question of responsibility will not, and the region will be rebuilding around both for years.

The reservoir will take years to refill even if the dam is rebuilt, and what remains of the water is now a contested resource on a front line. Reconstruction estimates already run into the billions of dollars, and they assume a peace that nobody has yet offered, which is the least certain input in the calculation.
