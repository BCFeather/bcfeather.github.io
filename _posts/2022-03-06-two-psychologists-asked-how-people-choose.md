title: "Two psychologists asked how people choose, and economics had to answer"
date: 2022-03-06 21:40:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "Thirty pages from 1979 replace the smooth utility curve with a reference point, a kink where the losses start, and a tendency to overweight what is unlikely."
---

Some papers are read for their results and some for the shape of the argument. The one that Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky published in *Econometrica* in 1979 belongs to the second kind: it takes the standard model of choice apart with a set of questions that a student can answer in a lecture hall, and it puts in its place a curve that bends the other way on either side of a reference point. Economists had assumed for a century that a person values a gain by its size alone.

Their experiments used simple lotteries. In one, people chose between a certain 3,000 and an 80 per cent chance of 4,000, and most took the certain sum even though the arithmetic favours the gamble. In another, the same sums framed as losses produced the reverse: the same people preferred the gamble to a certain loss. The pattern held across groups of students and of experienced decision makers, which is what made it interesting rather than a curiosity about undergraduates.

The replacement they proposed has three parts. Value is measured from a reference point, which is usually the position the person already occupies, so that a wage rise and a wage cut of the same amount do not feel like equal events. The curve is concave above the reference point and convex below it, which means that gains saturate and losses hurt more as they grow. Losses also weigh roughly twice as much as gains of the same size, a ratio that later work refined but never overturned.

Probability received the same treatment. People overweight small chances, which is why insurance sells and why a lottery with a vanishing probability of a large prize attracts money. They underweight moderate and high chances, which is why a certain sum looks better than a near-certain one. The combination produces the fourfold pattern that the authors set out: risk averse for gains at high probability, risk seeking for gains at low probability, and the mirror image for losses.

A later paper by the same authors, published in 1981, showed how much the frame matters. Two groups were given the same epidemic and the same two programmes, one described by the number who would be saved and the other by the number who would die, and the groups chose differently. The finding travelled further than the experiments that produced it, into medicine, into the wording of consent forms, and into the design of pension enrolment.

What the paper left behind is a research programme as much as a theory. Cumulative prospect theory, published in 1992, gave the model a form that could be fitted to data and compared with the alternatives. Kahneman received the Nobel prize in 2002, six years after Tversky's death, and the citation named the work on judgement under uncertainty rather than this paper alone. The criticisms since have been about magnitudes rather than existence: loss aversion is smaller in some populations, the laboratory choices may not map onto purchases, and the reference point is harder to observe in the field than on a printed page. The kink in the curve, thirty pages of it, has survived them.
