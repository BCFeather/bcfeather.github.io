---
title: "A bomb hits the theatre in Mariupol where hundreds were hiding"
date: 2022-03-16 23:55:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The word for children is painted in white on the pavement outside, visible from the air, and the building that carried it becomes the most documented ruin of the siege."
---

Shortly after 10 in the morning on 16 March, a bomb destroyed the Donetsk Regional Academic Drama Theatre in the centre of Mariupol, a building with a colonnade and a small park that had been the city's main stage since the Soviet period. The strike collapsed the roof over the auditorium and buried the people who were sheltering in the basement and in the corridors. Rescue work began at once and continued for days, with volunteers digging by hand while the shelling went on around them.

Marking had been done in advance. Two large words, the Russian word for children, had been painted in white on the pavement in front of and behind the building, so that a pilot could read them from the air, and the marking was visible in satellite images taken before the strike. Between 800 and 1,200 civilians were inside or in the grounds at the time, according to the monitoring mission of the United Nations, which based the figure on interviews with survivors and on the number of people who had been using the building as a shelter.

Estimates of the dead have never converged. The Ukrainian authorities said about 300 people were killed, a figure that the city's administration repeated through the spring, and an investigation by the Associated Press published two years later put the number at close to 600, on the evidence of 23 survivors and of the floor plans of the building. Amnesty International verified a smaller number of individual deaths through interviews, and the office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights described hundreds of casualties without fixing a total.

One strike sat inside a siege that had already destroyed most of the city. By the middle of March, satellite analysis counted damage to more than 90 per cent of the residential buildings in Mariupol, the water and heating systems had failed, and the hospitals that still worked were in basements. Aid convoys could not enter, the corridors that were announced failed, and the people who remained were living on stored food and on water from a pipe in one district.

The legal question was whether the theatre was a military objective. The Office of the High Commissioner said the building had been clearly marked as a place where civilians were sheltering, that no military target was visible in the immediate area, and that the attack was most likely a war crime. Russia denied the strike and said the building had been used by fighters, a claim for which no evidence was produced, and the investigations that followed assembled witness testimony and flight data rather than physical evidence from the site.

The word on the pavement was intended to make the building legible from the sky, and the building was hit anyway. What the ruin established in the record of the war is the argument that a civilian object does not become a military one because soldiers are near it, and that a sign painted in white is a promise that the side with the aircraft has to read. The theatre is now a shell with a roof that fell in, and the number of people under it remains a range that begins at 300 and ends near 600.
