---

A scenic area that used to admit anyone now sells a fixed number of slots per day. The number is set by a capacity study and it is enforced by a turnstile that reads a code. A visitor without a slot cannot enter, whatever the distance travelled.

The booking opens at a fixed hour, usually eight in the evening, seven days ahead. A popular site sells its weekend slots in four minutes. The agency that handles the booking runs a queue in front of the page. The queue is longer than the supply.

The change moved the crowd from the gate to the car park. A visitor who has a slot arrives at the hour printed on it, which spreads the arrivals across the morning. The road that used to jam at nine is busy from eight to eleven and empty afterwards.

The local guesthouses read the booking curve. A weekend that sells out three days early means a full house at a high price. A weekend that is still available two days before means a discount. The discount is offered on the platform rather than at the door.

The free admission policy complicates the count. A site that admits pensioners and children without a fee still has to count them against the capacity. The count is taken from the turnstile rather than from the ticket.

The cable car is the second bottleneck. A cabin that holds eight people and runs for twelve hours carries a few thousand a day. A site that admits twenty thousand has a queue at the bottom station by ten in the morning.

The staff have learned to manage it. A queue that reaches a fixed point is announced on the app with an estimated wait, and a visitor who sees ninety minutes often leaves. The announcement is the cheapest capacity tool the site owns.

The mountain itself is the reason for the limit. A trail that carries two thousand people a day erodes at a rate that the park measures every autumn. The limit was set from that measurement, and it is the number that the ticket office enforces.

The visitors who plan a trip now plan it around a booking. A family that wants a weekend in August chooses the site by the slot it can get, which has changed which mountains are crowded.

The ticket is also a data source. A site that sells by the hour knows how many people will arrive at eleven and how many will leave at four. The knowledge is used to staff the car park, to schedule the shuttle bus and to warn the restaurants on the summit.

The village at the foot of the mountain has mixed feelings about the limit. A shop that sells noodles to a queue of tourists earns less than it did five years ago, and it earns more reliably, because the crowd now arrives in a steady stream instead of in a single wave. The park publishes the number at the gate every evening, which is how a resident can tell whether the day was a good one without looking at the till.
