---
title: "The union puts a price on Russian diesel"
date: 2023-02-03 22:20:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "European Union member states agreed on 3 February 2023 to cap the price of seaborne Russian diesel at 100 dollars a barrel and other refined products at a 45 dollar discount to crude, a measure that took effect on 5 February."
---

Member states agreed on 3 February to cap the price of seaborne Russian refined products, completing a package that had begun with the crude embargo in December. Diesel and gasoil were capped at 100 dollars a barrel, and products such as naphtha and fuel oil at a discount of 45 dollars to Brent. The measure took effect on 5 February, alongside a ban on importing Russian refined products into the union, and it binds anyone who wants European shipping, insurance or finance for a cargo.

Its design reflects what the market for refined products looks like. Russia exports about a million barrels a day of diesel, more than any other single seller, and much of it goes to countries that have no interest in the embargo. A cap that only applied inside the union would have done nothing to the price that the rest of the world pays, so the mechanism was built to reach cargoes sold anywhere, as long as a European firm touches the transaction.

The arithmetic behind 100 dollars was a compromise between two fears. A cap set too high would leave the Kremlin with the revenue it had been earning in December, and a cap set too low would take Russian diesel off the market and push prices up for farmers in Africa and bus companies in Latin America. The commission's own analysis suggested that the market could absorb the loss of Russian volumes within a year, and that the first months would be the difficult ones. The commission had also promised a review in March, which gave the coalition a way of adjusting the level without reopening the whole package.

Prices in the weeks before the deadline told a story of preparation. The diesel crack in northwest Europe had risen through January as traders bought ahead of the ban, and floating storage filled with cargoes that had been bought while they were still legal. Once the cap was in force, the premium fell back, which suggested that the market had priced the disruption before it happened.

Enforcement was the part that no one could measure yet. A cargo sold above the cap could still move if the buyer arranged non-European shipping and insurance, and the coalition's answer was to ask for attestations and to threaten secondary penalties on the firms that falsified them. Whether that would be enough depended on how much of the trade was willing to leave the European financial system, and on how much of it was moving through small ports and ship-to-ship transfers.

The policy also illustrated the difference between a sanction and a price. An embargo removes a supplier from a market and lets the price find a new level, which is what December did to crude. A cap tries to keep the supplier in the market while lowering what it earns, which requires cooperation that the supplier has no reason to give. Europe had chosen the harder instrument, and the first months of 2023 were the test of whether it could be made to work.
