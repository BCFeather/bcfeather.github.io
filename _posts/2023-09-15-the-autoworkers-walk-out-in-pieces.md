---
title: "The autoworkers walk out in pieces"
date: 2023-09-15 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The United Auto Workers struck three plants belonging to General Motors, Ford and Stellantis at midnight on 14 September 2023, beginning a campaign of rolling stoppages rather than a national walkout."
---

The union's contracts with the three Detroit carmakers expired at midnight on 14 September, and rather than call out all of its members the United Auto Workers struck one plant at each company. About thirteen thousand workers walked out at the General Motors plant in Wentzville, Missouri, the Ford plant in Wayne, Michigan, and the Stellantis plant in Toledo, Ohio, which builds the Jeep Wrangler. The strategy, which the union's new president Shawn Fain calls a stand-up strike, is to keep the companies guessing about which plant will be next and to conserve the fund that pays strike benefits.

Demands are large and the companies' offers fall well short. The union asked for a thirty-six per cent general wage increase over four years, the restoration of cost-of-living adjustments that were given up in 2007, defined-benefit pensions for new hires, a thirty-two-hour week and the right to strike over plant closures. The companies have offered increases of about twenty per cent over the same period, some cost-of-living protection, and the conversion of temporary workers to permanent status. The gap is wide enough that both sides have described it as a historic dispute.

Context is profit and transformation. The carmakers made record profits in the past three years, and their chief executives' pay has risen faster than the wages of the workers who build the cars. At the same time they are spending billions on electric models and on battery plants, most of which will be owned through joint ventures whose workers sit outside the national agreement, and the union wants those plants organized before they become the centre of the industry. A decade of concessions after the 2009 bankruptcy is the history the members have in mind.

Politics surrounds the union's own leadership. Fain, elected in March after a federal investigation sent two former presidents to prison, has promised a more confrontational approach, and his public statements have been delivered in a style that borrows from the platforms his members use. The companies have accused him of refusing to bargain seriously, and he has accused them of waiting for a better offer from a president who will not be in office next year.

Economic effect depends on the length of the stoppage. A strike at three plants costs the companies a few hundred million dollars a week in lost production, which is tolerable, and a strike at the plants that make engines and transmissions would stop production across the country, which the companies cannot absorb. The union has about eight hundred million dollars in its strike fund, enough to pay benefits for roughly three months at the current level of action, and both sides know the number to the day, which is why the companies are talking about a settlement and the union about a schedule.

An industry that has been losing its grip on labour for forty years is being challenged by a union that has learned to move in small steps. The next plant will be chosen without notice, and the carmakers will spend the autumn trying to predict which one it is.
