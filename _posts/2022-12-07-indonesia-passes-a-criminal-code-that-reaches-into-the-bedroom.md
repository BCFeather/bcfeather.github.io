---
title: "Indonesia passes a criminal code that reaches into the bedroom and the press"
date: 2022-12-07 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "The new code replaces a Dutch colonial statute and punishes sex outside marriage, cohabitation and insults to the president."
---

The parliament passed a new criminal code on Tuesday that replaces the statute left by the Dutch, and the vote was unanimous among the parties that turned up. The code punishes sex outside marriage with up to a year in prison, and cohabitation with six months, and the articles apply to residents and to visitors. It also carries prison terms for insulting the president or a state institution, for spreading ideas the text calls communism and for practising what it calls black magic. The law takes effect in three years, and the government says the delay is for the courts to prepare.

A draft has been in the works for decades, and it was revived in 2019 and then postponed after protests in Jakarta and in Bali. This year's version passed with the support of the parties in the coalition, and the opposition was outnumbered by a margin that had been clear since the election. Officials argued in the debate that the code reflects Indonesian values and that the old statute reflected a colonial one. The justice minister said the aim was to unify a legal system that had been patched for two centuries.

Clauses that worry investors and hoteliers are the ones about morality. Tourism bodies in Bali said the code would frighten the visitors on whom the island depends, since a foreign couple staying in one room could in principle be prosecuted, and the government then issued a clarification saying that reports would have to come from a family member or a spouse. Business groups said the same about the investment climate, and the rupiah fell against the dollar on the day. The tourism minister promised that the rules would be applied with care.

Press clauses are the other problem. The code punishes the spread of false news and the insulting of state institutions, and the definitions are broad enough that the press council has asked how they will be used. Indonesia had already passed a law on electronic information that had been used against critics, and journalists said the new code would add to the tools available. The government said the articles would protect the public from disinformation and that the courts would interpret them narrowly.

Opposition in the chamber was small and loud. Two parties voted against the code, and one of them read out a list of the articles it said would be used against the poor, against women and against students. Human rights groups promised to challenge the law at the constitutional court, and lawyers said the test would be whether the morality clauses violate the right to privacy that the constitution guarantees. The president signed it within a week, and the countdown of three years began.

What the code does over the next few years will be decided by the courts, which have a mixed record on political cases. The articles about insulting the president carry terms that are long enough to matter, and the ones about morality will be used where a complaint is filed. The government's argument is that a country of two hundred and seventy million people needs a single code, and the argument against is that a single code written in a hurry will be read by police officers rather than by judges. Both of those statements will be tested after 2025.
