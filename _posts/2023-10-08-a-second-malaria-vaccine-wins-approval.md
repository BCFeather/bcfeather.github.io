---
title: "A second malaria vaccine wins approval"
date: 2023-10-08 15:30:00 +0800
categories: [science]
description: "The World Health Organization recommended the R21/Matrix-M malaria vaccine for children on 2 October 2023, the second such vaccine approved, with a three-dose schedule and an efficacy of about 75 per cent in a trial across four African countries."
---

The World Health Organization recommended a second malaria vaccine for children on 2 October, clearing R21/Matrix-M for use in countries where the disease is endemic. The recommendation follows a trial that enrolled about 4,800 children in Burkina Faso, Mali, Kenya and Tanzania and reported efficacy of roughly 75 per cent over a year when the vaccine is given before the rainy season, with protection waning unless a booster follows. The recommendation covers children in settings where transmission is moderate to high, and it allows a seasonal schedule in the Sahel.

Malaria kills about 600,000 people a year, most of them children under five in sub-Saharan Africa, and the existing vaccine, RTS,S, has been in use since 2019 with efficacy in the region of 40 to 60 per cent after four doses. R21 is cheaper to make, needs a smaller dose of the antigen, and the Serum Institute of India has said it can produce 100 million doses a year, which matters because the supply of RTS,S had been limited to a few million. The parasite has also developed partial resistance to the drugs that treat it, which makes prevention worth more than it was a decade ago.

The schedule is demanding. Three doses are given to infants from five months, followed by a booster a year later, and the timing has to fit with seasonal peaks of transmission in some countries and with routine immunization schedules in others. Gavi, the vaccine alliance, has to find the money for procurement, and health ministries have to find the staff to deliver doses to clinics that already run short.

There is a disease-modifying argument underneath the announcement. The vaccine reduces clinical episodes but does not prevent infection, and its benefit depends on bed nets, indoor spraying and prompt treatment with artemisinin combinations. Countries that scaled up those measures cut deaths sharply in the 2000s, and progress stalled around 2015 when funding flattened and resistance to insecticide spread. Nets still account for most of the reduction in mortality, and funding for them has fallen in real terms since 2018.

That decision also settles a scientific dispute about how much protection is enough. A vaccine that halves cases in the highest-burden settings changes childhood mortality, and the cost per death averted compares well with other interventions. The WHO's own modelling suggests that adding R21 to the existing tools could prevent tens of thousands of deaths a year, provided the doses arrive and the campaigns get run.

Roll-out is the test. Ghana, Nigeria and Kenya approved the vaccine before the recommendation, and the first large campaigns are expected in 2024 as supply expands. A vaccine that exists in a warehouse and a vaccine that reaches a child in a village are different things, and the gap between them is the part that the announcement leaves to health systems that are already stretched. A dose that costs a few dollars still has to be kept cold, scheduled and recorded, and the clinics that need it sit at the end of the longest supply chains.
