---
title: "The border that decides who is rescued"
date: 2023-02-08 23:30:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Aid for the earthquake-hit areas of northwest Syria had to pass through a single crossing at Bab al-Hawa on 8 February 2023, and the pace of the convoys became the most contested question of the response."
---

Aid for the earthquake zone in northwest Syria moved through one crossing at Bab al-Hawa, on the border with Turkey, in the days after the quake. The crossing had been the only one authorized by the Security Council for United Nations convoys since 2020, and it was closed for part of the first day because the road on the Turkish side had been damaged. Four million people live in the area, most of them displaced at least once by the war. It was also the only route for fuel and for the generators that hospitals needed, which made the queue at the border a queue for diesel as much as for food.

The first United Nations convoy reached Idlib on 9 February, three days after the earthquake, and it carried shelters, blankets and food rather than the heavy rescue equipment that the local teams were asking for. The White Helmets, the volunteer rescue organization that operates in the opposition-held area, said it had fewer than a hundred working machines and no ability to move them between sites quickly. Volunteers dug with their hands and with farm equipment for the first week. Digging with hands and with farm machinery was what the first week amounted to, and the volunteers said so on the record.

Politics attached to the crossings rather than to the aid. Syria's government said it would allow deliveries through two additional crossings for three months, a decision that was welcomed by the United Nations and read by Western governments as a concession made under pressure. Russia, which had blocked earlier proposals to add crossings, agreed to a short extension of the existing one, and the Security Council renewed the mandate for six months in July rather than for a year.

Distribution inside Syria was contested as well. The government insisted that aid be coordinated through Damascus, and the opposition authorities in the northwest insisted that they be the ones to receive and distribute it. Each arrangement satisfied one side and was described by the other as a way of stealing the response, and the practical result was that convoys waited at the border while negotiations were conducted in capitals.

The earthquake had struck an area that was already the worst place in the world to be a patient. Hospitals had been bombed, doctors had left, and the health system ran on donations and on a handful of facilities that had been rebuilt more than once. The quake damaged or destroyed dozens of those facilities, including maternity hospitals, and the patients who survived the collapse were treated in car parks and in unfinished buildings.

Aid numbers published in the following weeks showed how much of the response went to the government side. Turkey received billions in pledges and in credit, and the cross-border operation received a fraction of that, because its mandate was temporary and its access was negotiated every six months. The disaster made the asymmetry visible, and the visibility did not change it. The asymmetry was visible in the pledges, and the pledges were the part that the next earthquake would also depend on.
