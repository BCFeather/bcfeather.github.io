---
title: "Spirit Airlines files for bankruptcy protection"
date: 2024-11-16 20:55:00 +0800
categories: [business]
description: "Spirit Airlines filed for Chapter 11 protection on 18 November after years of losses, a failed merger and the collapse of its plan to sell aircraft, and said it would keep flying while it restructured."
---

Spirit Airlines filed for protection from creditors under Chapter 11 of the bankruptcy code on the eighteenth of November, after a year in which it had lost money in every quarter and had failed to find a buyer. Filing was made in the southern district of New York and came with an agreement with a majority of creditors on a restructuring that will convert debt into equity and leave the airline with about 350 million dollars of new financing. Company said flights and bookings would continue as normal through the process.

Carrier had been in difficulty since the pandemic. Its model was built on filling aircraft with leisure travellers at low fares, and it was among the last American airlines to recover from the travel collapse of 2020. Pratt and Whitney engine problems grounded dozens of its aircraft for inspection from 2023, which removed capacity at the moment demand was strongest, and the compensation the airline negotiated did not cover the lost revenue. Costs rose as pilots and cabin crew negotiated new contracts, and the airline's unit costs, which had once been the lowest in the industry, climbed above those of its larger competitors.

Merger that might have saved it failed in January, when a federal judge blocked the acquisition by JetBlue on antitrust grounds. JetBlue had offered 3.8 billion dollars, and the decision left Spirit without a partner and without the capital the deal would have brought. Talks with Frontier resumed in the autumn, and the two airlines announced a combination in the same month as the engine problems were worsening, but the agreement came too late to prevent the filing and was structured as a merger of two weak carriers rather than a rescue.

Restructuring is expected to take months and to leave equity holders with little. Creditors will take control of the company, the fleet will shrink as leases are renegotiated, and the airline has said it will remove routes that do not cover their costs. Bankruptcy financing of the kind agreed in this case is unusual in the sector, and analysts said it buys time rather than a solution: the airline still has to find a way to earn a margin at fares that its competitors now offer as well.

Passengers are largely sheltered from the immediate effects. Tickets remain valid, frequent flyer balances are protected under the terms of the restructuring agreement, and the airline's route network continues to operate. Risk for travellers is that the airline's schedule shrinks, which reduces the low-fare capacity that has kept prices down on routes between secondary cities, and that a second filing is possible if demand weakens.

Wider significance lies in what the case says about the discount model in America. Spirit was the largest of the ultra-low-cost carriers and the one that copied the European template most faithfully, charging for every extra and running aircraft for more hours than its rivals. Model worked while fuel was cheap and labour was plentiful. Both changed, and the airline's costs rose faster than its fares. Bankruptcy of the airline is therefore a verdict on a business model as much as on one company, and the remaining carriers of its kind will be read the same way.
