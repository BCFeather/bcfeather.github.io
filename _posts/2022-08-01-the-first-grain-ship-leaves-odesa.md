---
title: "The first grain ship leaves Odesa with twenty-six thousand tonnes of maize"
date: 2022-08-01 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [economy]
description: "A freighter bound for Lebanon clears the port under the deal signed in Istanbul, and the corridor is expected to carry five million tonnes a month."
---

The Razoni left the port of Odesa on Monday morning with about twenty-six thousand tonnes of maize and a destination of Lebanon, and it was the first commercial vessel to sail from Ukraine since February. The ship passed the minefields through a channel that the Ukrainian navy had cleared, and it was escorted to the edge of Ukrainian waters before heading south. Inspectors from Russia, Ukraine, Turkey and the United Nations were waiting at the Bosphorus to search it. The cargo is small by the standards of the trade and large by the standards of the last five months.

The deal that made the sailing possible was signed in Istanbul on 22 July, and it established three corridors from Odesa, Chornomorsk and Yuzhne. The agreement set up a joint coordination centre in Istanbul, staffed by the four parties, and it promised that ships would be inspected on the way in and on the way out. It also promised that the ports would not be attacked and that the ships would not be targeted. Both of those promises have been tested since, and both have held well enough for the trade to restart.

Ukraine's harvest is the reason the corridor matters. The country had about twenty million tonnes of grain in its silos when the war began, and the harvest that followed added more. The silos were full, the trucks were queuing at the western border and the alternative routes through Poland and Romania could carry a fraction of the volume. The food programme of the United Nations had warned that the price of wheat would rise further if the ports stayed closed, and the countries that depend on Ukrainian grain are mostly in North Africa and Asia.

Prices fell on the news, as they had fallen on the news of the agreement. Wheat had reached a record in March and had come down since, and the corridor added a reason to expect more supply. The fall is welcome in the importing countries and unwelcome for the farmers in Ukraine, who have to sell at the price that the market now offers. The costs of the war are being paid twice: once in the fighting and once in the price of the crop.

At the same time, the risks are visible in the insurance market. The premiums for a voyage to Odesa are several times the normal rate, and the shipowners who accept them are the ones with older vessels and with contracts that cover the risk. The crews are mostly Ukrainian and Russian, and the question of who is responsible if a ship is hit has been left to the lawyers. The coordination centre has said it will publish the movements of the ships, which reduces the chance of a mistake and increases the chance of a target.

What the corridor does is turn a blockade into a negotiation. The grain is leaving, the silos are emptying and the price is falling, and all of that depends on an agreement that either side can leave. The first ship is a small cargo, and the test is whether the hundredth one sails with the same ease. The answer will be known in weeks, and the harvest that has to be stored in the meantime will decide how much patience Ukraine has.
