---
title: "The security conference in Munich meets without the Russians"
date: 2022-02-18 23:45:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "For the first time since the Cold War no senior Russian official attends, and the Ukrainian president uses the stage to ask for sanctions before an attack rather than after it."
---

The Munich Security Conference opened on 18 February with no senior Russian officials in the hall, which the organizers said was the first time since the end of the Cold War. The annual meeting brings heads of state, ministers and defence officials to a hotel in Bavaria for three days of speeches and private meetings, and this year the subject was the Russian army on the Ukrainian border. Delegations from about a hundred countries came, and the Russian government sent no one.

President Zelensky of Ukraine came in person and spoke on 19 February. He asked the governments in the room to impose sanctions on Russia before an attack rather than after one, and he said that the security assurances his country had received in the 1990s had come to nothing. He also asked whether the alliance intended to admit Ukraine, and he said that a promise repeated at summits without a decision was a way of encouraging the government in Moscow.

American intelligence came with the delegation. Vice-President Harris told the conference that the United States had evidence of a plan to attack, and that the government in Moscow was looking for a pretext. President Biden said at the White House the same day that he was convinced the Russian president had decided to invade, and that the diplomacy had a short window left. The British prime minister said the intelligence was among the most credible his government had seen.

The Chinese foreign minister attended and said his country's position was that the sovereignty and territorial integrity of every state should be respected, and that the security of one country could not be bought at the expense of another's. The statement was read in Western capitals as an oblique criticism of NATO's expansion and, more quietly, as a warning to Moscow. China had signed a friendship treaty with Russia two weeks earlier and had said nothing about the troops on the border, and the reading in Western capitals was that the treaty set a limit to how far the criticism would go.

Scholz, the German chancellor, spoke on the last day and was criticized in the hall for a policy that had sent helmets rather than weapons and had kept the gas pipeline open. Ten days later, in the Bundestag, he would announce a fund of 100 billion euros for the army and a reversal of the policy that had governed German arms exports for decades. The conference in Munich was the last occasion on which that old position could be stated without embarrassment.

What the meeting is worth lies in putting the people who make decisions in the same building. It rarely produces an agreement, and this year it produced a set of warnings that were accurate about the timing and useless about the response, and a photograph of a hall in which the absent delegation was the subject of every speech. What the delegations took home from Bavaria was a clearer sense that the argument over Ukraine had become an argument about the alliance itself.
