---
title: "Estonia keeps its government and loses some ground"
date: 2023-03-05 23:40:00 +0800
categories: [news]
description: "Estonia's Reform Party won 37 of the 101 seats in the parliamentary election on 5 March 2023, and Kaja Kallas stayed on as prime minister, while the far-right party gained seats and the Centre Party lost ground."
---

Estonians voted on 5 March, and the result gave the Reform Party thirty-seven seats in a parliament of a hundred and one, which was the largest single bloc and enough to lead a coalition. Kaja Kallas, who had been prime minister since 2021, described the result as a mandate to continue, and she began talks with the Social Democrats and with a smaller party within a week. Turnout was 63.1 per cent, which was high by the standards of the previous two elections. The result was declared the same night, and the turnout was the highest the country had recorded since 2011.

The campaign had been dominated by two subjects that lay outside Estonia. The first was the war in Ukraine, where Estonia had been one of the most forward-leaning members of the alliance in proportion to its size, and the second was the cost of living, which had risen with energy prices across the Baltic states. Kallas had become one of the most visible European leaders of the year, and the visibility was both an asset and an argument for her opponents.

Having governed Estonia for most of the previous two decades, the Centre Party lost nearly a third of its seats and fell to third place. Its leader had been the mayor of Tallinn and had built the party around a rural electorate that had been shrinking, and the result was described by its own members as the end of a generation. The party's decline left the Reform Party as the only large liberal force in the parliament.

Known as the country's far-right grouping, the Conservative People's Party gained four seats and reached seventeen, which made it the second largest party in the chamber. Its support had grown among voters who were angry about energy prices and about the pace of the green transition, and its leader had spent the campaign arguing that the country's parties had converged on a single set of opinions. The party stayed outside the coalition, and its size made it the permanent opposition.

The coalition talks were about the budget rather than about ideology. The new government would have to raise taxes or cut spending to keep the deficit within the euro area's rules, and the two likely partners disagreed about which of those to do. The agreement was signed in April, and it committed the government to a defence spending increase and to a windfall levy on the energy companies that had profited from the price spike. The two parties also disagreed about the defence budget, which both wanted to raise and neither wanted to cut elsewhere.

Estonia's election was one of several in the region that were read as a test of the liberal consensus. The result kept the government in office and gave the far right more seats, which was the pattern that the year produced in Finland, in Slovakia and in the Netherlands. The pattern did not amount to a wave, and the parties that won it were different from each other, and the commentary treated it as one anyway.
