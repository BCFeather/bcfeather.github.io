Israel rescues two hostages in Rafah as strikes kill dozens

Israeli special forces freed two elderly hostages from a building in Rafah before dawn on 12 February, and the air strikes that covered the raid killed several dozen Palestinians, according to the health ministry in the territory.

Posted 14 Feb 2024
By Caozhe Bao
3 min read

Israeli special forces freed two hostages from a building in Rafah before dawn on the twelfth of February, in an operation covered by air strikes that left dozens of Palestinians dead. Both men, aged sixty and seventy, had been held since the attack of the seventh of October and were the first hostages rescued alive since a week-long truce in November. Military officials said the raid had been planned for weeks and that the strikes were aimed at the buildings around the target. Health ministry in Gaza said the bombing killed more than seventy people in the same district, and the figures could not be independently verified.

Operation was notable for what it revealed about the remaining hostages. Of the roughly two hundred and forty taken on the seventh of October, about a hundred and thirty remained in the territory at the start of the year, and the Israeli government believes several dozen of them are dead. Two men freed in Rafah had been held in a residential block in the middle of a district crowded with displaced people, which is where most of the remaining hostages are thought to be. This is why the raid was mounted by special forces rather than by a conventional unit, and why it required weeks of intelligence work.

Timing mattered politically. Negotiations over a second truce and the release of hostages had been going on for weeks, mediated by Qatar and Egypt with American involvement, and the Israeli prime minister had rejected the terms on offer as insufficient. Rescue gave him a result to point to and strengthened the argument that military pressure produces releases. It also gave the families of the hostages a reason to demand more operations of the same kind, an argument that runs against the negotiating track rather than alongside it.

Rafah itself is the larger question. City at the southern end of the strip had become the last place people could go, and by February it held more than a million displaced Palestinians, most of them in tents and shelters, pressed against the Egyptian border. Israeli government had said it intended to extend its ground operation into Rafah, and its allies had said repeatedly that this could not happen without a plan to move the civilians. The raid in the middle of the city demonstrated what such an operation would look like at a small scale, and the casualties that accompanied it were the argument against doing it at a large one.

Diplomatic track had not moved by the end of the month. Talks in Cairo and Doha continued without agreement, and the Israeli government described the terms on the table as delusional while the Hamas leadership described them as a starting point. Two hostages went home to their families, the district they were taken from was largely destroyed, and both of those facts became part of the argument each side made about the other.

Military campaign continued elsewhere in the meantime. Israeli forces pressed their operations in Khan Younis, where the hospitals had already stopped functioning weeks earlier, and the number of people killed in the territory since October passed thirty thousand, according to the health ministry, which does not distinguish between civilians and combatants. Aid agencies said the delivery of food and medicine through the southern crossings had become unreliable. Conditions in the north of the strip were reported to be approaching famine, and the agencies said the convoys that did get through were being held up by inspections.
