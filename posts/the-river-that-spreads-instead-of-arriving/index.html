The river that spreads instead of arriving

The Okavango ends in a fan of sand, and the flood that feeds it arrives in the dry season from rain that fell a thousand kilometres upstream.

Posted 5 Dec 2024
By Caozhe Bao
4 min read

A river that ends in a delta is usually reaching the sea, and the Okavango ends in a fan of sand in the middle of a dry country. The water comes off the highlands of Angola, runs south-east across Namibia, and then spreads into the Kalahari where the ground is flat and the sand absorbs what it can. The flood arrives in the dry season of the country it floods, because the rain that feeds it fell months earlier and a thousand kilometres upstream, and the timing is the reason the delta is a wetland in a desert. The catchment begins in a plateau that receives more rain in a wet year than the delta can absorb, and the surplus is stored in the sand rather than lost.

Channels move across the fan, and the shape is a set of them shifting. The main channel silts up, the water finds a lower line through the papyrus, and the vegetation that grows in the old channel dies as the new one opens. The movement is slow enough to be mapped in a lifetime and fast enough to defeat a lease that assumes a fixed shoreline. The channels also end in lagoons that fill and dry, and the island that a lodge was built on in one decade is a peninsula in the next. A channel that has been abandoned is colonized by papyrus within a season, and the papyrus is what makes the next channel hard to find from the air.

The flood is the event that the animals follow. Elephants move along the edge of the water as it advances, and the herds that come to the delta in the dry season are there because the water is there. The fish that hatch in the flood spread into the channels and the birds that nest in the reed beds arrive on the same schedule. The whole system is a pulse, and a pulse that arrives at the wrong time is a system that fails even when the volume is right. A flood that arrives early meets a herd that has not moved, and the calves are born into water that is too deep for them.

Water is allocated upstream. The river crosses Namibia and the abstraction is governed by a treaty that was signed when the countries had different plans, and the irrigation schemes that were proposed in Angola and Namibia would take water before it reached the fan. A delta that receives less water does not simply shrink; it changes its shape, and the channels that stay wet are the ones that were already the deepest. The allocation is a number in a treaty and the effect is a map that changes every year. A treaty that was signed before the schemes were designed is a treaty that does not bind the schemes, and the water is used anyway.

People live on the islands and the edges. The communities that have been in the delta for centuries fish, keep cattle on the flood plain in the dry season, and harvest reeds and thatch. The tourism that brings the money into the country is built on the same water, and the camps that are allowed in the delta are limited by a quota that was set when the flood was better understood than it is now. A change in the flood is a change in an economy that has no other river. A camp that is built on a channel that has moved is a camp that has to be rebuilt, and the rebuilding is part of the operating cost.

Measurement is done from the air and from the water. The flood front is mapped by satellite, the discharge is gauged at the entry point, and the groundwater is monitored in boreholes that were drilled for the purpose. The numbers show a flood that varies by a factor of several between years, and a variation that large is hard to separate from a trend over the decades of record. The management has to act on a signal that is smaller than the noise, which is the condition that every water authority in a dry country lives with. A flood that varies by a factor of three is a flood that cannot be predicted from the rain in the same year.

What the delta teaches is that a wetland can be the end of a river and the centre of a region. The water that arrives is the whole budget of the system, and every use upstream is a use that the flood plain does not get. The fan of sand and papyrus is not a place where the river stops being a river; it is the place where the river is spread thin enough to support everything that lives on it. A delta that is managed as a wetland and not as a river mouth is a delta that is managed as a system, and that is the shift that the last two decades have made.
