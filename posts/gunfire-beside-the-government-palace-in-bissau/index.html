Gunmen attack the government palace in Bissau and eleven people are killed

Heavy fire lasts more than five hours around the building where the president and his cabinet are meeting, and the government later blames the drug trade.

Posted 2 Feb 2022
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

Gunfire broke out beside the government palace in Bissau just after lunch on 1 February, and for more than five hours the president, the prime minister and the cabinet stayed inside the building while soldiers fought the attackers in the street. Cars carrying armed men drove through the district around the airport road, and residents who had been at work in the centre walked home by back streets. The shooting stopped in the evening, and the government said the attempt had failed.

Eleven people died in the assault, the tourism minister and government spokesman Fernando Vaz said the next day. Four of them were civilians, among whom were a senior official of the agriculture ministry and his driver, and the rest were soldiers and paramilitaries who had defended the compound. Several of the attackers were arrested, and the army set up a commission to establish who had sent them.

President Umaro Sissoco Embalo, elected in 2019 and in office for two years, said the men had come to kill him and the whole cabinet rather than to seize power for a faction of the army. He linked the raid to the campaign his government had begun against the trafficking of cocaine that passes through the country on its way from Latin America to Europe, and he promised to continue it. The presidency described the attack as an isolated act.

History here gives the claim some force. Guinea-Bissau has lived through four coups since independence from Portugal in 1974, the last of them in 2012, and a dozen attempts besides, and only one elected president has ever finished a full term. The armed forces have been part of the political argument for as long as the state has existed, and the drug trade has bought protection from officers for thirty years.

Across the region the pattern has become ordinary again. Soldiers took power twice in Mali since August 2020, once in Guinea in September 2021 and once in Burkina Faso in January, and the leaders of the Economic Community of West African States met in Accra on 3 February to decide what to do about the newest member of that list. At the summit the commission's president said the bloc would send a stabilisation force to Bissau, and the mandate is to keep a seizure of power by force from happening a second time.

What the force can do is limited in a country of two million people where the state raises most of its money from cashew nuts and where the army has its own economy. The eleven who died on 1 February included men from both sides of a fight that lasted an afternoon, and the commission of inquiry will report to a government that has already named the enemy. Outside the palace, the shops reopened on the second day and the traffic moved again, and foreign diplomats in Bissau said they had received no warning of the raid. The question of who armed the attackers went unanswered, and the men who were arrested gave statements that the government has not published.
