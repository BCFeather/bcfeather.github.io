The consumer day and the complaint that gets televised

The fifteenth of March is the day for consumer complaints, and the evening programme that broadcasts the worst of them has become an institution. The companies it names are punished within hours, and the question is what happens to the ones it does not name.

Posted 15 Mar 2025
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

Consumer day falls on the fifteenth of March, and the way it is marked in the country is a television programme broadcast in the evening that investigates a handful of businesses and names them. The format has been running since the early 1990s, the audience is large, and the effect is immediate. A company named at eight in the evening will issue an apology before midnight, a regulator will open a case within a day, and the shares of a listed offender will fall at the opening.

An unusual mechanism is behind the programme, and it is worth describing. A consumer complaint normally travels through a hotline, an arbitration, a court, and the cost of the journey is high relative to the value of the dispute, which is why most complaints are abandoned. The programme compresses the journey into a television segment, and the enforcement that would otherwise take months arrives in hours because the reputational cost is concentrated. It is a workaround for a slow system, and it works because the system is slow.

What the programme covers has changed with the economy. The early editions were about faulty goods, a broken appliance, an adulterated food. The recent ones are about services and platforms, a prepaid card for a gym that closes, a training course that sells a package and delivers a fraction, a delivery platform that charges a fee it does not disclose, a livestream that sells a gemstone of unclear origin. The disputes have moved from the object to the contract, which is harder to photograph and easier to hide.

The prepaid card is the case that recurs. A customer pays for a year of a service in advance, the business collects the cash, and the obligation sits on the balance sheet of a small company with no reserve. When the company closes, the customer is an unsecured creditor and recovers nothing. Several cities have introduced rules requiring a portion of the prepayment to be held in a supervised account, and the rules are enforced unevenly because the businesses they cover are numerous and small, and a city with a thousand gyms cannot inspect a thousand accounts.

Infrastructure is the part of the day that is not televised. The hotlines, the mediation centres, the online dispute platforms and the class action that a consumer association can bring have all been built up over two decades, and the volume they handle is enormous and mostly invisible. A complaint that is resolved by a phone call in three days is a success that produces no broadcast, and the broadcast is what the public remembers, which is why the programme gets more attention than the caseload behind it.

What the fifteenth of March measures, in the end, is the distance between a system that works and a system that is seen to work. The programme closes some of that distance every year, and the distance it does not close is the reason the programme exists, and the reason the hotline still rings through the year.
