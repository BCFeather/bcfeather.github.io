Spring's long, heavy afternoons

In mid-April the afternoons turn soft and heavy, and concentration becomes a small daily negotiation. A short essay on spring drowsiness and the body's quiet refusal to be fully on duty.

Posted 17 Apr 2026
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

There is a particular hour that arrives in mid-April and stays, for a few weeks, through the late afternoons. It is not tiredness after a bad night. It is a slow, warm heaviness that settles over the city around three or four o'clock, the light turning gold, the air turning still, and the mind, without any particular reason, slipping off its desk. People call it spring drowsiness, and every spring, a little after the Qingming rains, it comes back.

It is easy to treat it as a failure. A paragraph is read and re-read. The pen slows. The eyes drift to the window, where the new leaves are moving in a breeze that is too warm to work. In an office of thirty people, half of them are, at four in the afternoon, resting a cheek on a hand or staring at a screen that they have, in fact, stopped reading. The body is asking, quietly, for a pause, and the schedule, built for a more demanding season, will not allow it.

The curious thing is how universal it is. It is not the fault of a late night or a heavy lunch, though those make it worse. It comes with the season itself, with the lengthening light and the sudden softness of the air. The body, which has lived through a winter of bundled-up alertness, seems to interpret the warmth as a signal that the year has opened into a slower chapter, and it adjusts its expectations accordingly. The mind wants to walk. It wants to sit in a park. It wants, most of all, to stop negotiating with the screen.

What the season asks of attention is different from what the rest of the year asks. In the summer, concentration is defended against heat and noise. In the winter, against cold and darkness. In the spring, the fight is against a kind of pleasantness. The world outside is newly green and genuinely pleasant, and the body notices, and would rather be out in it than indoors. The drowsiness is not, underneath, a biological defect. It is a longing, expressed as a yawn.

The adults who handle it best do not fight it. They give in to it for ten minutes: a walk around the block, a cup of tea standing by the window, a pause that the schedule grudgingly permits. They have learned that the afternoon's heaviness is not an enemy to be defeated but a tide to be let pass, and that trying to force focus through it only makes the rest of the day worse. The cities of this season run on small concessions to the weather: an earlier break, a softer chair, a window left open to the warm air.

By evening the drowsiness lifts on its own, the way a fog burns off, and the city sharpens again for the night. For an hour or two each afternoon, though, the spring insists that people remember they are bodies as well as minds, and that a body in April would rather be elsewhere. It is a small, annual reminder, and resisting it has never once worked.
