The second of January and the gym that fills up

The first working day of the year is also the busiest day at a gym, and the rush that arrives in the first week of January is a business problem dressed as a personal one.

Posted 2 Jan 2025
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

January's second day is the first working day for most offices, and it is also the busiest week of the year at a gym. The memberships that were bought in December as gifts or as promises are activated in the first days of January, the classes fill up, the queue for a treadmill forms at seven in the evening, and by the middle of February the crowd has thinned to the people who were coming in November.

Gyms understand this pattern and prices for it. A gym that sells annual memberships collects the whole fee in January and knows that a predictable share of the buyers will stop attending by March. The economics of the industry rest on that share, and the number is usually quoted as somewhere between a half and two thirds of new members, which is why a gym can be crowded in one month and profitable in the next.

People who sign up are not making a mistake that a leaflet can correct. A resolution is a decision made at a moment when the year looks like a blank page, and the gym is the place where the decision is easiest to buy. What the first week of January sells is the version of the year in which the resolution holds, and the equipment is the physical form of that promise.

Class schedules tell the same story in another way. The spinning and the body pump fill first, because they are booked and they have a start time, and a person who has booked a place is more likely to arrive than a person who intends to go. The open floor is quieter for the first weeks, because the members who have not booked anything are the ones who drift.

What the gyms have learned is that the supply has to be sized for January and paid for in July. A studio with enough bikes for the first week is a studio that is half empty in June, and the studios that survive the cycle are the ones that sell class packs rather than annual passes, so the demand can be matched to the room.

Resolutions themselves have a longer history than the fitness industry. The new year is the one moment in the calendar when a change of habit is socially sanctioned, and the gym is only the most visible place where that change is attempted. The same week produces the enrolments in the language schools and the applications for the evening degrees, and the same pattern of attendance follows.

For the person who starts on the second of January, the useful advice is the one that the trainers give and the members ignore. The first month should be two sessions a week rather than five, the muscle needs time to adapt, and a plan that survives February is worth more than a plan that impresses the first week. The gym will still be there in April, and so will the resolution if it was made small enough to keep.
