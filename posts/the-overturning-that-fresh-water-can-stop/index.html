The overturning that fresh water can stop

The Atlantic circulation sinks because surface water is heavy enough, and the fresh water arriving at high latitudes works against exactly that.

Posted 25 Nov 2024
By Caozhe Bao
5 min read

Atlantic overturning is a description of water moving because of what it weighs. Warm salty water at the surface of the tropics is carried north by the wind, cools on the way, and sinks in the regions around Greenland and south of Iceland where the density of the surface water exceeds the density of what lies beneath. The return flow runs along the western boundary of the basin, through the deep Atlantic, and comes back to the tropics before rising. The whole circuit transports about a million cubic metres per second of water at its strongest, and it carries heat northward, which is why north-western Europe is warmer than its latitude suggests. The transport is measured in Sverdrups, a unit that hides how much water is involved, and the heat it carries is a sizeable fraction of the total that the northern hemisphere receives. A Sverdrup is a million cubic metres per second, and the heat carried by that flow is measured in terawatts.

What could stop it is fresh water. A surface layer that is too fresh is too light to sink, and the sinking is the engine, so any increase in the freshwater input at high latitudes works against the circulation. Greenland's ice sheet has been losing mass, Arctic sea ice has been retreating, and precipitation over the subpolar North Atlantic has increased, all of which add fresh water to the same region. The circulation also has a positive feedback that helps it resist: when it slows, less heat is carried north, the surface cools, and cooling makes the water denser and helps it sink again. That feedback is why the system has been stable through the glacial cycles instead of flickering. The freshwater forcing is small in absolute terms, a few thousand cubic kilometres a year against an ocean of a billion, and the sensitivity of the circulation to it is what makes a small number matter.

That feedback has a limit, and models that push the freshwater forcing far enough find a second state. In the collapsed state the sinking stops, the deep water is not renewed, and the heat transport goes to zero. The transition is abrupt in the models, and the models disagree about where the threshold lies, which is the same as saying that they disagree about how close the present state is to it. A system with two stable states and a noisy forcing can also flicker before it changes, and the early-warning arguments look for that flicker in the observational record. The two-state picture comes from the box models that were built in the 1990s, and the general circulation models that came after it have reproduced the transition in some configurations and not in others. The box models are crude, and their value is that they show the feedback structure rather than the geography of a real ocean.

The observations are short and indirect. The RAPID array has been measuring the transport across 26.5 degrees north since 2004, and its twenty years of data show variability that is dominated by the wind and by the seasonal cycle instead of by a trend. The array measures a single line, and a line cannot distinguish a slowing from a shift in the path, so the inference is always made with models that fill in the rest of the basin. Proxy records from sediments, corals and ice cores extend the record backwards, and their resolution is decades and not years. A trend in a twenty-year record is hard to separate from the variability of a system that has modes on the scale of a decade, and the statistical arguments about early warning have been applied to the record with mixed results. The array has been maintained through storms and through the loss of instruments, and the continuity of the record is an achievement in itself.

The cold blob is the visible part of the puzzle. The subpolar gyre south-east of Greenland has cooled over the past century while the rest of the planet warmed, and that pattern is consistent with a weaker overturning, since a slower circulation carries less heat north. It is also consistent with the wind changing, and with the freshwater anomaly sitting on the surface and insulating the ocean from the atmosphere. A single pattern cannot separate those explanations, and the papers that argue for a weakening use several lines of evidence at once. The cooling is largest in the region where the deep water forms, which is exactly where a slowdown would be expected to show, and the pattern has persisted through several decades of warming elsewhere.

Europe's climate is the consequence that gets attention. A collapse would cool the north-eastern Atlantic by several degrees, shift the storm track south, and change rainfall across the Sahel and the Amazon, because the monsoon systems are tied to the position of the tropical rain belt that the circulation helps to hold. Sea level along the eastern seaboard of North America would rise faster, because a weaker Gulf Stream raises the water on the coast through dynamics and not through added volume. None of those effects is speculative in mechanism, and all are uncertain in size. The rainfall consequences are the ones that matter for the people living in the regions, and the models that predict them disagree by more than the size of the signal.

Policy has to decide what to do with a system whose threshold is unknown. Cutting emissions reduces the freshwater forcing from Greenland in the long run and does nothing in the next decade, and no engineering scheme has been shown to work at the scale of an ocean basin. The scientific question is whether the threshold can be measured rather than modelled, and the answer so far is that the observing system is too young and too sparse to settle it. A circulation that has been stable for millennia is being pushed by a forcing that has no precedent, and the honest statement is that the outcome depends on a number nobody can yet measure. The alternative to waiting is to build the observing system that could answer the question, and the cost of that is small compared with the cost of being surprised. A decision made now on the assumption that the circulation is stable is a decision made on a number that has not been measured.
