Norris wins Mexico by 30 seconds as the field spreads out

The FIA classification of the Mexico City Grand Prix records Norris from pole by 30.324 seconds, with Verstappen third from fifth and Bearman fourth from tenth.

Posted 27 Oct 2025
By Caozhe Bao
3 min read

Mexico City sits at 2,240 metres, where the air is thin enough that wings produce less downforce and engines lose power, and the circuit's long straight rewards a car that can run low drag without losing the corners. Norris took pole there on 25 October with a lap of 1 minute 15.586 seconds, an average of 204.990 km/h, and the race a day later asked whether that starting position could survive 71 laps on a surface that offers little grip. Thin air changes the tyres too, since less downforce means more sliding in slow corners.

The stewards' final classification, published on 26 October 2025, records 71 laps over 305.354 km. Norris won in 1 hour 37 minutes 58.574 seconds, an average of 186.996 km/h, with Leclerc second at 30.324 seconds and Verstappen third at 31.049. Norris led every lap he completed, and the gaps behind him opened at a rate that made the second half a procession. Leclerc and Verstappen traded the remaining podium places for much of the distance and finished 0.725 seconds apart.

The margins are the story. Thirty seconds over a distance that takes an hour and a half means the leader was gaining roughly four tenths per lap, which on a track where overtaking needs a slipstream is close to uncontested. Verstappen started fifth and finished third, and Bearman started tenth and finished fourth, 40.955 seconds down, so the midfield moved forward while the front ran away. Bearman's gain of six places is the largest in the top ten, and it came at a circuit whose first sector is slow enough for a car with a good front end to pass.

Strategy explains part of the spread. A one-stop race around this circuit is the default, and a car that stops early trades track position for clear air, which is why a driver starting tenth can end fourth while a faster car stuck in a train loses time it cannot recover. Three retirements removed cars from that train at different points: Lawson on lap 5, Hulkenberg on lap 25, Alonso on lap 34, and Sainz stopped on lap 67. Four retirements in all is high for a race that ran without a red flag.

Retention of position is not the same as pace, and the classification cannot separate the two. Russell set the fastest lap of the race at 1 minute 20.052 seconds on lap 50, an average of 193.554 km/h, and finished seventh, which is what fresh tyres and a late stop produce for a car out of contention. Sector times and pit lane data are published elsewhere. A driver out of contention can also take a free stop for the fastest lap, which is why that award and the podium diverge where pit loss is small.

Reading the next round means looking at the same quantities: the pole lap against the field's spread, the gap at the flag, and the fastest lap set against the finishing position. A circuit at altitude also tests cooling and braking in ways a sea-level track does not, so the reliability record from this race is worth carrying forward rather than treating as a one-off. Championship arithmetic turns on consistency rather than wins, and a 30-second margin counts the same as a one-second margin in the table.
