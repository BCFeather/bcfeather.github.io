Bottlebrush strands stretch fortyfold without softening

Foldable bottlebrush polymers separate the modulus of an elastomer from its breaking strain, holding stiffness at 30 kPa while stretch rises to 800 per cent.

Posted 17 Apr 2024
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

Crosslinked rubber has carried a rule since Goodyear: a network that is stiffer is also less stretchable. The trade-off is molecular, since both properties come from the same strands, and a network with strands long enough to stretch far has few of them, which leaves it soft. The rule follows from how a network stores length: a strand stretched to its contour length stops being elastic, so a long strand is one that has to be sparsely crosslinked.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 14 April 2024 changes the strand. Instead of a linear polymer, the network is built from foldable bottlebrush polymers, a collapsed backbone carrying many linear side chains. Elongation unfolds the backbone and releases length stored in the collapse, while the side chains, not the backbone, set the modulus of the whole network.

Bottlebrush polymers have been used before to make soft networks, because grafted side chains dilute entanglements and lower the modulus. The move here is to collapse the backbone as well, so the strand carries a reservoir of length that opens under load. The two effects are usually bought together, and this architecture buys them separately, which is the point of the design and the reason the paper exists.

A series of unentangled single-network elastomers bears the claim out. Young's modulus stays near 30 kPa across the series while the tensile breaking strain rises from 20 per cent to 800 per cent, a fortyfold gain in stretch at constant stiffness. Entanglements are the other source of stiffness in a polymer network, and leaving them out isolates the architecture from everything else that could explain the result.

Two lengths do separate jobs. The modulus follows the mass of the network strand and is set by the side chains, which do not unfold under strain. The extensibility follows the stored length in the collapsed backbone, which does. Decoupling the two is what the architecture buys, and the authors report that the strategy carries over to other polymer species and to other network topologies, which is a claim about the strategy and not about one chemistry.

The numbers are for unentangled single networks, a clean case chosen so that entanglements do not confound the argument. Whether the decoupling survives in a filled rubber, in a network crosslinked twice, or after thousands of cycles is not shown, and fatigue is the usual way a soft network dies, while toughness, a different quantity from extensibility, is not measured.

What the result opens is a design space more than a material. Soft robots, stretchable electronics and tissue scaffolds all want a body that holds its shape at low strain and takes a large strain without stiffening, and each has bought that from a different compromise. A strand that stores length and a side chain that sets stiffness can be tuned apart, which is the separation that turns a curiosity into a parameter.
