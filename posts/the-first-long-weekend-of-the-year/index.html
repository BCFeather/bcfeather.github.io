First long weekend of the year

The Labour Day holiday gives the country five days off in a row, and the way people spend them says more about the year than the calendar does. A short essay on queues, hotels and the peculiar arithmetic of a break that everyone takes at once.

Posted 2 May 2025
By Caozhe Bao
3 min read

The first long weekend of the year is not really a weekend. It is five days taken together, stitched out of a statutory holiday and two borrowed working days, and the country spends them in a way that is entirely predictable and entirely its own. Trains sell out on the first day of ticket sales. Hotels near the coast triple their rates and fill anyway. The motorways out of every large city become car parks for six hours on the morning of the first day, and then empty, and then fill again on the last.

What is interesting is not the volume. It is the shape of the decision. For most of the year the calendar hands out one or two days at a time, and a person plans around them: a dinner, a visit, a lie-in. A five-day block is different in kind. It is long enough to leave, and leaving requires a destination, a booking, a budget, and a small negotiation with whoever else is coming. The moment a break becomes long enough to travel, it stops being rest and becomes a project.

The project has a logic of its own. People who live in the big cities go to the smaller ones, and to the mountains and the sea, because that is where the photographs are. People from the smaller ones come to the big cities, because that is where the shops and the museums are. The two streams cross somewhere in the middle of the country on the same day, and the trains carrying them are equally full in opposite directions. Nobody finds this strange, and it is not strange; it is simply what happens when forty million people make the same reasonable decision at the same time.

There is a quieter version of the holiday that does not appear in the travel numbers. Some people stay. They stay because the tickets were gone, or because the money is needed elsewhere, or because a five-day break in an empty city is its own kind of luxury. The streets near the office are deserted, the restaurants that serve lunch to workers close, and the parks fill with people who live within walking distance. For a few days the city belongs to whoever did not leave, and that is a rare thing in a place that is usually owned by the timetable.

By the fifth day the arithmetic reverses. The return journey is always worse than the outbound one, because everyone returns at the same hour and nobody has any flexibility left. Bags are heavier, the children are tired, and the photographs have already been sorted into the ones worth posting and the ones that are not. The last evening of a long holiday has a particular flavour: the pleasure is over, the rest has not yet arrived, and tomorrow is a working day that everyone has been pretending not to think about.

Then it ends, and the year resumes. What the holiday actually does is not rest people, though it is sold as rest. It gives the first half of the year a marker, a thing to have done, a story to tell in the following weeks. The calendar in this country is generous with such markers, and the country uses them. Five days, one decision, and the summer begins.
