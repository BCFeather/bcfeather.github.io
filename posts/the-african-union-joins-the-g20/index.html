The African Union joins the G20

The African Union became a permanent member of the G20 at the summit in New Delhi on 9 September 2023, a change that gives fifty-five countries a seat at a table where they had only been guests.

Posted 10 Sep 2023
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

Narendra Modi opened the summit on 9 September by inviting the African Union to take the seat of a permanent member, and the group's chair, Azali Assoumani of the Comoros, sat with the other leaders for the rest of the meeting. The union represents fifty-five countries and more than a billion people, and it had attended previous summits as a guest. Its admission was the most concrete result of a gathering arranged around the idea of giving the developing world a larger voice.

The declaration ran to eighty-three paragraphs and avoided naming Russia for its war in Ukraine. Western members had wanted a sentence condemning the invasion, and the Indian hosts, who have refused to take sides, produced language about the human suffering caused by war and about the need for a just peace. Ukraine's president had wanted the summit to endorse his peace formula, and he did not get it. The compromise was accepted by all the members, which is what the hosts wanted more than a strong text, and the group's communique has no paragraph that any member refused to sign.

Economic agenda was familiar and unfinished. The group repeated its promise to raise a hundred billion dollars a year for climate finance in developing countries, a promise made in 2009 and never met, and it supported a proposal to lend the International Monetary Fund's special drawing rights to countries that need them. It also called for reform of the multilateral development banks, with more money, cheaper terms and faster approval, a subject on which the American Treasury secretary and the Indian finance minister have been making common ground.

The African Union's admission changes the arithmetic of a club that makes decisions by consensus. The group now includes the European Union and the African Union alongside twenty-one countries, and the African members will arrive with an agenda about debt, about the price of borrowing and about the terms on which their minerals are bought. The continent's leaders have argued for years that a body that discusses Africa's debt should include Africa's creditors and Africa's debtors in the same room, which is now the case.

Diplomacy around the summit was as interesting as the text. President Xi Jinping and President Joe Biden did not meet, though both were in the city, and the American president held talks with the Indian prime minister about technology and railways. Vladimir Putin stayed away for a second year, and the Russian foreign minister attended instead. The Indian hosts used the meeting to advertise a corridor linking India with the Middle East and Europe, which they have been negotiating with Saudi Arabia and the Gulf states, and to sign a memorandum with the European Union on that route.

A club of the world's largest economies has added a continental organization, and the language it agreed was weaker than some of its members wanted. The African Union now has a chair at the table, and what it does with it will be judged by whether the promises about debt and finance survive the next summit.
