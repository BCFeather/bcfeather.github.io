Oldest armoured dinosaur carried a tail weapon

A new specimen of Spicomellus afer from the Middle Jurassic of Morocco confirms ankylosaur armour and pushes tail weapons back by tens of millions of years.

Posted 31 Aug 2025
By Caozhe Bao
3 min read

Armoured dinosaurs are known mostly from the Cretaceous, when they diversified into the group with tail clubs that museum halls display. The Jurassic record is thin, and the earliest ankylosaurs have been identified from fragments: a few osteoderms, a vertebra, a jaw. Whether the armour and the weapons were present early, or evolved late in the group's history, has depended on those fragments. Museum displays made the club the signature of the group, and the fossil record has been read backwards from it.

A paper in Nature on 27 August 2025 reports a specimen that settles part of the question. The material comes from Middle Jurassic beds in Morocco and belongs to Spicomellus afer, a taxon named from isolated armour plates in 2021. The new specimen preserves enough of the skeleton, including vertebrae from the tail, to confirm that the animal is an ankylosaur and not one of the several other armoured lineages. The specimen came from the same formation as the original plates, which ties the new material to the named taxon and not to a second one.

The armour is the elaborate part. The dermal plates carry spines and ridges arranged in a pattern more complex than what later ankylosaurs show, and the authors describe them as display structures as much as protection. An animal that invests this heavily in armour is signalling something, and the paper leaves open whether the signal is to predators, to rivals or to both. Spines along the neck and shoulder are the sort of structure a predator has to get past before it reaches anything vital.

The tail is where the implication lies. The vertebrae at the end of the tail are fused into a handle-like structure, the arrangement that in later animals supports a club. If that reading is right, a tail weapon existed in the Middle Jurassic, tens of millions of years before the Cretaceous clubs that the group is known for. A handle is a structure that carries the load of a swinging tail, and its presence is evidence of the motion, not of the club.

Two readings compete. The handle may be the beginning of the weapon, in which case the club is a later elaboration of a device already present. Or the fused vertebrae served a display function and the weapon evolved independently later, which would make the Jurassic structure an example of the same anatomy used for a different purpose. The two readings differ in what they predict about the Cretaceous, since one implies a continuous lineage of the weapon and the other implies a gap.

Evidence is not complete. No club is preserved, the specimen is not articulated, and functional inference from a vertebral handle is an argument by analogy with Cretaceous animals. The authors also note that ankylosaur armour became simpler through the Cretaceous, which reverses the usual expectation that elaborate structures accumulate. A functional model would have to test the mass of the tail, the stiffness of the handle and the force of a predator's bite together, and none of the three is available from a partial skeleton.

A quarry with more of the skeleton, and a biomechanical model of what a tail with a handle could do, would turn the inference into a measurement. Until then the oldest armoured dinosaur is a case of a structure arriving before the function that made it famous. The reversal is interesting on its own, since armour that becomes simpler over time is not what a story about increasing defence predicts. Display, or a change in the predator, could explain it.
