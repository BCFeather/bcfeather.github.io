Claude and Alpöge claim the Riemann Hypothesis, and the proof exists only as code

A four-page note from an Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude settled the Riemann Hypothesis and its general form in six weeks, and offers 17 million lines of Lean in place of a paper that no reader, including its author, has yet worked through.

Posted 21 Nov 2026
By Caozhe Bao
4 min read

At 13:49 UTC on 20 November, a four-page note appeared on the personal website of Levent Alpöge, a number theorist employed by Anthropic. It is titled Remark, and its first line reads, 'I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis.' The note goes on to claim the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, which covers zeros of a whole family of Dirichlet L-functions, and to expect the Extended form with more time. Alpöge states that the arguments are formalized in Lean without assumptions. The files behind that claim are fourteen gzip volumes on the same site, and there is no paper.

Alpöge is 34. He took a degree at Harvard, wrote a Princeton doctorate in 2020 under Manjul Bhargava, held postdoctoral fellowships at Columbia and Harvard and a Junior Fellowship in the Harvard Society of Fellows before joining Anthropic as a member of technical staff. In July he and Claude produced a counterexample to the Jacobian conjecture, an 85-year-old problem in algebraic geometry, and Terence Tao accepted it within a day. In August he and Ralph Furman checked a Claude result that raised the proven share of zeta zeros on the critical line from 41.6 to 67.2 per cent, a partial result that Anthropic described at the time as unlikely to grow into a proof of the full hypothesis.

The method descends from Stepanov's argument for the Riemann Hypothesis over function fields, where an auxiliary function with a zero of high multiplicity carries the proof. Multiplicity has no natural counterpart over the integers; Alpöge compares the difficulty to differentiating a whole number. His route is to imitate multiplicity natively in the automorphic world, chaining theta lifts from one unitary group to the next so that each link contributes another order. Claude supplied the length of the chain, a length of about the square root of X for a von Mangoldt sum of length X, and he describes the moment it arrived as one that would make fellow mathematicians fall out of their chairs.

A timeline in the note, which Alpöge attributes to Claude, runs from 6 October to 16 November. The first entry is the Stepanov determinant, and the date is the same day OpenAI released 722 mathematical papers at once, among them a partial result that pushed the known zero-free region of the zeta function out to a real part of 7/8. Alpöge credits OpenAI's taste in that batch, singling out a quiet Siegel argument that he recognized as Stepanov without multiplicity. Between that first entry and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis sit twenty steps and 41 days, against 167 years since Riemann's remark in 1859.

Written out, the argument would run past 250,000 pages, a figure Alpöge gives with some amusement. He calls that document unreadable and unfit to post, so the Lean project stands in for it: 175,187 files and 17.37 million lines, about 1.4 gigabytes unpacked from 229 megabytes of archives. For scale, the Lean formalization of Fermat's Last Theorem completed in September runs to 13 million lines.

Nothing has been checked yet. Elliot Glazer, a mathematician who works with Lean, said he was compiling the archive and expected it to take several hours, and put the chance of a bug in Lean itself below one per cent. Thomas Bloom said the Lean community was going through it, and remarked that he could not decide which was stranger, a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis or the 250,000 pages it would take to write one out. A successful build would establish that the code is internally sound; it would not establish that the theorem at the top of the file is the Riemann Hypothesis as mathematicians state it. The Clay Mathematics Institute also asks for publication in a qualifying journal, two years of scrutiny and general acceptance before it considers its prize, and the record of claims is long: Louis de Branges in 2004, Michael Atiyah in 2018, both rejected.

The note arrived one day after Anthropic's shares began trading. In New York on 20 November the stock fell more than 11 per cent, passed 86.79 dollars by midday and later traded near 84.50, close to the 83 dollar offer price, as AI shares fell across the market. Alpöge addresses the timing directly. He says he finished on a Sunday night, messaged Dario Amodei with a hypothetical, and worked out with the company's leadership what he could announce; he calls the timing horrible, acknowledges that a cynical reading exists, and says it is false.

His own account of the result is unusually candid. He is presenting a complete argument, he writes, before having mastered a large neighbourhood of the mathematics around it, and parts of the timeline paraphrase Claude's summaries and may make no sense to a specialist. What he promises next is the ordinary work: to understand the argument, to simplify it, to write it properly and to teach it.

If the build holds and the statement checks out, the interesting question changes shape. A proof that machines can verify and no person can yet read would move the bottleneck in mathematics from checking to comprehension, and it would put a six-week collaboration against a century and a half of failed attempts. Alpöge's closing line is that the argument offers a glimpse of what is coming, and of the wonder to be found in the simplest things.
