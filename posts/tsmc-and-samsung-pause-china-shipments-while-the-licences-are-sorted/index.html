TSMC and Samsung pause China shipments while the licences are sorted

A US rule that took effect on 19 November closed the exemption that let foundries ship advanced logic to Chinese designers without a licence, and the two largest contract makers have stopped some orders while they apply.

Posted 21 Nov 2026
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

TSMC and Samsung Electronics have told some Chinese customers that shipments are on hold while they apply for US export licences, as reported. The pause follows an interim final rule from the Bureau of Industry and Security, issued on 16 November and published in the Federal Register on 19 November, which closed routes that foundries had used to send advanced logic chips to Chinese design houses without individual approval.

The rule is titled 'Addressing Advanced Semiconductor Remote Access', and it does three things. It restricts Chinese access to high-end computing rented remotely from data centres abroad. It rewrites the export checks that apply to foundries and assembly houses. And it rescinds the AI diffusion rule drafted under the previous administration, which had never taken effect. The practical change for the foundries is the removal of an exemption: chips for non-AI uses, smartphones included, no longer pass automatically when the customer sits in a restricted region.

TSMC has instructed customers whose designs use 16 nanometres or below to stop orders unless they already hold a US licence, and work at older nodes of 28 and 40 nanometres reportedly continues under conditions. TSMC supplies processors to Xiaomi, Oppo and Vivo, and the Taiwanese company's customer list also includes design houses that sell into data centres. Samsung's Chinese customers include ByteDance and Kunlun, the AI data-centre unit of Baidu. Both foundries rely on American equipment, which is what gives the US rules reach over factories in Hsinchu and Pyeongtaek.

Licensing is expected to be granted for consumer chips, according to people familiar with the process, though the queue matters as much as the answer. A phone processor that arrives three months late misses a launch window, and the approval process has no published timetable. The rule also builds a presumption that advanced logic is destined for data centres unless the exporter can show otherwise, which puts the burden of proof on the foundry and its customer.

Beijing objected before the rule took effect. The commerce ministry said on 17 November that the measure generalises national security, abuses export controls and interferes with ordinary trade between Chinese and foreign firms, and that it would take countermeasures if the United States persisted. The statement also argued the rule undercuts the understanding reached between the two governments earlier in the year.

For the industry the immediate question is inventory. Chinese handset makers build several months of stock ahead of a launch, and a short suspension can be absorbed; a suspension that lasts into the spring cannot. Wafer starts at the leading nodes are booked months in advance, so a licence delay at 16 nanometres propagates into packaging, testing and the shipment of finished devices.

The longer question is whether the chokepoint has moved. The earlier rounds of controls aimed at the most advanced nodes and at AI accelerators; this one reaches down to mature advanced logic and takes in consumer products, which is a wider net over the same factories. Whether that produces leverage or simply reroutes orders to older processes is the part that will show up in the shipment data over the next two quarters.
