The American economy slows to 1.6 per cent growth in the first quarter

The Commerce Department reported on 25 April that gross output grew at an annual rate of 1.6 per cent in the first quarter, well below the 2.4 per cent expected, while a measure of prices rose at 3.7 per cent and imports surged.

Posted 25 Apr 2024
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

Commerce Department reported on the twenty-fifth of April that the American economy grew at an annual rate of 1.6 per cent in the first quarter, down from 3.4 per cent in the last three months of 2023 and well below the 2.4 per cent that economists had expected. The figure was the weakest since the second quarter of 2022, and it was accompanied by a measure of inflation that rose faster than in the previous quarter.

Composition of the number explains most of the shortfall. Consumer spending grew at 2.5 per cent, which is solid, and business investment in equipment rose. The drags came from inventories, which subtracted from growth after a strong build at the end of last year, and from imports, which jumped by about 7.8 per cent. Government spending grew more slowly than in previous quarters, and residential investment rose.

Prices in the report moved in the wrong direction. The measure of core prices that the Federal Reserve watches rose at 3.7 per cent, up from 2.0 per cent in the previous quarter, and the services component was the main source. Economists cautioned that the quarterly figure is noisy and that the annual measure was still falling, and the markets reacted to the combination of slower growth and faster prices as though the worst of both had arrived.

Word that appeared in commentary that afternoon was stagflation, which is the term for an economy that slows while prices rise. Analysts said the situation was far from the 1970s, since unemployment was below four per cent and growth was positive, and they said the more accurate description was a bumpy disinflation. Several said the import surge reflected strong domestic demand rather than weakness.

Market reaction was a fall in stocks and a rise in bond yields. The ten-year Treasury yield rose above 4.7 per cent, its highest of the year, and traders pushed the expected date of the first rate cut from September to December. The dollar rose against most currencies, and gold reached a record.

The personal saving rate fell to 3.6 per cent from 4.0, which suggests that households were spending out of savings rather than out of income, and analysts said that could not continue. The report also showed that spending on goods rose faster than spending on services for the first time in a year. Business investment in structures fell, which analysts attributed to higher borrowing costs and to the completion of several large factories.

What the twenty-fifth of April means is a quarter that complicated the story the government had been telling. The administration had argued that the economy was strong and that inflation was falling, and the report showed growth that was slower than expected and prices that were not. One quarter does not make a trend, and the revisions that followed raised the growth figure to 1.4 and then to 1.3, which did not change the picture. What the report established was that the path to a soft landing would run through data that did not cooperate.
