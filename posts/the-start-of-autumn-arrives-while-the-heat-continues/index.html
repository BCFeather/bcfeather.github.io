Start of autumn arrives while the heat continues

The calendar says autumn begins on the seventh of August, and the thermometer disagrees for another three weeks. The gap between the two is the most reliable thing about this season, and it has its own traditions, its own diet and its own small disappointments.

Posted 7 Aug 2025
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

By the calendar, autumn begins on the seventh of August, and the thermometer disagrees for another three weeks. This is the annual arrangement of the northern Chinese summer, and it produces a strange interval in which the name of the season has changed and nothing else has. The cicadas are still going. The afternoon is still thirty-five degrees. The evening is still too warm for a blanket. And the calendar insists that the year has turned.

The old calendar was an agricultural instrument and it is more accurate than it looks, which is why the solar terms have survived into a country where almost nobody farms. The start of autumn in the solar terms marks the point at which the sun's declination has passed a particular value, and it corresponds roughly to the beginning of the harvest in the north. The nights do start to lengthen. The mornings acquire a coolness that lasts until about nine. A farmer who works at dawn knows the season has turned several weeks before a person in an office does, and the calendar is a way of telling the second person what the first already knows. It is also a reminder that the two live in the same weather and describe it differently.

The traditions attached to the date are mostly dietary, and they are about weight. The summer is when people lose appetite and lose weight, and the start of autumn is when the appetite returns, so the custom is to eat meat and to weigh yourself against the summer. In the south the same date is marked with a different set of foods, and in some places with nothing at all.

What the interval actually feels like is a set of small signals that accumulate, and each one is noticed by a different person at a different moment. The first evening when the air on a balcony is cooler than the room. The first morning when the sunlight through a window is at a different angle and wakes you earlier. The first day when the sky at seven in the evening is dimmer than it was a fortnight ago. None of these is a season. Together they are the only evidence a person has.

The heat in that interval is often worse than the heat in July, because the same temperature arrives with a humidity that has not yet broken, and the body has been worn down by two months of it. The hospitals notice. The electricity demand stays at its peak. The forecasters keep issuing the same warning, and the public keeps reading it with a fatigue that is itself a hazard.

Season, in this sense, is a name given to a change that has already begun and will not be complete for a month. That is the honest description of most transitions, and the calendar is useful exactly because it forces a person to notice one that would otherwise pass without comment. By the end of August the evenings will be genuinely cool, and the interval will have ended, and nobody will remember which day it stopped being summer.
