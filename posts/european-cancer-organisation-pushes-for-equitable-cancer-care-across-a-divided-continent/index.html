European Cancer Organisation pushes for fairer care across a divided continent

The European Cancer Organisation, a federation of clinicians and patient groups, is pressing for narrower gaps in cancer survival across Europe, where outcomes for common tumors can differ by more than 25 per cent between member states.

Posted 12 May 2023
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

Where a person lives in Europe still shapes the odds of surviving a cancer diagnosis, and a federation of clinicians, researchers and patient groups has made that gap the centre of its campaign. The body, European Cancer Organisation, known by its initials ECO, brings oncology societies and patient advocates from across the continent into one room and turns their evidence into recommendations for national governments and for the European Commission.

Cancer is the second leading cause of death in Europe, and roughly 40 per cent of cases are considered preventable. The burden is also unevenly distributed: eastern member states record higher mortality for several cancers that western systems treat well, and the difference holds after age and income are taken into account. Prevention has therefore not translated into equal outcomes. Screening uptake, waiting times for scans and access to newer therapies vary sharply between western, eastern and southern member states, and pooled data from the European Cancer Inequalities Registry puts the survival gap for colorectal and breast tumors at more than 25 per cent between the best and weakest performing systems.

ECO does not run hospitals. It coordinates cross-border working groups, drafts policy recommendations and amplifies patient voices in the design of Europe's Beating Cancer Plan, the commission's public health framework launched in 2021. Its remit covers the whole pathway: prevention, early detection, diagnosis, multidisciplinary treatment and long-term support for survivors and carers.

Some of its most useful work concerns parts of care that budgets tend to skip. Roundtables organized by the federation have flagged underfunded services such as nutrition support during treatment, management of infection risk in chemotherapy and rehabilitation after remission. National systems pay for drugs and surgery readily and pay for follow-up badly, which is where quality of life after treatment is decided. The federation also promotes the Cancer Survivor Smartcard, a digital record meant to ease access to insurance, employment protection and continued medical checks across borders.

Digital infrastructure is the second pillar. ECO backs federated imaging databases, shared genomic platforms and the EU Network of Comprehensive Cancer Centres, which is designed to spread multidisciplinary care to regions with few specialized units. Federated systems let clinicians query anonymized data without moving patient records between jurisdictions, which speeds up trials and lets smaller centres adopt methods proven at larger institutions.

Money and staff remain the binding constraint. National budget squeezes, workforce shortages and uneven use of EU4Health grants slow the work, and eastern European systems still report long waits for scans and limited access to immunotherapy. Patient advocates add a complaint that rarely appears in statistics: discrimination against survivors in hiring and in financial services, which persists after successful treatment. ECO will present updated recommendations before the European Week Against Cancer later this year, and the test it sets is concrete. A patient in a rural town should face the same standard of care as a patient in a capital, and a plan that cannot deliver that is a plan on paper, whatever its budget line says.
