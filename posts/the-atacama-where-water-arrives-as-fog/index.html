The Atacama, where water arrives as fog

Rain shadow on one side and a cold current on the other leave a plateau that receives its moisture as a drifting cloud, harvested on mesh screens and accounted for by the litre wherever mining wants it.

Posted 8 Oct 2024
By Caozhe Bao
5 min read

Two things that happen elsewhere keep the Atacama dry. The Andes rise to the east, and the moisture that the trade winds carry from the Atlantic is wrung out on the eastern slopes before the air descends into the interior, dry and warm. The Pacific to the west is cold, chilled by the Humboldt current that runs up the coast from the Southern Ocean, and cold water does not evaporate readily, so the air above it holds little water vapour. Between the two, the plateau receives almost no rain, and in some weather stations the record is a few millimetres in a decade. The plateau sits above 2,000 metres for most of its length, and the air that descends into it warms as it sinks, which lowers its relative humidity further. Rain that falls on the eastern Andes runs east in any case, towards the Atlantic, and none of it crosses the divide.

The moisture that does arrive comes as fog. A layer of cloud forms over the cold sea, is carried inland by the afternoon breeze, and advances over the coastal range as a soft, low bank that the Chileans call the camanchaca. It does not rain; it drifts, and it condenses on anything colder than itself. The fog is the only water many of the coastal slopes ever see, and evolution on those slopes has been shaped by the fact that water arrives as droplets on a surface rather than as rain from a cloud. The bank usually forms in the late afternoon and clears by mid-morning, and its depth is often no more than a few hundred metres. Where the bank meets a slope the droplets collect on the leaves and the ground beneath them, and the soil a metre down is dry.

Plants that live on fog have a recognisable form. The Tillandsia bromeliads take water through scales on their leaves instead of through roots, and they grow in mats on the hillsides where the fog passes, grey and closed, opening after the bank has lifted. The lomas, the fog-fed meadows of the coastal hills, hold a flora that exists nowhere else and that turns green for a few weeks after the fog season and then dries to a colour that looks like nothing alive. Cacti and small shrubs survive there on dew as well as fog, and their roots spread sideways instead of down. The mats are grey when closed and green when open, which is why the hillsides look like they are breathing after the fog lifts.

The fog has also been harvested. Fog catchers are sheets of mesh strung between posts, and the droplets that collect on the fibres run down into a trough and a pipe; a large screen can yield several litres per square metre on a good day, and the villages in the coastal hills that have no well use them for stock and for orchards. It is a small technology with a long history and a modest yield, and it works best in exactly the places where nothing else does. The screens are cheap and the upkeep is not, since the mesh has to be cleared of salt and dust and the pipes have to be kept from freezing at altitude. A screen of a hundred square metres in a good site yields enough for a household and its animals, and in a bad site it yields nothing. A mesh that is strung across the wind collects the droplets and lets them run down into a trough, and the yield depends on the mesh size and on the wind, both of which are measured before a village commits to the panels.

Inland, the desert's economy has been built on what the ground contains. Nitrate was mined in the nineteenth century from deposits that had accumulated over millions of years, and the oficinas that processed it stand abandoned in the pampa, their machinery rusted, their wooden houses without roofs. Copper came next, and the mines that produce it are the reason the region has roads and ports, and the reason its water is accounted for in a way that no other dry place in the world accounts for it. The abandoned towns are now visited for their cemeteries and their theatres, and the machinery that was too heavy to move still stands where it was switched off. The nitrate was sold to Europe as fertiliser and the towns were built for the workers, and both were finished by the 1930s.

Mining draws on fossil water held in aquifers that filled in wetter climates, and the brine that lithium operations pump and evaporate is older still. The salars are the visible part of that: white basins ringed by darker ground, with rectangular ponds in which the brine is concentrated by sun and wind until the carbonate can be precipitated. The ponds are shallow and the evaporation is slow, and a tonne of lithium carbonate represents a volume of water that the basin will not refill. Brine is pumped to the surface and left in ponds, and the fraction that evaporates is the fraction that is lost, which is why the accounting is done in cubic metres. Copper is a different business, and the mines that run today consume freshwater that the basins had stored since the last ice age.

Above all this is the clearest sky on the planet, or near enough. The air is dry, stable and laminar, the cloud cover is negligible for most of the year, and the observatories on the high summits of the Andes have been built because of it. At dawn, when the fog bank sits against the coastal range and the sun has not yet reached the salt, the desert has the look of a place where two economies, one of water and one of light, happen to occupy the same ground. By mid-morning the fog has lifted, the salt is too bright to look at directly, and the domes on the summits have closed for the day because the seeing has gone. Between the fog and the light, the desert's two resources are both invisible for most of the day, and both are counted carefully by the people who use them.
