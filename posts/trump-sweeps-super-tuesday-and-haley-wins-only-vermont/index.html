Trump sweeps Super Tuesday and Haley wins only Vermont

Donald Trump won fourteen of the fifteen Republican primaries and caucuses held on 5 March, and Nikki Haley's single victory in Vermont left her with no realistic route to the nomination.

Posted 5 Mar 2024
By Caozhe Bao
3 min read

Donald Trump won Republican contests in fourteen of the fifteen states that voted on the fifth of March, taking a decisive share of the delegates available and moving close to the total needed for the nomination. Nikki Haley won Vermont by a narrow margin, which was her only victory in the primaries and caucuses that had been held until then. Biden won every Democratic contest on the same day, and he lost only to the uncommitted option in a small number of districts.

Scale of Trump's margins was the part that mattered. He took more than seventy per cent of the vote in most of the southern states, and in several he exceeded eighty, which is a level of support that the party has not seen from a non-incumbent since the era of Ronald Reagan. Exit polls showed that his support was strongest among voters without college degrees, in rural areas and among those who said immigration was their first issue.

Haley's campaign had argued that a large share of Republican primary voters wanted an alternative, and the results supported part of that claim. She took about thirty per cent of the vote in several states and won among independents and college graduates, a share that falls well short in a party whose primaries are dominated by registered Republicans. Her campaign said it would continue to the next contests, and it ran out of money within a week.

Democratic side of the ballot was uncontested in a way that has become normal for an incumbent. Biden won every state, and the only organized opposition came from voters who chose the uncommitted option, which appeared on the ballot in Michigan and Minnesota, where it took about thirteen per cent and nineteen per cent respectively. Organizers of that vote said they were protesting the administration's support for Israel in Gaza rather than opposing the president.

Calendar that produced the results had been compressed by the party's own rules. Republican National Committee had scheduled a large share of the delegates for early March, which meant that a candidate with a strong lead could accumulate an insurmountable advantage before the spring. Fifteen contests on a single day rewarded organization and name recognition, and the party's decision to hold them early was a change that favoured the front-runner.

Arithmetic of the delegate count made the outcome clear before the votes were counted. Trump needed 1,215 of the 2,429 delegates to the Republican convention, and he entered the day with about 276 after wins in Iowa, New Hampshire, Nevada and several smaller contests. The states voting on the fifth of March allocated more than eight hundred delegates, of which he took the great majority, and the remaining primaries were not enough for any rival to catch him even if every one of them went the other way.

What followed from the fifth of March was the end of the primary as a contest. Haley suspended her campaign the following week, and Trump became the presumptive nominee with a party that had been remade in his image. The general election that followed was the longest in recent memory, and the rematch that the results made certain was one that most voters said in surveys they did not want. What Super Tuesday settled was the nomination. What it did not settle was whether the party's coalition could expand beyond the voters who had turned out for it in March.
