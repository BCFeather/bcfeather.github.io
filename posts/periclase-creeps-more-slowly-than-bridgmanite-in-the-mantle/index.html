Periclase creeps more slowly than bridgmanite in the mantle

Magnesium oxide, the main mineral of the lower mantle, creeps more slowly than calcium oxide at the same conditions, which changes how convection in the deep Earth is modelled.

Posted 14 Jan 2023
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

The lower mantle is the largest reservoir of rock in the Earth, and its viscosity sets the pace of everything above it: how fast plates move, how long a slab takes to sink, and whether a plume can rise. Viscosity in the mantle is a property of how minerals creep, and the lower mantle's creep has been attributed mainly to bridgmanite, its most abundant mineral. The paper in Nature, 11 January 2023 examines the second mineral, periclase, which makes up about a fifth of the lower mantle. Patrick Cordier and colleagues model its deformation at the pressures and temperatures of the region and find that it creeps more slowly than bridgmanite, which reverses the assumption that the softer mineral dominates the flow.

The method is computational mineral physics. Creep happens by the movement of defects through a crystal, and the rate depends on how easily dislocations glide and climb. At lower-mantle pressures, the Peierls stress that resists glide rises sharply, and the modelling shows that periclase stiffens more than bridgmanite does over the same pressure range.

That result matters because the mantle's viscosity is not uniform. If one mineral is weaker, the aggregate's strength is set by it, and a model that assigns the weakness to the wrong mineral produces the wrong pattern of flow. Slab stagnation, plume ascent and the survival of ancient heterogeneity all depend on where the weak phase sits.

For geophysics the consequence is a recalibration rather than a revolution. Mantle convection models use a viscosity that is fitted to observations such as post-glacial rebound, and the fit does not care which mineral provides it. What changes is the interpretation: a stiff lower mantle becomes consistent with a periclase that resists deformation, and the temperature dependence of viscosity shifts accordingly.

Experimental difficulty explains why this took so long. Reaching 25 gigapascals and 2,000 kelvins while measuring deformation is at the edge of what diamond anvil cells can do, and the modelling fills the gap with theory that has to be anchored by the few experiments that exist.

What remains uncertain is the role of water and of grain size. A tiny amount of hydrogen weakens silicates dramatically, and the lower mantle's water content is unknown. The paper's results are for dry crystals, and a wet mantle would change the ranking. For the deeper question of how the Earth's interior evolves, the result is a reminder that mineral physics sets the boundary conditions for everything else. A convection model with the wrong weak phase will still fit the present, and it will mispredict the past.

Anisotropy complicates the conclusion further. Both minerals deform faster along some crystallographic directions than others, and a mantle rock with aligned crystals is weaker or stronger depending on the direction of strain. The paper's polycrystalline average is a starting point, and a texture-aware model is the next one. The result also reframes an old observation. Seismic waves slow down in regions where the mantle is hot, and the interpretation of that slowdown depends on which mineral softens first. If periclase holds its strength, then a low-velocity zone is a thermal feature of bridgmanite.
