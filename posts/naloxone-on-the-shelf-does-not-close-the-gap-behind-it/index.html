Naloxone on the shelf does not close the gap behind it

A nasal spray that reverses an opioid overdose was cleared for sale without a prescription in March 2023, and the 107,081 deaths recorded in 2022 show how much of the problem sits outside the pharmacy.

Posted 12 May 2023
By Caozhe Bao
9 min read

On 29 March 2023 the federal regulator cleared a naloxone nasal spray for sale without a prescription, which put an antidote for opioid overdose on the same shelf as cough medicine. In 2022, 107,081 deaths were recorded from drug overdose, and 68 per cent of them involved synthetic opioids other than methadone. The two facts are usually reported together, as though the second were the problem and the first the solution. The distance between them is the subject here: what a reversal does, whom it requires, and what it leaves untouched.

Pharmacologically the drug is close to ideal for its task. Opioids suppress respiration by acting on receptors in the brainstem, and naloxone displaces them from those receptors within minutes; a person who was blue and not breathing can be breathing again within five minutes. The weakness is duration. The effect of a single dose runs for somewhere between half an hour and an hour and a half, while the fentanyl that caused the overdose can outlast it, so the patient can sink again after the reversal has worn off. That is why protocols call for observation and a second dose, and why a reversal administered in a stairwell and followed by a departure is not the same intervention as one followed by an ambulance.

Bystanders are the binding constraint. A reversal requires someone present, able to recognize agonal breathing as an overdose and not as sleep, willing to administer a spray into the nose of a stranger, and willing to stay. Surveys of people who use drugs find that a majority have witnessed an overdose, and that fear of police involvement is the commonest reason for not calling for help. Good Samaritan laws, which shield the caller from prosecution for possession, exist in most states and differ in what they protect, and a caller who does not know the details may reasonably assume the worst.

Whether the shelf matters depends on the price and on who keeps it stocked. A two-dose pack retails for a sum that is trivial for a pharmacy chain and significant for a household, and insurance coverage varies by plan and by state. Rural counties carry fewer pharmacies and thinner emergency services, so the same product reaches the patient later where the distance is longer. Distribution programmes that hand kits to people who use drugs, and to their families, have the strongest observational support of any intervention in this field: areas with saturated distribution report fewer overdose deaths, and the effect is large enough to survive adjustment for the usual confounders, though the design is not randomized.

Geography decides the response time, and the response time decides the outcome. A volunteer ambulance service in a county of 30,000 people may take twenty minutes to reach a house, and a brain deprived of oxygen for twenty minutes is a brain with a poor prognosis. Naloxone given by whoever is present buys those minutes, which is the whole case for wide distribution. The same distribution produces a patient who wakes, refuses transport and is found dead the next morning, which is why protocols call for observation and why a reversal is best understood as a bridge to care. A bridge that ends nowhere is a bridge that gets used twice.

Pills are the part of the supply that a reversal cannot reach in time. A counterfeit tablet pressed to look like a pharmacy product can carry a lethal dose, and a young person who takes one at a party has no tolerance and no reason to suspect it. Testing strips, which show whether a sample contains fentanyl, cost little, are illegal in some jurisdictions and tolerated in others, and the evidence that they change behaviour is observational. What the strips do reliably is turn a guess into information at the moment of use, which is the same logic as the kit and the same limitation: both need someone who is still conscious.

A patient who leaves an emergency department after a reversal receives, in the best cases, a kit and a leaflet, and in the ordinary case a bill. The hour after discharge is the hour in which the drug is still in the body and the reversal is no longer, and the physician who has just saved the patient has no way to prescribe a bed. A referral to a peer programme, a warm handover to a clinic and a follow-up call within a week are the interventions with the best record, and each of them needs a telephone number that someone answers.

Treatment is the second half of the same problem, and it is thinner than the pharmacy shelf suggests. Methadone and buprenorphine cut mortality substantially among people who stay on them, and retention is the difficulty: waiting lists, daily attendance, and a dosing culture that treats constraint as therapy. Federal guidance in 2023 loosened take-home rules, which helps patients in rural areas, and the change arrives against a background of closed clinics and prescriber shortages. A reversal saves a life at the scene and returns the patient to a system that may have no appointment for weeks.

Nowhere is the risk sharper than at the gate of a prison or a detoxification ward. Tolerance falls during a period of abstinence, and a person who resumes at the dose used before can stop breathing on the first injection, which is why overdose deaths cluster in the weeks after release from prison or discharge from a detoxification programme. Continuity of medication is the instrument that works, and it depends on an appointment that exists on the day of release, a prescription that travels, and a clinic that will take a patient without insurance. Where those three line up, the post-release death rate falls; where they do not, the kits handed out at the gate are the only thing between the person and the next overdose.

Supply has its own logic, and it has been losing. Fentanyl is cheap to make, potent by weight, and easy to press into counterfeit tablets, so interdiction raises price at the border without removing the drug from the street for long. Precursor control has narrowed some routes and pushed others. Displacement is the recurring result: as heroin receded, methamphetamine and cocaine filled the space, and xylazine, a veterinary sedative with no approved use in people, appeared in fentanyl supplies and was detected in 10.9 per cent of fentanyl-involved deaths across 32 jurisdictions by June 2022. Xylazine has no antidote, and naloxone does not touch it.

The origin story still shapes the argument, though its force has faded. The first wave ran through prescription opioids, the second through heroin after the reformulation of one tablet, the third through illicit fentanyl. A policy built for the first wave, which regulated prescribing and monitored patients, does not fit a market in which the drug arrives in a press and the dealer is a phone. Institutions adapt slowly, and the treatment system inherited from the prescription era is oriented towards abstinence and towards people with insurance.

Against all of this stands a serious counter-case, and it should be stated at its strongest. Critics of harm reduction argue that easy access to an antidote reduces the perceived risk of use, that a spray sold without counselling shifts responsibility onto the individual, and that the money would do more in residential treatment. The evidence for the first claim is thin, and the studies that look for risk compensation find little; the second is a real design problem, since a kit without instructions is a kit that does not get used; the third is a question of budget share and not of principle. Decriminalization experiments cut both ways: Portugal's 2001 reform coincided with improved health indicators, and Oregon's 2020 measure coincided with a rise in deaths during the fentanyl influx, and neither case isolates the policy from the supply.

What can be counted is narrower than what is claimed. Kits distributed, reversals reported, and deaths per hundred thousand are the three numbers in circulation, and only the last is collected systematically. Reversal counts are self-reported and double-counted across programmes, and a reversal that ends in a later death at home is recorded as a death and not as a success. Death certificates have improved, with the specific drug named in the great majority of records, but 'involved' is not 'caused', and polysubstance is the norm rather than the exception.

Medicaid enrollees kept their coverage through the pandemic without a renewal, and in 2023 the reviews resumed, which means that millions of people had to prove eligibility in a year when the treatment system was already short of prescribers. A patient who loses coverage mid-course in a methadone programme either pays out of pocket, transfers to a clinic that will take an uninsured patient, or stops. Each of those transitions is a gap of days to weeks, and the mortality risk in a gap is the risk of a person whose tolerance has fallen and whose supply has not.

Distributional questions run through every layer. Medicaid covers naloxone in most states and not all, and the counties with the highest death rates are frequently the ones with the fewest prescribers, the longest ambulance times and the thinnest pharmacy networks. Age matters too: deaths among adolescents are rare in absolute terms and rising in relative ones, and the settings in which they occur, a bedroom at a party, a car, are the settings where no bystander protocol has been tested.

The unsettled question, then, is not whether naloxone works, which the pharmacology settles, but what combination of reversal, treatment, supply control and housing produces fewer deaths per dollar, and who pays for each element. A shelf is a start. A shelf without a bystander, a clinic and a bed is a product looking for a system.
