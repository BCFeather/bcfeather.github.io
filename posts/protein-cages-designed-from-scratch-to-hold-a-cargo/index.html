Protein cages designed from scratch to hold a cargo

Design pipelines now draw the shell before the sequence, and the resulting capsids close reliably in the flask, though none has yet been tested in an animal.

Posted 2 Sep 2024
By Caozhe Bao
5 min read

Protein shells are among the oldest devices in the cell, and their logic is modular. Ferritin stores iron in a hollow sphere of twenty-four subunits, carboxysomes package the enzymes of carbon fixation, and a virus is a genome inside a capsid built from one protein repeated sixty or a hundred and eighty times. One gene yields one chain, the chain assembles into a closed container, and the contents are separated from the cytoplasm without a membrane at all. Engineers have wanted that container for twenty years, for drug delivery, for enzyme scaffolding and for vaccine display, and until recently the usual route was to decorate a natural shell instead of building a new one, because a cage is only as good as the interfaces that hold it closed. A natural shell comes with those interfaces already debugged by evolution, and a designed one has to be given them from nothing.

The design problem is symmetry, and symmetry does not forgive approximation. An icosahedral shell places its subunit at sixty equivalent positions, every contact has to be the same contact, and an error of a few tenths of a nanometre at one interface is multiplied sixty times round the shell until it either fails to close or closes as a spiral. A designer therefore fixes the point group first, sets rigid bodies on the symmetry axes, and then has to invent a surface that will make the required contact with one specific partner and with no other copy of itself. The interface wants buried hydrophobic area, a polar cluster where the two surfaces meet, and enough shape complementarity that the halves cannot slide. Each requirement is easy to meet alone and awkward to meet together, and the buried area is the number a designer watches, because a small interface predicts a monomer and nothing larger.

Backbone generation has changed the order in which that work is done. A diffusion model trained on the entries of the Protein Data Bank will propose coordinates for a chain of a given length around a chosen symmetry axis, and an inverse-folding network then assigns a sequence compatible with the backbone it was handed. The designer no longer searches a library of natural folds for something that might tile; the geometry is drawn to specification and the sequence follows from it, which reverses the classical route in which a natural protein was mutated until it happened to assemble. Testing stays ordinary molecular biology. The gene is synthesized, expressed in Escherichia coli and purified, and the assembly is then examined by size-exclusion chromatography, by negative-stain electron microscopy and, where the particle is large enough, by cryo-electron microscopy at atomic resolution. What the network cannot promise is that the chain will fold to the backbone it was given; that is measured in the flask, not assumed on the screen.

Across the shells reported in the past two years, the ones that assemble share a recognisable anatomy. Buried surface at each interface is large, the contact runs through a small polar cluster in place of a lone salt bridge, and the subunits meet at an angle that lets the ring close without strain. Designs that failed in the flask failed in one of two ways: the protein formed dimers and stopped, or it formed open sheets that the microscope showed as long ribbons. Screening for a closed shell is therefore a matter of reading the particle size distribution, since a cage sediments as a single species and a ribbon does not, and the difference is clear in a gradient long before it is clear in a reconstruction.

Enclosure follows from geometry, and not from the sequence. A lumen lined with acidic residues will hold a cargo of the opposite charge, and a fusion to a short helix that packs against the inner surface raises the loading further. Cargo goes in by co-expression or by reassembly, and the two give different control. Co-expression keeps the cargo present while the shell assembles inside the cell, which is convenient and gives poor control over stoichiometry. Disassembly in urea or guanidinium and reassembly around a chosen cargo gives that control and costs yield, and it is the route taken when the cargo is a protein the bacterium would rather not fold. Neither route settles the question that matters later, which is whether the cargo stays inside once the particle meets serum and cells that have seen a capsid before.

The limits are the ones that decide whether the work becomes a technology. The shells are porous, and channels at the symmetry axes pass small molecules freely, so a cage that holds a protein will still leak a drug; the remedy is a redesign of the pores, since a thicker wall would change nothing. Stability in serum, the immunogenicity of a particle with sixty identical subunits, and the yield of a protein that must fold and then assemble are unresolved at the scale of one paper. Measurements in an animal are rarer than measurements in a cuvette, and the animal work that exists tends to use a cargo chosen because it is easy to detect, a fluorescent protein or a dye, rather than one that would be useful to deliver.

Two applications move faster than the rest, and both exploit the fact that a symmetric surface repeats an epitope at regular spacing. Vaccines display antigens on ferritin or on a designed shell, where the array is what makes the response strong and the geometry is what makes the batch reproducible. Delivery uses the lumen and needs a shell that opens on cue, and a shell that disassembles at a chosen pH or in the presence of a protease is a separate design problem with, so far, modest yields. What the field has now is a pipeline that works on paper and in the flask, a set of interfaces that can be drawn and tested, and a list of failures that are informative. What it does not have is a shell that has been through a trial, and that gap is where the next few years will be spent.
