Injury cover for riders arrives order by order

A scheme that charges platforms by the delivered order had enrolled 12.35 million riders across seven provinces by June 2025, and its expansion to seventeen provinces raises the question of whether cover attached to an order can protect a person.

Posted 15 Sep 2025
By Caozhe Bao
8 min read

A rider who falls on a wet road on the way to a customer has, for most of the past decade, occupied an awkward place in Chinese social insurance. Work injury insurance presupposes an employer who registers the worker and pays a contribution; the platforms that dispatch the order insist that they are matching supply with demand rather than employing anyone. The result was a gap that both sides could describe as the other's responsibility. From 1 July 2022, seven provinces began a pilot that fills it with a separate scheme, and by June 2025 it had enrolled 12.35 million people across those seven.

The pilot's footprint is narrow by design. It began in Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu, Guangdong, Hainan, Chongqing and Sichuan, and it covered seven platforms in three sectors: ride-hailing, instant delivery and intra-city freight. From 1 July 2025 it extended to seventeen provinces and eleven platforms, adding ten provinces and several large operators, with a plan to reach all provinces in 2026 and to consider other platform sectors in 2027. The staging is administrative caution and not a judgement about need: a scheme that pays benefits has to be actuarially tested before it is applied to thirty-one jurisdictions with different wage levels and different industrial profiles.

By declining to answer the employment question, the design answers it anyway. Enrolment runs by the day, the fee is charged by the order, settlement is monthly, and the platform enrols whoever accepts its orders, not whoever it employs. A rider who works for two applications is enrolled in both, and the cover travels with the task and not with the contract. Benefits are assessed against the categories used in work injury insurance, and the process for a small injury is deliberately short: confirmation of the injury, assessment of capacity and calculation of the payment are merged into a single application, and payments for minor cases are meant to arrive before the rider has exhausted the money available for treatment.

Cover attached to the order is also the scheme's central weakness. An order begins when the rider accepts it and ends when it is delivered, and the time between orders, the time spent waiting for a dispatch, the journey home and the journey to the first pickup sit outside the window or inside it depending on the local rule. A rider injured while crossing the city to reach a restaurant that has not yet accepted the order may be in a different position from one injured while carrying it. Multi-platform work multiplies the enrolment and divides the liability, and a claim that involves two applications can be argued about for months.

Who ends up paying the fee is the second question, and the pilot does not settle it. The platform pays per order, so the fee is a variable cost that scales with volume, and a variable cost can be passed to the customer through the delivery fee, absorbed by the platform's margin, or pushed down to the rider through the piece rate. Which of the three happens depends on the competitive position of each platform and on the density of riders in each city, and no published account has traced the fee through to the rider's earnings. The argument that mandatory cover reduces orders and therefore work is plausible at the margin and has not been tested.

Speed is the mechanism through which the job produces injuries. Dispatch systems estimate a delivery time from distance and traffic, the estimate becomes the promise made to the customer, and the promise becomes the standard against which the rider is measured. Lateness carries a penalty, ratings fall, the next allocation worsens, and the rider who is behind rides faster on a wet road. Electric bicycles reach speeds the cycle lanes were not designed for, and the rider is reading a phone and not the road. A benefit paid per confirmed injury addresses the consequence of that pressure and leaves the pressure itself untouched, which makes the sector's injury profile a question about the algorithm as much as about the road.

What the enrolment figures do not show is what the scheme has bought. The count is cumulative enrolments and not unique persons, so a rider who changed platforms is counted twice, and the 12.35 million is not a headcount of the workforce. No claims frequency, no return-to-work rate and no dispute rate has been published, which means the scheme's adequacy can be assessed only by its rules. A benefit schedule set against work injury insurance may still leave a rider with a fracture short of the weeks of income that a formal employee would receive, because the wage base is the order and not the month.

Platforms already carry commercial accident policies, and carried them before the pilot, usually a group contract that pays a lump sum for death or disability and a schedule of medical costs. The scheme sits on top of that cover, so a rider injured during an order may have a claim under both, which is better than a claim under neither and worse than a clear rule about which pays first. Handling is the friction: the insurer asks for a police report, the scheme asks for injury confirmation, and a family whose income has stopped is asked for the same documents twice. The single application for confirmation, assessment and payment is a real improvement, and it applies to the public benefit and not to the commercial policy beside it.

Litigation has run alongside the scheme and not behind it. In the first eleven months of 2024, the courts heard about 80,000 first-instance civil cases involving new forms of employment, and the recurring question in them is the same one the pilot sidesteps: whether a rider is an employee, a contractor, or a third category that the labour code has not yet named. The pilot's answer is that the question does not need to be answered for injury cover to work, which is true for the payment and less true for everything else, including dismissal, working time and collective representation.

The category the pilot avoids has a name in Chinese practice, the incomplete labour relationship, and the guidance issued in 2021 recognized it without settling what it means for dismissal, working time or bargaining. Calling a rider a partner keeps the platform outside the labour code and keeps the rider outside the protections attached to it; calling the rider an employee brings a contribution schedule that platforms say delivery prices cannot carry. The pilot shows that injury cover can be organized without resolving the classification, and it defers the classification to a court that has no guidance on what to do with it. A settlement that covers the medical event without settling the relationship is a pragmatic compromise, and compromises of that kind are tested by the next dispute and not by the next review.

An employee injured at work receives insurance funded by the employer, a wage-replacement period, rehabilitation and a right to return to the same job, and that is the standard against which the pilot should be read. A rider in the pilot receives a payment for a confirmed injury, calculated on a schedule, with no wage replacement beyond what the schedule provides and no right to a job. The scheme therefore covers the medical event and not the employment relationship, and the difference shows up in the weeks after the fracture and not in the week of it.

Small platforms are the remaining gap, and they are the ones whose riders are least able to absorb a loss. The expansion lists large operators first, and provincial authorities are meant to bring local platforms in over the following months, but the compliance cost per order falls harder on a small firm with thin margins, and the incentive to call riders partners instead of enrolling them is strongest exactly there. A scheme that covers the visible platforms and misses the long tail has covered the part of the market that needed it least.

What would settle the questions is data the pilot has not released: claims per thousand rider-days, the share of claims paid within thirty days, the median payment against the median medical bill, and the share of injured riders who return to the same platform within a quarter. Whether the fee appears on any statement the rider can read is the test of whether it is a contribution or a deduction. Until those numbers exist, the scheme is best understood as an institutional answer to a classification problem, tested at a scale that makes its actuarial logic visible and not yet at a scale that makes its adequacy visible.

Simple to state and hard to answer is the design question underneath. If the unit of protection is the order, then the scheme protects an activity and prices it per transaction, which suits a platform that dispatches transactions. If the unit is the person, then the scheme has to follow the rider across applications, across the hours between orders, and across the months after an injury when no order is being delivered. The pilot has chosen the first and called it the second, and the gap between the two is where the next round of cases will be argued.
