Transfer window closes on a record two billion pounds

English clubs spent more than two billion pounds in the summer, and the deals that closed on the final day were mostly loans and clauses rather than fees.

Posted 31 Aug 2024
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

Summer transfer window closed on 30 August with English clubs having committed more than two billion pounds in fees, a record for a single window and a figure that includes payments spread over several years. The final day produced fewer completed transfers than the deadlines of a decade ago. The total was helped by a handful of deals above fifty million pounds and by sales of academy players that counted as pure profit.

Accounting is the explanation. A fee is amortized over the length of the contract, and a club that signs a player on a five-year deal records a fifth of the cost this year, which is why a large transfer can fit inside a rule that limits spending relative to revenue. A rule that is written against revenue is a rule that a long contract can satisfy. A five-year contract is standard, and one club used seven to spread a large fee over a longer period.

Profit-and-sustainability rules are the constraint. Clubs that sell academy players record the whole fee as profit in the year of sale, and clubs that buy record the cost over the contract, which creates an incentive to trade homegrown players between clubs. An incentive that produces a market in teenagers is an incentive that the rule's authors did not intend. Sales of homegrown players are the gap the regulator has said it will close, and the clubs that used it most are the most exposed.

Loans are the workaround. A loan with an obligation to buy becomes a purchase in the next window, and a loan with a fee becomes revenue for the club that owns the player. A deal that is structured as a loan is a deal that both clubs can present differently to their auditors. A loan with an obligation becomes a transfer in the next window, which lets a club delay the cost by a year.

Wage bill is the real limit. A club that spends on fees and cannot pay wages is a club that will sell its best player in the next window, and the clubs at the bottom of the table are the ones whose wage bills are closest to their revenue. A record transfer window is a record that the smaller clubs fund by selling. The clubs in the bottom half spent least, and the gap between the top six and the rest is the gap the rules were meant to close.

What the window shows is that the rules have produced a market in accounting as much as in players. A club's success is now a function of how well its finance department reads the rulebook, and the football is played by the players who fit the spreadsheet. The window reopens in January, and clubs that have spent against future revenue will be selling then. A club that has already committed next season's television money is a club that will be a seller in January, whatever its position in the table.
