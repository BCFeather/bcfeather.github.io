A crystal soft enough to fail

The perovskite absorber heals its own defects and lets its ions move, and the same softness that makes the cell cheap is what limits its life.

Posted 15 Dec 2024
By Caozhe Bao
5 min read

A perovskite solar cell is a sandwich of a soft crystal between two contacts, and the softness is the reason it works and the reason it fails. The absorber is a lead halide with an organic cation in the cage, and its band gap can be tuned by changing which halide sits in the octahedra. The material absorbs light strongly over a thickness of a few hundred nanometres, so a cell is thin and cheap, and it is made by spin coating a solution at low temperature. The same softness that lets the crystal heal its own defects also lets ions move through it when the cell is warm and under illumination. The coating is done in air and the film is annealed at a temperature that the glass substrate can take, which is why the whole process is cheaper than the silicon it competes with. A spin-coated film is a film made by hand, and the record efficiencies are reported on areas smaller than a fingernail.

The defects are the point. A perovskite film made from solution has grain boundaries and vacancies, and a vacancy of a halide ion is a site that can migrate. The migration is fast at room temperature, and the ions accumulate at the interfaces when the cell is under bias. The accumulation changes the electric field in the absorber, which changes the current-voltage curve, and the effect is seen as hysteresis between a forward and a reverse sweep. A cell whose efficiency depends on the direction of the sweep is a cell whose measurement is not settled. The hysteresis is also a symptom of slow ions, and a cell that is swept slowly shows less of it than a cell that is swept fast. A cell that is measured once and reported once has a number, and a cell that is measured a hundred times has a distribution.

Degradation has several routes and they run at different speeds. Moisture attacks the crystal and breaks the organic cation away from the lead halide, and the product is a hydrate that is not a semiconductor. Heat drives the ions further and coarsens the grains, and oxygen under illumination creates superoxide that oxidizes the organic species. Ultraviolet light, which the glass absorbs, breaks the bonds at the surface. A cell in a sealed package with a good edge seal survives the moisture, and a cell on a roof is exposed to the other three. A module on a roof also sees the thermal cycling of day and night, and the expansion of the soft crystal against the glass is a mechanical stress that the package has to absorb. A package that is hermetic also has to survive the thermal expansion of the laminate, which is a problem for the edge seal.

Interfaces are where most of the chemistry happens. The electron contact is usually a metal oxide, and the perovskite reacts with it at the halide and the metal sites, which produces a barrier that grows with time. The hole contact is an organic layer, and the dopants in it diffuse into the absorber. A thin buffer layer is inserted to stop the reaction, and the buffer is itself a material that has to conduct the carrier it is named for. The number of layers in a high-efficiency cell is now five or six, and each of them was added to solve a problem at the interface below it. A buffer that is too thick adds resistance and a buffer that is too thin lets the reaction through, and the window between the two is narrow. The interface chemistry is also the reason that a cell made with a different transport layer has a different lifetime, even when the absorber is identical.

The measurement of stability is the part that has been standardized late. A cell that holds its efficiency for a thousand hours in a laboratory at room temperature is not evidence for a cell on a roof, and the protocols that cycle temperature, humidity, illumination and bias together were written after the first commercial modules had already been sold. The tests are now agreed, and the modules that pass them are the ones whose packaging was designed for the test rather than for the roof. The protocols now specify the irradiance, the temperature and the bias at which a module is held, and a module that passes at one condition can fail at another. A qualification that takes a year is a qualification that a start-up cannot afford before it has a customer.

Tandem cells are the commercial target. A perovskite on silicon uses the blue part of the spectrum that the silicon wastes, and the combined efficiency passes what either material can do alone. The perovskite in a tandem is thin, so the moisture problem is smaller, and the cell sits on top of a silicon cell that is already qualified. The tandem has to survive the same qualification, and the top cell has to match the current of the bottom cell within a fraction, which is a constraint on the composition. A tandem also has to be stable when the top cell is the one that fails, since a degraded perovskite on silicon is a cell whose current is limited by the top layer. A tandem whose top cell is stable for twenty years is a tandem that has not yet been made, and the field is working on the twenty-year part.

Commercially, the question is where the chemistry sits between a research record and a product. The highest efficiencies are certified on small areas that were made by hand, and the modules that are sold are larger and less efficient, and the gap between the two is the yield of the coating process. A process that coats a square metre uniformly at a thickness of a few hundred nanometres is a different problem from a spin coater, and the slot-die coaters that do it are still being tuned. The yield is also a function of the ink, and a solvent that evaporates too quickly leaves a film that is rough at the scale of the wavelength of light. A coater that is uniform over a square metre is a coater that has been engineered, and the engineering is the difference between a record and a factory.
