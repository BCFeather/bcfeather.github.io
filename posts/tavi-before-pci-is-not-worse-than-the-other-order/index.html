TAVI before PCI is not worse than the other order

A European trial randomized patients with severe aortic stenosis and coronary disease to valve replacement first or angioplasty first, and the valve-first strategy was noninferior at one year.

Posted 30 Aug 2026
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

Half of the patients who undergo transcatheter aortic valve implantation also have coronary artery disease, and guidelines have long recommended treating the coronary lesion first on the reasoning that a revascularized heart tolerates the procedure better. The recommendation rested on observational data and on surgical practice, and it had never been tested in a randomized comparison, which is how a routine sequence becomes a rule without evidence.

Investigators at 48 centres in Europe randomized 986 patients with severe aortic stenosis and coronary artery disease to a strategy of TAVI before PCI or PCI before TAVI, with an open-label design and a noninferiority margin of 6.6 percentage points. The primary endpoint combined death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, ischemia-driven revascularization, rehospitalization related to the valve, procedure or heart failure, and major bleeding at one year. Forty-eight centres is enough to capture variation in technique, which matters because the comparison is about a sequence rather than a product.

Events occurred in 105 patients in the TAVI-first group, 22.2 per cent, and 112 in the PCI-first group, 24.2 per cent. The risk difference was minus 2.0 percentage points with an interval from minus 7.4 to 3.4, and the trial met noninferiority with P<0.001. Serious adverse events were reported in 264 and 273 patients respectively. A difference of minus two points with an interval that crosses zero is the shape a noninferiority trial produces when the strategies are close.

Reversing the order has a practical logic that the trial's result now permits. A patient whose aortic valve is replaced first spends less time waiting for a second procedure, avoids the risk of bleeding on dual antiplatelet therapy in the interval, and leaves hospital with the obstruction that caused the symptoms already gone. The cost is that a coronary lesion is treated after the valve, in a patient who has just had a major procedure. Staging also has a cost in time, since the interval between procedures is days in some centres and weeks in others. Both orders end with the same two procedures, so the trial compares a schedule rather than a therapy.

The population is the limit. Patients were selected as suitable for either order, which excludes those with a lesion that must be treated urgently and those whose anatomy makes a delayed PCI unattractive. The one-year horizon also misses the late events that a staged strategy might shift, and noninferiority at one year is not equivalence over five.

The result reframes a guideline question as a scheduling question. When both procedures are needed and neither is urgent, the order can follow the patient's stability and the catheter laboratory's capacity rather than a rule that was never tested. That is a modest change in practice and a large change in how the recommendation is justified. The trial's contribution is to remove a rule from the category of things that are assumed, which is where most surgical sequences live.
