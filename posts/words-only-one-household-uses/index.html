Words only one household uses

Every household invents a handful of nouns and verbs for its own things, keeps them for decades without writing them down, and loses most of them when the person who used them unselfconsciously stops speaking.

Posted 16 Apr 2025
By Caozhe Bao
10 min read

A house keeps a dictionary that nobody writes down. In one flat the lid that never quite fits the rice pot is called the hat, and the long cushion a child slept on through two fevers is called the worm. The hat is a glass lid from a pot that broke in 1996, kept because it almost fits the replacement, and because throwing it out would take a decision nobody wants to take.

Words arrive by accident and stay by habit. A boy of two could not yet manage the word for blanket, and what came out was mimi. His parents said it back to him, because it was easier and because it was his, and by his fifteenth year nobody remembered that the word had once belonged to a toddler who could not pronounce anything. It outlived the lisp by half a century and was still in use when that toddler had a flat and a daughter of his own.

Older sisters do the preserving. The mispronunciation would have died in a month had nobody repeated it, and a girl of six found it funny enough to use at breakfast, at bath time, in front of guests, until the parents were saying it too and the word had lost every trace of being wrong.

Village nouns come in with a grandmother. She has words the standard language has never needed, for the heat that stays in a wall after the sun has moved, for the moment when a visitor should be told to come inside. Two of them are shorter than their replacements. One is kinder. Her daughter keeps the short ones and lets the third go, and cannot say later why she chose that way round, except that the third one sounded like her mother scolding.

A thing with a standard name stays furniture. A thing the house has renamed belongs to it, and renaming is how possession gets recorded in a family that owns almost nothing.

Sound carries the person who said it first. Saying the worm brings an aunt into the room, her pitch, her way of dropping the second syllable. Nobody hears the word neutrally, and none of them has ever tried.

Vocabulary attaches where a child needed it. There are house words for food, for sleep, for the dark at the end of the corridor, for the toilet at two in the morning, for the person who came when the crying would not stop. The abstract nouns stayed standard, because nobody needed them at three.

Bath time has a verb of its own, a two-word instruction that means tilt the head back and shut both eyes, and it has never been said any other way in that house. The towel that covers a whole child is the big one. Every other towel is a towel. Water is tested with a wrist and never with a thermometer, and the child who complains that it is cold is told to get in anyway.

Anger has one phrase. The father's dialect verb for hurry comes out only when the clock has already failed, and the children know from the verb alone how late they are, without looking at the door.

Death has a word too. The family says 'he went', or 'she is not here now', and the child learns the phrase years before he learns what it stands for. When the fact finally arrives, the phrase is already worn smooth and does the work without flinching. At the graveside it is said again, by four people at once, and it holds.

Money has several. Being short is being tight, a loan from an uncle is the favour, and the month when nothing was bought is referred to by its weather. None of these words is meant to deceive anyone. They keep the subject at a distance that the family finds bearable.

School arrives with its own dictionary. At five the teacher names the same object correctly and a red pen marks the house word as something else, and the child learns within a term that home has one vocabulary and the world another. The correction is made in front of the class, without malice, and it is the first time the child hears his own house described as a place where words are wrong.

Both words survive. The exam word goes on paper and the kitchen word comes out when the child is tired, and at twelve he notices that the second one carries more and cannot explain the difference to anyone who asks.

Arguments about the right word run for years and are settled by usage. The mother's version wins because she says it more often, or the grandfather's wins because it is funnier, and funnier is a stronger claim in a family than correct.

Marriage brings a second dictionary into the first. The wife says lid and the mother says hat, and for a year both are used in the same kitchen, and the couple negotiate the question without ever putting it into sentences.

Whose word wins is whose family wins, which is why the argument is never really about the lid. The wife's nouns arrived with the furniture and the mother's arrived with the cooking, and each of them was certain her own set described the room accurately. A marriage absorbs one set of nouns and loses the other, and the loss tends to be quiet, and tends to be the wife's.

No family is a single speech community. A word can belong to one branch and be unknown to the cousins, another can belong to a marriage and never reach the grandparents, and the dictionary turns out to be several books overlapping, each incomplete.

Nicknames sit at the edges of it. An aunt answers to a name that is not hers and has answered to it for thirty years, a grandfather is called by a vegetable, and a child who joins the family late has to be told which of the six names in the room belong to the same person.

The alley behind the house has a name that the street map does not use. Everyone within two doors knows it, the delivery riders learn it, and it appears in no address, which is the point of such names. The documents name the street. The people name the alley.

Gestures do work that words would do too slowly. Two taps on a shoulder mean come here, a lifted chin means the thing behind you, and a hand held flat and pushed down means wait, and a family can conduct a whole dinner that way.

At eighteen the child leaves and tests the vocabulary on strangers. Mimi goes to a roommate in a first flat, the roommate looks up, and the translation takes a second and a half. Nothing is lost in the meaning. Something is lost in the timing. The roommate's word for the same object is throw, and for a week both words are in the flat, and then only one of them is.

The loss shows in exactness rather than in anything exotic: the cushion with the stain that only one washing powder removed, the lid that fits one pot and no other. The dictionary records specificity, and specificity is what a shared language smooths away first. The pot has a dent on one side and the lid has to be angled to sit, and the house word covers the angle as well as the object.

Grandmother's words return as she ages. The standard nouns recede, the ones learned at school and used at work, and the village ones come back, and her daughter translates at the clinic while the doctor writes a standard word into the record.

When she dies those words lose their native speaker. Nobody says them unselfconsciously again. They become quotations, and a quotation is a word behind glass, still legible and no longer usable.

Residue stays in the body. A certain soap, a rain that starts the way it started in that village, the smell of oil heated past its point. The word arrives before the memory does, and the voice saying it is hers. She used it about the weather on a Tuesday in March, and her daughter has used it about the weather ever since.

A phone keeps a few of them. A voice note from a year when she was well, forty seconds about a pot, and a message that says the hat and means the lid, sent at a time when sending it cost her something.

Nobody writes the dictionary down, and it survives anyway. It lives in the way a family hands things over, the arm held out with the spoon already turned, the hand that takes the pot without being told which side is hot.

A new couple starts its own entries. The second door, the drawer that sticks, a private word for the hour after dinner when nobody wants to speak. Nothing is announced. It accumulates in the first year the way furniture does, without a decision attached. That last one came out of a joke about the neighbours and stopped being a joke within a month.

A child is born into that dictionary and treats it as the language. The first sounds a baby makes are hums closed by the lips, which is why unrelated languages arrive at something like mama for the mother and baba for the father. The house supplies the rest.

He invents one of his own. Dida, for the cat, from a sound that was probably the cat's. His parents adopt it, use it in front of guests without explaining, and the cat answers to it for nineteen years. Then the cat dies and the word has nowhere to go. A second cat arrives, and somebody tries dida once, and the new animal does not answer, and after a week the house calls it the cat. The furniture is the same and the house is not.

Loss works this way for people too. A relative who has stopped speaking, a child who has moved to a city and then to another country, a friend who now writes once a year. The vocabulary thins to what is needed, and what is needed is the practical part. The last exchange between two of them was entirely practical: the key, the number of the account, whether the heating had been left on.

A will lists a flat, a bank account and a share of a plot. It says nothing about the hat, the worm or the dida, because those are not property, cannot be signed over, and would have no column on the notary's form. The notary asks whether anything of sentimental value should be listed, and the family says no, because the question has already moved on and there is no place to write a cushion.

In another kitchen a lid is passed across the counter and called by a word that appears in no dictionary, and the child on the stool learns it the way the others were learned: by hearing it said without explanation, by hearing it said as though everyone already knew.
