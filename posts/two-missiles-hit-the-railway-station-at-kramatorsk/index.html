Two missiles hit the railway station at Kramatorsk as thousands waited to leave

A cluster munition strike on the platform killed at least fifty-seven people and wounded more than a hundred, in a city that had become the main evacuation point for the Donbas.

Posted 8 Apr 2022
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

Two missiles struck the railway station in Kramatorsk shortly after ten in the morning on 8 April, as several thousand people waited on the platform and in the forecourt for trains heading west. Ukrainian officials put the death toll at 57 and said more than a hundred were wounded, numbers that rose as rescuers worked through the afternoon, and the station's main hall was destroyed, along with the ticket windows and the kiosks where people had queued for water. An air raid warning had sounded that morning, but the station filled anyway, because the trains were the only reliable way out of the Donbas.

The weapons were Tochka-U ballistic missiles carrying cluster munitions, according to the Ukrainian security service and to munition fragments photographed at the scene. Cluster rounds release submunitions over a wide area, which is what makes a strike in a crowded station so lethal. A Tochka-U carries a warhead of about 480 kilograms, and this variant released its submunitions above the platform rather than detonating on impact, and images showed bodies in the forecourt and luggage scattered across the tracks.

Kramatorsk had become the hinge of the war in the east. The city sits on the last main line that still connects the Donbas front to the rest of Ukraine, and with the evacuation of civilians from Sievierodonetsk and Lysychansk under way, families had been told to gather at the station for trains to the west. Local officials said around 4,000 people were there when the missiles landed, many of them women, children and elderly residents.

Russia denied responsibility, and its defence ministry said the missiles had been fired by Ukrainian forces, an assertion that the weapon's own markings and its flight geometry contradicted. Investigators who examined photographs of the wreckage said the serial number and the Cyrillic inscription on the casing matched Russian inventory. The Russian president's spokesman had said days earlier that Russian forces were concentrating on the Donbas and would not strike civilian infrastructure there, a claim that this strike emptied of meaning.

The attack changed the tenor of the war's diplomacy within hours. European leaders who had been debating a further round of sanctions moved towards an energy embargo, governments that had resisted one faced a harder argument, and the foreign ministers of the Baltic states called for heavy weapons. President Volodymyr Zelensky said the strike showed what the Russian army does when it cannot win on the battlefield, and he repeated his request for air defence systems rather than aircraft. Among the fragments were submunitions that had failed to explode, which disposal teams had to clear before the platform could be searched.

Prosecutors opened a war crimes case the same day, and the evidence will again be photographs, munition fragments and testimony. The pattern of this spring is that each atrocity produces a case file, a sanctions package and a fresh argument about whether the weapons Ukraine needs will arrive before the next one. The station at Kramatorsk was rebuilt within a week, and the trains resumed from a temporary platform at the far end of the yard.
