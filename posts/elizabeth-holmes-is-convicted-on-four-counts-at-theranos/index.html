Elizabeth Holmes is convicted on four counts at Theranos

A jury found the founder guilty of defrauding investors but acquitted her on the counts concerning patients, a split verdict that ended a three-month trial and left the sentencing for another day.

Posted 3 Jan 2022
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

Elizabeth Holmes was convicted on 3 January of four of the eleven counts she faced over her role at Theranos, the blood-testing company she founded in 2003 and wound up in 2018. The jury found her guilty of three counts of wire fraud against investors and one of conspiracy, and acquitted her on the counts that concerned patients and their test results. Jurors deliberated for seven days after a trial that ran nearly four months.

The verdict is a story about money and about medicine at once. Theranos promised to run dozens of tests from a fingerprick of blood on a device it called the Edison, and it raised about 700 million dollars from investors on that promise. Former employees testified that the device could perform only a handful of the tests claimed and that the company ran the rest on modified commercial machines while telling partners otherwise. Prosecutors called laboratory directors who described results run on machines the company had bought from Siemens and modified without telling partners. The defence replied that those witnesses had their own grievances and that the board had approved the claims.

Holmes's defence was that she believed the claims and that the failures were those of the people around her. She testified for seven days, said she had been abused by her former partner and co-executive, Ramesh Balwani, and described the pressure of running a company from the age of nineteen. The jury did not accept that argument on the investor counts, and it did not accept it on the patient counts either, where the acquittal suggested a different view of what she knew.

The split verdict turned on the evidence available for each group. Investors were given projections, contracts with drug companies and a board stacked with former secretaries of state, and their case rested on documents. Patients were told results that were wrong, and the counts against her required proof that she knew the tests were unreliable when they were run, a standard the jury evidently found harder to meet. Balwani's trial was scheduled for March, and it would draw on the same witness list, which meant that two defendants would be tried twice on one set of facts.

Sentencing was set for the following September, and the four counts carry a maximum of twenty years each, though federal guidelines and the absence of a patient conviction will shape the actual term. The company's former president and chief operating officer, Balwani, faced his own trial in the same courthouse, with the same documents and a different defence.

Theranos has now produced the conviction that the venture industry spent years avoiding. What the case established is narrower than the legend: a founder who raised money on claims her own engineers knew to be false can be prosecuted, and a founder whose company harmed patients is much harder to convict, because the law around devices is built to regulate companies rather than the people who run them. What remains open is the standard for the next case, since the prosecutors won on documents about money and lost on evidence about patients.
