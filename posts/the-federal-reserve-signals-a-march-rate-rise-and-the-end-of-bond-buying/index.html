The Federal Reserve signals a March rate rise and the end of bond buying

The committee held rates near zero, said its asset purchases would end in early March and left the door open to a half-point move, as inflation ran at a forty-year high.

Posted 26 Jan 2022
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

The Federal Reserve held its target rate at zero to a quarter of a per cent on 26 January and said that its purchases of Treasury and mortgage bonds would conclude in early March, which removes the last obstacle to an increase at the meeting in the middle of that month. The chair, Jerome Powell, told reporters that the committee was of a mind to raise rates in March and that it would move sooner and faster if the inflation data required it. Two officials had said in public that a half-point increase was on the table for March, which is unusual before a meeting and which the chair did not repeat at his press conference. The bank also published principles for shrinking its balance sheet, and it said the process would begin once rate increases were under way.

That statement marked the end of an era of extraordinary support. The Fed had bought trillions of dollars of securities since March 2020, when the pandemic shut the economy, and its balance sheet had swollen to about nine trillion. Ending the purchases is the first step in the sequence that officials had sketched for the year: stop buying, raise rates repeatedly, and then shrink the balance sheet. Officials had been divided about how fast to move, and the January statement settled the argument in favour of the faster camp.

Inflation supplied the reason. The consumer price index had risen 7 per cent over the year to December, and the employment cost index, released two days after the meeting, showed wages rising at the fastest pace on record. Officials had spent 2021 arguing that the pressure would fade, and the January meeting was the point at which the committee's public language stopped promising patience. The committee also said that it would consider selling securities once the balance sheet had shrunk, which is a step no central bank had attempted at this scale.

Market reaction was orderly and telling. Stocks had fallen through January on the expectation of tightening, and bond yields flattened, with the two-year rising above one per cent while the long end stayed anchored. A flattening curve is the market's way of saying that the first hikes are coming and that the later ones may collide with slower growth.

Path beyond March was the real question. Officials had floated a schedule of three or four increases for 2022, and the futures market priced more, partly because the war that began in February would add energy costs to the headline index. Every increase would also raise the cost of servicing a federal debt that had grown by a third during the pandemic, which is a constraint the committee never mentions and never escapes.

Officials spent 2021 describing inflation as a supply phenomenon, and the January statement came from a bank that had concluded the demand side mattered too. The bond purchases had also begun to distort the housing market, and ending them was meant to let private buyers set the price again. The cost of both admissions is that tightening now has to work on prices that the bank's own support helped to create.
