Thin snow line moves up the mountain

The winter sports season that ended this month was the warmest on record for the Alps, with the snow line sitting hundreds of metres higher than the historic norm and a third of the region's resorts unable to open their lower slopes. The industry's adaptation is no longer a question of if; it is a question of which towns get to keep their winter.

Posted 23 Feb 2026
By Caozhe Bao
3 min read

The winter sports season that ended this month closed as the warmest on record for the Alps, with the snow line sitting hundreds of metres above the level that built the region's ski industry, and the statistics have acquired a grim regularity: a third of the region's resorts never opened their lower slopes, the glacier runs were closed by mid-season at several major sites, and the snowmaking machines ran from October, which is the new telltale of a failing natural product. The industry has been adapting for a decade, and this season made the end point of the adaptation visible: not whether winter tourism survives, but which towns get to keep their winter.

Economics of the shift are brutal for the towns at the bottom of the gradient. The high-altitude resorts, the ones with terrain above two thousand metres, had a tolerable season, shortened at the edges but viable in the middle, and their season passes sold at record prices. The mid-mountain towns, the ones whose economies were built on family skiing and weekend traffic, spent the winter running snowmakers at a loss and closing their lower runs by February. The pattern is not new, but this season's warmth moved the dividing line decisively upward, and the towns on the wrong side of it have started making the decisions that used to belong to a distant future: which lifts to retire, which slopes to abandon to summer hiking, which villages to stop marketing as winter destinations.

The strange part of the adaptation is how much of it has become invisible to visitors who can afford the high resorts. The industry's own data shows that the average skier is getting older, richer and more concentrated, and the resorts that serve that skier are consolidating their lift systems, expanding their glaciers' summer skiing and pricing out the casual market with a calm that resembles inevitability. The visitor experience at the top of the mountain is better than ever, which is the industry's achievement, and the cost of the achievement, the abandoned mid-mountain towns, the failed businesses, the winter economies that simply stop, is being paid somewhere else.

The climate story here is told best by the towns rather than by the glaciers. The glacier retreat is measured in metres and makes the news; the village decline is measured in shuttered hotels and makes the obituaries. The policy question for the Alpine countries is not whether to save the ski industry, which is already answered, but how to manage the orderly retreat of the parts of it that cannot be saved, and the honest answer is that most governments are not doing it well. The transition funds announced this decade have been spent on snowmaking subsidies, which postpone the problem, rather than on the retraining and infrastructure that would let a mountain town live without its winter.

The season's final numbers will be published in March, and they will show the familiar mixture of record revenues at the top and record losses at the bottom. The snow line has moved, and it will not move back on any timescale that the industry can plan around. The resorts that survive will be the ones that treat the high season as a bonus rather than a birthright, that build their summer businesses seriously, and that stop pretending that the weather owes them anything. The mountains will keep their beauty; the winter will keep shrinking; and the map of who can still afford to live by it will keep getting smaller, one quiet season at a time.
