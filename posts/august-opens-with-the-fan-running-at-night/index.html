August opens with the fan running at night

The first days of the eighth month keep the habits of July: windows shut at noon, windows open at midnight, and a household timetable built around the temperature.

Posted 3 Aug 2025
By Caozhe Bao
2 min read

August begins without a ceremony. The calendar turns on a Friday, the shops keep their summer hours, and the only announcement is a change in the sound of the city at about half past five in the morning, when the first buses leave before the light has properly arrived.

Heat in the basin does not leave when the sun does. Concrete keeps what it took during the day and returns it after dark, so the flat stays warm until two or three in the morning and the fan becomes the loudest object in the room. People who work outdoors learn to read the sky by the colour of the haze rather than by the forecast, and the ones who work indoors learn to read it by the number of bottles left in the corridor. Delivery riders schedule their long runs before ten and their short ones after four, and the security guards at the gate keep a chair in the shade of the wall, moving it twice a day as the shadow travels.

A schedule forms around this. Laundry goes out at six and comes in at eight, before the sun clears the neighbouring block. Groceries are bought in the evening, when the vegetables have been sprinkled with water twice and the price of peaches has fallen by a fifth. Children who were allowed to stay up in July are allowed to stay up a little longer in August, because the argument that it is too hot to sleep is difficult to answer.

By the first week of the month the light has already begun to shorten, though the thermometer has not noticed. Sunset comes four or five minutes earlier than it did at the solstice, and the shadow of the building reaches the corner of the street before seven. Nobody remarks on it. What people remark on is that the evenings are still thirty degrees, that the air conditioner in the bedroom is running at twenty-six, and that the electricity bill for July has arrived. The bill is the only part of the month that arrives with an argument attached, and it is usually settled by turning the air conditioner up a degree and opening the window a little wider.

Relief, when it comes, comes sideways. A thunderstorm builds over the western hills in the afternoon, drops its rain on one district and misses the next, and leaves the temperature four degrees lower for a day and a half. The newspapers call this a cold front; the neighbours call it a reprieve; the fruit seller calls it a reason to raise the price of grapes.

Nothing about this is dramatic, and that is the point. The month opens with the same small negotiations it closed with last year: whether to close the window at night, whether to water the plants twice, whether to walk the extra block to the shaded side of the road. August will be judged later by what it did to the harvest, the reservoirs and the school timetable, but it begins as it always begins, with a fan running at night and someone deciding, at ten past eleven, that it is finally cool enough to sleep.
