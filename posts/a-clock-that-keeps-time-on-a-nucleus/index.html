A clock that keeps time on a nucleus

Thorium-229 and scandium-45 have transitions a laser can reach, and two demonstrations in 2024 turned a nuclear spectroscopy problem into something that can be locked and interrogated.

Posted 6 Nov 2024
By Caozhe Bao
5 min read

Timekeeping has been an atomic business for seventy years, and the atom in question has always been an electron. Caesium, rubidium, strontium and ytterbium have transitions between electronic states that fall in the microwave or the optical, and a clock is built by locking a laser or a microwave source to one of them. The transition frequency is set by the charge distribution of the atom and by the Coulomb force that holds the electrons, and both are known well enough that the resulting second is defined to fifteen digits. The trouble with electrons is that they are sensitive to everything: a stray electric field shifts the levels, a collision with a background gas shifts them, and blackbody radiation from the walls of the vacuum chamber shifts them by an amount that has to be measured and subtracted. The clock that defines the second is a machine the size of a room, and its accuracy is limited by the care taken over the vacuum and the magnetic shielding around the atoms. A caesium fountain in a laboratory is a metre tall, and the apparatus that surrounds it fills the room.

A nucleus is a different kind of object. The nucleons sit in a well that is ten thousand times deeper and a hundred thousand times smaller, and the transitions between nuclear states run at energies of keV to MeV, far above the electron volts of an atomic transition. Those frequencies are far above anything a laser can reach, which is why nuclear clocks were a speculation for decades instead of a programme. The exception is thorium-229, whose first excited state sits at about eight electron volts, low enough that a vacuum-ultraviolet laser can drive it. That number is the whole reason the field exists, and it took twenty years of spectroscopy to pin it down. The number matters because a transition at eight electron volts sits in a part of the spectrum where lasers are inefficient, and every photon lost in the conversion from infrared to the vacuum ultraviolet is a photon that cannot interrogate the nucleus.

Two results in 2024 moved the field from measurement to clockwork. One group excited the thorium transition in a thin film of thorium fluoride laid on a silicon substrate, using a frequency comb locked to a stable laser and a vacuum-ultraviolet source derived from it, and read the excitation out optically. Another group used scandium-45, whose isomer lies higher but whose transition can be driven with a laser that is easier to build, and demonstrated the same principle in a different nucleus. Neither is yet a clock that runs for a month, and both show that the transition can be locked and interrogated. The solid-state sample is the point of the thorium work, since a nucleus held in a crystal can be interrogated with a laser that does not need an atomic beam, and the crystal can be cooled to suppress the thermal motion that broadens the line. The vacuum-ultraviolet comb that drives the transition is itself a result of fifteen years of work on broadband frequency combs, and it is the part of the experiment that other groups will have to reproduce.

What makes a nucleus attractive is the same physics that makes it hard to use. A nuclear transition energy depends on the strong force and on the fine structure constant, and only weakly on the electronic environment, so a nucleus in a crystal and a nucleus in a beam should tick at nearly the same rate. That insensitivity is what a clock wants: no Stark shift from the trap, no blackbody shift from the room, no dependence on the collision rate. It also means the signal is small and the readout is indirect, and a clock whose output has to be inferred from a photon count is a clock with a counting problem. A clock that ignores its surroundings is also a clock that can be moved, and a movable clock is what geodesy and navigation want.

The reason to bother is the constants. A clock built on a nuclear transition and a clock built on an electronic one would drift apart if the fine structure constant changed, and a comparison of the two over a year sets a limit on that change which is sharper than any astrophysical bound. The same comparison is a search for dark matter, since a coupling between the dark sector and the nucleus would show up as an oscillation in the ratio of the two frequencies. Both experiments are being built, and the thorium system is the one with the sensitivity. The comparison does not need a clock that beats the best optical clocks on stability, only one whose frequency is settled by a nuclear transition and not an electronic one, and the two systems are being developed on parallel tracks.

Practical limits decide what the apparatus can be. The vacuum-ultraviolet laser has to be stabilized to a linewidth narrower than the transition, the solid-state sample has to be cooled and the fluorescence collected without heating it, and the whole apparatus is a table of optics instead of a box on a rack. A clock that needs a dilution refrigerator and a synchrotron-grade laser is not going to replace caesium, and the near-term use is measurement rather than timekeeping. The current demonstrations run for hours and not months, and a clock that drifts between runs cannot be compared with anything.

Where the field goes next is a smaller apparatus. A thorium clock in a cubic metre would be portable enough for geodesy, where the gravitational redshift over a kilometre is already measurable with optical clocks, and it would be sensitive enough to look for the oscillations that a dark matter search needs. The nuclear transition sits between the two communities, and the 2024 demonstrations are the point at which the two stopped talking about different things and started building the same instrument. A compact nuclear clock would also be a sensor, since a nuclear transition responds to the local gravitational potential and to any field that couples to the nucleus, and the search for those fields is a table-top experiment once the apparatus fits on a table. The measurement of a gravitational redshift over a hundred metres would be a calibration, and the search for an oscillating coupling would be a discovery.
