A central bank that has one policy rate and a balance sheet worth more than the economy has to decide which instrument to move first, and the Bank of Japan chose the balance sheet. On 17 June it left the policy rate at 0.5 per cent, where it had sat since January, and announced that from April 2026 it would reduce its bond purchases at a slower pace than previously planned.

The rate decision was the easy half. Inflation had been above target for three years, but the headline number had been pushed up by rice and by administered prices rather than by wages, and the board judged that the underlying trend was not yet strong enough to justify a fourth increase. A hike into a tariff shock is the kind of mistake a cautious board avoids.

Rice is the political item. Prices for the staple had roughly doubled over two years after a poor harvest and a distribution system that the government had mismanaged, and the ministry had begun releasing stockpiles in the spring. A central bank that raises rates because rice is expensive is a central bank that is fighting a supply shock with demand.

Wages are the other side of the ledger. The spring round of negotiations produced the largest nominal increases in three decades, and real earnings turned positive for the first time in months, which is the condition the bank had set for normalising policy. The condition arrived later and weaker than the headline suggested.

The balance sheet matters for the currency. The bank had been buying about 400 billion yen of bonds a month and had planned to halve that by early 2026, and slowing the taper keeps more duration in the market and more yen in the system, which is a quiet form of easing that the exchange rate noticed.

Politics sits behind the decision. A government that faces a fiscal squeeze and an upper house election in July does not want a rate rise in June, and a bank that wants to be seen as independent has to weigh that against the credibility it spent a decade building. Independence is a practice and not a statute.

What would settle the year is whether core inflation excluding fresh food and energy stays near 2 per cent without rice. A rate that settles there is a rate that the bank can raise again in the autumn; one that falls back towards 1 per cent removes the argument entirely. A bank that slows its taper while holding its rate is a bank that has decided to remove one source of tightening and to leave the other in place for another year. Whether the yen stabilizes near 145 to the dollar or slides past 160 is the variable that decides how much of the bank s caution it can afford. A government that has just spent a fiscal package on household electricity bills has a strong interest in a currency that does not collapse.
