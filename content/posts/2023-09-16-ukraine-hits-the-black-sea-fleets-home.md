Ten cruise missiles hit the Sevastopol shipyard in the early hours of 13 September, according to the Ukrainian air force, and the Russian defence ministry acknowledged that two ships under repair had been damaged. Satellite images published the next day showed a large landing ship and a diesel-powered submarine in dry dock with visible holes in their hulls, and Russian officials said twenty-four people had been injured. The submarine, able to fire cruise missiles at Ukrainian cities, was one of four of its class in the fleet.

A week of pressure on the peninsula preceded it. Ukrainian units had landed on the western tip of Crimea in late August, destroyed a battery of air defences near Yevpatoria, and struck a military airfield at Saky, where satellite photographs showed craters and burned aircraft. The strikes have been carried out with British and French cruise missiles and with drones, and the Ukrainian commanders have described a campaign to make Crimea untenable for the fleet and for the aircraft that use it as a base.

Russian defenses have not adapted quickly enough. Sevastopol is protected by air defence systems, harbour booms and patrol boats, and the missiles arrived anyway, which suggests either that the defences were stripped to protect other fronts or that the missiles flew a route the batteries did not cover. The fleet has moved some of its ships east to Novorossiysk, and its commander has been replaced twice since the war began. Satellite images have also shown new barges and nets placed at the harbour mouth.

The strategic effect is about basing rather than battles. A fleet that cannot repair its ships in its own yard has to send them elsewhere, and a submarine in dry dock cannot fire missiles. Ukraine has no navy capable of challenging the fleet at sea, and it does not need one if the fleet cannot use its bases. The commander of the Ukrainian navy has said that the aim is to make the Russian ships stay in port, and to make the port itself a place they cannot keep.

Politics runs through the Black Sea and beyond. Russia's claim that Crimea is permanently Russian is contradicted by the explosions inside Sevastopol, and the attacks give Ukraine an argument to make to allies who have been reluctant to supply long-range weapons. Western officials have said they do not object to the use of their missiles against targets in Crimea, which Russia regards as its own territory, and the distinction between Crimea and Russia proper has become an argument in the capitals that supply the weapons.

War has now reached the harbour where the Russian fleet has been based since the eighteenth century, and it reached it with missiles that Ukraine's allies supplied, which is the part of the story that Moscow has to explain to its own public. The fleet will adapt and the attacks will continue, and the argument about what the west allows Ukraine to hit is now being made with wreckage in dry dock.
