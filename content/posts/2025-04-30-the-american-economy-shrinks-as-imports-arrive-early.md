A tariff announced for the future is a purchase order placed today. Firms that expect a duty on imported components in April buy them in March, and the national accounts count an import as a subtraction, so stockpiling shows up as weaker growth in the quarter the stockpile is built. That is the mechanism behind the first estimate of American output published on 30 April.

Real gross domestic product fell at an annual rate of 0.3 per cent in the first quarter, after a rise of 2.4 per cent in the fourth quarter of 2024, according to the advance estimate from the Bureau of Economic Analysis. Imports rose and subtracted from the total, and federal government spending fell. Investment, consumer spending and exports all rose, and were not enough.

Net exports subtracted 4.83 percentage points, which is an enormous drag for a single quarter and almost entirely a quantity effect. Consumer spending grew 1.8 per cent, down from 4.0 per cent in the previous quarter, and contributed 1.21 points. Federal spending fell, state and local spending was roughly flat, and the public sector took a little away rather than adding anything.

Composition tells you what kind of slowdown this is. A recession that begins with consumers retrenching looks different from one that begins with firms moving inventory across a border. Here consumption slowed but did not collapse, investment rose, and the entire negative came from the trade line. An economy importing capital goods ahead of a tariff expects to keep producing, which is not the behaviour of a contraction. Firms that feared a price rise bought machines, and machines are bought to be used.

The estimate is an advance figure, built from incomplete source data, and it will be revised twice over the following two months. Revisions of this magnitude are routine and the direction is not knowable in advance. What is knowable is that the first quarter's number will be quoted as a recession signal by people who do not read the trade line, and revised by people who do. A first estimate that lands in the middle of a policy shock is a number with a publishing date and a political life.

Political economy matters as much as the arithmetic. A negative print arriving weeks after a tariff announcement is a gift to whoever argues the policy is working and a weapon for whoever argues it is not. Both sides will use the same figure, because the figure is true and its cause is a choice.

What would settle the interpretation is the second quarter. If the imports were pulled forward, the second quarter should show a smaller trade drag and a rebuild of inventories, and growth should recover, with the imports reappearing as inventories instead of as a deficit. If imports substituted for domestic production, the drag persists and the slowdown is real, and the difference will show up in the inventory data before it shows up in employment.
