description: "A study proposes classifying marine heatwaves by cumulative intensity rather than peak anomaly, and reclassifies 40 per cent of the satellite record. Biological damage tracks the integral."
---

A marine heatwave is usually defined as a period when sea surface temperature exceeds the 90th percentile for that place and season for at least five days. A paper published this month in *Nature Climate Change* argues that the definition should rest on duration and cumulative intensity, and that 40 per cent of the events in the satellite record change category when it does.

The argument is biological. Corals, seagrasses and fish bleach or die in proportion to how long they sit above a threshold, not to how far above it the peak reaches. A two-week event at 3 degrees above normal does less damage than a two-month event at 1.5, and the current classification ranks the first as the more severe.

The proposed metric is degree-days above the threshold, integrated over the event and normalized by the local climatology. The authors compute it for 1,200 events between 1982 and 2025, and find that the ten most damaging, judged by observed coral mortality, all sit in the top fifteen by degree-days while only four sit in the top fifteen by peak anomaly.

Inputs come from satellite radiometers calibrated across four generations of instruments, with a bias correction documented in an appendix. The uncertainty is 0.15 degrees per cell, small against the anomalies being classified.

The consequence for forecasting is practical. A seasonal forecast that predicts a long, mild anomaly can now be converted into an impact estimate, which is what fisheries and reef managers need. Two agencies have adopted the metric for bleaching alerts this season.

The critique the paper answers is that a new metric fragments the record. Events classified under the old definition are in the news and in the literature, and a change makes year-on-year comparison awkward. The authors propose reporting both and using the new metric for impact.

Limits are explicit. Damage data come from monitoring programmes concentrated on reefs and temperate coasts, and the open ocean has almost no biological observation, so the calibration is biased towards places people watch.

Fishery management is the application with the clearest consequence. A stock assessment that uses a heatwave indicator to project recruitment needs a metric that matches the mechanism, and the duration-based measure improves the projection's error in three of the four stocks the paper tests.

The equity question is who bears the cost of a revised metric. Events reclassified upward will trigger closures and compensation claims, and the agencies that adopted the metric this season have published the old classification alongside the new one to keep the record comparable.

Insurance and coral restoration are the sectors that will use the metric. A reef restoration project prices its work against the frequency of severe events, and a metric that misranks severity distorts the budget. Two projects have adopted degree-days for their planning, which is a small and telling indication that the biological definition is winning.

For a reader, the deeper point is that a definition is a policy instrument. What counts as a heatwave determines what is counted, funded and reported, and a metric chosen for statistical convenience will misrank the events that matter biologically.
