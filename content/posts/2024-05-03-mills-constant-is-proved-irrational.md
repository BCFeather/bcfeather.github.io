In 1947 Mills showed that a real number exists whose floored powers are always prime: take a number above one, raise it to the third, ninth, twenty-seventh power and onwards, and the integer part is prime every time. The property is demanding, since one number fixes a whole sequence, and the sequence has to hit a prime at every third power however far it is continued.

The construction is where the constant comes from. Mills took a sequence of primes that grows quickly enough for each one to lie below the cube of some common number, and the existence of such a sequence follows from a theorem on gaps between consecutive primes. The constant is therefore an inheritance from the primes and not a formula anyone wrote down.

Its value is about 1.3063778839, and the digits are not where the difficulty lies. The constant is defined through a property of the primes and not by a formula, so questions about it are questions about the distribution of primes in disguise: every digit is a computation, extended over the years into the thousands, and every arithmetic property is a theorem about the sequence behind it.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 30 April 2024 proves that Mills' constant is irrational. The result does not assume the Riemann hypothesis, and the paper adds partial results on whether the number is transcendental, which stays open. No new digits are computed along the way, which is the point: the argument concerns what the number is and not what it equals to ten decimal places.

Irrationality has to be argued without digits. The constant is an infimum over the reals that carry the prime property, so the proof must characterise that class before it can exclude the rationals, and the argument therefore runs through the sequence the property generates and not through an approximation of the constant itself. A rational lying in that class would have to survive every third power, and the proof shows that none does.

Partial results on transcendence are not transcendence. Whether the constant is algebraic or transcendental is left open, and nothing is said about the normality of its digits, which would be a far stronger claim than irrationality and is not established for most constants of this kind. Irrationality is the weakest of the three properties such a number might have, and it is the one that came first, which says something about how little any of them is settled.

The number is useless for computation, and the interest lies in what the proof says about its sequence. Primes generated this way cannot be as regular as a rational parameter would make them, which is a small and permanent addition to what is known about the primes themselves, and a line in the short list of constants shown irrational for reasons other than their expansion.
