Perineuronal nets are lattices of chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan that wrap the soma and proximal dendrites of certain neurons, and their function is usually described as structural, a scaffold that stabilizes a mature circuit. A paper has given them a role in pain. That role is now extended into the signalling that a neuron receives from its immediate environment, and the environment in the dorsal horn turns out to be a gate for the transmission of pain.

The work examined the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, where lamina I projection neurons integrate nociceptive input and carry it to the brainstem and the thalamus. The paper appeared in *Science*, 26 May 2022. The projection neurons that the paper focuses on are the ones that decide whether a signal reaches the brain, and the gate that they control is not a synapse but the coat that surrounds the cell.

After nerve injury, microglia in lamina I degraded the perineuronal nets that selectively enwrap the spinoparabrachial projection neurons. Degradation increased the activity of those neurons and produced pain-related behaviours, and blocking the degradation prevented the hypersensitivity. Blocking the degradation prevented the hypersensitivity, which is the intervention that turns a correlation into a mechanism and gives the result its weight.

Selectivity is the interesting part, because the nets surround some neurons and not others, and the microglia removed the ones around the cells whose output determines whether a signal reaches the brain. The circuit therefore has a gate that is physical rather than synaptic. A gate made of a sugar lattice is a gate that a cell can remodel during inflammation, and the microglia are the cells that perform the remodelling.

Enzymes that degrade the nets are the likely mechanism, since microglia release chondroitinase and matrix metalloproteinases under inflammatory conditions, and the study identifies the cells responsible for the release.
