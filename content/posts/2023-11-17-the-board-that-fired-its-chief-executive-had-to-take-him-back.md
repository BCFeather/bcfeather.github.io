description: "OpenAI's board removed Sam Altman on 17 November 2023, triggering a week in which staff threatened to resign, Microsoft offered him a job, and the directors agreed to reinstate him under a new board by 29 November."
---

OpenAI's board removed Sam Altman as chief executive on 17 November, in a statement that said he had been not consistently candid with the directors and that the company needed new leadership. The decision was taken at a meeting that the other executives learned about afterwards, and it came a day after a video call in which the board had asked him about the company's governance. Greg Brockman, the president, resigned the same day in protest.

The company's staff responded within hours. Roughly seven hundred of about eight hundred employees signed a letter saying they would resign unless Altman returned to the job, and the letter named the board members whose departure they wanted. Mira Murati, who had been named interim chief executive, made clear that she supported the staff position. The board responded by appointing Emmett Shear, the former head of Twitch, as interim chief executive, a choice that did nothing to calm the company.

Microsoft, which had invested more than ten billion dollars in OpenAI and runs its models on Azure, moved first. Satya Nadella announced on 20 November that the company would hire Altman and Brockman to lead a new artificial intelligence unit, and the move was read as a signal that the most valuable customer had chosen a side. The offer was public within hours, and it came with equity for the two men and a budget for the unit.. Within a day, the two sides were negotiating a return, and the board's position weakened as the legal and commercial consequences became clear.

An agreement reached on 21 November and confirmed on 29 November, reinstated Altman and Brockman and replaced the board with a smaller group: Bret Taylor as chair, Larry Summers, and Adam D'Angelo, the only member of the old board who stayed. The new board was given a mandate to review the governance that had produced the crisis, and the company said it would strengthen its processes for oversight.

Reasons behind the removal remain contested. The board's letter pointed to candour, and reporting afterwards described a dispute about how much to tell the directors about product plans, safety research and the company's relationship with Microsoft. The directors had no clear authority to remove a chief executive in a company whose investors sat outside the board structure, and the structure itself was a product of a charter designed to put the mission ahead of shareholders.

One question the episode exposed has no answer yet. A company that builds systems with consequences for millions of people needs oversight that cannot be captured by its commercial partners, and it needs leadership that can be removed without paralysing the institution. OpenAI's experiment attempted both and achieved neither. The new board inherits the task of designing a structure that can survive its own success. The staff letter was published within a day of the removal and was signed by researchers, engineers and executives, who asked the directors to reverse course and to explain themselves, a demand that the board answered only through the settlement that followed.
