Federal Reserve left its target range for the federal funds rate unchanged at 5.25 to 5.5 per cent on the first of May, the sixth consecutive meeting without a change and the eleventh month in which rates have stood at their highest level since 2001. Decision was unanimous. Statement repeated the formulation that the committee does not expect it will be appropriate to reduce the range until it has gained greater confidence that inflation is moving sustainably towards two per cent.

Data since the previous meeting had gone the wrong way for the bank. Consumer prices rose faster than expected in the first quarter, and the annual rate of core inflation stopped falling at about 2.8 per cent. Employment had continued to grow, and household spending had held up despite the level of rates. Chair Jerome Powell said at the press conference that the readings had not given the committee the confidence it needs, and that gaining it would take longer than had been assumed at the start of the year.

Balance sheet policy was changed alongside the rate decision. Committee said it would slow the pace at which it is shrinking its holdings of Treasury securities, from sixty billion dollars a month to twenty-five, which reduces the risk of turbulence in money markets as the balance sheet falls. Move was technical and was read by markets as a signal that the bank intends to keep rates high without draining liquidity at a rate that could force an earlier change.

Market reaction was limited because the decision had been expected. Yields on two-year notes fell slightly after the statement, and equities were little changed before selling off later in the day. Traders pushed back the expected date of the first cut from the summer towards the autumn, and by the end of the week they were pricing fewer than two reductions this year, down from six at the start of January.

Political pressure around the decision was louder than usual. The former president and the incumbent both commented on the bank's policy during the campaign, and several senators called for a cut before the election. Powell repeated that the bank would make its decisions on the data and would not be influenced by the electoral calendar, a formulation he has used in every meeting of the cycle and one that has become less convincing to critics as the election has approached.

What followed from the decision is a longer period in which borrowing costs stay at their current level. Mortgage rates moved back above seven per cent, and applications for home loans fell to the lowest level in three decades. Companies with debt maturing in the next year face refinancing at rates they have not paid since before the pandemic. Whether the bank cuts before the end of the year depends on a set of inflation readings that have been unreliable for two years, and Powell was careful on the first of May not to promise anything that the data might take away.
