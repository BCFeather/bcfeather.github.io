The holiday ends on the fourth of February, and the journey that a family makes on that day is the one that the transport system has been preparing for since December. The car is packed in the morning, the trunk is loaded with the gifts that were received and the food that was cooked, and the navigation app shows a red line on the highway that leads to the coast.

The return is the second peak of the travel period and it is the one with the tighter schedule. The outward journey is spread across a fortnight because the factories close on different days, and the return is compressed into two because the offices reopen on the fifth. The compression is what makes the fourth of February the busiest day of the year on some routes.

The road is the part of the return that has changed most. A family that drives has its own timetable and its own stops, and the journey that used to be a train with a fixed departure is now a car with a flexible one. The flexibility is what produces the congestion, because every family chooses the same hour, and the highway that was built for the average day is used on its worst one.

The railway's answer to the peak is the extra train. The overnight services that leave at eleven in the evening and arrive at six in the morning are scheduled for the return, and the tickets are sold in a batch that the operator releases in January. The passenger who takes one arrives at work without a night of sleep, and the calculation is one that a lot of people make.

The household also returns with a different set of things from the ones it took. The trunk that was full of gifts on the way out is full of food on the way back, and the jars of pickles, the cured meat and the frozen dumplings are the cargo that the grandmother insisted on. The weight of the car is a measure of the visit.

The first evening back in the city is its own ritual. The flat is cold, the fridge is empty, the plants that were watered before the trip are alive or not, and the meal that is cooked is usually the simplest of the year. The holiday ends with a plate of noodles and an early night, and the year resumes on the following morning.

What the fourth of February shows is a system at the point where it is least flexible. The holiday is fixed, the reopening is fixed, and the millions of people who move between the two have a single day of slack. The road back is the visible cost of that arithmetic, and the traffic is the way the country pays it. A system that moves this many people in two days is a system that has accepted the queue as the price of the holiday, and the family that leaves at five in the morning is the family that has learned to pay it early.
