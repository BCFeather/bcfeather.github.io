Fifteenth of the first lunar month is the end of the festival, and in this city it is marked by a lantern fair in the square beside the river that lasts three evenings and ends with the lanterns being taken down. Last evening is crowded in a way the first two are not, because everyone knows it is the last one, and parents are there with their children in the knowledge that the term begins in the morning. Lanterns hang from wires strung between poles, red and yellow and a few in the shape of rabbits, and riddles are written on strips of paper and read aloud by people who cannot reach them.

Cold is the part outsiders do not expect. The festival ends in the last week of February, and the river wind comes up the valley in the evening and makes the square colder than the street behind it. People stand with their hands in their pockets and their collars up, and children run to keep warm, and vendors selling sweet rice balls do better than the ones selling cold drinks. Usually there is a light rain. This year there was, and the lanterns looked better for it, their colours reflected in the wet stone.

Food is the same as it has been for a thousand years in one form or another. Dumplings of glutinous rice with sesame or peanut inside are boiled and served in the broth they were cooked in, and the tradition says the round shape stands for reunion and the number of them should be even. Nobody counts. There is also the candied fruit on sticks, a northern thing that arrived here with a travelling fair a decade ago and stayed, and there are the fried sesame balls that the older people prefer and the children ignore.

What the evening actually does is harder to name. Holiday has been going on for two weeks, and the household has been in a state of suspension in which the ordinary rules of bedtime and homework have not applied. Lanterns mark the point at which the suspension ends. Tomorrow there will be uniforms and the seven o'clock alarm and the walk to school in the dark, and parents will go back to work, and the pots of pickled vegetables will be put in the cupboard. Last evening is a farewell to the festival, held in public, with lights.

By nine the square is emptying. Vendors begin to fold their tables and the wire is lowered so that the lanterns can be taken down and stacked for next year. Someone sweeps up the paper strips with a broom, a child is crying because a lantern has gone out, and the adults are talking about the term and about the examination in June. Lights go off row by row, and the square is dark and wet and ordinary again.

Resumption is what the evening is preparing everyone for. In the house the schoolbag has been packed since the afternoon, and the uniform has been laid out on a chair, and the last bowl of rice balls has been eaten. The term that begins tomorrow will run without a break until the summer, and the next holiday is four months away. Nobody in the square says this out loud, and every family there is thinking it. The festival ends the way it ends every year: with a mother telling a child that it is late and they have school in the morning, and with the lights going off one row at a time.
