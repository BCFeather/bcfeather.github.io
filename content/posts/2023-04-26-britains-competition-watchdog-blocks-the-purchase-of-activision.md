title: "Britain's competition watchdog blocks the purchase of Activision"

description: "The regulator said the deal would harm the competition in the cloud gaming market, a conclusion that the two companies will appeal."
---

Britain's competition authority blocked Microsoft's purchase of Activision Blizzard for 68.7 billion dollars on 26 April. The regulator said the deal would give the company an incentive to withhold the games from the rival platforms and to favor its own cloud service. The two companies said they would appeal to the Competition Appeal Tribunal. The decision came after a review of about eight months, and the final report had been delayed twice. The companies have 60 days from the ruling to lodge the appeal, and the tribunal would hear the case in the autumn.

Cloud gaming was the part that decided it. The regulator looked at the market for the games streamed from the remote servers, which is small today and growing. It concluded that Microsoft already has a strong position through the Windows licences and the Azure infrastructure, and the addition of the titles would make it stronger. The cloud gaming market was valued at about 1.5 billion dollars in Britain, and the regulator said it could grow to about 5 billion by 2030. The panel said the games were the input that the new services need, and the control of the catalogue was the control of the market.

Console arguments were dropped. The regulator had worried about the exclusivity of the games on the Xbox, and it accepted the remedies that Microsoft offered on that front. The cloud was the part that the remedies could not fix, in the view of the panel. The regulator accepted that the console market was competitive, and it said the catalogue was not essential there. The console remedies had included a ten-year licence to the rival platforms, and the panel said they had worked on that front.

Both companies had offered to license the titles to the rivals for ten years. The regulator said the offer was not enough, because the terms would have to be monitored and the incentive to cheat would remain. The licensing of a catalogue is a promise about the future, and the regulator preferred the market that is not reshaped. The European Commission approved the deal in May on the condition that the titles were licensed to the rivals, which the British panel had considered. The American regulator has sued in its own court, and the hearing is set for the summer.

About 40 countries have cleared the deal, including the European Union, which approved it with conditions. The American regulator has sued to stop it, and the case is in the court. The British decision is the one that the companies cannot route around. The tribunal has about nine months to rule, and the companies have said they would not close the deal without the British approval. The breakup fee would be about 2.9 billion dollars if the deal fails, and the two sides have been arguing about who owes it.

This is the second ruling in a year in which the British regulator has blocked a large technology deal. A watchdog that stops a purchase of 69 billion dollars is a watchdog that the dealmakers now have to plan around. The case is the first in which the cloud gaming has been treated as a market of its own, and the precedent is the part the industry will argue about. The shares of Activision fell about 11 per cent on the day, and the spread to the offer price widened to about 20 per cent.
