---

The price of a loaf of bread and the price of wheat are connected through a supply chain that takes time, and the connection is not symmetric. A study published this month analyzed scanner data from 12,000 grocery products over eight years and found a lag of five to seven months from commodity cost to shelf price, shorter on the way up than on the way down.

Asymmetry carries the finding. When wheat rose, shelf prices adjusted within five months; when it fell, the adjustment took seven, and the pass-through ratio was 0.7 on the way down against 0.9 on the way up.

Method is a panel regression with product and store fixed effects, using futures prices for the commodity input and the observed shelf price. The lag is extracted by testing the coefficient on a distributed lag of the input price.

Mechanisms proposed are two. Menu costs mean a retailer changes price infrequently, and a rise is worth a change while a small fall is not. Competitive structure means a fall in cost is an opportunity to widen margin where a local market has little competition.

Heterogeneity across categories is large. Fresh produce passes through within weeks because the retailer buys on spot; packaged goods pass through in months because contracts are annual. The lag varies by a factor of ten across the basket.

Implication for inflation measurement is practical. A consumer price index samples shelf prices monthly, and a lag of six months means the index trails the cost cycle by half a year. That lag is why core inflation can look sticky after commodity markets have turned.

The policy angle is about competition. The asymmetry is larger in categories with fewer competing brands, and the study's data show a 0.2 difference in pass-through ratio between categories with three or more brands and those with fewer.

Store-level variation is larger than the national average suggests. A discounter that changes prices weekly shows a lag of three months, and a supermarket that prints monthly tickets shows eight. The difference is the menu cost, which is a physical constraint on how often a price can move.

Promotions complicate the series. A temporary price cut is not a pass-through, and the study separates it by using the regular price from the shelf label rather than the promoted price.

The farmer's share is where the lag begins. A commodity's price reaches a processor in weeks and a shelf in months, and the study's data show that the processor's margin widens least in the categories with the most competition, which is consistent with the mechanism.

For a reader, the useful point is that a grocery price is a negotiated output rather than a mechanical reflection of an input. The lag and the asymmetry are properties of the retail market, and they explain why a fall in commodity prices reaches a shopper later than a rise.
