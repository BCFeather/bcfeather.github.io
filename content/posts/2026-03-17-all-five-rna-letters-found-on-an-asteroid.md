Ryugu is a carbonaceous asteroid that has never been heated enough to melt, and it has been sampled twice, first by remote observation and then by a spacecraft that brought material home. The material is the closest thing available to the unprocessed building blocks of the early solar system, and every analysis of it is a test of how much chemistry happened before biology did.

A team led by researchers at the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology examined the returned grains and detected all five nucleobases used in DNA and RNA: adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine and uracil. The paper appeared in *Nature Astronomy* on 17 March as an open-access article, and the finding extends earlier work on the same asteroid, which had reported uracil alone.

The significance is in the completeness. Finding one nucleobase in a meteorite is consistent with terrestrial contamination, because amino acids and nucleobases are common in laboratories, in dust and in the soil where a meteorite lies before it is collected. A single positive result therefore carries an argument as well as a measurement, and the argument is usually harder to win than the measurement. Finding the full set, in proportions that do not match a contamination profile, in material that was sealed inside a spacecraft, is a different kind of claim.

Previous sample-return missions complicate the picture in a useful way. Meteorites have yielded a wider variety of nucleobases than Ryugu has so far, and the samples from the asteroid Bennu have produced similar results. Different parent bodies, heated to different degrees, deliver different inventories, and the comparison is turning into a map of which chemical routes survive which histories, which is the sort of map that a mission planner can use when choosing a target.

The chemistry that produces nucleobases without life is well studied in the laboratory. Hydrogen cyanide, ammonia and water, under conditions that exist in the outer regions of a protoplanetary disc, form purines and pyrimidines, and the yields are modest but not negligible. The question has never been whether the chemistry can happen, since laboratory work settled that decades ago, but whether it happens often enough, in enough places, and survives long enough to matter on a planet that was molten for its first hundred million years.

Delivery is the part of the story that remains speculative. The Earth was molten for its first hundred million years, and any organic inventory it accreted early would have been sterilised. Late accretion of carbonaceous material, after the crust had cooled, is the standard explanation for how the ingredients arrived, and the Ryugu result is evidence that the material being delivered contained the right molecules.

None of this establishes that life began with asteroid chemistry. A nucleobase is not a nucleotide, a nucleotide is not a strand, and a strand is not a cell, and each step is a research programme rather than a paragraph. What the result does establish is that the raw alphabet was present, in a plausible delivery vehicle, at a time when the planet was ready to use it.
