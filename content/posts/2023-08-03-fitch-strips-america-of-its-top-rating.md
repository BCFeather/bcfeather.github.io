Fitch reduced the United States from AAA to AA+ on 1 August, the second downgrade in the country's history and the first from this agency. Its statement cited a steady deterioration of governance around the debt ceiling, the absence of a medium-term plan to control spending, and the growth of the debt burden relative to output. It also noted that the ceiling had been suspended rather than reformed, and that no plan existed to raise the revenue that would close the gap. The rating was cut two months after a standoff that took the government to the edge of default, which is the sort of timing that reads as a verdict on the standoff rather than on the arithmetic.

Janet Yellen disagreed in public and in writing. The Treasury secretary called the decision arbitrary, said the agency had relied on outdated data, and pointed out that the economy had strengthened over the year in which the rating was cut. Officials briefed reporters that the analysis ignored the administration's investments and the improvement in the deficit as a share of output. The disagreement is technical in form and political in effect, because a rating is an opinion and opinions about credit are worth what the market pays for them.

The market paid very little. Yields on ten-year Treasuries rose a few basis points and then carried on climbing for other reasons, the dollar stayed firm, and equity indexes fell for a day over growth worries that had nothing to do with Fitch. Investors had lived through 2011, when Standard and Poor's made the same move after a similar fight and stocks fell sharply before recovering within months, and they had learned that demand for American debt does not depend on a rating agency's opinion.

The text matters more than the ticker. Fitch's report describes a political system that resolves its fiscal arguments by creating crises and then postponing them, and it locates the problem in the process rather than in any single budget. That is the criticism that foreign holders of American debt have made for a decade, and the agency put it in a language that ratings committees understand. S&P kept its AA+ rating and Moody's kept its AAA, so the three agencies now disagree about the same borrower, which is unusual and instructive.

Debt service has become a budget line of its own. Interest costs passed defence spending in the past year, and the average rate on the federal debt rises each month as older bonds mature and are replaced. Neither party has campaigned on the measures that would change that trajectory, and both have campaigned against the other party's measures. The downgrade will be cited in the next fight and forgotten in the one after that.

Ratings estimate the probability of default, and the American government has never missed a payment. What the cut describes is a country that keeps promising to pay and keeps threatening not to, and a creditor that has decided to say so out loud.
