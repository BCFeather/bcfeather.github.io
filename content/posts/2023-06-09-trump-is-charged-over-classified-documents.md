A federal indictment unsealed on 8 June charged Donald Trump with thirty-seven counts arising from his retention of classified documents after he left office, including willful retention of national defence information and obstruction of justice. The charges were brought by a grand jury in Miami and signed by the special counsel appointed to investigate the case, and they describe documents that were kept in storerooms, in a bathroom, and on a stage at Trump's club in Florida, alongside newspaper clippings and photographs. It is the first federal criminal case ever brought against a former American president.

The indictment is unusually detailed for a charging document. It quotes a recording in which Trump describes a document as secret and says he can no longer declassify it, and it describes conversations with an aide and with lawyers in which he is said to have suggested that material be withheld from investigators. Photographs taken by the FBI show boxes of papers in a ballroom and in a storage room, and the indictment says the boxes were moved before a lawyer's search. The most serious counts carry a maximum of twenty years, though federal sentencing guidelines would produce far less in practice.

This case is a legal matter with a political calendar attached. Trump is also campaigning for the Republican nomination, and his supporters have described the charges as an attempt by the justice department to remove a rival from the race. The special counsel has said his work is independent, and the attorney general has said the same, and the difficulty is that the assertion has to be tested in a country where the prosecutor's decisions are read as political acts whatever their basis.

Retention of classified documents raises a wider question about how the American state keeps its secrets. Every administration since the second world war has struggled with the boundary between the president's records and the president's property, and the Presidential Records Act was written precisely to close the gap that Trump is accused of exploiting. The indictment alleges that he treated classified material as personal possessions and that his staff moved boxes without regard to where they went, which is a description of a filing system rather than of a conspiracy.

Any trial, if it happens before the election, will be held in a Florida courthouse in a state that Trump won twice, and the jury pool will be drawn from a region where his support is deep. His legal team will have every incentive to delay, and the prosecutors have every incentive to proceed, and both know that the timing may matter more than the law. The charges are grave; the country that will decide what they mean is divided; and the case now has a date on which the argument will be resolved, whatever the outcome.

No date has been set for trial, and the campaign has many. Between them sits a judge who will decide how much of the case the public hears before it votes, and a defendant who has built his candidacy on the claim that the case is the campaign.
