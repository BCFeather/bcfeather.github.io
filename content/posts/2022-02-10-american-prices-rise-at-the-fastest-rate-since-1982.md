Consumer prices in the United States were 7.5 per cent higher in January than a year earlier, the labour department reported on 10 February, which is the fastest rate since February 1982. The figure excluding food and energy, which economists watch to see where the trend is going, rose 6 per cent. Energy costs were up 27 per cent on the year, petrol 40 per cent, and used cars and trucks 40.5 per cent, and food cost 7 per cent more than it had twelve months before.

The monthly change was 0.6 per cent, which is slower than the autumn but well above the rate that would bring the annual figure back towards the target. Rents, which carry a large weight in the index and move slowly, were rising at the fastest pace in twenty years. Wages rose about 5 per cent over the same period, so the average worker lost ground, and the loss fell hardest on households that spend most of their income on fuel and food.

Policy had already begun to turn. The Federal Reserve finished buying bonds in March and had signalled that the first increase in its policy rate would come at its meeting in the middle of the month, and the market expected a quarter of a percentage point with more to follow. The central bank had spent two years describing the rise in prices as transitory, and the word had gone out of its statements in the autumn. Its chair, Jerome Powell, said in January that the economy no longer needed emergency support.

Politics attached itself to the number at once. The White House said the figures showed the effect of the pandemic on supply chains and of the oil price, and the opposition said the spending of the previous year had put money into an economy that could not produce the goods to match. Both were partly right, and neither explanation was complete: the fiscal support raised demand, and the shortage of ships, chips and workers limited supply, and the two met in the same index.

Elsewhere the same arithmetic ran. Inflation in the euro area reached 5.1 per cent in January, and the Bank of England had raised its rate twice by the time the American figure appeared. The price of oil was above 90 dollars a barrel and would pass 100 within two weeks, and the cost of shipping a container from Asia to Europe was several times its level before the pandemic.

What follows from a 7.5 per cent print is a sequence of decisions rather than an event. The Fed's rate path steepened through the spring, mortgage rates passed 5 per cent by the summer, and the growth that had carried the recovery slowed in the second half of the year. The inflation of 2022 turned out to be the highest for four decades and the most politically consequential number of the year, and it began to fall only when the energy shock faded and the supply chains reopened.
