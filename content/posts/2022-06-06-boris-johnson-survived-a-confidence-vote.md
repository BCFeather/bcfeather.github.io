Conservative members of parliament voted on the evening of 6 June on whether to keep Boris Johnson as their leader, and he won by 211 votes to 148. Under the party's rules, a prime minister facing such a ballot needs a simple majority of the parliamentary party, and the result was read out in a committee room shortly after nine o'clock. He remained in office, and the number against him became the figure that every later story about his leadership would cite. The result was announced to journalists in a corridor of the Palace of Westminster, and the prime minister spoke outside Downing Street an hour later. He gave no interview that evening.

The ballot had been triggered by letters from at least fifty-four MPs, fifteen per cent of the party in the Commons, and it was held within a day of the threshold being reached. Johnson's supporters argued that a win was a win and that no alternative candidate commanded a majority, while his critics pointed out that forty-one per cent of his own members had voted to remove him, which is worse than the result that ended the leadership of Theresa May.

Grievances had accumulated for months. Fines issued by the police for gatherings in Downing Street during lockdowns, a report by a senior civil servant that described a culture of drinking and parties in the building where the rules were written, and a series of statements that turned out to be inaccurate had together exhausted the patience of a section of the party. The vote was about those events rather than about policy. Several ministerial aides had resigned earlier in the year over the same issue.

What kept him in place was the absence of an agreed successor and the fear of an early general election. Several potential candidates had declared that they would not stand, and MPs who disliked the prime minister privately calculated that a change of leader in the middle of a war in Europe and a cost-of-living crisis would look like a party settling its own affairs while the country waited for help. The mood of the party was reported in every newspaper the next morning.

Consequences followed quickly. Two parliamentary by-elections later in June returned majorities against the Conservatives in seats the party had held for decades, and the prime minister's authority in the Commons weakened on issues that required discipline, including the protocol governing trade with Northern Ireland. Ministers who had defended him in public began to distance themselves from the more personal parts of the story.

A confidence vote settles a procedure and rarely settles a question. Johnson remained prime minister, the letters that had triggered the ballot remained on file, and the rule that gave him twelve months of immunity from a second challenge meant that the party had either to live with him or to change the rule. Both options were discussed openly by MPs on the evening of the result. The arithmetic of the vote was left for the party's annual conference to settle.
