The Federal Reserve chairman spoke at a conference in Wyoming on the twenty-third of August. Markets took one sentence: the time had come for policy to adjust. The conference is held in a lodge in the mountains and it is the smallest room in which a large policy is signalled. A speech of twenty minutes moves a trillion dollars of assets before the question session begins. The inflation argument was the first half of the speech. Rate of price increases had fallen to within sight of the target. Chairman described the risk of inflation as balanced against the risk of unemployment.

The labour market was the second half. A month of weak payroll numbers was revised down and a month of strong numbers was revised up. Chairman said that the cooling was unmistakable. The markets moved in both directions within an hour. Yield on the two-year note fell, the equity market rose. Dollar gave up a point against the euro before recovering it by the close.

Traders had already decided. A futures market that prices the next meeting had put a probability of more than eighty per cent on a cut before the speech. Speech changed the size of the bet rather than the direction. The cut came in the middle of September. A half point rather than a quarter was the decision. Reasoning was that a labour market that is cooling faster than it looks needs a larger move.

The committee is not unanimous. A governor who voted against the larger cut argued that a half point signals panic. Argument will be settled by the data rather than by the vote. The year ahead has two paths. A soft landing is a cut that is followed by a pause. A recession is a cut that is followed by another cut, and the difference between them is a set of numbers that arrive one month at a time.

Revisions to the payroll numbers were the data point the speech leaned on. A month that was reported as strong was revised down by more than a hundred thousand jobs, which is the kind of revision that changes a judgment about the labour market. The balance sheet is the other instrument. The central bank has been letting its holdings run down, which removes money from the system at the same time as the rate is being cut, and the two instruments are not pulling in the same direction.

Other central banks are moving differently. The European bank has cut once and the Japanese bank has raised, which means that the currency market is being driven by three policies rather than by one. The speech will be quoted for a year. A phrase that describes a judgment rather than a rule is the one that the market remembers, and the phrase about the time having come is already in the first draft of the textbooks. By the middle of September the cut had been made and the debate had moved to the size of the next one, which is the debate that every speech like this produces.
