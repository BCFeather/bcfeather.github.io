Myeloma treatment has improved enough that most patients now live five years, and the improvement has hidden a subgroup that does not. Between a fifth and a quarter of patients carry disease that relapses within a year of the best standard regimen, and their outcomes have barely moved in two decades. Trials that enrol myeloma generally enrol everyone, and a benefit that is real for the average patient can leave this group untouched. Classification schemes exist for exactly this purpose, and their weakness is that they are applied after the first relapse in most health systems.

The OPTIMUM trial took the opposite approach. Investigators at the Royal Marsden and the Institute of Cancer Research assigned treatment according to a risk classification, giving high-risk patients an intensified regimen built around the biology that makes their disease aggressive, and followed them for six years. The results appeared in *The Lancet Oncology* on 8 September, with funding from Myeloma UK and the David Forbes Nixon Foundation. The trial's design also meant that every participant received a regimen chosen for their disease rather than a single protocol applied to all comers.

Almost seven in ten patients on the trial, 69.1 per cent, were alive at around six years, against roughly 40 per cent on standard therapy. Progression-free survival held at 53.8 per cent at six years, against 17.6 per cent in a matched external control group drawn from the Myeloma XI trial. Median survival was not reached in the trial arm, while the comparison group reached about 57 months. A population of 69 per cent alive at six years is unusual in myeloma, where historical cohorts report survival curves that flatten much lower.

The subgroup analysis carries the mechanistic argument. Patients identified as high-risk by gene expression profiling with the MMProfiler SKY92 test, who would not have been flagged by conventional staging, had 62.3 per cent alive and progression-free at six years, against 20.3 per cent on conventional treatment. That difference is large enough to suggest the classification itself, not only the drugs, is doing the work.

An external control is the design's weak joint. Patients in the trial were selected, consented and treated at specialist centres, and a matched cohort from another trial cannot balance every prognostic factor, including the ones nobody has measured. The comparison is a reasonable inference and not a randomised result, and the paper is explicit that the control group is historical. Investigators have published the classification before the regimen, which is the right order for a strategy that depends on diagnosis.

What the trial implies for practice is a diagnostic question as much as a therapeutic one. Gene expression profiling is not routine in the National Health Service, so many of these patients are currently classified as standard risk and receive standard treatment. A test that identifies them would change what they are offered, and the cost of the test is small next to the cost of a regimen that fails.
