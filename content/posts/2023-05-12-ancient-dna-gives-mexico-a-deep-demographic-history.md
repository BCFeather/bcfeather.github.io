description: "Genomes from ancient Mexicans show a long record of isolation, admixture and migration rather than a single founding population, according to an analysis published in *Science* on 12 May 2023."
---

A region's demographic history is written in its genomes, and the writing is legible only if the genomes span the region's whole depth. A study of living people sees the survivors of every earlier event.

The analysis in *Science*, 12 May 2023 reads ancient genomes from Mexico. The authors sequence individuals from across the region and from across millennia, and compare them with each other and with living populations.

The result is a history with structure. The earliest lineages persist in some regions and disappear from others, and later arrivals mix with the earlier ones rather than replacing them.

The admixture is directional. A migrating group contributes more to the receiving population than it receives, and the asymmetry shows which direction the movement went.

The isolation is the other thread. Some regions' populations remained distinct for thousands of years, which suggests that geography, rather than distance alone, structured the gene pool.

The method is the standard one for ancient DNA. Contamination is controlled with overlapping reads and with damage patterns, and the analysis of shared segments identifies relatedness.

The linguistic correlation is suggestive. A genetic boundary that coincides with a language family's edge is evidence that the two histories are linked, and the paper notes the coincidence without claiming a cause.

The limitation is the sample's geography. A collection that is dense in one valley and sparse in another describes the first valley better, and the authors' coverage map is the honest statement of that.

What the paper does not resolve is the earliest entry. A genome from the oldest layer is a genome that still has ancestors outside the record, and the entry date remains a question for archaeology.

For the region's history, the value is a correction. A narrative of one founding population followed by one conquest is a simplification, and the genomes support a longer and more plural account.

The colonial period is a separate layer. European and African genomes enter the record after the sixteenth century, and the paper's separation of the pre-colonial structure from the later admixture is what keeps the two histories distinct.

The museum specimens are the enabler. A tooth or a petrous bone yields enough endogenous DNA for a genome, and the collections that were assembled for other purposes are the archive that makes this work possible.
The sample's age range is the study's frame. A genome from each millennium is a point on a trajectory, and a trajectory needs points on both sides of an event. The authors' oldest individuals are the ones that constrain the entry, and their youngest are the ones that connect to the present.
The population size is an inference the genomes support. Diversity within a genome reflects the size of the population that produced it, and a lineage that passed through a bottleneck carries the signature. The paper's estimates of size are drawn from that signature.
