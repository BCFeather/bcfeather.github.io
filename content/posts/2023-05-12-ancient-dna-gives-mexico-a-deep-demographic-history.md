A region's demographic history is written in its genomes, and the writing is legible only if the genomes span the region's whole depth. A study of living people sees the survivors of every earlier event.

In *Science* on 12 May 2023, ancient genomes from Mexico are read. The authors sequence individuals from across the region and across millennia, and compare them with each other and with living populations.

Structure runs through the history. The earliest lineages persist in some regions and disappear from others, and later arrivals mix with the earlier ones rather than replacing them. Admixture is directional, since a migrating group contributes more to the receiving population than it receives, and that asymmetry shows which way the movement went.

Isolation is the other thread. Some populations stayed distinct for thousands of years, which suggests geography rather than distance alone structured the gene pool. Method stays standard for ancient DNA, because contamination is controlled with overlapping reads and damage patterns while shared segments identify relatedness, and the age range is the frame, since a genome from each millennium is a point on a trajectory and a trajectory needs points on both sides of an event.

Language offers a suggestive correlation, because a genetic boundary coinciding with a language family's edge indicates the two histories are linked, and the paper notes the coincidence without claiming a cause. Population size is an inference the genomes support, since diversity within a genome reflects the size of the population that produced it and a lineage that passed a bottleneck carries the signature.

Geography sets the sample's limit. A collection dense in one valley and sparse in another describes the first valley better, and the coverage map states that honestly. Museum specimens are the enabler, because a tooth or a petrous bone yields enough endogenous DNA for a genome, and collections assembled for other purposes become the archive.

Earliest entry stays unresolved, as a genome from the oldest layer still has ancestors outside the record, which leaves the date a question for archaeology. The colonial period forms a separate layer, since European and African genomes enter after the sixteenth century, and separating pre-colonial structure from later admixture keeps two histories distinct. For the region, the value is a correction: a narrative of one founding population followed by one conquest is a simplification, and these genomes support a longer and more plural account. A denser transect through the highlands would test whether the isolation reported here is a property of geography or an artefact of where the samples happened to be collected, and a dated genome from the coast could settle whether the earliest lineages arrived by one route or by several. Dates carry the argument, since a genome without a reliable age is a genome that cannot be placed on the trajectory, and the paper's radiocarbon work is what makes the sequence of events a sequence.
