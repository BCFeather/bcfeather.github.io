Carbon chemistry on the icy Galilean moons has been inferred rather than measured, because the bands that identify carbon-bearing species sit in a part of the infrared that needs a large telescope above the atmosphere to resolve. Callisto is the outermost of the four and the least processed by charged particles, which makes its surface a comparatively gentle record of what the moon started with and what the plasma around Jupiter has since made of it.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 30 January 2024 reports spectral cubes of both hemispheres. The observations used the NIRSpec integral field unit and its G395H grating on the James Webb Space Telescope, which returns spatially resolved spectra across a wavelength range that contains the carbon dioxide bands at 4.25 and 4.38 microns and the emission lines of the gas between 4.2 and 4.3 microns.

The solid bands are strong and unevenly placed. Absorption from carbon dioxide ice peaks at low latitudes near the centre of the trailing hemisphere, and a weaker 4.38 micron feature, which the authors attribute to the carbon-13 isotopologue, sits in the same region. A 4.57 micron band, plausibly from carbon-bearing organics with a nitrile group, is markedly stronger on the leading hemisphere, so the two features are spatially anti-associated, not co-located.

Gas is present above both hemispheres. Rovibrational emission between 4.2 and 4.3 microns appears on the leading and trailing sides alike, which is the first detection of carbon dioxide gas over the trailing side and a confirmation that the tenuous atmosphere is global, not patchy. The distribution is offset from the subsolar point on each hemisphere, which is where a purely thermal explanation would put the peak.

Production follows from that offset. Radiolysis, driven by magnetospheric plasma hitting native water ice mixed with carbonaceous material, makes carbon dioxide in the surface, and the strongest ice band sits where the plasma flux is heaviest. Sputtering then lifts the molecule into the atmosphere, which explains why gas appears away from the warmest region and why the atmosphere survives on a body too small to hold it gravitationally.

What the 4.57 micron band means stays open. The authors read its leading-side concentration as evidence for material native to the moon or for dust accumulated from Jupiter's irregular satellites, which would make it a remnant of accretion rather than a product of irradiation, and the difference in its placement from the carbon dioxide band is the argument for treating the two as separate reservoirs.

Several things are unresolved. The features are weak enough that abundance ratios are not quoted, other candidate assignments exist for the subtler bands, including carbon monoxide, carbonyl sulfide and sodium-bearing minerals, and linking a surface spectrum to an atmospheric column needs laboratory measurements that have not been made. NASA's Europa Clipper and the European Space Agency's JUICE will arrive with instruments built for exactly those questions, and the paper presents its spectra as preparation for them.
