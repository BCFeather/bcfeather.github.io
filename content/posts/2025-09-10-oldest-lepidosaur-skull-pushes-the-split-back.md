description: "A Middle Triassic skull from Devon belongs to the tuatara branch of the family tree and is older than any lepidosaur previously known."
---

Lizards and snakes number around 12,000 species, and the tuatara of New Zealand is the single survivor of the other branch of the same family tree. The two branches split during the Triassic, and the fossil record has been unhelpful about when: many specimens are lepidosauromorphs that belong to neither branch, so the divergence has lacked a reliable anchor. The group includes lizards, snakes and the tuatara, and the last common ancestor of the two branches lived well before the end of the Permian.

A skull and partial skeleton from the Middle Triassic Helsby Sandstone Formation of Devon supplies one. The authors assign the specimen to a new rhynchocephalian on the evidence of the dentition, the palate and the braincase, and a phylogenetic analysis places it inside the tuatara branch instead of outside both branches, which is the placement that earlier fossils could not support. The specimen retains the skull roof, both lower jaws and much of the vertebral column, which lets the scoring proceed without reconstruction.

Date matters as much as identity. The specimen is around 3 to 7 million years older than the oldest lepidosaur previously described, so the divergence between squamates and rhynchocephalians moves earlier, and both branches can be shown to exist by the Anisian stage of the Middle Triassic. Anisian age places the animal within a few million years of the Permian boundary, when the first archosaurs were also appearing. The authors correlate the sandstone with marine beds that carry conodonts.

The anatomy also tests predictions made from living animals. A non-mobile skull, an open lower temporal bar and the absence of large palatine teeth are what a primitive rhynchocephalian should show, and the specimen has all three. The jaw apparatus suggests an insect specialist, which fits the picture of small-bodied animals feeding on arthropods. Tooth wear on the dentary points to a gripping jaw instead of a crushing one, and the palate lacks the broad tooth plates of herbivorous relatives.

Palaeontologists have described a Triassic Revolution, the period when modern-style terrestrial ecosystems took shape. A lepidosaur with a feeding adaptation of its own, present early in that period, supports the argument that the revolution involved diversification within lineages as well as replacement of older ones. The argument rests on several lineages coexisting with distinct feeding modes, which is what a modern ecosystem looks like. In the Devon assemblage that coexistence appears within a single formation.

One specimen carries the weight of the argument, and the skeleton is incomplete. Character coding decides the phylogenetic position, and a different treatment of the temporal region could move the animal. The stratigraphic dating is solid but the regional record is thin, so the exact age of the split remains a range until more skulls of the same age turn up. A second skull from the same bed would settle the coding question, and the authors say the material is being prepared.
