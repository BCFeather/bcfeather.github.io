---

Metabolic acidosis in a patient with shock is a signal that the circulation is failing, and clinicians have given sodium bicarbonate for decades on the reasoning that correcting the pH will improve the response to vasopressors and to inotropes. The evidence for that reasoning has always been thin. The two commonest settings are sepsis and cardiogenic shock, and in both the lactate that drives the acidosis is produced by tissue that is not being perfused. The sickest patients are the ones most likely to receive it, and confounding by indication is why an observational signal has persisted for so long.

SODa-BIC investigators, with the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society Clinical Trials Group, randomized critically ill adults with metabolic acidosis and shock to sodium bicarbonate or placebo, and reported the result in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 12 June.

In-hospital mortality by day 30 was 25.4 per cent with bicarbonate and 24.0 per cent with placebo, an adjusted difference of 1.8 percentage points with a confidence interval from minus 5.6 to 9.2. The interval spans zero and extends in both directions, so the trial excludes a large benefit without establishing equivalence. A confidence interval that wide also means the trial cannot exclude a small benefit, which is the honest description of a neutral result in critical care. Enrolment required a pH below a protocol threshold alongside vasopressor support, so the trial population is narrower than the ward population that receives the drug.

Adverse effects were few, with four patients in the bicarbonate group and none in the placebo group, which means the intervention is safe enough that the question is about benefit rather than about harm. A therapy that does nothing and costs little is still a therapy that consumes attention and fluid. Hypernatraemia and hypercapnia are the mechanisms by which bicarbonate can do harm, and the low adverse-event count suggests the doses used were modest. Fluid loading can produce the same sodium load, which is one reason the comparison is not simply against doing nothing.

Physiology explains the result more convincingly than the statistics do. Correcting a number does not restore perfusion, and the acidosis in shock is a consequence of hypoperfusion rather than its cause in most cases. Treating the marker leaves the mechanism untouched. Vasopressor dose, lactate clearance and urine output are the variables that actually track recovery, and none of them responds to bicarbonate.

De-implementation is the practice consequence, and it is harder than adoption. Bicarbonate is given routinely in many units, and a neutral trial changes behaviour only when it is paired with a protocol that says what to do instead. The trial's value is that it makes the routine habit a choice with evidence against it. De-implementation needs a visible alternative, and in this case the alternative is treating the cause: fluid, vasopressors and source control.
