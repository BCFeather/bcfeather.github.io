James Webb found them in its first year and nobody could agree on what they were. The objects are compact, red, numerous at high redshift and bright enough to be conspicuous, and two explanations competed: young galaxies full of stars, or unexpectedly massive black holes accreting gas at a rate that would strain the physics of early growth. Both explanations strained something, which is usually a sign that neither is right. Two explanations rarely survive a measurement this clean, and the satisfaction of the result lies in how narrowly it separates them.

Vadim Rusakov and colleagues analysed the highest-quality hydrogen spectra available for these objects, which is the measurement that distinguishes the two stories. Hydrogen emission lines broaden for identifiable reasons, and the shape of the broadening carries the signature of the gas doing the emitting. The analysis appeared in *Nature* on 15 January and was the cover of the issue.

The answer is neither of the original options, and it borrows a piece of each. The little red dots are young supermassive black holes, much smaller than the mass estimates that made the first explanation attractive, and the light does not come directly from gas falling onto them. It comes from a dense cocoon of electrons surrounding the black hole, where scattering rather than bulk motion produces the broadened lines.

That distinction matters because the two mechanisms imply different physics and different masses. Doppler broadening comes from gas moving fast, which requires a deep gravitational well and therefore a large black hole. Scattering broadening comes from photons bouncing repeatedly through a dense medium, which requires a lot of gas and no particular mass. Reading the line profile correctly rescues the black hole masses from the implausible values that had been inferred.

If the objects are small black holes in thick cocoons, they are also inefficient radiators, since much of the energy is reprocessed by the surrounding gas before it escapes. That changes how the objects fit into the early universe's energy budget, and it changes how they appear at different wavelengths, which is why the red colour is consistent with the model and not incidental to it.

The work is an analysis of spectra, not a resolved image, and the inference rests on radiative transfer models of the cocoon and on assumptions about its geometry. Those models have parameters, and a fit that reproduces a line profile is evidence and not proof. Independent groups will test the interpretation against objects at different redshifts, where the cocoon properties should differ. A model that fits one line profile and fails at another redshift would be checked quickly, and the next dataset is already being taken.

What the result does establish is that the debate has a resolution and that it was hiding in the line shape. Astronomy often advances this way, by finding the one measurement that separates two hypotheses which both fit the broadband data, and the red dots have now supplied theirs.
