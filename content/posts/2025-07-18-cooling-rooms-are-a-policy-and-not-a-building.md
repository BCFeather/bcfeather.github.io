title: "Cooling rooms are a policy and not a building"
date: "2025-07-18T14:05:00+08:00"

description: "Municipal cooling centres open every summer and are used by a fraction of the people at risk, because the obstacle to escaping heat is often transport, money or isolation and not the absence of a cold room."
---

Every summer a city opens a library, a sports hall or a community centre as a cooling room and every summer the attendance is lower than the number of people who die of heat, and the gap between those two numbers is the subject that heat planning has not solved.

Heat kills indoors. Most excess deaths in a heatwave happen at home, in apartments without cross ventilation, among people who are elderly, chronically ill or living alone, which means a policy that counts only the people who arrive at a centre counts the wrong population and reports a success that the mortality data will contradict. A room that is open from nine to five is a room that serves the people who are already mobile, and the people who die are, by the pattern of every mortality study, the ones who are not.

Access is the first barrier. A centre at the end of a bus route is useless to someone who cannot walk to the stop, and a centre that closes at six is useless to someone whose risk begins when the flat heats up in the evening, so siting and hours decide usage more than capacity does.

Cost is the second. Air conditioning is a recurring bill, some households ration it, and a means-tested payment that arrives in September does not help in July, which is why direct assistance and a tariff that does not punish consumption during a health alert are the instruments that actually reach people. A payment that arrives after the bill is a payment that has been financed by the household at the worst possible moment, and the design of the transfer matters more than its size. A centre that opens at nine and closes at five also misses the evening peak of indoor temperature, which arrives three or four hours after the outdoor peak and lasts until the small hours.

Isolation is the third and the least visible. A neighbour who knocks, a general practitioner who calls, a list of residents over 80 that the municipality keeps updated, these are the mechanisms that find the person who will not go to a centre because there is nobody to tell them to.

Design is the long answer and the slow one. Trees, shading, reflective surfaces, night ventilation and a building code that treats overheating as a defect would reduce the exposure of everyone in the street and not only that of the few who come to a room, and they are the measures that a council can fund from a capital budget. A planting programme that takes ten years to shade a street is a programme that a council can start now and be judged on later, which is the timescale that heat policy actually has.

What would settle the argument is whether cities start counting heat deaths the way they count traffic deaths. A number published every year, with a map and an age breakdown, would tell a council where to plant trees, and the absence of that number is why the policy keeps producing buildings and not outcomes.
