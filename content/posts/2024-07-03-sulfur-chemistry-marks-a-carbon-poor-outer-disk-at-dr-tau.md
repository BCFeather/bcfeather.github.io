title: "Sulfur chemistry marks a carbon-poor outer disk at DR Tau"
date: "2024-07-03T14:25:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "New millimetre observations of the DR Tau disk find the lowest carbon-to-sulfur contrast reported for a T Tauri disk, and thermochemical models read it as a gas-phase carbon-to-oxygen ratio below one."
---

The outer disk of a young star is cold enough that carbon and oxygen lock into different molecules, and the ratio of the two in the gas is what decides which species dominate the millimetre spectrum. A carbon-rich gas produces strong hydrocarbon emission and a high ratio of carbon monosulfide to sulfur monoxide, while the reverse holds when carbon is scarce. The balance is struck where the disk is cold enough for water ice to form, since freezing oxygen out of the gas leaves carbon in the molecules that radiate.

DR Tau has looked carbon-rich by that test, which is why the disk was observed in detail. A preprint posted to arXiv on 1 July 2024 reports NOEMA observations of \(\mathrm{CS}\), \(\mathrm{SO}\), \(\mathrm{C}_{2}\mathrm{H}\), \(\mathrm{HCN}\), \(\mathrm{HCO}^{+}\) and \(\mathrm{H}^{13}\mathrm{CO}^{+}\) in the disk, at a beam of about 0.4 arcseconds, or 80 au. The lines trace gas at radii of tens of astronomical units, where the emission is optically thin enough that a ratio of two channels reports on the chemistry.

The result goes the other way. The disk-averaged ratio of \(\mathrm{CS}\) to \(\mathrm{SO}\) falls between 0.4 and 0.5, the lowest value reported for a T Tauri disk, and the hydrocarbon emission is faint rather than strong. The ratio is the standard diagnostic, so a low value in a disk previously called carbon-rich challenges the classification.

A clump complicates the picture. At a projected separation of about 180 au north-east of the star, the \(\mathrm{SO}\) maps show a feature with no counterpart in the other lines, and the local \(\mathrm{CS}\) to \(\mathrm{SO}\) ratio drops below 0.2 there. Such a feature is what a localized chemical or dynamical event would look like in a map that traces sulfur alone.

Thermochemical modelling explains the averages. Models computed with DALI reproduce both the low ratio and the faint \(\mathrm{C}_{2}\mathrm{H}\) emission when the gas-phase ratio of carbon to oxygen is below one at the radii the observations trace, so the disk is carbon-poor in the region that the lines sample. The carbon-to-oxygen ratio is the one number the models tune, and a value below one is what the observed line ratios require.

Infall is the likely cause. Comparing the \(\mathrm{SO}\) emission with extended structure traced by \(\mathrm{^{13}CO}\) suggests that late infall of carbon-poor material is diluting the outer disk, which would lower the ratio over the radii where the infalling gas lands. Infalling gas with little carbon would mix into the upper layers first, which is the region where the sulfur-bearing lines form.

What the observation cannot yet do is separate the two explanations of the clump. A localized enhancement of \(\mathrm{SO}\) could come from a shock where infalling gas meets the disk, or from a region where the dust has released sulfur, and a map at higher resolution would tell which.
