---Archaeology has long inferred weaning from nitrogen isotopes in bone collagen, which average over years, or from the age at death of infants, which says nothing about diet. A paper in *Science Advances* on 7 April reports a method with monthly resolution: sampling enamel in thin layers along the tooth's growth axis and reading the barium-to-calcium ratio in each layer.

The chemistry rests on a simple fact. Barium is abundant in soil and in plants that take it up, and it is nearly absent from breast milk, because the mammary gland excludes it. A child who is breastfed accumulates little barium; a child eating solid food accumulates a lot. Enamel forms in daily increments that are not remodelled afterwards, so each layer preserves the ratio at the moment it mineralized.

The authors measured 34 children from two sites, one in the Near East and one in northern China, using laser ablation coupled to inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, with a spot size of about 30 micrometres. Position along the tooth was converted to age using known enamel formation rates for the deciduous first molar, calibrated against histological sections from a reference collection.

Results place weaning between 18 and 26 months across the sample, with the transition taking four to seven months rather than ending abruptly. The Chinese site showed an earlier start and a longer overlap between breast milk and solid food, which the authors connect to the availability of pre-chewed cereal foods, though they flag this as inference rather than measurement.

Method limits are worth naming. Enamel formation rates differ between tooth types and between individuals, so the age axis carries an uncertainty of two to three months; diagenesis can exchange ions with the burial environment over millennia, which is why the team screened sections for post-mortem alteration under polarized light and excluded 9 of 43 specimens. Sample size is small for any population claim.

What makes the paper useful is that it converts a single tooth into a calendar. Weaning age is one of the few life-history variables that can be read from remains, and it predicts fertility intervals, child mortality and the division of labour within a household. Getting it to monthly resolution is the difference between saying a community weaned late and saying when, in a child's life, the change happened.

A second limitation is dietary. Barium tracks the intake of solids in general, not the intake of a specific food, so the method locates a transition without identifying what was eaten. A community that introduced gruel at fourteen months and meat at twenty-four shows one rise, not two.

The Comparison with collagen isotopes is instructive. Nitrogen-15 enrichment in bone collagen averages over the several years a bone remodels, which is why the classical weaning literature reports ages as ranges of three to five years. Enamel does not remodel, so a single tooth carries a record that bone cannot, at the cost of being tied to the formation schedule of whichever tooth happens to be preserved.

A reasonable reading is that this is a technical advance with a modest but real archaeological payoff, not a revolution. The next step, the authors write, is a reference collection of known-age teeth from modern populations so that formation rates can be measured rather than assumed.
