description: "Foreign ministers met in Brussels on 4 April for the alliance's seventy-fifth anniversary and discussed a five-year fund for Ukraine worth about one hundred billion dollars, along with a larger role for NATO in coordinating arms deliveries."
---

Foreign ministers of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization met in Brussels on the fourth of April to mark the seventy-fifth anniversary of the treaty signed in Washington in 1949 by twelve countries. The alliance now has thirty-two members, after Finland joined in 2023 and Sweden in March, and its secretary general, Jens Stoltenberg, used the meeting to argue for a change in the way the alliance supports Ukraine.

Proposal at the centre of the discussion was a fund of about one hundred billion dollars over five years, to be counted partly against existing national commitments rather than added on top of them. Stoltenberg said the alliance needed a structure that would survive changes of government in member states, and he said the arrangement should be agreed before the summit in Washington in July. Several ministers said the sum was ambitious and that the accounting question was the difficult part.

Second element of the proposal was a shift in coordination. American officials have run the logistics of arms deliveries through a command based in Wiesbaden, and the alliance has offered to take over part of that work under a new mission that would be staffed by NATO personnel. The advantage is that the mechanism would continue if American support faltered, and the disadvantage is that it draws the alliance more directly into a war that some members describe as a war they are supporting rather than fighting.

Political context of the anniversary is the American election. Stoltenberg was asked repeatedly about the possibility that the United States would reduce its commitment, and he said the alliance had survived disagreements before and that the answer was to spend more on European defence. European members have increased spending since 2022, and about two thirds of them now meet the target of two per cent of economic output, which was agreed in 2014 and which the alliance has cited as evidence that burden sharing is working.

Members with objections made themselves heard. Hungary's foreign minister repeated his government's opposition to sending weapons and said the alliance should seek a ceasefire. Slovakia's new government had stopped arms deliveries from its own stocks before the meeting and said it would not support the fund. Turkey linked its approval of various NATO measures to its own requests, which is a pattern that has held since Sweden's accession.

What has stayed the same is the difficulty of persuading democracies to pay for a defence they hope never to use. Officials said the fund would be reviewed at the summit in Washington in July, and that no decision would be taken before then.

Meaning of the fourth of April is an alliance that has enlarged and rearmed without settling the question of what it is willing to spend. The anniversary produced statements about unity and a proposal that will be negotiated for three months, and it left open whether the fund will be new money or a rearrangement of commitments that already exist. What has changed since 1949 is the number of members and the location of the threat.
