The Wadden Sea is a coast where the tide moves the sediment twice a day, and the order the sediment ends up in is a sorting problem. A rising tide carries sand and silt over a flat that is dry at low water, and the current slows as the flat shallows, so the coarsest grains drop first and the finest travel furthest. The result is a floor of sand bars and mud flats arranged in bands, and the bands shift with every storm. The sea is shallow enough that a person can walk across it at low tide, which is why it has a history of people walking across it. A flat that is exposed for six hours and a flat that is exposed for four hold different faunas, and the difference is visible in the counts. A tide that runs over a flat for six hours leaves a ripple pattern in the sand that marks where the current began to slow.

Flats are a nursery and a larder. The mud holds a fauna of worms and bivalves that the birds eat, and the birds that come to the Wadden in spring and autumn are counted in the millions. A flat that has been disturbed by dredging loses the fauna for years, and the birds move to the next flat, which is why the counts are a measure of the whole sea and not of one site. The sea has been a staging ground for migratory birds for as long as the flyway has existed. A bird that arrives to find its flat covered has to move, and the energy it spends moving is energy it does not spend on the flight north. A dredger that works a flat removes the worms with the sediment, and the recovery is measured in years rather than in tides.

Islands are the same sediment, sorted differently. A barrier island is a dune core with a beach in front of it, and the storms that arrive from the north-west cut into the beach and move the sand along the shore. The island that is not fed with sand migrates, and the island that is fed grows, and the difference is a matter of the tidal inlets that change their position. The villages on the islands are maintained by engineering that pumps sand onto the beach, and the sand is taken from the same sea that moves it. A beach that is nourished with sand from further offshore is a beach that is maintained, and the maintenance is a budget line in every coastal state. A village that is protected by a dike is a village that is protected at the expense of the flat outside it.

Land and dikes enclose the sea, and the enclosure is the history of the region. Storm surges that flooded the coastal plains killed thousands of people, and the response was to build a wall and then to build a bigger one. A dike that is built across an inlet changes the tides behind it, and a basin that no longer fills loses its flats and its birds. The compromise that has been adopted is a dike with a gap that closes only in a storm, and the gap is the part of the engineering that the ecologists asked for. A gap that opens in a storm and closes afterwards is a gap that lets the tide in for a few hours, which is exactly the regime the flats need. A basin that is held at a fixed level has no tide, and a basin without a tide accumulates fine sediment that nothing sorts.

The water is also warming and rising. A mud flat that is covered for longer at each tide is a flat that the birds cannot use, and a rise in sea level that outruns the accretion of sediment drowns the flats. The sediment supply comes from the rivers that flow into the sea and from the North Sea itself, and both have been reduced by the engineering of the coast. The accretion can keep pace with a moderate rise and not with a fast one, and the rate is the number the management plans are written against. A flat that accretes faster than the sea rises keeps its birds, and a flat that lags loses them to the next estuary with a better budget. A rise of a few millimetres a year is within the range the accretion can match, and a rise of a centimetre a year is not.

What the sea shows is a coast that is an engine. The sediment is moved by the tide, sorted by the current and held by the vegetation that grows on the flats, and the ecology follows the sediment. A coast that is fixed with concrete stops moving and stops sorting, and the flats that were the reason for the protection are the first thing that the protection removes. An engine that is driven by the tide has no operator, and that is the argument for letting it run. A coast that is left alone is a coast that moves, and the movement is what keeps the ecology in place.
