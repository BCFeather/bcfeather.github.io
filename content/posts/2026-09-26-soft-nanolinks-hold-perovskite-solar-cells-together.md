Perovskite solar cells reached efficiencies that silicon took forty years to approach, and then ran into the problem that the material is soft. A crystal held together by ionic bonds and a volatile organic cation decomposes under heat and moisture, and the interfaces where it meets the transport layers are where the decomposition starts. Encapsulation helps and cannot fix an interface that is mechanically mismatched from the beginning.

A paper published in *Nature Photonics* on 29 September describes a trans-interface treatment that addresses the mismatch. The authors create vertically aligned soft nanolinks that span the perovskite and the adjacent charge-transport layer, rather than treating each layer separately, and the links are designed to accommodate the strain that builds up when the stack is heated and cooled. Cells built this way had a certified power conversion efficiency of 26.67 per cent.

Stability numbers accompany the efficiency number, and they matter more. The cells retained their performance with high reproducibility across batches, which addresses a weakness that has dogged the field for a decade: a record efficiency reported from the best cell in one laboratory often cannot be reproduced in the same laboratory a month later, let alone in another one. Reproducibility is a manufacturing property and not a scientific curiosity. Certification by an independent laboratory is the only efficiency number worth quoting, and both papers used it.

A separate paper in the same journal reported a certified efficiency of 27.31 per cent from high-entropy molecular contacts that improve hole transport, with negligible loss after 1,300 hours at 85 degrees Celsius. Taken together the two results show a field converging on interface chemistry as the place where the remaining gains are, after years in which composition engineering dominated. Two independent routes to the same conclusion is stronger evidence than either paper alone.

Certified efficiency is measured on a small cell, and modules lose several points when scaled because a larger area has more chance of a defect. The gap between a certified cell and a commercial module has closed from fifteen points to about five, and closing the rest requires uniformity across metres of coating, which is a machine problem as much as a chemistry problem.

Stability protocols used in these papers are accelerated and short, and the acceleration factors are generous by field standards. A cell on a rooftop will experience thousands of thermal cycles, not the few hundred in a laboratory chamber, and it will be exposed to voltage transients from the inverter. The community has standardised protocols over the last five years and still reports conditions that are gentler than the field.

Where this leads commercially is a tandem cell on silicon, which is the product most likely to reach the market, since silicon provides the mechanical and electrical stability and perovskite supplies the extra absorption. Interfacial engineering is the shared requirement of every tandem design, and the papers this month specify how the links should behave.
