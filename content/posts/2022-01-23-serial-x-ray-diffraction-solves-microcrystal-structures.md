Hybrid inorganic-organic materials are easy to make and hard to characterize. They grow as microcrystals, often a few micrometres across, with low symmetry and a sensitivity to radiation that destroys them under a conventional X-ray beam before enough reflections are collected. The result is a characterization bottleneck: structures are reported from powders by analogy, and new phases are named without coordinates.

Small-molecule serial femtosecond crystallography offers a way around the radiation problem by outrunning it. A suspension of microcrystals is injected across an X-ray free-electron laser beam, and each pulse, lasting femtoseconds, produces a diffraction pattern from a crystal that is destroyed immediately afterwards. Thousands of randomly oriented patterns are collected, and the structure is assembled computationally. The demonstration appeared in *Nature*, 19 January 2022.

The team determined unit cells by pooling spot-finding results into high-resolution powder diffractograms, then indexed the sparse serial patterns with a graph-theory approach and refined the datasets with standard single-crystal tools. They solved mithrene, AgSePh, thiorene, AgSPh, and tethrene, AgTePh, ab initio; the latter two had not been reported before. The structures came from crystals grown in suspension, not from a single crystal selected under a microscope.

The gain is not only in avoiding damage. A serial experiment samples many crystals, so twinning, stacking faults and polymorph contamination show up as variation rather than as a failed refinement, and the powder diffractogram built from the same data provides an internal check on the unit cell. Indexing sparse patterns is the hard step, and the graph approach works because the spots from a still crystal lie on a lattice even when only a fraction of the reflections are recorded.

Limits remain those of the source. An X-ray free-electron laser is a scarce facility, beam time is allocated by competition, and the injection of a suspension consumes milligrams of sample that many synthetic chemists cannot spare. The method also assumes that the microcrystals are homogeneous, which is exactly the assumption that a poorly characterized material violates.

Wider access would come from laboratory sources with serial capabilities or from electron diffraction, which has improved quickly. For the hybrid materials that dominate new structure reports, the immediate value is a route to coordinates that does not require a crystal large enough to survive a synchrotron beam.
