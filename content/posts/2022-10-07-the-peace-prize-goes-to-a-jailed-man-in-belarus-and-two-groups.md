The Norwegian committee announced the peace prize on Friday morning in Oslo, and it went to a jailed Belarusian activist, to a Russian human rights group that has been dissolved, and to a Ukrainian group that documents war crimes. The three are Ales Bialiatski, Memorial and the Center for Civil Liberties. The committee said it honoured three outstanding champions of human rights, democracy and peaceful coexistence in their own countries. The announcement came an hour later than usual, and the room at the Nobel Institute was fuller than it has been since the pandemic.

Bialiatski, who is sixty, founded Viasna in 1996 to defend people arrested at the protests that followed a constitutional referendum. The group recorded the names of the detained, paid for their lawyers and published the lists that the state preferred to keep quiet. He was arrested in 2021 and charged with financing protests, and he has been held in prison since then. The court that hears the case sits in Minsk and has refused to release him on bail. His trial continued through the summer, and the committee noted that he could not travel to Oslo.

Memorial was founded in 1989 by a group of activists that included Andrei Sakharov, and its first work was to record the victims of the Soviet camps. It published the lists of the executed, mapped the burial sites and kept a database that scholars still use. The Supreme Court liquidated the group in December 2021 at the request of the prosecutors, and its archive was seized. Its staff had been raided and its bank accounts frozen long before the liquidation. Its branches had spent the previous decade documenting the war in Chechnya and the treatment of political prisoners.

The Center for Civil Liberties was founded in Kyiv in 2007 and began by recording the cases of people detained during the protests of 2013 and 2014. Since the war began in 2014, its lawyers have collected testimony from the occupied territories and have published reports on the treatment of prisoners. Prosecutors in several European countries now use those reports in their own cases. Its director, Oleksandra Matviichuk, said on Friday that the prize belonged to the people whose testimony the group had taken down.

Officials in Minsk called the award a political decision and said the committee had been misled. A spokesman for the Kremlin said the prize had gone to people whose activities had been the subject of criminal cases. The committee's chair, Berit Reiss-Andersen, said the three had been chosen together because the prize is about civil society in three neighbouring countries rather than about one government. Asked whether the award would provoke the two governments, she said the committee does not pick laureates for the convenience of states.

Worth ten million kronor, the prize will be handed over in Oslo in December as usual. Whether the laureates can attend remains open, since one of them sits in a cell and another works in a country at war. The award is the third time the committee has honoured a body rather than a single person, and the first time it has honoured three laureates from the same region in one year.
