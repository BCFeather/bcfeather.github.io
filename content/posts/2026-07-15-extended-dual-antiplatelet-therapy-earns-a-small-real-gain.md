After a stent is placed, dual antiplatelet therapy is standard for a year, and what happens after that year is a genuine trade-off. Stopping reduces bleeding; continuing reduces stent thrombosis and new events elsewhere in the coronary tree. Patients with disease in more than one vessel sit on the riskier side of that trade-off, because their atherosclerosis is not confined to the segment that was stented.

A trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 15 July 2026 tested extended dual therapy in exactly that group. Eight thousand two hundred and fifty patients with multivessel coronary artery disease were randomised after their initial period of treatment, 4,125 to continued dual antiplatelet therapy and 4,125 to aspirin alone. Median follow-up was 34.3 months, and the primary efficacy endpoint combined cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction and stroke. The trial excluded patients who had a bleeding event during the first year, which is the group for whom the question is hardest.

A primary efficacy event occurred in 222 patients on dual therapy and 266 on aspirin alone. The 36-month cumulative incidence was 5.8 per cent against 6.8 per cent, a hazard ratio of 0.82 with a confidence interval from 0.69 to 0.98 and a *P* value of 0.03. Clinically relevant or major bleeding occurred in 51 and 57 patients, 1.4 per cent against 1.5 per cent, a hazard ratio of 0.89 that was not statistically significant.

The result is small and real. A one percentage point absolute reduction over three years is worth having in a population whose baseline risk is high, and the absence of a bleeding penalty is the finding that makes the trade-off acceptable. Bleeding rates this low also reflect selection: patients who had already tolerated a year of dual therapy are the ones who entered the extension, and the frail and the bleeders had dropped out before randomisation. Whether the benefit reflects fewer stent events or fewer events in unstented plaques cannot be separated from the composite, and the two mechanisms imply different durations.

Two caveats limit the conclusion. The trial's *P* value sits just under the conventional threshold, and the upper confidence bound of 0.98 leaves room for a benefit that is barely there. The endpoint is also a composite, and the components were not individually powered, so the claim that cardiovascular death fell would be an overread. The population was overwhelmingly east Asian, which affects both ischaemic and bleeding risk.

Guidelines will have to decide whether a one-point gain justifies three more years of a second drug, and that decision depends on cost and on how the patient weighs bleeding against infarction. The more useful next step is a risk score that identifies the patients whose ischaemic risk is high enough to make the arithmetic comfortable, rather than extending treatment to everyone who tolerated the first year.
