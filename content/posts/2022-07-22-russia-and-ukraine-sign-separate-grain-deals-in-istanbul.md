The agreement to reopen Ukraine's Black Sea ports was signed in Istanbul on Friday, and the ceremony produced two documents rather than one. The Russian defence minister and the Ukrainian infrastructure minister signed separately in the presence of the Turkish president and the secretary general of the United Nations, and they did not sit at the same table. The deal allows the export of grain from three ports, and it sets up a coordination centre in Istanbul to inspect the ships. The first vessels are expected within days.

Narrow and specific, the terms describe a corridor and nothing more. The Ukrainians will guide the ships through the mined waters along marked channels, and the Russians have promised not to attack the ports while the shipments are under way. The centre will register each voyage and the inspectors will search the ships on the way in and on the way out to make sure that they carry grain and not weapons. The agreement is to last for a hundred and twenty days and it can be renewed.

In this dispute every stake is counted in tonnes. About twenty million tonnes of grain were in the silos when the deal was signed, and the harvest that is now being brought in will add several million more. The United Nations has said that the closure of the ports has contributed to the rise in the price of food, and the countries that depend on the imports are in North Africa and in Asia. The programme that feeds the poorest has warned that it will have to cut the rations if the corridor does not work.

More was being performed in Istanbul than was written down. The Turkish president has been trying to present himself as a mediator since the spring, and he has not joined the sanctions on Russia. The United Nations has been negotiating the terms for two months, and the secretary general said the agreement was a step for the poorest people in the world. The Russian president did not attend, and the Ukrainian president said the deal was a victory for his country's diplomacy.

The history of this war justifies the scepticism. The previous attempts at a corridor failed in the spring, and the ports have been attacked since the invasion began. The Russians have said that they will not use the corridor to move troops, and the Ukrainians have said that they will not trust the promise. The ships that enter will be in range of the Russian artillery for part of the route, and the insurance will cost several times what it did before February.

Both sides need the mechanism, and neither controls it. Russia needs the grain to be sold because its own exports depend on the same shipping lanes, and Ukraine needs the revenue because its economy is running on foreign aid. The Turkish and the United Nations role is to make the mechanism credible, and the credibility will be tested by the first ship that is stopped or the first port that is hit. The next hundred and twenty days will decide whether the corridor becomes a routine or a memory.
