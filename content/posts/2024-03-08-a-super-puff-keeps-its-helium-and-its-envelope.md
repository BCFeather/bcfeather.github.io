Super-puffs are planets with bulk densities below a tenth of a gram per cubic centimetre, which for a planet of a few Earth masses means an envelope many times larger than the core. Theory says the low-mass examples should lose that envelope to photoevaporation within a few hundred million years, and the higher-mass ones should hold on, so the population as observed is a test of the mechanism.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 8 March 2024 runs that test on TOI-1420b, a warm super-puff with a mass of 25.1 Earth masses, a radius of 11.9 Earth radii, a density of 0.08 grams per cubic centimetre and an equilibrium temperature of 960 kelvins. The planet sits on the massive side of the divide, and the question is whether its atmosphere is still escaping. The system is old enough that a photoevaporation time shorter than its age would already have removed the envelope, so the observed radius is itself an argument about the rate.

The measurement is helium in the metastable state. The authors observed one full transit and one partial transit with the helium filter on Palomar/WIRC, and the transit in that filter comes out 0.671 per cent deeper than the transit measured by TESS, a difference significant at 8.5 sigma, which is the signature of an extended absorbing atmosphere rather than of the planet's opaque body.

Modelling turns the depth into a rate. A one-dimensional hydrodynamic calculation fixes the thermal structure of the outflow under different assumptions for the stellar ultraviolet spectrum, and a three-dimensional simulation fed by those results reproduces the observed absorption with a mass-loss rate near 10 to the power 10.82 grams per second. The absorption also constrains where the gas sits, since helium in the metastable state absorbs over a narrow range of velocities and the depth depends on how much of the outflow lies in the line of sight.

That rate is small against the planet. At that pace the planet would take about 70 gigayears to lose its envelope, far longer than the age of the system, so TOI-1420b is not being stripped and can have arrived at its present envelope fraction by formation rather than by erosion.

An alternative explanation survives. Tidal circularization could have heated the interior and inflated the envelope after formation, which would bring the envelope fraction into line with what core-nucleated accretion predicts, and the helium measurement cannot distinguish an inflated planet from one that formed large. Eccentricity is the quantity that would make such a history legible, since a planet that has just circularized carries the energy of that damping in its interior.

The test that would separate the two is a comparison across the population. If the massive super-puffs all retain helium and the low-mass ones all lose it, the divide is where theory puts it; if retention instead tracks orbital distance or eccentricity, the heating story gains ground, and the sample needed for that comparison is the one now being assembled. Both outcomes would be informative: retention by mass points to formation, and retention by orbit points to heating, and the current sample is large enough to start telling them apart.
