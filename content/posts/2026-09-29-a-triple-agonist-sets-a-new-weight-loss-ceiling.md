Obesity drugs have been compared on a single axis for four years, which is the percentage of body weight lost, and each generation has pushed that number higher. Semaglutide reached the mid-teens, tirzepatide the low twenties, and the question for the next molecule was whether the ceiling had arrived or whether adding a third receptor would move it again. Retatrutide agonizes the receptors for GLP-1, GIP and glucagon, and the glucagon arm is the one that increases energy expenditure as well as reducing intake.

The TRIUMPH-1 trial randomized 2,339 adults with obesity, without diabetes, to placebo or to one of three doses of retatrutide for the main weight endpoint. Mean weight change was minus 17.6 per cent at 4 milligrams, minus 23.7 per cent at 9 milligrams and minus 25.0 per cent at 12 milligrams, against minus 3.9 per cent on placebo, with *P*<0.001 for the comparisons. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 29 September.

Two secondary endpoints addressed conditions that obesity causes rather than the weight itself. Among participants with knee osteoarthritis, pain scores fell by 3.2 to 3.6 points on the hybrid estimand against 1.9 on placebo, and among those with obstructive sleep apnoea, apnoea-hypopnoea events per hour fell by 22.9 to 34.3 against 9.9. Both are endpoints regulators now accept as evidence of clinical benefit, and both are the outcomes patients notice first.

Handling of intercurrent events is unusually careful in this paper. Weight endpoints used a treatment-regimen estimand under intention to treat, while the osteoarthritis and sleep apnoea endpoints used a hybrid estimand with a hypothetical strategy for treatment failure, and the investigators report the intention-to-treat results alongside. That transparency matters because the two estimands give different answers when participants stop treatment, and stopping is common with this class.

Gastrointestinal adverse events were the most frequent, as they are for every incretin-based drug, and the trial reports them without the detail that would let a reader judge how many participants discontinued. Tolerability at 12 milligrams will decide whether the highest dose is usable outside a trial, since a weight loss of 25 per cent is worth little to a patient who cannot stay on the drug.

The glucagon component raises a question the trial was not designed to answer. Increasing energy expenditure can raise heart rate, reduce bone density and affect the liver, and the duration of the trial is short relative to the time a patient would take the drug. Longer studies will look at lean mass, since a quarter of body weight lost in a year includes muscle, and muscle is what keeps an older patient independent.

Cost and access will decide the practical effect. A drug that produces a 25 per cent reduction is a serious therapeutic advance and an expensive one, and the health systems that struggle to supply semaglutide will struggle more with this. The clinical argument is settled; the distribution argument is not.
