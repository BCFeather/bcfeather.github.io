On the afternoon of 17 September, the pagers began to explode. Across Beirut and the south of Lebanon, in markets and shops and homes, the small devices that Hezbollah had distributed to its members as a security measure, the low-technology alternative to mobile phones, detonated in their owners' pockets, first a single beep, then a blast that severed hands and tore through bodies at waist height. A second wave came the following day, this time of walkie-talkies, and it delivered the same lesson to the funerals of the first. When the two days were over, more than thirty people were dead and close to three thousand wounded, civilians and fighters alike, and the world's understanding of what an attack can be had changed overnight.

Mechanics are the story, and the mechanics are terrifying in their ordinariness. Pagers purchased by Hezbollah months earlier had been intercepted somewhere in the supply chain between manufacturer and buyer, fitted with a small explosive charge and a triggering device, and delivered to the organization's members with the bomb already inside the package. When the signal came, the devices the fighters had carried as protection killed them on contact. Attack of that kind strikes no building and no convoy. It is an operation against a procurement system, and its reach extends to anyone carrying a device, a shopkeeper, a bystander, a child. Distinction between combatant and civilian, already fragile in this war, was erased by the logistics of the weapon.

Attribution was never in doubt. Lebanon and Hezbollah blamed Israel within hours, and the Israeli government, in its usual manner, neither confirmed nor denied; American officials later confirmed that Israel had been responsible and had informed Washington in advance. Strategic context made the purpose legible. The operation came as the war between Israel and Hezbollah approached its escalation, and the strikes demonstrated that no method was beyond reach. Hassan Nasrallah, the head of Hezbollah, called it a crossing of 'all red lines' that amounted to a declaration of war, and the phrase, for once, described the situation accurately from both sides' perspective.

Precedent is the reason the attack will be remembered beyond the war that produced it. Every government, every military, every organization that buys equipment from abroad has just been shown that the product it trusts can be turned against it, and the demonstration does not require any particular sophistication: the devices were ordinary consumer electronics, the interception happened at the level of distribution, and the explosives were small. Response will be a search for alternatives, a demand for domestic supply chains, suspicion of anything made abroad. Suspicion of that kind is rational, which makes it all the more corrosive. Trade as the globalized economy built it runs on the assumption that goods are what they appear to be. The attack in Lebanon was an argument, delivered in the most direct way possible, that the assumption is optional.

Deeper question, the one that will be argued in the ethics committees and the war colleges for years, is what the attack was. It was, by any definition, an act of war, and it was also an act of terrorism in the sense that it deliberately struck at civilians in public spaces, however precise its intelligence about the devices was. It killed fighters and non-combatants alike, it was carried out without warning, and it transformed a piece of everyday life into an instrument of death, and every one of those properties will be cited, by someone, as either the operation's justification or its condemnation. Arguments will not be settled, and neither will the technology. Devices that explode in pockets have joined the repertoire of conflict, and the next war will have learned the lesson of the pagers. Precedent is set.
