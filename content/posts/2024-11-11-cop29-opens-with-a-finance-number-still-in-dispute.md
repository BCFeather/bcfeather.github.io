Delegations opened the climate conference in Baku with a single question at its centre: how much money the developed world will commit to the developing world after 2025, when the old hundred-billion-dollar pledge expires. The numbers on the table ranged from a floor that rich countries could accept to a figure several times larger that the recipients described as the minimum for adaptation alone.

Structure of the goal matters as much as the amount. A headline number can be counted from public budgets, from private investment mobilized by guarantees, or from loans that add to a country's debt. A recipient that is already paying interest on climate loans is not helped by a target that counts lending as finance. A grant that a donor can reclaim is a grant that a finance ministry books as a liability.

Adaptation is the part that has been underfunded. Mitigation attracts private capital because a solar farm sells electricity, and a sea wall sells nothing. The countries that need the wall are the ones with the weakest credit, and the money that reaches them has mostly arrived as loans rather than as grants. A country that needs a sea wall is a country that a bank prices as a risk, and the price of that risk is the interest rate. A loan that must be repaid in hard currency is a loan that a finance ministry records as debt rather than as support.

Politics of the venue were awkward. The host is a petrostate whose economy depends on the exports that the conference is meant to phase down, and several delegations arrived with the argument that a country in that position cannot chair the process fairly. The presidency's answer was that every region gets a turn. A host that exports the fuel in question is a host that has an interest in the pace of the transition. A president who is chairing the process has an interest in a text that does not name the fuel.

Negotiations also carried the rules for carbon markets. A mechanism that lets a country pay for a reduction elsewhere had been waiting for a text since the Paris agreement, and the text that emerged would decide whether a credit can be counted twice. A market with double counting is a market that produces paperwork rather than reductions. A rule that lets one reduction be counted in two countries is a rule that cancels the reduction. A text that leaves a mechanism to a later meeting is a text that postpones the argument rather than settling it.

What Baku could deliver is a number and a timetable. What it could not deliver is the confidence that the money will arrive, and the credibility of the goal depends on a delivery record that the previous pledge missed by two years. A pledge that missed its date by two years is a pledge that a recipient remembers when the next number is announced.
