Tidal streams are predictable and dense, and the machines that harvest them are expensive to keep in the water. A turbine sized for rated flow meets faster water at springs and slower water at neaps, and the resulting load swings drive fatigue in blades, hubs and foundations. The standard answer is an active pitch system that feathers the blades, a set of bearings and actuators packed into a nacelle that is hard to service at sea.

Zhang, Tan and Viola tested a passive alternative, described in *Nature*, 11 November 2026. Their blades are moulded from rubber with a flexible trailing edge, free to deform along the chord as hydrodynamic load rises, and they were run in a flume against two rigid rotors: one limited by speed control alone, and one with the state-of-the-art pitch-and-speed controller. All three were evaluated over the same simulated tidal period, from a cut-in speed of a fifth of rated to a maximum of 1.2 times rated.

Across those cycles the flexible rotor returned the same total energy as the pitching turbine. High-frequency load fluctuations fell by up to one-third, and the quasi-steady thrust matched the actively pitched machine. Thrust behaviour separates the designs. A rigid blade controlled only by rotor speed reached about 1.65 times rated thrust as inflow rose, close to the peak that the pitching turbine produced, while the rubber blade held its peak near the pitched value.

Digital image correlation of the blade tip showed the mechanism directly, at an inflow of 0.708 metres a second. Under load the trailing edge lifts, the cambered profile flattens and the blade sheds thrust the way a pitching system would, a controlled feather with no actuator behind it. Stiffness sets the balance: a blade too stiff keeps its camber and generates high mean and unsteady thrust, a blade too soft over-pitches and gives up power, and the mould geometry stays the same while the rubber compound changes the machine. Off design, a flexible blade carries a slightly higher thrust coefficient than a rigid one, though the absolute force at low speed is below the cycle peak.

Model-scale tank work carries limits that the authors state. Power and thrust are quasi-steady quantities averaged over a cycle, so the comparison rests on load statistics and on root-bending moments rather than on measured material fatigue. One blade family at one scale cannot show how rubber behaves after years of salt water and ultraviolet light, and the tests used a single turbine geometry. Capital and maintenance savings are argued from lower peak and fatigue loads, with no costed build behind them.

Open-water trials at full blade scale would test whether the rubber keeps its stiffness in service. A fatigue rig on a complete chord would convert load variance into expected life. Array-level control studies would show whether passive blades simplify the farm controller as much as they simplify the nacelle.
