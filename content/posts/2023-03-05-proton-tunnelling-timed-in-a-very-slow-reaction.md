Quantum tunnelling is usually invoked for light particles at low barriers, and chemistry textbooks treat it as a correction. A reaction that proceeds almost entirely by tunnelling, at an astronomically small rate, is a case where the correction is the phenomenon.

In *Nature* on 1 March 2023, that case is measured. Robert Wild, Markus Nötzold, Roland Wester and colleagues study the reaction between molecular hydrogen and the deuteride anion, a proton-transfer step, and measure a rate constant of about one in ten to the eleventh collisions.

Slowness is the signature. A reaction that required a barrier crossing would be faster at higher collision energy, and this one is not, so the rate is consistent with a wavefunction leaking through the barrier. Deuteride makes the experiment possible at all, because it is a bare proton with two electrons and its reaction with H2 transfers the lightest nucleus, whereas a heavier analogue would tunnel far more slowly and would not be measurable.

Because the authors scan collision energies over nearly four orders of magnitude and the rate stays close to constant, the energy range carries the second result, which is what tunnelling predicts and what a thermally activated pathway does not. Merged beams supply the technique, since reactants are prepared in beams that overlap at a defined collision energy and the products are counted at a rate at the edge of detectability.

Background is the experimental difficulty, because a count rate of one event in a long run competes with noise from the apparatus, and background subtraction is what turns a handful of counts into a rate constant with an error bar. Barrier shape is the theoretical interest, since a tunnelling rate depends on the barrier's width and height and a scan over several energies constrains both, which makes the numbers a test any theory can be run against.

Astrophysics supplies the motive. H2 and its isotopologues dominate the chemistry of cold molecular clouds, and a reaction proceeding at a tiny but nonzero rate sets deuterium fractionation over astronomical times, so a rate that is slow by laboratory standards is fast across millions of years.

Exit channel stays unresolved, because a reaction that tunnels can produce different product states and their distribution needs state selection the experiment does not have. Physical chemistry gains a benchmark, since the slowest measured ion-molecule rate is a limit that every theory of tunnelling has to reproduce, and a limit that is measured is worth more than one that is estimated. A reaction this slow also matters for models of cold plasmas, because a rate that is negligible at room temperature is not negligible in a cloud that persists for a million years. The experiment also fixes the temperature dependence, and a rate that hardly moves as the beams warm is the clearest statement that the barrier is crossed without the energy to climb it. A limit measured this precisely is a target for theory, and the next decade of calculations will be judged against it.
