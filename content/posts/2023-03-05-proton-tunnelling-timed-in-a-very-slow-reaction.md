Quantum tunnelling is usually invoked for light particles at low barriers, and chemistry textbooks treat it as a correction. A reaction that proceeds almost entirely by tunnelling, at a rate that is astronomically small, is a case where the correction is the phenomenon.

The measurement in *Nature*, 1 March 2023 is that case. Robert Wild, Markus Nötzold, Roland Wester and colleagues study the reaction between molecular hydrogen and the deuteride anion, a proton-transfer step, and measure a rate constant of about one in ten to the eleventh collisions.

The slowness is the signature. A reaction that requires a barrier crossing would be faster at higher collision energy, and this one is not. The rate that the experiment measures is consistent with a process that happens by the wavefunction leaking through the barrier.

The technique is merged beams. Reactants are prepared in beams that overlap at a defined collision energy, and the products are counted. The count rate is low, and the experiment's achievement is a measurement at the edge of detectability.

The energy range is the other result. The authors scan collision energies over nearly four orders of magnitude, and the rate remains close to constant, which is what tunnelling predicts and what a thermally activated pathway does not.

The astrophysical relevance is the reason the reaction matters. H2 and its isotopologues dominate the chemistry of cold molecular clouds, and a reaction that proceeds at a tiny but nonzero rate is a reaction that sets the deuterium fractionation over astronomical times.

The theoretical interest is the barrier's shape. A tunnelling rate depends on the barrier's width and height, and a measurement over a range of energies constrains both. The paper's numbers are a test that a theory can be run against.

What the experiment cannot resolve is the exit channel. A reaction that tunnels can produce different product states, and the distribution among them is information that a merged-beam experiment with state selection could provide.

The interstellar consequence is a timescale. A rate of one in ten to the eleventh collisions is slow by laboratory standards and fast by astronomical ones, because a cloud has millions of years.

For physical chemistry, the result is a benchmark. The slowest measured ion-molecule rate is a limit that any theory of tunnelling has to reproduce, and a limit that is measured is worth more than one that is estimated.

The deuteride anion is the reactant that makes the experiment possible. It is a bare proton with two electrons, and its reaction with H2 transfers a proton, which is the lightest nucleus. A heavier analogue would tunnel far more slowly and would not be measurable.
