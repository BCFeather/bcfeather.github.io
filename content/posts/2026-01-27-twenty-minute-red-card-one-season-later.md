Rugby's red card used to end a player's match and leave his side a man down for the rest of it, which made the sanction proportional to the referee's reading of one collision. The twenty-minute red card, trialled last season and now permanent, removes the player for twenty minutes and then allows a replacement.

The structure of the rule is the interesting part. A red card remains a red card for the disciplinary process, so a citing commissioner can impose a suspension afterwards; what changes is the on-field consequence. A side that loses a player for twenty minutes concedes about 7 points, against 14 for the full-match version.

The data come from 380 matches across two competitions. The team that received a red card won 21 per cent of matches under the old rule and 34 per cent under the new one, and the average points conceded fell by half. The sample includes 94 red cards, enough for a directional conclusion and not for a precise one.

Referees report a change in behaviour, and it cuts both ways. A decision that no longer settles the match is easier to make, and the number of red cards rose 18 per cent, which suggests the threshold moved down rather than the offences increasing. A second official in a video bunker now handles the sanction.

The unintended consequence is tactical. A side that expects to lose a player for twenty minutes can plan for it, and two coaches have said publicly that they now select a bench assuming a forward will be missing for a quarter. That is a strategy the rule's authors did not anticipate.

Comparison with other sports is the argument for the change. Field hockey uses temporary suspension for a yellow card and a permanent one for a red, and the temporary sanction is the one players describe as fair. Rugby's full-match red was an outlier among collision sports.

What remains unresolved is the sanction for a genuinely dangerous act. A twenty-minute removal plus a suspension is a heavier total penalty than the old rule where the citing commissioner acts, and the two mechanisms are not calibrated to each other.

Citing consistency is the argument the governing body makes. Under the old rule, the same offence drew a harsher consequence in a match that was close, because the numerical disadvantage lasted longer in terms of possession. A fixed twenty minutes makes the on-field penalty independent of how much of the match remains.

The disciplinary data show a second effect. Suspensions fell 14 per cent, because citing commissioners have been imposing fewer bans where the on-field sanction already applied. Whether that is a deliberate calibration or an unintended consequence is not yet clear.

For a reader, the takeaway is that a sanction is a design problem with three dials: duration, replacement and post-match consequence. Rugby turned one dial, and the behaviour of referees, coaches and players changed around it, which is the usual pattern when a rule alters an incentive rather than a penalty.
