---

A major film festival runs for ten days and hands out awards at the end, and for the industry the awards are the least important part of the week. The market that runs alongside the screenings is where films are sold territory by territory, where a finished title finds a distributor and where a project that has a script and a star finds the rest of its budget. A festival without a market is a celebration, and a market without a festival is a trade fair. A screening that is scheduled for eight in the morning is a screening that a buyer attends because the film is already on a list.

Currency in this market is the presale. A sales agent takes a film to a buyer in a country before it is finished and sells the distribution rights for that territory, using a package of script, cast and director to justify the price. A presale pays for production, and a film that cannot be presold is a film that has to be financed another way. A presale contract specifies the minimum marketing spend and the release date, and a distributor that fails to meet either loses the rights.

Selection is the marketing. A film that is invited to a competition gains a reason for a buyer to look at it, and the invitation is worth more than an advertisement. That is why producers spend money on a premiere at a major festival even when the fee is small: the selection is the credential that the market prices. A competition slot that is announced in April is worth more than one announced in confidence, and the announcement is the product.

Streaming buyers changed the arithmetic. A platform that buys worldwide rights pays more for a single title than a group of territorial distributors, and it removes the risk of unsold territories. A platform that buys fewer titles concentrates its spending on the ones it can market, which leaves the middle of the market without a buyer. A platform that buys a film for one region leaves the rest to the territorial buyers, and the territorial buyers now bid lower.

Economics of the festival itself are fragile. A programme of two hundred films costs money to screen, to host and to insure, and the public funding that supports it is justified by cultural value rather than by the market. A festival that loses its market still has its red carpet, and the red carpet is the part that the public sees. A festival that is funded by a city is a festival that the city uses to fill hotels in a shoulder season.

What the market decides is what gets made. A genre that sells in every territory is a genre that gets financed, and a film that depends on one country's audience is a film that struggles. That is a commercial filter applied before a single frame is shot, and it shapes the cinema that a festival later celebrates. A filter that is applied by buyers is a filter that the audience never sees, and its effect on the catalogue is larger than any award.
