A bacterial cell packs a genome of several million base pairs into a nucleoid a micrometre across, and does it while transcribing, replicating and dividing. How the folding works has been studied with chromosome conformation capture, which crosslinks DNA segments that sit close in space and counts the pairs. The standard version returns contact maps at a resolution of a few thousand base pairs, too coarse to see the elements that organize a genome this small. The nucleoid has no membrane, so its shape comes from physics and from a small set of DNA-binding proteins.

A paper in *Nature* on 13 August 2025 pushes the method down to 10 base pairs. Its authors modified Micro-C, the high-resolution variant, and applied it to *Escherichia coli*, resolving structures they call chromosomal hairpins and chromosomal hairpin domains: loops where a stretch of DNA folds back on itself, and clusters of such loops. Resolution at this scale is what makes a hairpin distinguishable from the general looping of a long polymer, which at lower resolution looks like uniform contact.

The proteins that build them are known ones. Histone-like nucleoid-structuring protein, H-NS, and its paralogue StpA organize the hairpins, and the structures turn out to sit over genes acquired by horizontal transfer, keeping them quiet. Removing H-NS reorganized the map and reduced the number of hairpins, and removing both proteins disassembled them entirely, raised transcription of the imported genes and delayed growth. The repression is specific in the map: the hairpins sit over the imported genes, not across the chromosome as a whole.

A chemical test supports the picture. Netropsin, which competes with H-NS and StpA for AT-rich DNA, produced effects resembling the double deletion, which ties the structures to the sequence preference of the proteins and not to some unrelated role they might have. The resemblance is suggestive, not exact, since netropsin binds many AT-rich sites besides the ones the two proteins occupy. Neither perturbation can be read as a clean loss of function, because each changes the chemistry of the chromosome as well as its shape.

The other finding concerns active genes. Every transcribed operon formed a chromosomal interaction domain of its own, appearing on the contact map as a square pattern that reflects contacts across the whole transcribed region. These domains depend on transcription: switch the polymerase off and the squares go. The authors argue that the interaction between hairpins organizes the rest of the genome into isolated loops, which would insulate an active operon from its neighbours.

Limits are those of any fixed-condition map. The work describes one strain grown in one medium at one moment, crosslinking captures what was close at that instant, and a 10 base-pair map still averages over millions of cells. How the nucleoid rearranges through the cell cycle, and whether the hairpins move, are questions the method can now be aimed at but has not answered. A map taken during slow growth, when the chromosome is less busy, would show whether the hairpins persist or dissolve with the transcription they help to silence.
