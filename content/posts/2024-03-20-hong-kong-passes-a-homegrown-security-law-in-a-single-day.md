title: "Hong Kong passes a homegrown security law in a single day"
date: "2024-03-20T23:30:00+08:00"

description: "Hong Kong's legislature passed the Safeguarding National Security Ordinance on 19 March, creating new offences for treason, sabotage and the handling of state secrets, and the law took effect on 23 March despite objections from Western governments."
---

Legislative Council of Hong Kong passed the Safeguarding National Security Ordinance on the nineteenth of March, completing a bill that the government had promised to enact since 2020 and that had been postponed once. Bill was approved by all eighty-nine members present, with no recorded opposition, and it took effect on the twenty-third of March after the chief executive signed it. Debates in the chamber lasted about seven hours, which is short for a law of more than two hundred pages.

Content of the ordinance adds offences that the security law of 2020 did not cover. It creates crimes of treason, insurrection, sabotage, inciting mutiny and collusion with foreign forces, and it introduces penalties of up to life imprisonment for some of them. It also strengthens rules on the handling of state secrets, gives police powers to extend detention without charge to fourteen days, and allows courts to restrict access to proceedings. Journalists and lawyers said several of the provisions could be applied to ordinary reporting and to legal advice.

Process speed was the subject of most criticism. Government published a consultation paper in late January and closed it at the end of February, and the bill reached the council in early March. Members were given a few days to read it, and the government resisted amendments. Officials said the territory had waited twenty-six years for the law and that further delay would leave a gap in the security framework, which is a reference to the requirement in the basic law that Hong Kong enact its own national security legislation.

Business reaction was muted and specific. Several foreign chambers of commerce said they understood the need for security legislation and that the definitions in the bill were broader than those in other jurisdictions, which they said could affect the handling of information in regional offices. The government said the law would not affect normal commercial activity, and it pointed to the territory's role as a financial centre as evidence of stability rather than risk.

International response was critical but limited. The British government said the law would further erode the autonomy promised in the joint declaration of 1984, and it suspended talks on a prisoner transfer agreement. The European Union said it was considering the implications for its citizens and for the territory's courts. American officials said the law could trigger sanctions under legislation passed in 2020, and no new measures were announced in the weeks that followed.

What followed from the nineteenth of March is a security apparatus that is now complete in its formal architecture. Hong Kong has a police force, a prosecution service and courts that operate under rules that make political cases difficult to defend, and the government has said it will use the new offences only against a small number of people. What the law changes in practice depends on how it is used, and the first prosecutions under it were filed within a month. The territory's separate legal system, which was the basis of its attraction for international business, now looks more like an instrument of the same government that administers the mainland.
