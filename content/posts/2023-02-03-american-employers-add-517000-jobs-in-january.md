Payrolls rose by 517,000 in January, the Labour Department reported on 3 February. Economists had expected about 187,000, and the range of forecasts ran from 100,000 to 260,000. The unemployment rate fell to 3.4 per cent, the lowest reading since May 1969, and it has now been under 4 per cent for eleven months. The participation rate for workers aged 25 to 54 rose to 83.1 per cent, the highest since 2002.

Gains were concentrated in services. Leisure and hospitality added 128,000 jobs, health care added 79,000, and retail added 30,000. Manufacturing added 19,000, fewer than in the previous months, and construction added 25,000 despite the mortgage market. Government payrolls rose by 74,000, mostly at local level. Temporary help services shed 3,000 jobs, which is usually an early warning of slower hiring ahead. Construction hiring held up because of infrastructure projects funded by the previous year's legislation.

Average hourly earnings rose 4.4 per cent from a year earlier, slower than the 4.8 per cent of December. The monthly increase was 0.3 per cent, and the annual figure has been drifting down since the spring. Economists said the combination of strong hiring and slower wage growth was the most benign reading of the cycle so far. The employment cost index, published two weeks later, showed wages rising 5 per cent for the year. Wage growth for production and non-supervisory workers was 4.8 per cent, a point above the overall figure.

Participation rose to 62.4 per cent, and the employment-population ratio for prime-age workers reached 80.2 per cent, the highest since 2001. Those numbers explain where the workers came from: people who had left the labour force during the pandemic returned, and immigration recovered. The household survey, which is separate from payrolls, showed a larger increase than the establishment survey. Immigration recovered to about a million people in the year, which is roughly the pre-pandemic pace.

Markets read the report as a reason for the central bank to keep raising rates. Futures priced a peak above 5 per cent for the summer, and the two-year yield rose 0.2 points on the day. The next meeting was in March, and officials had said they would look at the labour market before deciding the size of the step. The ten-year yield rose 0.1 points and the dollar strengthened against most currencies. Officials had said they wanted to see the labour market loosen before slowing the pace of increases.

Revisions added to the strength. The figure for December was revised up to 260,000 from 223,000, and November to 290,000. Over the previous three months the average was 356,000, well above the pace that keeps unemployment steady. The report was the last before the March meeting, and it set the tone for the spring. The establishment survey covers about a third of the workforce, and the household survey the rest. Payroll growth of that size with unemployment at 3.4 per cent is a combination the models did not predict.
