Immune checkpoint inhibitors work by removing the brakes that tumours use to switch off T cells, and they work well in a minority of patients. Predicting who that minority will be is one of the practical problems of oncology, and the gut microbiome has been one of the most promising places to look, because the composition of the intestine changes what the immune system is primed to recognize.

A paper in *Nature* on 28 August reports a specific strain behind part of that effect. The authors compared the microbiomes of patients who responded to checkpoint therapy with those who did not, and found a new strain of the genus *Hominenteromicrobium*, designated YB328, that was present in responders and absent in non-responders. The difference was not a matter of abundance across many species but of the presence of one organism. That is a different kind of claim from the usual association study, and it is the reason the paper is worth reading closely.

The test was straightforward in design and difficult in execution. The strain was cultured, sequenced and introduced into mice with tumours, and the animals that received it controlled their tumours better than controls when treated with checkpoint inhibitors. The immune response was stronger, with more active T cells in the tumour, which suggests that the bacterium is supplying an antigen or an adjuvant rather than merely changing the environment. The distinction matters because an antigen can be identified and synthesized, while a change in the microbial ecology is much harder to reproduce.

Mechanistic work of this kind usually ends with a molecule. The paper points toward the bacterial surface and its metabolites, and the identity of the active component is the next question. If it is a lipid or a peptide that can be synthesized, then a pill becomes possible; if it is the living organism, then the product is a defined probiotic, which is a harder thing to regulate and to manufacture consistently.

The clinical implication is a test. A stool sample that detects YB328 would identify patients who are likely to benefit, and its absence would argue for a different approach, which is valuable in a class of drugs that costs a great deal and works in perhaps a fifth of patients. Whether the strain is a cause or a marker is the question that the mouse experiments begin to answer, and they lean toward cause.

There is a broader point about the microbiome literature. Many studies report correlations between a genus and an outcome, and few identify a strain that can be cultured, sequenced, transferred and shown to change the outcome. This one does, and that is why it is worth the attention even though the clinical translation is years away. The strain has to be grown at scale, stabilized for a capsule, and tested in a trial that regulators will accept, and each of those steps has failed for other organisms before.
