Ammonia is the second most produced chemical in the world and the Haber-Bosch process that makes it consumes about one per cent of global energy and emits a comparable share of carbon dioxide. The reaction runs at 400 to 500 degrees Celsius and 10 to 30 megapascals, which means a plant of enormous scale, and a plant of enormous scale cannot be distributed to the farms that use the product.

Lithium-mediated electrochemical synthesis is the alternative that has been pursued for a decade. Lithium metal reduces nitrogen to lithium nitride at ambient conditions, and the nitride is then protonated to ammonia, so the whole process can run in a cell at room temperature and pressure. The obstacle has been the solid electrolyte interphase on the electrode, which controls how lithium ions move and which breaks down at high current.

Zhang and colleagues engineered that interphase to increase lithium ion flux and to keep it uniform, which is the change that allows the reaction to run at a current density where it produces useful quantities. The system reached 100 milliamps per square centimetre in continuous flow at ambient pressure, with an energy efficiency of 21 per cent for ammonia and stable operation. The paper appeared in *Science* on 12 February, and the team is based at Shanghai Jiao Tong University. Uniform flux matters because lithium plating at a high current density is the failure mode that ends experiments, and a stable interface is what turns an experiment into a rate.

The comparison with previous work is what makes the number meaningful. Ammonia partial current density in earlier lithium-mediated systems had been limited to about 8 milliamps per square centimetre, and the improvement to 100 is the difference between a laboratory curiosity and a process that could be scaled. Energy efficiency of 21 per cent is still far below a thermal process, and it is achieved at ambient conditions where the thermal process cannot operate at all.

Selectivity is the remaining problem. The competing reaction is hydrogen evolution, which consumes the electrons that should reduce nitrogen, and an energy efficiency of 21 per cent means that most of the charge went somewhere else. The interphase design suppresses hydrogen evolution partly and cannot eliminate it, and the paper's own numbers describe the balance that was struck.

Economics are the test that this chemistry has not yet passed. Ammonia sells for a few hundred dollars a tonne, and an electrochemical route has to compete with a process that benefits from a century of engineering and from cheap natural gas. A process that runs on renewable electricity wins where gas is expensive or where carbon is priced, and it loses where neither condition holds.

The value of the result is that it moves an electrochemical route from a set of promising half-reactions to a continuous process with a stated rate and efficiency. Whether green ammonia becomes a commodity depends on the cost of the electrons, and that depends on decisions made outside the laboratory. Those decisions are about electricity prices, about electrolyser costs and about whether ammonia is treated as a fuel or as a fertiliser feedstock.
