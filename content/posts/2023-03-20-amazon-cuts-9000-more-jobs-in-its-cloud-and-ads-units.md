---

Word of the cuts came in a note to staff on 20 March. The reductions fell on the cloud division, the advertising business and the unit that runs the company's recruiting. They came on top of the 18,000 announced in January, which had already been described as the largest in the company's history. The note was published on the company's internal site and was reported by the press the same afternoon.

Hiring had added 400,000 people between 2019 and 2021, and headcount had reached 1.6 million. The pandemic had pushed demand for delivery and cloud services, and the company had built capacity for a market that did not persist. Its retail business had slowed as shoppers returned to shops, and its cloud growth had fallen from 40 per cent to 16 per cent in a year. Its warehouse space had grown by 40 per cent in two years, and it had sublet some of it in the autumn.

Employees in the affected units were told they could apply for other roles inside the company. The note said the company had to be leaner in the units it was cutting, and that the reductions would be completed by April. It also said the company would continue to hire in the areas it considered strategic, which it named as grocery, healthcare and satellite internet. The company said the affected units employed about 60,000 people, so the cuts were about 15 per cent.

Shares rose 1 per cent on the day, and they had gained 20 per cent since the start of the year. Investors had been asking for cost discipline since the autumn, and the company had slowed its warehouse expansion and closed some of its experimental stores. The finance chief had said in February that the company was looking for savings of 5 billion dollars. The shares had fallen 50 per cent in 2022, the worst year since the dot-com collapse.

Both units were told to focus on fewer products, and the company said it would end several small services. The cloud division was the unit that generated most of the profit, and the cuts there were read as a sign that growth had slowed rather than as a retreat. The advertising business had been built over five years and had reached 38 billion dollars in revenue. The advertising business had grown 20 per cent in the quarter, and it was the fastest-growing unit.

February also brought a return-to-office policy, which caused a petition from 30,000 employees. The chief executive said in the note that the policy would not change, and that the company needed people in the same room to train new staff. The return-to-office policy required three days a week, and it applied from May. At about 27,000, the reductions were the largest among the technology companies that cut jobs in that period. The company's headcount fell by 4 per cent over the year, and its operating margin rose from 2.4 per cent to 6.4 per cent. The chief executive's pay fell from 212 million dollars to 1.2 million, because his award was tied to the share price. The company's shares finished the year 80 per cent higher than they began it.
