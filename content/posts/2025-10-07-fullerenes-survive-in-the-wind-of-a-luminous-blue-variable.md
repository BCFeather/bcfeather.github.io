Big carbon molecules were expected to form in cool, dense, chemically relaxed outflows, the shells around dying stars and the envelopes of young ones. A luminous blue variable offers the opposite conditions: a hot star losing mass at a high rate, with ultraviolet radiation that should break bonds faster than they form. Finding fullerenes there would mean the molecule can be assembled and can survive in a wind still being irradiated, which changes where the carbon inventory of the interstellar medium is assembled.

*Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society* reported the detection on 3 October 2025. The evidence comes from archival Spitzer Infrared Spectrograph pointings of WRAY 16-232, a strong candidate for the class. The spectra carry vibrational bands of C60 at 17.4 and 18.9 micrometres, the pair that identifies the molecule, together with strong polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon features in the same sightline. Those bands are the ones the molecule produces in absorption against a continuum, so the detection rests on a reanalysis of archival data rather than on new observing time.

Complementary optical spectroscopy from the South African Large Telescope's High Resolution Spectrograph shows multiple P Cygni profiles in the hydrogen-alpha line and in He I and Fe II lines. Those profiles are the signature of a dense, expanding wind with substantial mass loss, and they establish that the fullerenes sit inside that outflow rather than in a foreground cloud. Decade-long photometry adds short-term brightness changes of about 0.5 magnitudes. Taken together, the infrared bands and the optical profiles put the molecule where the wind is dense enough to shield it.

The mechanism the authors favour is processing inside the ejecta. Carbon grains and polycyclic aromatics form first, and the same ultraviolet field that threatens them also drives the dehydrogenation and annealing that converts aromatics into cages. Shielding within dense clumps lets a fraction survive, and the observed mix of fullerenes and aromatics in one envelope is what such a two-stage history would leave behind. Annealing needs temperatures near 1,000 kelvins held long enough for the cages to close, a demanding condition inside an outflow that is also expanding and cooling.

What the spectra do not establish is the mass in fullerenes. Band strengths are sensitive to temperature and to the assumed absorption cross section, and no column density is derived. Nor is the star's classification settled: WRAY 16-232 remains a candidate luminous blue variable, so the argument concerns a candidate, not a confirmed member of the class. The variability is reported, not explained. Without a column density, the contribution of this star to the interstellar carbon budget cannot be added to anything.

Tests are available. A higher-resolution map of the shell would show whether the C60 bands follow the dense clumps or fill the outflow uniformly, and that distinguishes in situ formation from survival in shielded pockets. Repeated infrared spectroscopy would track the bands against the photometric changes, which would tie the molecule to a specific phase of the eruption instead of to the envelope as a whole. Neither test needs new instrumentation, which is why the detection is likely to be checked before it is extended.
