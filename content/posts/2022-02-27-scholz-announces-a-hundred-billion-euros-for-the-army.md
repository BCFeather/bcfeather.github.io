---The German chancellor spoke to a special session of the Bundestag on the morning of 27 February, three days after the invasion began, and he used a word that the German debate would use for the rest of the year. He called the war a Zeitenwende, a turning point in time, and he said that the country's foreign, security and energy policy would have to change with it. The speech lasted about twenty-five minutes and it reversed positions that his party had held since the 1980s.

Money came first. A special fund of 100 billion euros would be set up outside the ordinary budget and spent on the armed forces over five years, and defence spending would rise above 2 per cent of national income, the level that the alliance had set as a target in 2014 and that Germany had never reached. The fund was to be written into the constitution, which requires a change of the basic law with a two-thirds majority, and the government negotiated it with the opposition in the following weeks.

Weapons came second, and there the reversal had already happened. Germany had refused for decades to send arms to countries in conflict, and the government had offered helmets to Ukraine in January in a decision that was widely criticized. On 26 February, the day before the speech, it approved the delivery of 1,000 anti-tank weapons and 500 shoulder-launched missiles, and the chancellor said in the Bundestag that the country could not stand aside. The change was made by a coalition in which the two junior parties had campaigned against arms exports.

Energy policy changed in the same week. Germany imported about half of its gas and a third of its oil from Russia, and the government had built its climate policy on the assumption that the gas would keep coming while it closed its nuclear plants. The chancellor said the country would build two terminals for liquefied gas at Wilhelmshaven and Brunsbuttel, fill its storage to a mandated level, and buy gas from other suppliers at whatever price the market asked. The economy ministry, run by a Green minister, was put in charge of the programme. The terminals opened in the winter, and the gas that they handled replaced part of what the pipelines had carried.

The Reaction in the hall was muted and then enthusiastic. The opposition's leader said the government had done too little for too long, and he voted for the fund. The defence minister said the armed forces had been run down for thirty years and that the money would be spent on equipment that did not yet exist in the inventory. The finance minister said the fund was a one-off and that the ordinary budget would have to absorb the rest. The constitution was amended in May, the fund was established, and contracts were signed for aircraft, air defence and armoured vehicles.

The promise was kept in part. Deliveries were slow, as the industry had not kept the capacity for a rapid expansion, and the 2 per cent target was reached in 2024 with the help of the fund rather than through the ordinary budget. What the speech did was give a name to a change that had already happened. A country that had defined its foreign policy by the restraint it showed after 1945 decided, in an hour, that the restraint had become a way of free-riding on others. The debate that followed turned on how much of that decision was permanent, and the answer is still being given.
