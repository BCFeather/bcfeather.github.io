Employers in the United States added 254,000 jobs in September, the labour department reported on the fourth of October, a figure well above the 150,000 that economists had expected and a sign that the slowdown in hiring earlier in the summer was shorter than it had appeared. Unemployment rate held at 4.1 per cent, and average hourly earnings rose by 0.4 per cent on the month and 4.0 per cent on the year. Report was the last major reading on the labour market before the election in November.

Revisions added to the picture. Department raised its estimates for July and August by a combined 72,000 jobs, which turned a pair of weak months into ordinary ones, and the three-month average moved back above 180,000. Health care and social assistance accounted for a large share of the gain, as they have all year, and construction and government added several thousand each. Manufacturing shed a small number of jobs, and the sector has now been flat for most of the year.

Wages are the part of the report that matters for the inflation argument. Annual growth of 4.0 per cent is above the rate consistent with the two per cent target but has been falling slowly for eighteen months, and the September figure suggested the decline had stopped rather than reversed. Economists said the combination of solid hiring and steady pay growth would make it harder for the Federal Reserve to justify a second large cut at its meeting in November, after the half-point reduction it made in September.

Market reaction was to reduce the odds of another large cut. Yields on two-year Treasury notes rose, and traders moved towards pricing a quarter-point reduction rather than a half-point one. Dollar strengthened against most currencies. Several banks revised their forecasts for the path of rates through 2025, and the consensus moved towards a slower series of cuts on the argument that an economy adding a quarter of a million jobs does not need emergency support.

Political context of the release was unavoidable and brief. Report came five weeks before the election, and the administration pointed to it as evidence that its economic programme had worked. Opposition said job growth had been concentrated in government and health care and that household incomes had not kept up with prices. Independent economists noted that a single month says little, and that the strike at Boeing and the hurricanes of late September would distort the October figures.

Deeper question is whether the labour market has reached a stable plateau or is still cooling. Participation among prime-age workers is at its highest in two decades, immigration has added to the supply of labour, and the number of job openings has fallen back to where it was before the pandemic, all of which point to a market that has absorbed a large amount of tightening without breaking. September report supports that reading. It does not settle whether the next twelve months bring a further cooling or a second acceleration.
