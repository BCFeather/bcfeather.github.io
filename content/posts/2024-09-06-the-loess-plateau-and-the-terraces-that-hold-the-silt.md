The plateau takes its name from what lies beneath it rather than from what stands on top. Loess is a wind-blown silt, quarried from the deserts of the north and carried south-east through the Pleistocene by the winter monsoon, and it lies over Gansu, Ningxia, Shaanxi and Shanxi in a blanket that is tens of metres deep at the edges of the region and rather more in the troughs. Its particles are finer in the south-east than in the north-west, which is the signature of a wind that drops its load as it slows, and the whole deposit is young enough that a road cutting still shows the layering as a set of pale bands. The plateau itself is not flat: the silt drapes an older relief of ridges and basins, and where the cover is thin the shape of the bedrock shows through as a line of hills running in one direction.

The material behaves in one way that shapes everything built on it. Loess stands in a vertical face, because the silt is cemented by calcium carbonate and the pores left by ancient grass roots run the same way, and a cliff of it can stay upright for decades. Wet it, and the structure gives way at once, the cement dissolves, and the ground subsides beneath whatever is standing on it. That property explains the landscape: the plateau is cut by gullies that deepen from their heads, the gullies meet, and the edge of the tableland retreats until a broad flat top is left with a fringe of steep silt below it. A cutting made for a road or a canal exposes fissures that run vertically for several metres, and those fissures are what let water in and the face fail.

Water is the instrument of that erosion, and the rains that do the work fall in July and August, when a thunderstorm can drop a month's worth in a day. Runoff that cannot infiltrate collects in the gullies, and the load it carries reaches the Yellow River, which is named for it. The figures usually quoted are a sediment load of about 1.6 billion tonnes a year in the middle of the last century and something nearer a tenth of that now, and the difference is one of the largest deliberate changes to a river's load anywhere. The load travels in suspension because the particles are fine, and it is the load that gives the water its colour and its reputation. In a dry year the load falls with the rain, and in a wet one the river runs brown from March until October.

In the villages the deposit is architecture. Houses are cut into the silt as arched rooms, warm in winter because the mass around them holds the heat and cool in summer for the same reason, and the flat top of the plateau is farmed in terraces that follow the contour, each level held by a lip of earth. In the gullies, check dams are built across the channel every few hundred metres, and each one fills with silt within a few years and becomes a field, which is the point: the sediment is stopped where it is wanted instead of being exported to the river. The dams are small, a few metres of earth across a gully, and there are thousands of them; each holds a field, and together they hold a large share of the plateau's sediment.

Policy changed the surface within a single generation. After the floods of 1998 and the drought that followed, the Grain for Green programme converted steep farmland to grass and shrub from 1999, bans on grazing in the hills came with it, and payments and resettlement moved people off land that could not hold them. Satellite records show the plateau greening through the 2000s and 2010s, and the terraces on the steep slopes abandoned. Orchards, mostly apple, replaced wheat on the flatter ground, and they now hold soil and water in a way that annual crops never did. The programme has also been criticised for what it does to water, since a hillside under shrub loses more to the atmosphere than one under wheat, and the runoff reaching the river fell as the vegetation grew.

Sediment records from the river show the result of that change, and so does the water. Less silt reaches the channel, which starves the delta at the coast and has forced engineers to think about how a river that no longer builds land can be made to build it again. Less runoff reaches it too, because a hillside under grass and shrub consumes more water than a hillside under wheat, and the reservoirs that were built for flood control and irrigation fill with silt faster than the models expected. Every gain in soil retention is paid for somewhere downstream. The regulated releases that flush the reservoir behind Xiaolangdi each summer exist to move the stored silt on in pulses and keep the channel from silting up. That arithmetic is the one the plateau now lives with.

Autumn is the season that shows how the arrangement works. Millet and sorghum stand in the terraces in September, the apricot trees along the roads turn, and the wind that built the plateau in the first place returns with the first cold front and lifts dust from the gullies that have not yet been stabilised. The villages are quiet at that time of year, the young having left for the cities, and the old houses cut into the silt stand open. What the plateau shows at that hour is a landscape that has been worked for a very long time and has been reworked, in one generation, into something the previous generations would not recognise. The dust that arrives with the first cold front is the same wind that built the plateau, and it still lifts from the gullies that have not been stabilised; on a bad day the sky over the terraces turns the colour of the ground.
