Five days in Beijing Yizhuang, from 8 to 12 August, put the World Robot Conference through three halls, more than 200 companies and about 1,500 exhibits. The numbers are large, but the change that matters is smaller and easier to see: at the back of the exhibition, beside a stall selling sugar-coated haws, a robot shop listed machines with prices.

The list was specific. Tian Gong 2.0 at 779,000 yuan, Booster T1 at 199,000, Tianyi 2.0 at 350,000. Buyers could use vouchers issued for the parallel consumer festival, with subsidies of up to 1,500 yuan for an individual and up to 250,000 for a company. A machine that had been a research platform in April had become a line item in August. The shop also offered leasing, maintenance contracts and customization, which is the part of the business that decides whether a buyer signs.

On the stands, the engineering arguments were about endurance rather than novelty. UBTECH showed Walker S2, which carries a 15-kilogram load, performs sub-millimetre work with a four-finger hand, and swaps its own battery in about three minutes so that it can keep working through a shift. A smaller firm, Luming, demonstrated a full-size humanoid that gets up from lying flat in about one second, against the three to five seconds that most models need, which is a statement about control loops rather than about strength.

Industry figures came out alongside the exhibits. Revenue for Chinese robot companies rose 27.8 per cent in the first half of the year; industrial robot output rose 35.6 per cent and service robot output 25.5 per cent; the country has been the largest industrial robot market for twelve consecutive years, with 302,000 units sold in 2024, and robot density in manufacturing now ranks third in the world.

Those figures explain why the price list appeared here rather than at a trade fair for consumers. Industrial buyers have been the reliable customers, and the humanoid makers are trying to move from selling prototypes to industrial users into selling batches to factories, hospitals and, eventually, households. The bottleneck is not the demonstration. It is the service network, the spare parts, the insurance and the question of who is liable when a machine drops a box on a foot.

A parallel experiment started two days after the conference closed. At the Ice Ribbon arena, 280 teams from 16 countries competed in a humanoid games meeting: sprinting, football, boxing, and a 100-metre obstacle course that one machine completed in a little over two minutes. Sport is a poor proxy for factory work, but it is a good proxy for reliability under conditions nobody scripted. What the week showed, in the end, is a shift in the argument. Five years ago the question was whether a humanoid could walk across a room without falling. This August the question was what it costs, who maintains it, and how quickly the battery can be changed.
