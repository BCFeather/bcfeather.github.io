South Africa won the Rugby World Cup for the fourth time on 28 October, beating New Zealand 12-11 in a final at the Stade de France that turned on penalties, discipline and a missed kick in the seventy-ninth minute. Handre Pollard kicked four penalties, and the only try of the match went to New Zealand through Beauden Barrett. The All Blacks played most of the second half and part of the first with fourteen men after Sam Cane's yellow card was upgraded to red for a high tackle.

The numbers describe a team that won without dominating. South Africa won its three knockout matches by a single point each, against France in the quarter-final, England in the semi-final and New Zealand in the final, and it spent long periods of those matches without the ball. The pattern is deliberate: a rush defence, a kicking game that pins opponents in their own half, and a bench of forwards that arrives in the second half to win the collisions.

New Zealand's preparation had been uneven. The team lost to France in the opening match, then rebuilt through the tournament and produced its best performance in the semi-final against Argentina. In the final it created chances and kicked poorly from the tee, missing a conversion and a long-range penalty that would have changed the result, and its captain's dismissal left the pack short against the strongest set piece in the competition. The loss was New Zealand's first in a World Cup final, and it came in a match where the scrum held and the lineout did not.

The tournament itself expanded. France hosted with matches spread from Marseille to Lille, Portugal and Chile made their debuts, and the pool stage produced record crowds and a television audience that the organizers said exceeded the 2019 edition. The quarter-finals delivered three of the best matches of the year, including France's one-point defeat to South Africa and Ireland's loss to New Zealand, and the sport's leading nations arrived at the end in a narrower band than at any previous World Cup.

For South Africa the victory completes a cycle that began with the 2019 title and includes a British and Irish Lions series. Siya Kolisi, the captain, has become the most visible figure in the country's public life beyond politics, and the team's transformation programme, which selects from a much wider pool than a decade ago, has been presented by the governing body as a model. The political symbolism of a Springbok captain from a township carries weight in a country where sport and identity are inseparable.

Attention turns immediately to 2027, when Australia hosts an expanded field of twenty-four teams, and several leading players will retire before then. South Africa has won half of the World Cups held since 2007 with a style that depends on physical superiority and accurate kicking, and the teams that lost by a point this month will spend four years working out how to take those margins back.
