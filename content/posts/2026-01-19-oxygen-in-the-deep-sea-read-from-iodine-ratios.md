Oxygen in the ancient ocean is inferred rather than measured, and the proxies available are indirect. A study published this month in *Science Advances* used the ratio of iodine-129 to iodine-127 in marine carbonates to reconstruct dissolved oxygen in deep water, and placed a rise in oxygenation 10 million years earlier than the previous estimate.

Proxy rests on chemistry. Iodate is the stable form of iodine in oxygenated seawater, and iodide dominates under low oxygen; the two have different affinities for carbonate minerals, so the iodine concentration in a carbonate encodes the redox state of the water it formed in. Paired with a cosmogenic isotope for dating, the ratio becomes a recorder.

Sampling came from three ocean drilling sites, with carbonate from a 40-million-year interval and independent age control from foraminifera. The team measured 260 samples, and the ratio shifts abruptly at a horizon dated to 430 million years ago, which the paper interprets as a rise in deep-water oxygen.

Magnitude matters. The shift corresponds to an increase from roughly 40 micromoles per kilogram to 90, which is still low by modern standards of 200 but enough to support a larger animal community. The paper argues that this rise precedes the appearance of large burrowing animals in the record by 15 million years.

Alternative explanations were tested and partly excluded. The ratio can be altered by diagenesis, the chemical change of sediment after burial, and the team selected samples with primary fabric preserved under microscopy and cross-checked a subset against a second proxy based on molybdenum isotopes.

What the result changes is the timeline. The previous placement of the oxygenation step coincided with a known radiation of animal body sizes, which made a causal story convenient. The new date severs that coincidence, and the paper's discussion suggests that oxygen availability preceded the ecological change rather than triggering it.

Limits are the usual ones for a single proxy. Iodine in carbonate is sensitive to the local sedimentation rate and to the carbonate's mineralogy, and the calibration comes from a small number of modern analogues. A replication at a fourth site is in progress.

Calibration is the weakest link in the proxy. The fractionation between iodate and iodide is measured in a handful of culture experiments, and the transfer to carbonate depends on the mineral phase: aragonite and calcite incorporate iodine differently, and the paper's samples are mixed. The authors correct for mineralogy using a separate measurement and describe the correction as approximate.

The proxy's advantage over alternatives is that it is cheap. A molybdenum isotope measurement needs a clean laboratory and a mass spectrometer; an iodine ratio needs the same instrument and a simpler chemistry, which is why the method is spreading to groups that could not previously afford redox reconstructions.

For a reader, the interesting feature is how much of Earth's history is reconstructed from trace elements. The oxygen content of an ocean that disappeared 430 million years ago is read from a few micrograms of iodine in a rock, and the uncertainty in that reading is the uncertainty in the whole story of when complex life could breathe.
