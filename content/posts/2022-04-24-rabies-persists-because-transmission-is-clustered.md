title: "Rabies persists at low prevalence because transmission is clustered"

description: "In 50,000 dogs in Tanzania followed from 2002 to 2016, rabies prevalence never exceeded 0.15 per cent, and persistence depended on local depletion of susceptible animals and on the movement of a few rabid dogs."
---

A pathogen that kills its host quickly should burn out, and rabies does not, which has puzzled modellers for decades. The answer lies in the spatial structure of transmission, and it needs data at the scale of individual dogs in individual villages. Rabies is transmitted by saliva during a bite, and a rabid dog is infectious for a few days before it dies, so the window in which transmission happens is short and the contact network is what determines the spread.

A study followed 50,000 dogs across Tanzania from 2002 to 2016, with spatially resolved case data, and fitted individual-based models to those records. The paper appeared in *Science*, 28 April 2022.

Its prevalence never exceeded 0.15 per cent, which is a level at which a simple mass-action model predicts extinction. Best-fitting models instead showed appreciable depletion of susceptible animals at local scales, because deaths cluster in villages and because dogs already incubating the virus are removed from the susceptible pool before they can transmit. Depletion of susceptibles is normally a mechanism that drives a pathogen extinct, and here it operates locally while the metapopulation replenishes the patch from its neighbours.

Individual variation in the behaviour of rabid dogs is the second mechanism. A rabid dog that travels further than the average disperses the virus between villages, and the lineages that result cocirculate in a metapopulation rather than replacing one another. Cocirculation of lineages is a signature of repeated reintroduction, because a lineage that persists in one patch without contact with its neighbours would be replaced by a fitter one.

The metapopulation structure is what allows a virus with a short infectious period to persist: local outbreaks fade, and reintroduction from a neighbouring patch restarts them. The scale at which the patches operate is smaller than the district and larger than the village. A metapopulation model needs the movement of animals between patches, and the study infers it from the genetic relationships among viruses sampled in different villages. A model that assumes homogeneous mixing predicts extinction at this prevalence, and the failure of that prediction is the reason the spatial structure had to be measured.

A control implication is that dog vaccination has to reach a high fraction of the population across a connected region, because a campaign that covers one village and misses the next leaves a reservoir. The same logic applies to measles and to other acute infections in spatially structured populations. Vaccination campaigns that reach 70 per cent of dogs in a district have eliminated rabies in several settings, and the model explains why the threshold is high in a connected landscape.

Data come from one country and one ecological setting, and the mechanisms are general enough to be tested elsewhere. The study is a demonstration that persistence can be a property of geography rather than of the pathogen alone. Elimination programmes in Latin America and in parts of Africa have shown that sustained vaccination can interrupt transmission, and the study explains why lapses are followed by rapid return.
