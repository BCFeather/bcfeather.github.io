First-line treatment for advanced non-small-cell lung cancer without a targetable driver is pembrolizumab, with chemotherapy added when PD-L1 expression is low. The regimen works and then stops working, and the field has spent five years looking for a partner drug that extends the benefit without adding a second cytotoxic backbone. Antibody-drug conjugates are the most promising class, because they deliver the cytotoxic payload to tumour cells and spare much of the rest. The class's promise is that it can be combined without adding a second cytotoxic regimen, which is what the pembrolizumab backbone was waiting for.

Sacituzumab tirumotecan targets TROP2, a surface protein expressed across most non-small-cell tumours, and it was tested with pembrolizumab against pembrolizumab alone in PD-L1-positive disease. The interim analysis, reported in *The Lancet* on 15 July 2026, followed patients for a median of 10.5 months. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival by blinded review. TROP2 is also expressed in normal epithelium, which is why stomatitis is the class's signature toxicity rather than a rare event.

Median progression-free survival was not reached in the combination arm, with a confidence interval starting at 13.6 months, against 5.7 months with pembrolizumab alone. The stratified hazard ratio was 0.35, with a confidence interval from 0.26 to 0.47. The benefit appeared in both PD-L1 strata, with a hazard ratio of 0.28 in tumours scoring 1 to 49 per cent and 0.47 in tumours scoring 50 per cent or more. The confidence interval's lower bound of 13.6 months against a 5.7-month control is the more conservative statement of the gain.

Stratum analysis is the interesting part. Checkpoint monotherapy works best at high PD-L1, and a combination that helps most where monotherapy is weakest suggests that the conjugate is contributing independent cytotoxicity rather than merely sensitising the tumour to immunotherapy. If that reading holds, the regimen should be tested in PD-L1-negative disease, where the unmet need is largest. The trial's population was also restricted to PD-L1-positive tumours, so the negative group remains untested.

Interim analyses of a surrogate endpoint carry their own risks. The not-reached median is an artefact of short follow-up, and the confidence interval's lower bound is the honest number. Overall survival is immature, and the toxicity profile of a TROP2 conjugate includes stomatitis and neutropenia, which matter in a population that is often elderly and comorbid. A hazard ratio of 0.35 in an interim analysis is also the kind of number that tends to soften as follow-up matures.

Design leaves one question unanswered, since pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy remains a standard for the low-PD-L1 group. Whether the conjugate beats chemotherapy as the partner, rather than merely beating monotherapy, is the comparison that a guideline committee will want. A three-arm trial would settle it, and until then the regimen is a promising option rather than a new default. A positive answer would move the conjugate into the first-line backbone, and a negative one would leave it as an option for patients who cannot take chemotherapy.
