Officials at the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security published a revised set of export controls on advanced computing chips and semiconductor manufacturing equipment on 17 October, and the document runs to several hundred pages. The change replaces the measure that had governed the 2022 rules, which tracked interconnect bandwidth, with a combination of performance and density thresholds, and it adds licence requirements for a longer list of countries.

The technical purpose is to close a loophole rather than to widen a ban. Chipmakers had designed products for the Chinese market that sat just below the 2022 threshold, and the new rules try to capture a class of processors by measuring computing power per unit of area, which is harder to engineer around without giving up performance. Analysts named the A800 and H800 as products that the revision was written to catch, along with several data-center parts sold under other labels. The thresholds are written as formulas rather than lists, so a chip falls inside or outside depending on how its transistors are arranged.

The practical question is how much of the market is affected. China accounted for a large share of data-center revenue for the largest American supplier, and the company said it would review the rules before saying what they would cost. Exporters now need licences for sales that previously needed none, and the licence process allows for presumptive denial, which means shipments can be stopped without a finding that they would be used for military purposes. The company's China revenue had already fallen as customers stockpiled parts ahead of earlier deadlines.

Beyond chips, the rules reach the tools that make them and the companies that service them. American firms may no longer provide maintenance or spare parts to certain Chinese fabrication plants without approval, and the list of restricted end users has been extended. Governments in Asia and Europe have complained that the controls apply to their companies through the extraterritorial reach of American technology, and several have asked for exemptions that the rules leave to case-by-case review. Those exemptions are decided in Washington, and the governments that seek them have little leverage.

Enforcement is the weak link. The controls depend on knowing where a chip ends up, and the trade in advanced parts runs through distributors, assemblers and resellers in third countries. Officials have said they will work with allies on inspections and on the tracking of end users, and the same officials acknowledge that the resources for that work are smaller than the volume of the trade. Customs officers cannot inspect a processor, and the paperwork that would reveal its destination is filed by the exporter.

Computing power now gets treated as a strategic material, and the industry's response has been to redesign around whatever threshold the government publishes. A rule written in pages of specifications creates a design problem that engineers are paid to solve, and the next revision will be judged by whether the thresholds hold or by whether the products simply change shape again.
