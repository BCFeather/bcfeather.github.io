A rare disease with 60 known patients has no commercial incentive for a drug programme, and academic screens have to substitute breadth for depth. A screen reported this month used zebrafish embryos carrying a mitochondrial mutation, tested 3,000 approved compounds, and found two that restored normal swimming behaviour.

The model is the enabling choice. Zebrafish embryos are transparent, develop in 72 hours, and their muscle activity can be recorded as a movement trace. A mitochondrial defect shows up as reduced swimming and as a change in the pattern of spontaneous contractions.

Throughput is 96 embryos per compound in a 96-well plate, and the assay reads movement with a camera and a tracking script. The primary screen took four months and produced 42 hits, of which 8 were confirmed in a dose-response assay.

Mechanism was identified for two compounds. Both act on the same pathway, a kinase that regulates mitochondrial fission, and the paper shows that the mutation causes excessive fission which the compounds suppress. That is a plausible target for a small molecule.

One compound is approved for an unrelated indication and has human safety data, which is the reason the result is interesting rather than theoretical. A repurposing trial in the 60 patients is being designed, with a primary endpoint of a six-minute walk test.

Validation in a second model is the standard the paper meets. The team repeated the rescue in mouse fibroblasts carrying the human mutation, and in a cell line derived from one patient. Both showed restored respiration.

Limits are the usual ones for a screen. Three thousand compounds is a small library, and the hits are what a library of approved drugs contains; a broader library would find more. The zebrafish dose is not the human dose, and the pharmacokinetics are untested.

Dose-response confirms the mechanism rather than the hit. The two compounds rescued swimming at 0.5 and 2 micromolar, and the effect disappeared above 10, which is consistent with a specific target rather than a general antioxidant effect.

The path to patients is short for a repurposing candidate and not trivial. A trial in 60 patients needs a natural-history study first, because the disease's progression varies, and the team has begun one with 22 patients enrolled.

The assay's design is what made 3,000 compounds feasible. The screen used a swimming behavior measured by a camera and a tracking algorithm, at 12 embryos per compound, and the primary hit rate was 0.4 per cent, which is high enough to justify the screen and low enough to trust it.

For a reader, the case is a template for how rare disease research proceeds without a market. Academic screening, repurposing candidates with existing safety data, and a small trial with a functional endpoint is a route that costs a few million dollars rather than a hundred million, and it is the only route available for patients this few.
