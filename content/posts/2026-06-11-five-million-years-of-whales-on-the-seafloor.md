When a whale dies in the open ocean its carcass sinks and becomes a concentrated patch of food on a seafloor that is otherwise close to empty. The resulting community, a whale fall, supports worms, bivalves and crustaceans for decades, and some of those organisms are found nowhere else.

A survey reported in *Nature* on 11 June mapped that phenomenon at scale. The authors identified 485 fossil whale sites and five active whale falls at depths between 4,200 and 7,000 metres, with the density of remains reaching 759.5 individuals per square kilometre at the richest site. The authors combined bathymetric mapping with submersible photography and sampling, which is the only way to distinguish a whale skeleton from a rock of similar shape. That mapping is slow and expensive work.

Isotopic dating puts the oldest fossils at roughly 5.3 million years, in the Early Pliocene, which means the region has been receiving carcasses for at least that long. A process that depends on the death of a large animal is thus a stable feature of the deep sea over geological time. A carcass that reaches the abyss is an event with a duration: the soft tissue is consumed within months, the bone lasts decades, and the community that depends on it changes composition as the supply runs down.

The communities differ from the surrounding fauna. Jellyfish, brittle stars, bone-boring worms and chemosynthetic bivalves dominate, and many of the species appear to be new to science. Chemosynthesis is the reason the assemblage can support itself: bacteria oxidize sulfur compounds from the decomposing bone, and the animals feed on them.

Most of the fossils belong to beaked whales, both living and extinct, including a species new to science that the authors name *Pterocetus diamantinae*. Beaked whales dive deep and spend long periods at depth, which makes them the group most likely to end up on the abyssal plain rather than on a shelf where scavengers and currents disperse a carcass. The new species is a reminder that a survey of this kind produces taxonomy as a by-product, and that the deep sea still yields animals that no collection contains.

The survey also matters methodologically. Whale falls are found by accident, usually during a submersible dive for something else, and a systematic count from a mapped region turns an anecdote into a rate. That rate is what a model of deep-sea connectivity needs, because a whale fall is a stepping stone for species that otherwise could not cross an ocean basin. If whale falls are this common, then the deep sea is not a uniform habitat but a mosaic of enrichments, and the connectivity of its fauna depends on how often those patches appear. The survey also gives a baseline that future whaling or ship-strike policy can be measured against, since a population decline would show up first as a fall in carcass supply.
