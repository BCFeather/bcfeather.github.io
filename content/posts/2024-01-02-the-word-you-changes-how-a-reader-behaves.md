Written communication often addresses the reader directly, and the pronoun that does the addressing is a small grammatical choice with a large behavioural literature behind it. A paper in *Nature Communications* on 2 January 2024 tests what second-person address does. An experiment that varies a pronoun varies very little and measures something large, and small manipulations are what make a causal claim credible. The laboratory is a setting in which the reader has nothing at stake, and the field is where the stake exists. A reader who is addressed is a reader who is implicated, and implication is the mechanism the paper proposes rather than the effect it measures. Aggregate effects of this size are what policy evaluations report, and reporting them is how a small manipulation becomes a published recommendation.

The studies compare messages that use you with messages that avoid it, and the outcomes are measured rather than inferred. Behaviour is the endpoint, not the impression of the text. Compliance is the outcome a communicator cares about, and a study that measures compliance is a study a practitioner can use. A result about the laboratory is still a result, and the paper says so rather than generalizing from it. Practitioners write messages for a living, and a result about pronouns is a result they can apply without training.

Readers addressed directly respond differently from readers addressed indirectly. The difference appears in compliance and in engagement, and it appears across settings that the authors chose to vary. Engagement is the second outcome, and an engaged reader is one who continues rather than one who agrees at once. The size of the effect is modest, and a modest effect applied to millions of messages is a large effect in aggregate.

The mechanism proposed is psychological distance. A message that names the reader closes the distance between sender and recipient, and closing distance is what changes a decision. Variation across settings is what protects the result from being an artefact of one message, and the authors chose settings that differ in stakes. A modest effect is also the size that replicates, and replication is what a communication claim needs before it is adopted.

Distance is a construct with a long history in social psychology, and the paper connects a grammatical device to it. Naming the reader is a way of making the message about that reader, and a message about the reader is a message a reader acts on.

Field settings are where the effect matters most, since a message is usually written to produce an action. Social psychology has measured distance with proxies for decades, and a grammatical device is a cleaner manipulation than a proxy.

A laboratory result that does not survive a field trial is a result about the laboratory rather than about communication. A field trial is the test that a communication effect has to pass, and passing it is what separates an effect from a demonstration.
