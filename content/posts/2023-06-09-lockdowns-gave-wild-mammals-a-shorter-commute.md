title: "Lockdowns gave wild mammals a shorter commute"
date: "2023-06-09T09:25:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "Tracking data from mammals around the world show that movement increased and journeys lengthened when human activity fell during COVID-19 lockdowns, according to an analysis published in *Science* on 9 June 2023."
---

Human activity is a constraint on animal movement, and the constraint is usually measured indirectly. A pandemic that abruptly reduced the constraint provided an experiment that no ecologist could have designed.

The analysis in *Science*, 9 June 2023 uses it. The authors assemble GPS tracks from mammals on several continents, compare the animals' movement during lockdown periods with movement before and after, and find that the animals travelled further and more freely when human disturbance was low.

The effect is a release. An animal that avoids roads and settlements during the day can use them when traffic stops, and the tracks show movements into areas that the animals avoided in normal times.

The duration is the limitation. A lockdown lasted months, and an animal's response over months is a behavioural adjustment rather than an evolutionary one.

The species vary. A large carnivore's range responds to prey and to roads, and a small herbivore's responds to the immediate presence of people. The authors' comparative design is what separates the responses.

The measurement is the achievement. Tracks from many studies, collected for other purposes, are harmonized into a common comparison, and the harmonization is the paper's technical contribution.

The policy reading is immediate. A protected area's boundary is a line on a map, and the animals' use of the land outside it depends on how people use that land.

The tourism question follows. A park that attracts visitors concentrates disturbance at the times and places the animals would otherwise use, and the lockdown data show how much the concentration costs.

What the paper does not show is a population effect. A change in movement is a behavioural response, and whether it translates into survival or reproduction is a separate measurement.

For conservation, the value is a quantified baseline. The value of quiet is often asserted and rarely measured, and the lockdown interval gives the assertion a number.

The comparison across species is the study's strength. A species that expanded its range during the quiet and a species that did not tell the authors which traits mediate the response, and the traits are the ones a manager can act on.

The habitat's fragmentation matters more than the disturbance's intensity. An animal in a continuous landscape can avoid a road, and an animal in a fragmented one cannot. The authors' analysis of patch size addresses the interaction.

The seasonal timing is a confounder the authors handle explicitly. Spring movement differs from winter movement for reasons unrelated to people, and a comparison that mixes seasons would attribute a seasonal change to a lockdown.

The data's bias is acknowledged. Tracks come from populations that researchers chose to study, and those populations are not a random sample of the world's mammals.

The recovery is the natural follow-up. A question that the dataset can eventually answer is whether the animals returned to their old ranges when the people returned, and whether the return was immediate or gradual.
