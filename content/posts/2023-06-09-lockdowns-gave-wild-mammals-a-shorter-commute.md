title: "Lockdowns gave wild mammals a shorter commute"
date: "2023-06-09T09:25:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "Tracking data from mammals around the world show that movement increased and journeys lengthened when human activity fell during COVID-19 lockdowns."
---

Human activity is a constraint on animal movement, and the constraint is usually measured indirectly. A pandemic that abruptly reduced it provided an experiment no ecologist could have designed. In *Science* on 9 June 2023, that experiment is used. The authors assemble GPS tracks from mammals on several continents, compare movement during lockdown periods with movement before and after, and find that animals travelled further and more freely when human disturbance was low.

Release is the effect. An animal that avoids roads and settlements by day can use them when traffic stops, and the tracks show movements into areas the animals avoided in normal times. Fragmentation matters more than intensity, because an animal in a continuous landscape can avoid a road while one in a fragmented landscape cannot, and the analysis of patch size addresses that interaction.

Species differ in response. A large carnivore's range responds to prey and roads, a small herbivore's to the immediate presence of people, and the comparative design separates the two. That comparison is the study's strength, since a species that expanded during the quiet and one that did not tell the authors which traits mediate the response, and those traits are what a manager can act on.

Measurement is the achievement. Tracks collected for other purposes are harmonised into a common comparison, and that harmonisation is the technical contribution. Seasonal timing is a confounder handled explicitly, because spring movement differs from winter movement for reasons unrelated to people, and a comparison mixing seasons would attribute a seasonal change to a lockdown.

Duration sets the limitation. A lockdown lasted months, and an animal's response over months is a behavioural adjustment rather than an evolutionary one. Data bias is acknowledged too, as tracks come from populations researchers chose to study, and those populations are not a random sample of the world's mammals.

Population effect stays unshown, because a change in movement is a behavioural response and whether it becomes survival or reproduction is a separate measurement. Conservation gains a quantified baseline, since the value of quiet is often asserted and rarely measured, and the lockdown interval gives the assertion a number.

Tourism raises a follow-on question, because a park attracting visitors concentrates disturbance at the times and places animals would otherwise use, and the lockdown data show what that concentration costs. Policy reads it immediately, since a protected area's boundary is a line on a map while animals' use of land outside it depends on how people use that land. Recovery is the natural follow-up, and the dataset can eventually say whether animals returned to old ranges when people returned, and whether the return was immediate. A second lockdown, had one come, would have shown whether the response was learned, and the absence of one leaves the study with a single natural experiment. A control set of tracks from areas that never had a lockdown would strengthen the comparison, and some of the dataset's regions approximate that control by accident.
