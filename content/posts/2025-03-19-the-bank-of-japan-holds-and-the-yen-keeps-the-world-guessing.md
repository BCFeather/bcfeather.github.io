On the nineteenth of March the Bank of Japan left its policy rate unchanged, at the level it had reached after a year of increases that were small by the standards of other central banks and enormous by the standards of Japan. For most of three decades the country's short-term rate was at or below zero, and the money that the world borrowed in yen was effectively free. The change since last spring is the beginning of the end of that arrangement, and the beginning is going slowly because the end has consequences.

Carry trade is the mechanism that matters. An investor who borrows in a currency with a low interest rate and invests in one with a high rate earns the difference, and the yen has been the funding currency of choice for that trade because its rate was the lowest available. The trade is enormous and it is not measured anywhere, because it runs through hedge funds, insurance companies and the treasury desks of ordinary firms. When the funding currency starts to cost something, the trade becomes less profitable and some of it unwinds, and the unwinding is what produces the sudden moves in the exchange rate that the newspapers report as mystery.

Domestic arguments for raising rates are the ones the bank has been making. Inflation in Japan has been above the two per cent target for three years, wages have risen in the annual negotiations for two consecutive years, and the labour market is tight in a way that it has not been since the 1980s. A central bank that keeps a zero rate in those conditions is a central bank that has decided that the currency and the price level matter less than the cost of borrowing.

The cost of borrowing is the political part. The government carries a debt that is the largest in the rich world relative to the size of the economy, and every basis point of higher rates adds to the interest bill. A rise of a quarter of a point is small for a household and large for a treasury with a debt of that size, and the bank and the finance ministry have been arguing about the pace for the whole of the past year.

What the pause in March means is that the argument has been settled for now on the side of caution, and that the next meeting will be watched more closely than this one. The bank will move again when the wage data and the consumption data justify it, and the market will guess the date wrong several times before it happens. The yen will strengthen and weaken around those guesses, and the exporters who benefit from a weak currency will complain about a strong one, and the households that buy imported food will benefit from the change, which is the argument the bank makes in public and the one the exporters dispute in private. The rate decision is therefore a decision about which of those two groups pays.
