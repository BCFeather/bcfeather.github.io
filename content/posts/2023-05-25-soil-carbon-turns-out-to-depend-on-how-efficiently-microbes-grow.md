Soil holds more carbon than the atmosphere and the vegetation combined, and the size of that reservoir is a central number in the climate problem. Models predicting it have disagreed about which process dominates.

In *Nature* on 24 May 2023, one process is identified. The authors assemble global soil profiles and microbial measurements, and find that carbon use efficiency, the fraction of carbon microbes convert into biomass rather than respire, is the strongest predictor of soil organic carbon worldwide.

Budget is the mechanism. Carbon entering the soil is either retained in biomass and necromass or released as carbon dioxide, and the efficiency sets the split, so a community growing efficiently leaves more behind. Across biomes the pattern holds: efficiency varies with temperature and substrate quality, and that variation maps onto the observed distribution of carbon from boreal peat to tropical oxisol.

Minerals offer the competing explanation. Carbon bound to clay particles is protected from decomposition, and a model ignoring binding overstates efficiency's role, which is why including both predictors matters. Depth complicates the picture as well, because surface soil and subsoil hold carbon under different conditions of oxygen, temperature and root input, and separating the horizons beats averaging them into one number.

Structure follows for modelling. A model treating microbes as a black box respiring at a fixed rate misses the variation entirely, while one carrying efficiency as a variable reproduces the observations, and that difference explains why several earlier estimates diverged. Management supplies a lever, since practices favouring efficient growth, including added organic matter and reduced tillage, could increase storage, and the analysis quantifies how large the effect might be.

Measurement is the caveat. Efficiency is measured in the laboratory on incubated samples, and an incubation is not a field, so the use of many sites is what makes the aggregate meaningful rather than merely large. Feedback complicates the forecast, because warming raises respiration more than growth in many soils, which lowers efficiency and turns a storage sink into a source.

Long-run capacity stays out of reach, as a soil storing more carbon under a practice may saturate, and the saturation point is a parameter the data do not pin down. Climate policy gains a measurable target: a soil's carbon stock can be sampled, and a predictor explaining its variation is what a monitoring programme needs in order to know whether a practice worked. A tracer experiment would settle how much of the retained carbon passes through microbial biomass rather than through mineral binding, and until such partitioning exists the two explanations cannot be weighed against each other directly. Seasonality also enters, because microbial growth in a temperate soil follows root input and temperature, and an annual average hides a cycle that a sampling campaign at one time of year would misread. A warming experiment on intact cores would be the direct check, since incubations can be held at several temperatures while a field site cannot, and the direction of the response is what the carbon forecast turns on. An explicit microbial model coupled to a climate projection would then carry the feedback rather than assuming it, and that coupling is what separates a plausible mechanism from a useful one.
