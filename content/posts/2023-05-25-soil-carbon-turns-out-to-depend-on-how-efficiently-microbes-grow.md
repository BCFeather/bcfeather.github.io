Soil holds more carbon than the atmosphere and the vegetation combined, and the size of that reservoir is a central number in the climate problem. Models that predict it have disagreed about which process dominates.

The analysis in *Nature*, 24 May 2023 identifies one. The authors assemble global soil profiles and microbial measurements, and find that carbon use efficiency, the fraction of carbon that microbes convert into biomass rather than respire, is the strongest predictor of soil organic carbon across the world.

The mechanism is a budget. Carbon that enters the soil is either retained in biomass and in necromass or released as carbon dioxide, and the efficiency sets the split. A community that grows efficiently leaves more behind.

The geography is the pattern. The efficiency varies with temperature and with the substrate's quality, and the variation maps onto the observed distribution of soil carbon.

The modelling consequence is structural. A model that treats microbes as a black box that respires at a fixed rate will miss the variation, and a model that carries efficiency as a variable reproduces the observations.

The management implication is a lever. Practices that favour efficient microbial growth, including the addition of organic matter and the reduction of disturbance, could increase storage, and the size of the effect is what the analysis quantifies.

The measurement's difficulty is the caveat. Efficiency is measured in the laboratory on incubated samples, and an incubation is not the field. The authors' use of many sites is what makes the aggregate meaningful.

The feedback is the complication. Warming increases respiration more than it increases growth in many soils, so that efficiency falls as the climate warms, which turns a storage sink into a source.

What the paper cannot establish is the long-run capacity. A soil that stores more carbon under a practice may saturate, and the saturation point is a parameter that the data do not pin down.

For climate policy, the value is a measurable target. A soil's carbon stock can be sampled, and a predictor that explains its variation is what a monitoring programme needs.

The model's fit is its argument. A single origin fails to reproduce the diversity of ancient genomes, and a weakly structured stem reproduces it without positing a migration that the fossil record does not support.

The date matters for archaeology. A split around 120,000 years ago is consistent with fossils of that age, and a model that fits both the genes and the stones is stronger than one that fits only the genes.
The mineral association is the competing explanation. Carbon that binds to clay particles is protected from decomposition, and a model that ignores binding will overstate the role of efficiency. The paper's inclusion of both is what makes the comparison informative.
The depth profile matters. Surface soil and subsoil hold carbon under different conditions, and an analysis that separates the horizons is more informative than one that averages them.
