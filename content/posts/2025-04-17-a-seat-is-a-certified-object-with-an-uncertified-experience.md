title: "Airline seats are certified for a crash rather than comfort"
date: "2025-04-17T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [business]
description: "Airlines have added rows by thinning the seat and shrinking the pitch, and the certification that governs the result tests a crash rather than the flight."
---

An airline seat is certified for a sixteen-g acceleration in a crash, and that standard sets the structure rather than the comfort. A seat that passes the test can be made with a thinner cushion and a shorter pitch, and the manufacturer sells the airline a seat that fits more rows into the same cabin. The passenger experiences the result as a knee against the back of the row in front. A test that measures a seat's ability to protect a body in a crash does not measure how that body feels after four hours.

The pitch has narrowed over two decades. Economy class on a narrowbody has moved from about thirty-two inches to thirty or less, and the low-cost carriers that pioneered the change have been followed by the full-service airlines on short-haul routes. A thirty-inch pitch is a number that a tall passenger notices on every flight, and the airlines sell the exit row as the remedy. A pitch of twenty-eight inches is now common on the shortest routes, and the passengers on those routes are the ones who booked the cheapest fare.

Weight is the other driver. A thinner seat weighs less, and a kilogram removed from every seat in a widebody saves fuel over the aircraft's life. A seat that is both thinner and lighter is a seat that pays for itself twice, which is why the manufacturers compete on grams. A seat frame that is made of composite rather than aluminium saves several kilograms, and the saving is multiplied by three hundred seats.

Regulation has been slow to follow. The evacuation certification tests whether a cabin can be emptied in ninety seconds, and a tighter cabin makes that harder. The regulators have looked at seat pitch in the context of evacuation rather than in the context of health, and the studies that link immobility to thrombosis are not a certification standard. A regulator that tests evacuation has to fill a cabin with passengers and count them out of the exits, and the test is done once per aircraft type.

The market has responded with segmentation. A passenger who wants legroom pays for it, and the fare that includes a seat with space is now a premium product on a route where the base fare is a ticket and a bag. That is a coherent business model and a redistribution of comfort rather than an increase in it. A premium seat with forty inches is sold as a product, and the passenger who buys it is subsidizing the one behind.

What is not measured is the cost of the discomfort. A flight of four hours in a thirty-inch seat is a flight that leaves a passenger tired, and the productivity lost on arrival is real and unpaid. The industry's accounting does not include it, and the passenger's does. A passenger who arrives unable to work has lost a day, and the airline has not paid for it.
