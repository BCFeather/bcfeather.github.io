Two national freight railways in Canada locked out their employees on 22 August, stopping most freight traffic in the country, and the government ordered binding arbitration less than a day later. Service resumed on 26 August, and the dispute over scheduling and fatigue rules moved to an arbitrator. The lockout began within an hour of the union's deadline, and the companies said they would rather close the network than run it under temporary rules.

Interdiction was the immediate damage. A chlorine supplier that serves water utilities said it would run out within days, and the ports that depend on rail for inland movement warned that containers would stack. A supply chain that assumes rail is a supply chain with no substitute for bulk chemicals. Grain was the largest exposure, because the harvest moves by rail and the elevators have storage for weeks rather than months.

Dispute is about rest. The unions wanted rules that guarantee a minimum interval between shifts, and the companies wanted flexibility to move crews as traffic demanded, which is the same argument that railways in other countries have had. A fatigue rule is a scheduling rule, and the schedule is where the profit is made. The companies argue that rest rules written for one network cannot fit two railways sharing track, which is the technical core of the argument. The union's position is that a crew that has worked a night shift should not be called back after eight hours, and the companies say that the terminals cannot be staffed that way.

Government intervention was unusual in its speed. A minister who orders arbitration within a day has decided that the economic cost of a stoppage exceeds the political cost of legislating, and the precedent is what both sides will remember at the next negotiation. An emergency power used quickly is an emergency power that becomes routine. Opposition parties accused the government of legislating on the companies' side, and the government replied that the alternative was a stoppage of weeks.

Arbitrator's award will set the terms for several years. A decision that imposes rest intervals raises the cost of operation, and a decision that leaves scheduling to the companies leaves the unions with little to show for a stoppage. A third party's award is the outcome that neither side can spin. The award will be published within months, and the unions have said that a decision ignoring rest would justify a new mandate.

What the four days show is that a national economy is more dependent on a single network than its operators are willing to admit. A railway that can stop the water supply is a railway that has more bargaining power than its customers, and the customers have begun to say so. Customers hurt in the four days have asked for a rule requiring service during a dispute, which the railways oppose. A network that carries grain, chemicals and containers is a network that a government cannot let stop for long, and the four days were long enough.
