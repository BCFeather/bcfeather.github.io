description: "*Science* reports that a gene-editing experiment in human embryos has produced a cleaner result than earlier attempts, while the safety questions that stopped the field remain unanswered."
---

Editing the DNA of a human embryo is the one experiment in genetics that everyone agrees needs a bar higher than the one for a mouse. The 2018 case that produced gene-edited children was condemned worldwide, and the field has since been effectively paused in most jurisdictions while the technical problems were worked on.

What improved in the work reported in *Science* on 11 June is the efficiency and precision of editing at the earliest stage, where the embryo is a single cell and the edit has to be copied faithfully into every descendant. Earlier methods produced mosaics, embryos in which some cells carried the edit and others did not, which is both a technical failure and a clinical hazard. Precision at the single-cell stage is the technical core, because an embryo that is edited after the first division carries the change in some lineages and not others.

A cleaner result is progress and not a green light. The safety questions that matter are off-target edits, large deletions and rearrangements that standard assays may not detect, and the long-term consequences of editing a germline that will be inherited by people who cannot consent. Detecting a large deletion or a rearrangement requires whole-genome sequencing of single cells, which is expensive and rarely done in these experiments.

Herd immunity of the ethical argument is also unchanged. Editing an embryo to remove a disease gene is indistinguishable, in its consequences for the child, from selecting an embryo that does not carry it, which is already legal in many places. The difference is that editing opens the possibility of changes that selection cannot produce, including enhancement. The distinction between treating a disease and enhancing a trait is not written into the DNA, and a technique that cannot tell them apart cannot be regulated by intent alone.

Regulatory responses have been uneven. Some countries prohibit the implantation of edited embryos outright, others regulate it through existing fertility law, and a few have no specific rule. A technique that becomes reliable in a laboratory increases the pressure on that patchwork, because the scientific objection weakens while the legal one stays. The regulatory patchwork is unlikely to survive a reliable technique, because the laboratories that can do the work are not all in jurisdictions that forbid it.

The legitimate use that remains is research: studying early human development, and testing whether an edit corrects a specific defect in the cells that would form an embryo. That work can proceed under strict oversight, and the question the paper raises is whether the oversight will hold when the technique stops failing and starts working. The oversight question is whether committees that approve research on embryos have the expertise and the independence to police a technique that is improving quickly. A moratorium with a technical threshold is the compromise most likely to hold, since it lets the work proceed while keeping implantation out of reach.
