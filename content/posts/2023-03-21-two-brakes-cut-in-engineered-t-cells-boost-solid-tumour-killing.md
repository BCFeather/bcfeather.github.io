title: "Two brakes cut in engineered T cells boost solid tumour killing"
date: "2023-03-21T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "Engineered T cells lacking both Regnase-1 and Roquin-1, two regulators of inflammatory transcripts, control solid tumours better than cells lacking either alone, according to work published in *PNAS* on 21 March 2023."
---

Engineered T cells work in blood cancers and struggle in solid tumours. The reasons include a hostile microenvironment and a cell that runs out of drive, and the second has a genetic remedy: remove the brakes that limit a T cell's inflammatory programme.

The study in *PNAS*, 21 March 2023 removes two of them. The authors disrupt Regnase-1 and Roquin-1, proteins that destabilize inflammatory transcripts, in engineered T cells and test the cells in models of solid tumours.

The result is a synergy. Cells lacking either regulator alone improve on controls, and cells lacking both improve further. Two brakes in the same pathway are not redundant, and removing both releases more of the programme than either removal alone.

The mechanism is transcript stability. Regnase-1 and Roquin-1 recognize related sequence elements in messenger RNAs and target them for degradation, and a cell without both accumulates messages that a normal T cell turns over quickly.

The safety question is the reason the field is careful. A T cell with an unrestrained inflammatory programme can damage healthy tissue, and sustained cytokine production is a toxicity that has been seen in other engineered cells.

The models are the limitation. Mouse tumours and human cells in a mouse are approximations, and the toxicity that a human immune system would show is not fully reproduced.

The design's advantage is the cell's own regulation. An engineered receptor provides the specificity, and the genetic edit provides the persistence, so that the therapy does not need an exogenous cytokine to stay active.

What the paper cannot show is the durability. A T cell that is hyperactive may exhaust sooner, and a longer follow-up would test whether the early gain persists.

The comparison with cytokine-armoured cells is the strategic choice. A cell that secretes its own cytokine is a cell that can be controlled by a promoter, and a cell with a genetic edit is a cell whose behaviour is harder to switch off.

For cell therapy, the value is a target list. Regulators of transcript stability are a class, and the paper shows that two members of the class are worth removing together.

The clinical path is long. An edit that improves a mouse model has to survive manufacturing, and a cell product that is edited at two loci is harder to produce than one edited at a single site.

The receptor context matters. The authors test the edits in two clinical-stage receptor formats, which is what makes the claim general rather than specific to one construct.

The inflammatory programme is not only about killing. A T cell that persists in a tumour needs to resist exhaustion, and the transcripts that the two regulators control include genes that sustain a cell's activity over weeks.
The manufacturing question is concrete. A dual edit requires two guide RNAs and two cuts, and the efficiency of both has to be high in the same cell. The paper reports the editing rates, which is the number a process engineer would want.
