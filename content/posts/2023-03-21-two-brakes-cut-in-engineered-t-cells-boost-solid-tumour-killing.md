title: "Two brakes cut in engineered T cells boost solid tumour killing"
date: "2023-03-21T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "Engineered T cells lacking both Regnase-1 and Roquin-1, two regulators of inflammatory transcripts, control solid tumours better than cells lacking either alone."
---

Engineered T cells work in blood cancers and struggle in solid tumours. The reasons include a hostile microenvironment and a cell that runs out of drive, and the second has a genetic remedy in removing the brakes that limit a T cell's inflammatory programme.

In *PNAS* on 21 March 2023, two of those brakes are removed. The authors disrupt Regnase-1 and Roquin-1, proteins that destabilise inflammatory transcripts, in engineered T cells and test the cells in models of solid tumours.

Synergy is the result. Cells lacking either regulator alone improve on controls, and cells lacking both improve further, so two brakes in one pathway are not redundant and removing both releases more of the programme than either removal alone.

Because Regnase-1 and Roquin-1 recognise related sequence elements in messenger RNAs and target them for degradation, the mechanism is transcript stability, and a cell without both accumulates messages that a normal T cell turns over quickly. Killing is not the whole of that programme, since a T cell persisting in a tumour has to resist exhaustion and the transcripts under control include genes that sustain activity over weeks.

Safety is why the field is careful. A T cell with an unrestrained inflammatory programme can damage healthy tissue, and sustained cytokine production is a toxicity seen in other engineered cells. Cytokine-armoured cells are the strategic comparison, because a cell that secretes its own cytokine can be controlled by a promoter while a cell with a genetic edit has behaviour that is harder to switch off.

Models set the limitation. Mouse tumours and human cells in a mouse are approximations, and the toxicity a human immune system would show is not fully reproduced. Manufacturing is the concrete difficulty, since a dual edit needs two guide RNAs and two cuts with both efficiencies high in the same cell, and the reported editing rates are the number a process engineer would want.

Durability stays unshown, because a hyperactive T cell may exhaust sooner and a longer follow-up would test whether the early gain persists. The design's advantage lies in the cell's own regulation, with an engineered receptor supplying specificity and the edit supplying persistence so that no exogenous cytokine is needed. Cell therapy gains a target list, since regulators of transcript stability form a class and two of its members are worth removing together. The clinical path remains long, because a product edited at two loci is harder to produce than one edited at a single site, and the edits were tested in two clinical-stage receptor formats, which is what makes the claim general rather than tied to one construct. Which transcripts accumulate is the question a follow-up would ask, since the two regulators share targets but not all of them, and a sequencing comparison of single and double knockouts would show where the synergy comes from and which extra messages carry the risk. Which extra messages carry the toxicity is the part of the risk that a mouse model cannot settle, and a human cell system with a matched receptor is the closest available proxy for it.
