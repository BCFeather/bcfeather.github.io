Aleksandar Vucic won Serbia's presidential election on 3 April outright, taking more than half the vote in the first round and avoiding a runoff against a field of rivals who had split the opposition vote among themselves. His Serbian Progressive Party finished first in the parliamentary contest held the same day, but it fell short of a majority in the 250-seat assembly, which means the government he forms will need partners. Results came through the night from the state election commission, and the count was finished before the morning news.

Public attention went to the presidency, while the constitution puts most executive power in the government. That gap has suited Vucic for a decade: he moved from prime minister to president in 2017 and kept control of the party machine and the security services, and a fresh personal mandate lets him arbitrate the bargains that a minority government requires over ministries, budgets and appointments. Coalition partners from the socialist party have supplied that arithmetic before, and they were expected to do so again.

The campaign turned on two themes that have nothing to do with each other. One was Kosovo and the refusal to recognize its independence, which every serious candidate affirmed, since the dialogue brokered in Brussels has stalled and the Serbian list in Kosovo's assembly withdrew from its institutions in protest. The other was the war in Ukraine, where Serbia has voted against Russian aggression at the United Nations while declining to join sanctions and while its gas still arrives through the Balkan stream. The new contract has been presented at home as a diplomatic win, with terms below the spot price that other buyers in the region were paying.

Observers from the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe reported irregularities and an uneven playing field, and the opposition complained about pressure on public sector employees and about access to broadcast time. Turnout was solid and the result was accepted by the main challengers, the normal pattern in a country whose elections are competitive while administration and media tilt beneath them.

Energy is where the war reaches Belgrade directly. The government has refused to impose sanctions on Russia, signed a new gas contract with Gazprom, and depends on the route that carries Russian gas to its border, which leaves it exposed to the same payment dispute that had already cut supplies to Poland and Bulgaria on 27 April. Accession talks with the European Union have moved slowly for a decade, and the war has slowed them further. Keeping both the European Union accession process and the Russian supply alive is a hedge, and hedges cost money and credibility.

Two stories ran on the same day, and both concern institutions rather than personalities. A president with a majority of the vote is strong, a governing party that needs partners to legislate is weak, and Serbia now has both. A directly elected presidency turns the office into a plebiscite on a person, which is precisely the contest that a party machine can win.
