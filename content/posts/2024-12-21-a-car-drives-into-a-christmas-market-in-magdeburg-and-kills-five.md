description: "A man drove a rented car through a Christmas market in the German city of Magdeburg on the evening of 20 December, killing five people and injuring more than two hundred, in an attack that revived arguments about security and asylum."
---

Driver took a black rented sport utility vehicle through the Christmas market at the Alter Markt in Magdeburg shortly after seven in the evening on the twentieth of December, following a route of about four hundred metres between the stalls. Police said the car entered at one end of the market and drove towards the town hall, where it was stopped, and that the driver was arrested at the scene. Attack lasted less than two minutes and left a corridor of broken stalls, scattered goods and injured people across the cobbles.

Dead included a child of nine and four adults, and by the following morning the count stood at five, with more than two hundred injured, about forty of them seriously. Hospitals in Magdeburg and in neighbouring cities took casualties through the night, and the state government opened crisis centres for witnesses. Christmas market was closed and the city cancelled its New Year events. A week later a sixth person died of injuries, though the figure used by officials during December was five.

Suspect was identified as Taleb A., a fifty-year-old psychiatrist from Saudi Arabia who had lived in Germany since 2006 and had been granted refugee status. He worked in a clinic in Bernburg and ran an online advice service for people leaving Islam, and he had made public statements accusing the German authorities of failing asylum seekers from his home country. Police said he had rented the car shortly before the attack, and that he had made threats that were reported and investigated in earlier years.

Warnings were the part that the authorities had to explain. State prosecutors confirmed that the man had been the subject of a criminal complaint in 2013 and that there had been contacts with police in the autumn of 2024, and the interior minister of Saxony-Anhalt said the file had been assessed as not indicating a specific danger. Questions about how a man who had publicly threatened acts of violence could rent a car and drive it into a market followed within hours, and they were put to the federal interior minister in Berlin the next day.

Political reaction was immediate and shaped by the election calendar. Chancellor Olaf Scholz visited the market on the twenty-first of December and spoke of a dreadful act, and the far-right party Alternative for Germany held a rally in the city days later at which its leaders blamed immigration policy. Parties across the spectrum promised tougher surveillance of markets and more police presence, and the interior ministers of the states met in the first week of January to discuss vehicle barriers and intelligence sharing.

Attack came at the end of a year in which similar ones had already changed the political weather. Car attacks at markets in Europe, including one in Berlin in 2016 and one in Muenster in 2018, had each produced new security rules, and none had prevented a determined attacker with a rented vehicle. Debate that opened in Magdeburg therefore concerns something harder than barriers: whether the authorities can act on the warnings they receive without acting on the ethnicity of the people who generate them.
