Competition should end with one species, and nature is full of many. The explanation has been sought for a century, and the answer has usually been a mechanism giving each species an advantage when it is rare. In *Nature* on 29 June 2023, the mechanisms are separated. The authors distinguish those that stabilize coexistence, pushing a community back towards a mixed state, from those that equalize competitors, slowing one species' advantage without reversing it.

Consequence follows from that distinction. A stabilized community persists indefinitely, an equalized one persists only until a fluctuation tips it. Niche difference is the classical answer, since two species using different resources limit themselves more than they limit each other, and that asymmetry makes coexistence stable.

Empirical test is the design. The authors examine communities in which the mechanisms can be measured and ask which is doing the work in each case. Fluctuation supplies a subtle mechanism, because two species responding differently to a varying environment each have a period of faster growth, and averaging across periods can maintain both; the storage effect is the formal version, since a species persisting through a bad period and growing in a good one gains from variability, and the gain requires different responses.

Mixture is the result. Some systems are stabilized by niche differences, others held together by fluctuation-dependent processes in which a species' advantage reverses as conditions change. Framing is the contribution, since the field often treated coexistence as one problem and this separation gives each mechanism a testable prediction.

Data set the limitation, as measuring the mechanisms needs long time series and experimental manipulation, and few communities have both. Measurement is the field's bottleneck, because a stabilization mechanism leaves a signature in a time series, and extracting it needs a model right about noise as well as signal.

Prediction is what the paper does not do, since a classification is not a forecast, and whether a particular pair coexists remains a question for that pair's data. Ecology gains a cleaner question, because the old question was why species coexist and the new one is which mechanism keeps them coexisting, which experiments can answer.

Conservation reads it directly. A community held together by fluctuation can collapse when the environment changes, while one stabilized by niche differences is more robust. Invasion follows the same logic, because a stabilized community resists an invader and a merely equalized one may fall to a species with a small advantage; whether fluctuation mechanisms are rare in nature remains the unresolved dispute, and these cases are evidence either way. An experiment that removes the fluctuation would test the classification, because a community that collapses when the driver is damped was held together by variability rather than by niche. A pair of species with a small fitness difference would be the cleanest case, because the equalizing and stabilizing contributions can be separated by the speed at which exclusion proceeds when one is removed.
