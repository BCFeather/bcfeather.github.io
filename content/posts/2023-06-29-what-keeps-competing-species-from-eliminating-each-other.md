Competition should end with one species, and nature is full of many. The explanation has been sought for a century, and the answer has usually been a mechanism that gives each species an advantage when it is rare.

The analysis in *Nature*, 29 June 2023 separates mechanisms. The authors distinguish those that stabilize coexistence, meaning that they push a community back towards a mixed state, from those that equalize competitors, meaning that they slow one species' advantage without reversing it.

The distinction matters because the two have different consequences. A stabilized community persists indefinitely, and an equalized one persists only until a fluctuation tips it.

The empirical test is the paper's design. The authors examine communities in which the mechanisms can be measured, and ask which of them is doing the work in each case.

The result is a mixture. Some systems are stabilized by niche differences, and others are held together by fluctuation-dependent processes in which a species' advantage reverses as conditions change.

The fluctuation mechanism is subtle. If two species respond differently to a varying environment, then each has a period in which it grows faster, and the averaging over periods can maintain both.

The theory's framing is the contribution. The field has often treated coexistence as a single problem, and the paper's separation gives each mechanism a testable prediction.

The conservation reading follows. A community held together by fluctuation is one that a changed environment can collapse, and a community stabilized by niche differences is more robust.

The limitation is the data. Measuring the mechanisms requires long time series and experimental manipulation, and few communities have both.

What the paper does not do is predict. A classification of mechanisms is not a forecast, and whether a particular pair will coexist is still a question for that pair's data.

For ecology, the value is a cleaner question. The old question was why species coexist, and the paper's question is which mechanism keeps them coexisting, which is a question that experiments can answer.

The niche difference is the classical answer. Two species that use different resources limit themselves more than they limit each other, and the asymmetry is what makes coexistence stable.

The storage effect is the fluctuation's formal version. A species that can persist through a bad period and grow in a good one gains from variability, and the gain requires that the species respond differently.

The measurement problem is the field's bottleneck. A stabilization mechanism leaves a signature in a time series, and extracting it requires a model that is right about the noise as well as the signal.

The application to invasion is direct. A community that is stabilized resists an invader, and a community that is merely equalized may be invaded by a species with a small advantage.

The theoretical dispute is unresolved. Some ecologists argue that fluctuation mechanisms are rare in nature, and the paper's cases are evidence either way.
