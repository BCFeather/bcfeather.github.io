title: "Denervating the pulmonary artery eases heart-failure congestion"

description: "In a Chinese multicentre trial, ablating the sympathetic nerves around the pulmonary artery halved clinical worsening at two years in pulmonary hypertension caused by left heart disease."
---

Pulmonary hypertension that follows left heart disease has no approved therapy, and the reason is that the vessels are not the primary problem. Pressure rises because the left side of the heart cannot accept the blood, and drugs that dilate pulmonary arteries can worsen the congestion by increasing flow into a backed-up circuit. Sympathetic overactivity is part of how the circulation compensates, and it is also part of how the disease progresses. The population is large, elderly and poorly served, which is why a device with a modest effect attracts attention.

Pulmonary artery denervation targets that limb of the response by ablating the nerve fibres that run along the artery, using a catheter placed through the right heart. Investigators in China randomised 264 patients with pulmonary hypertension associated with left heart disease and heart failure, 134 to denervation plus guideline-directed medical therapy and 130 to medical therapy alone, and followed them for a median of 338 days. Ablation is delivered through a catheter in the pulmonary artery, and the procedural risk is that of a right heart catheterisation rather than of cardiac surgery.

The endpoint was clinical worsening, a composite of death, heart or lung transplantation, hospitalisation for heart failure, outpatient worsening and a decline in the six-minute walk distance. The Kaplan-Meier estimate of worsening at two years was 25.7 per cent in the denervation group and 51.5 per cent in the medical therapy group, a hazard ratio of 0.49 with an interval from 0.30 to 0.82 and *P*=0.006.

Procedure safety is the part that decides whether a device like this can be offered broadly. Access-site haematomas occurred in two patients in the denervation group and one in the medical group, and no other procedural complications were reported, which is a benign profile for a catheter intervention delivered into the pulmonary circulation. Two access-site haematomas across 264 patients is a rate that a trial this size can describe but not bound.

The composite is broad, and a halving of it could come from any of its components. Hospitalisation is the one that would justify the procedure economically, and the report does not provide the component breakdown at this stage. A decline in walk distance is a softer endpoint that can be influenced by how hard the patient is encouraged, so the effect needs decomposing before it is read as a survival benefit.

Whether the result replicates outside China is the question that regulation will ask. Pulmonary hypertension from left heart disease is a large population with few options, and a device-based treatment that reduces admissions would change practice if a second trial in a different health system confirms it. The trial's own authors frame the finding as a mechanism confirmed rather than a therapy established. Regulators will also ask about the sham, since an open-label device trial cannot exclude a placebo effect on a composite that includes symptoms.
