Washington's export control regime has been built in layers since October 2022, and the layer added in December 2024 is the one that reaches furthest into the supply chain. High-bandwidth memory, the stacked DRAM that sits beside an AI accelerator, was brought under control for the first time, together with a list of etching, deposition and lithography tools and the software that runs them. The stated purpose is to slow the production of advanced accelerators, and the mechanism is a licensing requirement that applies to foreign firms as well as American ones. The package also added more than a hundred Chinese entities to the list that requires a licence for any American supplier.

Extraterritorial reach is what makes the rules bite. The foreign direct product rule says that an item made abroad with American software or equipment is subject to American jurisdiction, and the December package extended that principle to a wider set of end uses. A memory maker in Asia that uses American deposition tools to build a chip destined for a Chinese data centre now needs a licence from a department in Washington, and the licence is presumed to be denied for the most advanced parts.

Beijing's answer came three days later, and it was aimed at materials rather than at machines. Exports of gallium, germanium and antimony to the United States were banned, with a stricter review for graphite. Gallium is used in compound semiconductors, germanium in infrared optics and in optical fibre, and antimony in flame retardants and in ammunition primers. China refines the majority of each, and the ban is a reminder that the choke points run in both directions.

Design is the next bottleneck. Restricting tools slows the expansion of capacity, and restricting memory slows the deployment of the systems that need it, but neither stops design work that happens on a laptop. The rules that touch design are the ones covering electronic design automation software, and those have been the hardest to write because the licences attach to a person rather than to a shipment.

Compliance has become a cost centre of its own. Distributors now ask customers for end-use declarations, foundries run audits on their own tool inventories, and law firms in Singapore and Taipei have built practices around the question of what counts as a controlled item. The paperwork changes what firms are willing to sell, and that is part of the intent. A foundry that cannot document where a tool came from risks losing access to American spare parts, and that risk is now priced into contracts.

Whether the policy works is an empirical question that will be answered by capacity data rather than by announcements. A country that cannot buy the most advanced memory can still build accelerators around slower memory, and it can buy the tools it needs from second-tier suppliers. The controls raise the cost and the delay of each step, and the cumulative effect of a delay at every step is the thing the rule was written to produce. The departments that write the rules have also learned that a narrow definition invites a redesign around it, which is why each revision has widened the scope.
