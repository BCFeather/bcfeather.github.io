title: "Trauma-focused therapy works in psychosis too"
date: "2026-06-17T09:20:00+08:00"

description: "The STAR trial, published in *The Lancet*, finds that trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy reduces PTSD symptoms in people with psychosis, a group usually excluded from such trials."
---

People with psychotic disorders experience post-traumatic stress at high rates, and the trauma is often caused by the illness itself: involuntary admission, restraint, and the fear that accompanies psychosis. Treatment guidelines have nonetheless excluded them from trauma-focused therapy, on the grounds that talking about the trauma might destabilize the psychosis. The exclusion rests on a clinical intuition rather than on trial evidence, and the intuition is that discussing trauma will inflame delusions or trigger relapse. Trauma-focused work asks a patient to revisit the memory deliberately, which is precisely what services have feared.

STAR tested that assumption. It was a multicentre, pragmatic, randomized trial of trauma-focused therapy integrated with cognitive behavioural therapy for psychosis, reported in *The Lancet* on 17 June, with 305 participants randomized and 267 attending follow-up. The intervention was integrated into usual care rather than delivered as a separate service, which is what a pragmatic design is for.

At nine months, 241 participants provided primary outcome data. CAPS-5 scores fell by an adjusted mean difference of 8.67 points against the control arm, with a confidence interval from minus 13.41 to minus 3.94 and a Cohen's d of 0.73, which is a large effect for a psychological intervention. A Cohen's d of 0.73 is large for psychotherapy, and it is large enough that the effect should be visible in functioning as well as in scores. The control arm received usual care, which in this population means case management and medication rather than a talking therapy.

Twenty-two of 27 secondary outcomes favoured the intervention, including delusions, paranoia, hallucinations in other modalities, suicidal ideation, depression and anxiety. The authors note that these were unadjusted for multiple testing, which is the correct caveat and does not erase the pattern. Suicidal ideation improved, which is the secondary outcome that matters most in a population with high mortality.

Effects on voices were not significant, which is the one domain where the therapy did not reach. That is an honest finding and it matters, because psychotic voices are the symptom patients most want treated and the one that trauma-focused work is least designed to address. The absence of an effect on voices is consistent with the theory, since trauma-focused work targets memories and appraisals rather than the auditory phenomenon itself.

Exclusion, it turns out, was a prejudice rather than a safety rule. A therapy that reduces PTSD symptoms without destabilizing psychosis removes a barrier that has kept a large population out of psychological care, and the next question is how to train enough therapists to deliver it within mental health services that are already stretched. Training is the bottleneck, and therapists who work in psychosis services need supervision that most talking-therapy programmes do not provide. Supervision also protects therapists from the secondary trauma that this work produces. The effect size also means that the therapy is worth commissioning rather than merely worth offering.
