description: "A review argues that the standard definition of bioactive glass, tied to bone bonding, is too narrow, and proposes instead that the material be defined by the biological activity it was designed to produce."

Bioactive glass is the material that bonds to bone, and the definition has been tied to that bond since the 1970s. Bioglass was the first example, and the mechanism was understood early: the glass dissolves, a calcium phosphate layer forms, and bone grows into it. The bond is the trademark, and generations of papers were written inside that frame. A material that bonds to bone does so by dissolving in a controlled way, and the dissolution is why the class was defined by its result rather than by its composition.

The frame has become too small. Glasses and glass-ceramics are now used for soft tissue repair, for drug delivery, for magnetic hyperthermia and for phototherapy, and none of those applications involves a bone bond. Work posted to arXiv on 1 December 2022 argues that a definition tied to bone excludes the clinic, and proposes a different one. Soft tissue does not offer a surface for a calcium phosphate layer to grow on, so the mechanism of the bone bond does not carry over, and a definition built on it cannot classify the newer materials.

The proposal is to define a bioactive glass as a non-equilibrium, non-crystalline material designed to induce a specific biological activity. A bioactive glass-ceramic is then an inorganic, non-metallic material with at least one crystalline phase in a glassy matrix, designed for the same purpose. The phrase designed to induce is the load-bearing part, since it moves the definition from a property to an intention. A definition by intention is what makes a class rather than a list, and a class is what a regulator or a textbook needs.

Intention is what a materials scientist controls. A glass is designed by choosing its composition, its processing and its surface, and the biological response follows from those choices. Tying the category to design lets the field compare a bone-bonding glass with an ion-releasing glass on the same terms, which the old definition could not do. The same glass can be designed for two functions, and the definition allows that, which is what a clinician who uses one material for two purposes requires.

Several functions are listed as admissible. A glass can deliver therapeutic ions at a specified concentration, heat a tumour under a magnetic field, or release a drug, and the authors argue that each of these is a biological activity of the same kind. The list is a definition by role rather than by composition, and that is its advantage. A menu is the honest form of a definition that has to cover a growing field.

What the definition does not do is set a threshold. A material that induces a weak response and one that induces a strong one both qualify, and a reader who wanted a number would not find one. The authors are explicit that the definition is a classification rather than a specification. A threshold is what a standard sets, and a review that proposed one would be legislating.

Classification is what a field needs before it can regulate itself, and the paper's contribution is that it makes the boundary visible enough to argue about. Standards are produced by exactly this kind of argument, and a review that names the terms is where such an argument starts.
