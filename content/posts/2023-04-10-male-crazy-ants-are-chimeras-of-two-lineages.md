An animal's cells normally share one genome, and a male that carries two is a chimera, usually an accident of development rather than a system on which a species depends. Yellow crazy ants have made the accident a way of life, and *Science* described the arrangement on 7 April 2023.

Male yellow crazy ants, *Anoplolepis gracilipes*, are chimeras in which one lineage contributes the germline and the other the body, and the arrangement is obligate rather than incidental. Two lineages, labelled R and W, circulate in the species: a male's somatic tissue matches one, his sperm the other, which makes him a composite of two parents rather than a recombinant of one. A queen mates, and the sons that result carry both genomes in different tissues, so the two coexist without fusing into a single nucleus in every cell.

Inheritance follows the split. A male passes on only the germline's genome, the somatic one being a dead end in evolutionary terms, and selection therefore acts on the two differently. Marmosets, which exchange cells in utero and become chimeras of siblings, supply the incidental version of the same word; the ant's arrangement is a feature of its life cycle, and the authors argue that selection maintains it.

How the split arises is unresolved. A chimera can form from the fusion of two embryos, from a parasite's tissue, or from a developmental programme that keeps lineages apart, and the data narrow the possibilities without choosing among them. Karyotyping adds a second line of evidence, because the lineages differ in chromosomal arrangement, and preparations of somatic and germline tissue show the difference directly instead of inferring it from markers.

Range and colony structure set the ecology. Yellow crazy ants are invasive on several continents, and a reproductive system this unusual may be part of why a small founding population can establish; a colony with many queens and dispersed nests has to be compatible with the arrangement, and the population data address that fit. What the study cannot measure is the fitness consequence, since a chimeric male may be more or less competitive and the field data that would settle it are absent.

Terminology matters for the claim, and so does the anatomy beneath it. A chimera differs from a hybrid, which is a single genome from two parents, and from a mosaic, which is one genome with mutations; the ant is chimeric in the strict sense. The evidence rests on preparations that show the two lineages in different tissues of the same male, and a claim about chimerism stands or falls on that anatomical separation. Evolutionary biology gets a case that stretches a definition, since an individual is usually one genome and an ant that is two argues that individuality comes in degrees.

What preserved material cannot show is behaviour, since a colony observed once yields anatomy rather than a lineage, and the sequencing of single males across generations is the experiment that would date the split.
