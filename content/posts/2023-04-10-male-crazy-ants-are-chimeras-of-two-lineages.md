An animal's cells normally share one genome. A male that is a mixture of two genomes is a chimera, and chimerism is usually a accident of development rather than a system that a species depends on.

The species described in *Science*, 7 April 2023 depends on it. Male yellow crazy ants, Anoplolepis gracilipes, are chimeras in which one lineage contributes the germline and the other contributes the body, and the arrangement is obligate rather than incidental.

The genetics are unusual. The species carries two distinct lineages, labelled R and W, and a male's somatic tissue matches one while his sperm matches the other. A male is therefore a composite of two parents' genomes rather than a recombinant of one.

The reproduction is the puzzle. A queen mates, and the male offspring that result carry both lineages in different tissues, which means the two genomes coexist without fusing into a single nucleus in every cell.

The consequence for inheritance is a split. A male passes on only the germline's genome, and the somatic genome is a dead end in evolutionary terms. A system that separates the two is a system in which selection acts on them differently.

The comparison with other chimeras is the context. Marmosets exchange cells in utero and become chimeras of siblings, and that chimerism is incidental. The ant's is a life-cycle feature, and the paper argues that it is maintained by selection.

The mechanism is unresolved. A chimera can arise from the fusion of two embryos, from a parasite's tissue, or from a developmental programme that keeps two lineages apart, and the paper's data narrow the possibilities without settling them.

The ecological setting is the species' range. Yellow crazy ants are an invasive species on several continents, and a reproductive system this unusual may be part of why a small founding population can establish.

What the paper does not establish is the fitness consequence. A chimeric male may be more or less competitive, and the field data that would measure it are not in the study.

For evolutionary biology, the value is a case that stretches a definition. An individual is usually one genome, and an ant that is two is an argument that individuality is a question of degree.

The cytology is the evidence's foundation. The authors' preparations show the two lineages in different tissues of the same male, and a claim about chimerism stands or falls on that anatomical separation.

Colony structure is the setting. A crazy ant colony has many queens and dispersed nests, and a reproductive system with two lineages has to be compatible with that structure. The paper's population data address that fit.

The terminology needs care. A chimera differs from a hybrid, which is a single genome from two parents, and from a mosaic, which is one genome with mutations. The ant's case is chimerism in the strict sense.
Karyotyping adds a second line of evidence. The two lineages differ in their chromosomal arrangement, and the authors' preparations of somatic and germline tissue show the difference directly rather than inferring it from markers.
