A jobs report that is merely ordinary is now the best news a market can get, and the May report was ordinary in the right way. Nonfarm payrolls rose 139,000, above the 125,000 that forecasters expected, and the unemployment rate held at 4.2 per cent for a third consecutive month, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics on 6 June.

Pay was the surprise. Average hourly earnings rose 0.4 per cent on the month and 3.9 per cent on the year, double April's monthly pace, which is the number that decides whether the Federal Reserve can treat the labour market as loose. A participation rate that fell two tenths to 62.4 per cent, the lowest since late 2022, makes the wage figure easier to explain.

Composition was narrow. Health care added 62,000 jobs, nearly half the total, leisure and hospitality added 48,000 and social assistance 16,000, while federal government employment kept falling as the layoffs of the spring worked through the payroll. An economy whose job growth comes from care is an economy with demand that does not depend on the cycle and does not fall when factories cut shifts.

Revisions took some of the shine off. April was marked down from 177,000 to 147,000, which means the three-month average is closer to 150,000 than to the 177,000 that had been celebrated a month earlier. A labour market that is slowing gently is the one the committee has been trying to engineer.

The market reaction was relief. The S and P 500 rose about 1 per cent and briefly touched 6,000, the Dow added more than 400 points, and the Nasdaq gained 1.3 per cent, which is what happens when a report removes a recession worry without removing a rate cut. A report that had been too strong would have done the opposite.

The Federal Reserve meets on 17 and 18 June and the report does not change its arithmetic. Inflation at 2.4 per cent is above target, unemployment at 4.2 per cent is not a crisis, and the committee has said that tariff effects may still arrive. Waiting costs a quarter of foregone demand and moving costs credibility, and the committee has spent two years buying the first with the second.

What would settle the argument is whether the next two reports keep the average near 150,000. A labour market that holds at that pace with wages near 4 per cent is a labour market that can absorb tariffs without a recession, and it is the single condition under which the year ends without a cut. The next report lands five days before the committee meets, which gives the argument one more piece of evidence and no more time. A labour market that keeps producing 150,000 jobs a month while participation falls is a labour market that is absorbing fewer people and paying more for them. Whether that is strength or exhaustion depends on the participation rate in June.
