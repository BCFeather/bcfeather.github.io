Centre Court had not seen a final like this since the last time someone young enough to be unknown arrived and refused to be impressed. Carlos Alcaraz lost the first set in twenty-seven minutes, was broken in the opening game, and looked for a while like a clay-court player who had wandered into the wrong tournament. He then won the second set on a tie-break, took the third in twenty-nine minutes, lost the fourth, and broke Djokovic in the fifth game of the decider before serving out the match at 6-4.

Novak Djokovic was chasing his twenty-fourth grand slam title, his eighth at Wimbledon, and a calendar-year sweep that had begun with the Australian Open and the French Open. He had won the tournament four times in a row and had not lost a completed final here since 2013. He was beaten by a player who hit harder from both wings, moved better than anyone on the surface, and treated the occasion as a match rather than a coronation.

The statistics describe a match of fine margins. Djokovic converted two of six break points; Alcaraz converted four of eleven. Alcaraz hit forty-five winners, including a forehand pass at 15-30 in the eighth game of the fifth set that will be replayed for years, and double-faulted at the worst possible moments. Djokovic, who had won every fifth set he had played at Wimbledon, lost the one that mattered, and the crowd, which had spent a decade cheering him out of habit, spent the afternoon discovering that it wanted the new man, loudly.

At twenty, Alcaraz now holds two major titles, the United States Open and Wimbledon, and two surfaces. The last man to win majors on both surfaces at that age was Bjorn Borg, and the comparison is being made in every newspaper that covers tennis. Alcaraz is the third Spaniard to lift the trophy here, and the first man born after 2000 to win a major on grass. His game is built on speed and invention rather than the attrition that has defined the last fifteen years of men's tennis, and he plays with an expression that suggests he is enjoying himself, which is a change in a sport that had grown used to solemnity.

For Djokovic, the loss raises the question that follows every champion into his late thirties: how many more chances will there be. He remains the best player of his generation and probably of any generation, and he remains capable of winning majors, as his three titles this year before Sunday had shown. The players who could beat him used to be one man his own age; now there are several, and the youngest of them is twenty.

The trophy was handed over by the Princess of Wales, the scoreboard was switched off, and the grass was swept. The ovation that followed the final point lasted longer than the ceremony that came after it. Alcaraz will be back for the American hard courts in August, and Djokovic will be back for a tournament he has won more often than anyone.
