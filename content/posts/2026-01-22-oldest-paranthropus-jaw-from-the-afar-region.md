The genus *Paranthropus* is the lineage of robust-jawed hominins with enormous molars and a face built for chewing, and it is the textbook example of a specialist that coexisted with early *Homo* for a million years. Its fossils cluster in East African rift sites and in southern African caves, and the genus appears in the record around 2.3 million years ago. A older and geographically displaced specimen changes the story of where the lineage began. The genus is familiar enough from skulls nicknamed after their owners that the jaw's characters are recognizable to any student, and the site is a gully in the Ethiopian lowlands that has produced little else.

Alemseged and colleagues describe a left mandible with two worn molar crowns recovered from Mille-Logya in the Afar region of Ethiopia, in sediments dated to about 2.6 million years ago. The specimen preserves enough of the corpus and of the tooth morphology to be assigned to *Paranthropus*, and the assignment was tested against the known range of variation in the genus. The report appeared in *Nature* on 22 January.

Age and morphology combine to make the case. At 2.6 million years the jaw predates the oldest accepted *Paranthropus* fossils by several hundred thousand years, and the teeth already show the characters that the genus is known for, which means the specialization was established earlier than the record had suggested. Fitting a robust jaw into a landscape that also contains early *Homo* raises the question of what the two were eating.

Geography is the more disruptive part. Mille-Logya lies in the Afar, well north of the sites where *Paranthropus* is usually found, and a specimen there at that date implies a lineage with a wider distribution and a longer history than the East African and southern African record suggests. Hominin biogeography of this period is reconstructed from a small number of sites, and each new region changes the map.

The dietary inference that usually accompanies the genus is about fallback foods. Large molars and thick enamel are read as adaptations to hard or abrasive items that become important when preferred foods fail, which is a strategy that can coexist with a generalist rather than compete with it. A jaw does not test that hypothesis, and the paper is careful to describe the specimen as morphological evidence.

Identification of a fragmentary mandible is always an inference. The authors compare the specimen with the available hypodigms and find it within range, and a mandible without a cranium cannot settle every question about affinity. Dating is the firmer contribution, since the sediments are well characterized and the age is consistent with the stratigraphy.

What the fossil does is shift the timeline and the map at once, which is the kind of contribution that a single good specimen can still make in a field where every fragment is contested. Its value will be tested by new excavations at the site, where a cranium or a limb bone would settle questions that a mandible cannot.
