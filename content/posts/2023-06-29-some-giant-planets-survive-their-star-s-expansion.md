A star's red giant phase expands its envelope, and a planet in a close orbit should be swallowed. The expectation is that such planets are destroyed, and the population of close-in giants around evolved stars should therefore be empty. In *Nature* on 29 June 2023, the expectation is contradicted. The authors report a giant planet in a close orbit around a star that has passed through its expansion, and the planet's survival requires an explanation.

Orbit supplies the explanation. A planet migrating outward during the expansion can stay ahead of the envelope, and a companion's gravitational influence is the mechanism driving that migration. Mass loss offers the other route, since a star shedding mass reduces its gravity and a planet's orbit expands as the pull weakens, and expansion can outpace the envelope with timing deciding which wins.

Envelope behaviour is the alternative. A star losing its envelope quickly, in a wind rather than a steady expansion, gives the planet less time to be engulfed, and the stellar modelling addresses which path the star took. Tides are the competing explanation, because a close planet raises tides in its star and dissipation can either inspiral it or push it out, with direction depending on rotation.

Detection is the evidence. A planet's presence is inferred from radial velocity or from a transit, and the inference around an evolved star is difficult because the star's own variability mimics a signal. Radial velocity is the workhorse here, since a planet's pull shifts the spectrum periodically and the amplitude gives its mass, while extracting that shift from a pulsating giant requires modelling the star's oscillations.

Asteroseismology supports the measurement. A star's interior structure is readable from its oscillations, and that structure tells the authors how far the envelope expanded. Population implication is the point, because one survivor implies others exist, and a surviving population changes the statistics of planetary systems around evolved stars.

Sample size sets the limitation, as one system is an existence proof rather than a rate, and the rate is what a formation model needs. Planet history stays unestablished, because a survivor may have formed further out and migrated inward later, and the two histories leave different signatures.

Planetary science gains a case where a prediction failed, since a theory saying such planets cannot exist has to accommodate one that does, and the accommodation is how the field improves. The solar system's fate offers a useful frame, because the Sun will expand in several billion years, and which planets survive is the same question answered here for another system. Rarity stays open, since a single detection cannot distinguish a common outcome from a freak, and a survey of evolved stars is how to find out. A population study of evolved stars would give the rate directly, since the fraction of survivors among close giants is the number a formation model needs. Whether the survivor still has a detectable atmosphere is the follow-up measurement, since an atmosphere stripped during the expansion would change the planet's radius.
