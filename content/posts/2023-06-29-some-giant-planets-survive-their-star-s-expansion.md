A star's red giant phase expands its envelope, and a planet in a close orbit should be swallowed. The expectation is that such planets are destroyed, and the population of close-in giants around evolved stars should therefore be empty.

The system in *Nature*, 29 June 2023 contradicts the expectation. The authors report a giant planet in a close orbit around a star that has passed through its expansion, and the planet's survival requires an explanation.

The explanation is the orbit. A planet that migrates outward during the star's expansion can stay ahead of the envelope, and a companion's gravitational influence is the mechanism that would drive the migration.

The alternative is the envelope. A star that loses its envelope quickly, in a wind rather than in a steady expansion, gives the planet less time to be engulfed, and the authors' stellar modelling addresses which path the star took.

The detection is the evidence. A planet's presence is inferred from the star's radial velocity or from a transit, and the inference around an evolved star is difficult because the star's own variability mimics a signal.

The population implication is the point. One survivor implies that others exist, and a surviving population changes the statistics of planetary systems around evolved stars.

The asteroseismology is the supporting measurement. A star's interior structure is readable from its oscillations, and the structure tells the authors how far the envelope expanded.

The limitation is the sample. One system is an existence proof and not a rate, and the rate is what a formation model needs.

What the paper does not establish is the planet's history. A planet that survived may have formed further out and migrated inward later, and the two histories leave different signatures.

For planetary science, the value is a case where a prediction failed. A theory that said such planets cannot exist has to accommodate one that does, and the accommodation is how the field improves.

The tidal interaction is the competing explanation. A planet in a close orbit raises tides in its star, and the dissipation can either inspiral the planet or push it out. The direction depends on the star's rotation, which the authors estimate.

The star's mass loss is the other route. A star that sheds mass reduces its gravity, and a planet's orbit expands as the star's pull weakens. The expansion can outpace the envelope, and the timing decides which wins.

The radial velocity method is the workhorse here. A planet's pull shifts the star's spectrum periodically, and the shift's amplitude gives the planet's mass. Around a pulsating giant, extracting that shift requires modelling the star's own oscillations.

The comparison with the solar system's fate is a useful frame. The Sun will expand in several billion years, and the question of which planets survive is the same question the paper answers for another system.

The rarity question is open. A single detection cannot distinguish a common outcome from a freak, and a survey of evolved stars is the way to find out.
