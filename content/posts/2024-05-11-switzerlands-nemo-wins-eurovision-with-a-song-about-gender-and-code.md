Nemo, a twenty-four-year-old from Switzerland, won the Eurovision song contest in Malmö on 11 May with a performance that mixed operatic singing and rap. The contest drew an audience of more than a hundred and fifty million across the participating broadcasters. The winner was the bookmakers' favourite from the semi-final onwards, and the margin in the public vote was large enough that the jury result could not have reversed it.

Voting is the mechanism. Half of the score comes from national juries and half from viewers, and the two halves rarely agree, which is why the winner is often the entry that is least disliked rather than the one that is most loved. A system that averages two tastes is a system that rewards the broad middle. The juries reward technical quality and the viewers reward novelty, and the winning entry was strong on both, which is the combination that the format has been designed to find.

Staging is the craft. The winning entry used a single performer, a strong light design and a language switch in the middle of the song, which the producers of the show have learned is what survives a three-minute slot. A song that is written for a television format is a song that is written for the camera rather than for the room. The three minutes are rehearsed for weeks, and the camera positions are fixed in advance, which means the performance that the audience sees is closer to a filmed scene than to a concert.

Politics of the venue is the background. The host country's broadcaster was criticized for its handling of the contest during a period of controversy, and several delegations raised objections on stage. A cultural event that is broadcast across a continent carries the arguments of that continent whether or not it wants to. The broadcaster that hosts pays for the production and for the security, and the controversies that year involved access and accreditation rather than the songs themselves.

Economics are the reason the contest exists. Hosting costs tens of millions, the tourism and the broadcast fees are worth more, and the smaller countries treat a good result as a route to a market they cannot reach otherwise. A festival that is watched by everybody is the cheapest advertising that a small country can buy. The winning country gets the hosting costs in the following year, which is an obligation that a small broadcaster can barely carry, and several have said they would rather not win.

What the evening shows is that a competition invented to unite a set of broadcasters after a war has become a machine for producing one career at a time, and the winner is the one whose three minutes survive the most votes. A contest that produces a career every year is a contest that the record industry uses as a shop window, and the winner's contract is usually signed before the confetti has settled.
