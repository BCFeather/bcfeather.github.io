Labor won the Australian federal election on 21 May, taking 77 of the 151 seats in the House of Representatives and ending nine years of Coalition government. The Liberal and National parties fell to 58 seats, the Greens won four, and ten independents were elected, several of them women running in formerly safe Liberal seats on climate and integrity. Scott Morrison conceded that night, and Anthony Albanese was sworn in as prime minister on 23 May, then flew to Tokyo for a summit of the Quad the following day.

Arithmetic made the result unusual. The Coalition won 35.7 per cent of first-preference votes and Labor 32.6 per cent, which meant the government was formed by the party that finished second on the primary count, carried over the line by Green and independent preferences. Labor's majority is exactly one seat, and the parliament it will face has the largest crossbench in the country's history, sixteen members outside the two major parties who owe nothing to either leadership.

Those independents are the election's other story. Monique Ryan unseated Josh Frydenberg, the deputy Liberal leader and the architect of the previous government's economic policy, in Kooyong. Zoe Daniel won Goldstein, Allegra Spender took Wentworth, Kylea Tink won North Sydney and Sophie Scamps took Mackellar, campaigns funded by the same climate-focused network and run against a government that had spent a decade arguing about emissions. The teal wave did not extend to the Senate, where the arithmetic stayed closer to the old pattern.

Morrison's defeat had personal as well as policy causes. He had taken a holiday to Hawaii during the bushfires of 2019, told a radio interviewer that he did not hold a hose, and spent the pandemic years centralising decisions in his office while the states ran the response. Voters in the safest Liberal seats, particularly women in suburban electorates, moved against him in numbers that his party's own polling had recorded and its strategists had discounted.

Albanese campaigned on a small list: cheaper childcare, a wage increase for aged-care workers, a 43 per cent cut in emissions by 2030, a national anti-corruption commission, and manufacturing subsidies. He had lost one election already, in 2019, when Labor offered a large tax reform programme and the country rejected it, and the lesson taken from that defeat was to promise less and to say it more slowly. The plan he carried into office was deliberately modest, and the crossbench he must now manage was elected on promises that go further.

A government with a one-seat majority and sixteen crossbenchers cannot govern by decree, and the country's immediate problems sat outside the campaign's agenda: inflation at five per cent, interest rates rising for the first time in a decade, and a health system short of staff. The election changed who sits in the cabinet room, and it also changed what the Senate and the crossbench can demand in exchange for their votes, which is the part of the result that will last longer than the swing.
