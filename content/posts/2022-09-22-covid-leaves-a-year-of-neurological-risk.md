---

A virus that causes a respiratory illness is not expected to leave a year of neurological diagnoses behind it, and the expectation is what a large cohort can test. The cohort that tested it came from the records of a single health system. The question is whether the expectation is right, and a cohort of a million people is what makes the answer precise.

The cohort held 154,068 people with COVID-19, 5,638,795 contemporary controls and 5,859,621 historical controls, and used inverse probability weighting to balance the groups. The paper appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 22 September 2022. A weighting scheme balances the groups on measured variables, and the variables it cannot measure are the ones that observational epidemiology always lacks.

A pooled hazard ratio for any neurological sequela at 12 months was 1.42, with a burden of 70.69 per 1,000 people, and the elevated risk covered ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke, cognition and memory disorders, peripheral nervous system disorders, episodic disorders such as migraine and seizures, extrapyramidal and movement disorders, mental health disorders, musculoskeletal disorders, sensory disorders, Guillain-Barre syndrome and encephalitis or encephalopathy. A list of a dozen categories is the finding as much as the hazard ratio is, because the breadth of the list is what says the effect is not one disease.

Risk was elevated even in people who did not require hospitalization during the acute illness, which is the finding that matters for a virus that most people experience mildly. A risk that survives the exclusion of severe cases is a risk that the whole population carries. The whole population is the denominator a health service plans for, and the planning is what the burden estimate enables.

A historical control group is the design that separates a pandemic effect from a secular trend, and the comparison with both contemporary and historical controls is the reason the estimates are credible. A contemporary control shares the pandemic and a historical control shares the baseline. A comparison with both contemporary and historical controls separates a pandemic effect from a secular trend, and that separation is the design's main strength.

A sample that was mostly White and male, which is a limitation the authors state, and the burden of neurological sequelae may differ in other populations. A cohort drawn from one health system describes the people that system serves. Whether the same pattern holds in other health systems is a question the design cannot answer, and a replication in a European or Asian database would be the natural next step.

No mechanism is identified, and the paper is an epidemiological description rather than an explanation. A description of a risk is what a health service needs to plan, and the explanation is what a laboratory will supply later, though the two are usually produced years apart. Whether the mechanism is vascular, immune or both is the question the next studies will take up.
