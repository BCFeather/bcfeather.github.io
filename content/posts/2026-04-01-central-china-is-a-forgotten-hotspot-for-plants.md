Biodiversity hotspots are defined by the number of species that live nowhere else, and the map of them has been drawn mostly from the tropics. Temperate Asia is not on that map in the way its flora suggests it should be, and the reason is partly historical: the region has been botanised for two centuries, so its species are well described, and being well described is different from being counted in a way that sets priorities.

A group publishing in *Nature Ecology & Evolution* on 1 April built a dated phylogeny covering 16,585 species, which is a family tree with estimated divergence times attached to its branches. Phylogenetic diversity measures how much evolutionary history a set of species represents, and it is a better currency for conservation than species counts, because a hundred recently diverged species represent less history than ten that split apart before the dinosaurs.

Applying that measure to China's flora, the analysis identifies central China as a concentration of endemic lineages that is not reflected in existing global hotspot maps. The region is mountainous, wet and unglaciated through the last ice age in parts, which is the combination that preserves lineages elsewhere and that has preserved them here while the surrounding lowlands were repeatedly reorganised by climate and by agriculture.

What the authors argue is that central China deserves designation as a global biodiversity hotspot, and the argument is about representation and not about charisma. A hotspot list that contains the Cape Floristic Region and the Atlantic Forest but not the mountains of Hubei and Sichuan is a list that under-represents temperate Asia, and a list is a budget document as well as a scientific one, because a designation is what makes a region legible to a funder who has never been there.

Conservation priority is also about threat, and the threat here is specific. The region is the heartland of Chinese hydropower, of tea cultivation and of a growing network of expressways, and each of those fragments habitat in a different pattern. A hotspot designation would not stop any of it, and it would change which projects are scrutinised and which surveys are required before they proceed.

Phylogenetic diversity has a limitation that its advocates acknowledge. The measure depends on the phylogeny, and a phylogeny built from a subset of genes and a subset of species is an approximation whose uncertainties propagate into the ranking. The paper reports the uncertainty, and a reader should treat the ranking as robust in its broad shape and provisional in its detail, which is the standard condition of any analysis that converts a tree into a priority list.

The practical consequence is a list of places where money would do more good per species saved. The paper names central China as the priority, and the mechanism it proposes is the one that conservation has used for thirty years: get the region onto the map, and the funding and the attention follow the map, which is a crude mechanism and the one that has worked.
