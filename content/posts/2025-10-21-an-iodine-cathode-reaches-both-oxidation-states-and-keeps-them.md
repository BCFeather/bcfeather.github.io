Aqueous zinc-iodine batteries are attractive because the electrolyte is water, the anode is zinc metal, and the cathode material is cheap. The catch is that a conventional cell uses only the two-electron couple between iodide and iodine, which caps the capacity at about 211 milliampere hours per gram. Reaching the iodonium state as well would nearly double that, and the obstacle has been that the higher oxidation state reacts with water while the intermediate polyiodides migrate across the separator. Both limits are chemical, and neither yields to a thicker separator.

*Advanced Materials* published a cathode design in October 2025 that attacks both problems with one additive. The authors ball-mill 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride with carbon nanotubes, so the ionic liquid attaches to the tube surface through pi stacking and stays put as a quasi-solid phase inside the electrode. The electrode then contains the chloride it needs without a corrosive liquid halide electrolyte. Ball milling matters because grinding exposes fresh tube surface and lets the ionic liquid coat it without a solvent step that would later have to be removed.

The capacity confirms the chemistry. The cathode delivers 418.9 milliampere hours per gram at 0.5C, close to the four-electron theoretical value, and holds 93.4 per cent of its capacity after more than 600 cycles at 1C. A pouch cell reaches an areal capacity of 7.1 milliampere hours per square centimetre and keeps 95.8 per cent over 150 cycles at 6.3 milliamperes per square centimetre, roughly 0.5C. The 418.9 figure sits within a few per cent of what the four-electron reaction allows, which is the paper's argument that the second couple is used rather than merely present.

The additive works in two stages. First the imidazolium cation binds the polyiodides, which keeps the soluble intermediates near the cathode instead of letting them reach the zinc. Then the chloride, held inside the electrode, speeds the conversion to the iodonium state and pairs with it as a complex that resists hydrolysis. One component therefore does the job that usually needs a membrane and a separate electrolyte. A membrane would add resistance and a liquid halide electrolyte would corrode the zinc, so folding both functions into the electrode removes two failure points at once.

What the paper does not report is a full cell energy density. Areal capacity and retention are measured in a pouch format, but the zinc anode, the separator and the electrolyte mass are outside the quoted figures, so a comparison with lithium cells is not available. Rate capability above 1C is also thin, and the highest current density demonstrated is the 6.3 milliamperes per square centimetre of the pouch test. Cycle counts stop at 600 at 1C and at 150 in the pouch, enough to show a trend and not enough to show a life.

Two engineering questions follow. Zinc deposition at practical areal capacity still limits cycle life, and the additive has to survive being coated at scale rather than mixed in a milligram batch. A cell that couples the new cathode to a textured anode would show whether the four-electron couple adds capacity at the device level, which is the number a manufacturer would need. Coating a slurry onto a current collector also changes how the ionic liquid distributes itself, which is a different experiment from ball milling a powder.
