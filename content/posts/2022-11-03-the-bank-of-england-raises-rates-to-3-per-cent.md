Seven members voted for the increase, and one wanted 50 points, and one wanted 100. The Monetary Policy Committee raised the bank rate by 75 basis points on 3 November, to 3 per cent. It was the largest single increase since 1989 and the eighth of the year. The rate had been 0.1 per cent in December 2021, and the rise was the fastest in three decades. The committee was divided. That was the widest split since the bank began publishing votes in 1997. The governor said the committee was united on the direction and divided on the pace. The rate had been 2.25 per cent before the meeting, and it had been 0.1 per cent in December 2021.

Gloomier than anything the bank had published, the forecast projected inflation peaking at 11 per cent in the fourth quarter and falling to 5 per cent by the end of 2023 and to 2 per cent in 2025. It projected eight consecutive quarters of contraction, which would be the longest recession since reliable records began in the 1920s. The member who wanted 100 points said the bank was behind the curve and that credibility was at stake.

Output would fall 2.9 per cent from peak to trough, and unemployment would rise to 6.4 per cent by 2025. The recession it described was mild. The bank said the level of output would not return to its pre-pandemic path, and it described the period as a lost decade for living standards. The bank's projection for growth in 2023 was a fall of 1.5 per cent, and for 2024 a fall of 0.5.

Part of the arithmetic was the energy guarantee. The government had capped household bills at 2,500 pounds for two years, and the bank said the cap would lower inflation in the short run and raise it later, because the support ends. It also said the cap would add to demand while the bank was trying to reduce it. The bank said real household incomes would fall 3 per cent in the year, the largest since the 1970s.

Mortgage markets had been disrupted by the budget of 23 September. The number of mortgage products available had fallen by 40 per cent, and the average two-year fix was 6.3 per cent. The bank said the effect on consumption would be larger than its model implied, because 1.8 million households were due to refinance. The bank began selling the bonds it had bought during the pandemic on 1 November. It planned to reduce its holdings by 80 billion pounds over the year, and it said the sales were not a tightening of policy beyond what the rate path implied. The first auction sold 750 million pounds of gilts.

Rates reached 4 per cent in December and 4.25 in February 2023, and the bank paused in September at 5.25. Inflation fell to 4 per cent by the end of 2023, faster than the bank had forecast, and the first cut came in August 2024. The recession the bank projected did not happen: output was flat in 2023. The bank said 1.8 million households would pay more, and the average increase would be 400 pounds a month.
