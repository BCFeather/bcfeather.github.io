About a tenth of human disease-causing variants are nonsense mutations: a single base change turns a coding codon into a stop, and the protein is cut short. Suppressor tRNAs offer a general answer, inserting an amino acid at that premature stop and letting translation finish. The difficulty has been delivery and dose. Supplying a suppressor from outside the genome requires repeated dosing, overexpression carries toxicity, and there is the risk that the same tRNA will also read through the stops that are supposed to be there.

David Liu's group at the Broad Institute reports a different installation method in *Nature*, 19 November 2025, which they call prime-editing-mediated readthrough, or PERT. Starting from all 418 human tRNA variants, the team iteratively optimized leader sequence, tRNA body and trailer sequence to identify the strongest suppressor backbones. They then used a prime editor to install one of these engineered tRNAs at an endogenous locus, without overexpression, and report 60 to 80 per cent editing in mammalian cells.

In principle, the same edited tRNA can read through any premature stop that shares its codon, which is the disease-agnostic part of the design. In human cell models of Batten disease, Tay-Sachs disease and cystic fibrosis, the authors observed efficient readthrough and rescue of the corresponding proteins. In a mouse model of Hurler syndrome, a single in vivo delivery of one prime editor converted the endogenous homologous tRNA into a suppressor and broadly rescued the disease pathology, including reductions in lysosomal storage and markers of tissue damage.

On safety, the central measurement is that PERT did not induce detectable readthrough of natural stop codons, and it produced no significant changes in the transcriptome or proteome. That distinction separates a suppressor tRNA from a general translational error: the engineered tRNA recognizes a premature stop while leaving ordinary termination apparently intact. A therapy that rescues one protein while mistranslating every transcript would not be usable. The absence of a detectable signal is not proof of zero off-target translation, but it is the relevant measurement to have made.

Overproduction is the other problem that earlier suppressor approaches carried, and the design avoids it. Because the edited tRNA sits at its native locus, it is expressed at roughly the cell's usual level rather than at a pharmacologically forced one. That matters for a chronic condition: a tRNA that works only when continuously supplied is a drug, while one installed in the genome is, in principle, a one-time intervention.

What the paper does not yet provide is clinical evidence. The experiments are in cell lines and in a mouse model, and 60 to 80 per cent editing is not 100 per cent. A therapy must function in the tissues that matter, at the doses that can be delivered, for as long as the condition lasts. Off-target editing by the prime editor itself is a separate concern that the paper does not remove, and the long-term behaviour of an altered endogenous tRNA has not been followed beyond the reported experiments.

A non-human primate study with a clinically relevant delivery route is the decisive test, measuring edited tRNA levels in several tissues over months. If those data show durable editing without evidence of mistranslation, PERT would become the first genome-editing strategy aimed at an entire class of mutations, and not at one gene.
