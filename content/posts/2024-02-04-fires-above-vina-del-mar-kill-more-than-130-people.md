---

Fires that began on the second of February in the hills above Viña del Mar and the neighbouring town of Quilpué had burned through more than fifteen thousand hectares by the following weekend, and the death toll passed a hundred and thirty. Forensic teams and the national disaster agency counted the dead street by street in the days afterwards, and hundreds of people remained unaccounted for. Fourteen thousand homes were damaged or destroyed, and thirty-nine thousand people were recorded as affected. President Gabriel Boric declared a state of emergency and two days of national mourning, and sent the army to help with the search.

Nothing on this scale had happened in Chile since the earthquake and tsunami of 2010, and nothing worse had happened in the country's long history of summer fires. The area that burned is not wilderness. Between Viña del Mar and Quilpué, the hills carry a mix of pine and eucalyptus plantations and dense residential streets that climb into the ravines, and fire moved through both with the speed that only wind can give it. Residents described flames arriving without warning, and in several places the only road out was blocked within minutes of the first smoke.

Weather made the conditions. A long drought had left the vegetation dry, and temperatures in the central region reached the high thirties for several days. A coastal wind that turns in the afternoon pushed the fire uphill in the morning and through the settlements later on, which is the pattern that makes these fires so lethal. Meteorologists at the World Meteorological Organization pointed to the drought, the heat and the wind as part of a pattern that has become more common in South America, and the environment ministry has been recording a steady increase in the area burned each summer.

Chilean institutions have struggled with the aftermath. Shelters opened the same night and payments were announced within days, which is fast by regional standards, and it still took a week for forensic teams to reach parts of the burned area. Promise of reconstruction is the part that will be tested over years rather than weeks. Boric said the state would rebuild the houses, and the mayor of Viña del Mar said many of the destroyed homes had been built without permits on land never meant for housing, which is a problem that predates the fire and will outlast it.

Cause is still being investigated and is contested. The prosecutor's office said it was examining whether the fires were started deliberately, and has charged one former firefighter in a separate case. Arson is a familiar explanation in Chile, where a small number of people have been convicted over the past decade for setting fires in the same region, and the public has heard it before. Survivors will remember the speed rather than the cause, a siren and a message on the phone that arrived, in some streets, after the fire had already crossed the road.

Recovery will take longer than the emergency. Rebuilding fourteen thousand houses at the pace the government has promised is a five-year task in a country where the construction industry is short of labour, and the land titles in the ravines are in many cases unclear. Insurance covers a minority of the destroyed homes, and the state has said it will pay for materials while owners rebuild. The municipality has begun clearing burned trees from the streets, which is dangerous work and will continue through the winter, and the first temporary settlements are expected to stay up until the end of the year. Aid agencies have warned that the displacement will be measured in years, not months.
