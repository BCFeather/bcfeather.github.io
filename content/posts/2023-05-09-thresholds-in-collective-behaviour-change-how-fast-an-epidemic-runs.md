title: "Thresholds in collective behaviour change how fast an epidemic runs"
date: "2023-05-09T09:35:00+08:00"

description: "Models that give people a threshold before they change their behaviour predict epidemic trajectories that differ sharply from models with a smooth response."
---

An epidemic's path depends on how people respond, and most models construct that response as a smooth function in which more cases produce more caution. A population does not behave that way, because caution arrives when a threshold is crossed and not before.

In *PNAS* on 9 May 2023, the threshold is made explicit. The authors treat behaviour as a collective decision in which individuals adopt precautions once a perceived prevalence passes their personal threshold, and they compare the resulting trajectories with those of smooth-response models.

Timing carries the difference. A threshold produces a sudden change in transmission, and a sudden change produces a peak that is later and lower than a smooth model's, with a longer tail afterwards. Heterogeneity supplies the mechanism, since a population holds a distribution of thresholds and the collective response is the sum of many individual switches, which is more abrupt than any single one.

Feedback is the loop that matters. Prevalence drives behaviour and behaviour drives prevalence, and a loop with a threshold can oscillate where a smooth loop damps. Asymmetry adds a second consequence, because people adopt precautions faster than they abandon them, and a model with hysteresis produces a decline slower than the rise it follows.

Communication is where policy reads the result. A message raising perceived prevalence crosses more thresholds, and the same message delivered earlier crosses them at a lower true prevalence. Vaccination extends the same logic, since willingness to be vaccinated is also threshold behaviour, and a model treating uptake as smooth overestimates coverage at low perceived risk while underestimating the difficulty of reaching the hesitant.

Structure interacts with the threshold. A well-mixed population crosses it everywhere at once while a clustered population crosses it in patches, and the comparison of mixing assumptions shows the same threshold producing different curves. Evidence remains a model rather than a measurement, because the thresholds are parameters and the claim concerns the consequences of a plausible structure, not the values of a particular population.

Data set the limit, since behavioural surveys record reported precautions and a reported precaution is not necessarily a transmission-relevant one, which leaves the parameters hard to estimate. Prediction for a real outbreak stays out of reach, because a trajectory depends on thresholds and thresholds depend on trust, experience and the epidemic's own history. The frontier is the measurement problem itself, because a threshold can be estimated from the shape of a curve only if that curve is observed finely, and surveillance data are usually too coarse to resolve a step. Public health modelling gains a structural warning, since a model assuming a smooth response misplaces a peak, and a misplaced peak is a misallocated hospital bed. Trust is the parameter no model sets from data, and a survey of how confidence in health authorities moved across the pandemic would give the distribution an empirical anchor. A threshold model also has more than one equilibrium, and a population that has crossed one may stay on the other side after the prevalence falls.
