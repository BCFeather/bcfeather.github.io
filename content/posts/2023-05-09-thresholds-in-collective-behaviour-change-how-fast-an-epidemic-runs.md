title: "Thresholds in collective behaviour change how fast an epidemic runs"
date: "2023-05-09T09:35:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "Models that give people a threshold before they change their behaviour predict epidemic trajectories that differ sharply from models with a smooth response, according to work published in *PNAS* on 9 May 2023."
---

An epidemic's path depends on how people respond, and most models construct that response as a smooth function: the more cases, the more caution. A population does not behave that way, because caution arrives when a threshold is crossed and not before.

The modelling in *PNAS*, 9 May 2023 makes the threshold explicit. The authors treat behaviour as a collective decision in which individuals adopt precautions once a perceived prevalence passes their personal threshold, and they compare the resulting epidemic trajectories with those of smooth-response models.

The difference is in the timing. A threshold produces a sudden change in transmission, and a sudden change produces a peak that is later and lower than a smooth model's, with a longer tail afterwards.

The heterogeneity is the mechanism. A population has a distribution of thresholds, so that the collective response is the sum of many individual switches, and the sum is more abrupt than any single switch.

The feedback is the loop that matters. Prevalence drives behaviour and behaviour drives prevalence, and a loop with a threshold can produce oscillations that a smooth loop damps.

The policy reading is about communication. A message that raises the perceived prevalence crosses more thresholds, and the same message delivered earlier crosses the same thresholds at a lower true prevalence.

The evidence is a model, not a measurement. The paper's thresholds are parameters, and the claim is about the consequences of a plausible structure rather than about a particular population's values.

The limitation is the data. Behavioural surveys measure reported precautions, and a reported precaution is not a transmission-relevant one, so the parameters are hard to estimate.

What the paper does not show is a prediction for a real outbreak. A trajectory depends on the thresholds, and the thresholds depend on trust, on experience and on the epidemic's own history.

For public health modelling, the value is a structural warning. A model that assumes a smooth response will misplace a peak, and a misplace peak is a misallocated hospital bed.

The asymmetry of the response is a second consequence. People adopt precautions faster than they abandon them, and a model with hysteresis produces a decline that is slower than the rise it follows.

The comparison across scales is the generalization. A threshold in a small community and a threshold in a country are the same structure with different parameters, and the paper's point is that the structure, not the parameter, changes the shape of the curve.
The network's structure interacts with the threshold. A population that is well mixed crosses a threshold everywhere at once, and a population that is clustered crosses it in patches. The paper's comparison of mixing assumptions shows that the same threshold produces different curves.
The vaccination case is the natural extension. A willingness to be vaccinated is also a threshold behaviour, and a model that treats uptake as smooth will overestimate coverage at low perceived risk and underestimate the difficulty of reaching the hesitant.
The measurement problem is the field's frontier. A threshold can be estimated from the shape of a curve only if the curve is observed finely, and most surveillance data are too coarse to resolve a step.
