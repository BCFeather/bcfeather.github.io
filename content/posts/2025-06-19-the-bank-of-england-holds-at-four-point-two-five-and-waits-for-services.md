A committee that cut in May and held in June is a committee that wanted more evidence, and the evidence it received was mixed. The Monetary Policy Committee left Bank Rate at 4.25 per cent on 19 June, having cut by a quarter point at its previous meeting, and its statement pointed to services inflation and to wage growth as the two numbers that had not yet settled.

Inflation release of the previous day gave both sides a sentence. Headline inflation fell to 3.4 per cent, which is progress, and core inflation rose to 3.5 per cent, which is not, and services inflation fell to 4.7 per cent, which is progress at a level that is still too high. A committee that reads the same release in three ways is a committee that is genuinely divided.

Growth picture argues for cutting. The economy had grown weakly since the spring, the payrolls data had shown hiring slowing, and the survey measures of activity had fallen back after the tariff announcements, all of which is the usual case for cheaper money. A committee that waits for inflation to fall to target before cutting will always be late.

Fiscal picture argues for waiting. The government's budget measures of April raised employer National Insurance contributions and the minimum wage, both of which raise costs in the sectors that set service prices, and the committee has said that the effect will be visible in the second half. An administered cost increase is not a demand shock and cannot be cut away with a rate.

Exchange rate is the constraint that nobody mentions. Sterling had strengthened against the dollar through the spring, which lowers import prices and does the committee's work for it, and a rate cut that weakened the currency would give some of that back. A small open economy with a large import content in consumption has an extra channel to consider.

Markets priced the next cut for August and a second before the end of the year, which is roughly the path the committee has described in its guidance. A path that is priced is a path that has to be delivered without surprises, and the committee has spent a year avoiding surprises.

What would settle the autumn is whether services inflation falls below 4.5 per cent in the July print. A print at that level would make August a decision about timing; one near 5 per cent would make it a decision about direction. A committee that holds while the headline falls and the core rises is a committee that has decided the direction matters more than the level. A hold in June is a bet that the second half brings both a slower services rate and a softer labour market, and both bets have to win. A committee that is divided six to three has to publish minutes that satisfy both sides, which is why the vote count is read more closely than the decision.
