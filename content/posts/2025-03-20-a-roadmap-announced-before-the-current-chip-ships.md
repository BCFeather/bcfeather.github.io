NVIDIA held its developer conference in San Jose from 17 to 21 March and used the keynote to lay out four generations of hardware on a single slide. The current platform, Blackwell, entered volume production in 2024 and is still constrained by packaging capacity. The next one, an upgraded Blackwell variant with more memory, is scheduled for the second half of 2025. Two further architectures, named for physicists, are mapped to 2026 and 2027. A customer who commits to a campus in 2026 is buying the platform that the slide promised, and the slide is the closest thing to a delivery date the industry offers.

A yearly cadence is unusual in a business that used to run on a two-year rhythm. The reason is that the buyers are building data centres on a schedule of their own, and a platform that arrives on time is a platform that can be designed into a building. A roadmap that slips by two quarters forces a customer to delay a campus, and that cost is larger than the price of the chip. A building takes two years, a power connection takes four, and the chip is the only component that can be swapped late.

Memory is the variable that decides the cadence. Each generation carries more high-bandwidth memory, and the memory stacks are supplied by three firms whose capacity is sold out for years. A platform that needs eight stacks per package instead of six consumes capacity that does not exist, and the roadmap is a negotiation with the memory makers as much as a design decision. A memory maker that allocates capacity to one customer for three years is a memory maker that has given up the spot market.

Packaging is the second constraint. The largest packages combine two reticle-sized dies with eight memory stacks on a silicon interposer, and the assembly yield falls as the package grows. A company that announces a bigger package is a company that has secured the capacity to assemble it, and the announcements describe a supply chain rather than a product. An interposer of that size is produced on a panel rather than on a wafer, and the panel capacity is concentrated in a few plants.

The software layer is where the lock-in lives. A library that has been optimized for one architecture for five years is a library that does not port to a rival in a quarter, and the conference is largely a developer event for that reason. The hardware roadmap is visible, and the moat is the collection of kernels that run on it. A developer conference is a sales channel, and the developers who attend are the ones who decide which framework a lab adopts.

What the cadence costs is the customer's ability to depreciate. A buyer who replaces a system every two years is a buyer whose capital cost per token is higher than one who waits four, and the industry has responded with rental models and with a secondary market for older machines. A roadmap that is announced before the current platform has shipped is a roadmap that tells the buyer to wait, and the seller has decided that the announcement is worth more than the wait. A buyer who waits is a buyer who is not buying, and the announcement is a bet that the wait is shorter than the alternative.
