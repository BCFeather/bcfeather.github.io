title: "Dripping alteplase into a reopened artery rarely helps"
date: "2022-03-02T09:30:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A phase 2b trial in Catalonia gave intra-arterial alteplase to 61 patients after successful thrombectomy and found no clear improvement in independence at 90 days, though the study stopped early."
---

Removing a clot from a large cerebral artery restores flow in the main vessel while leaving the small vessels downstream partly blocked. That residual obstruction is thought to explain why only about 27 per cent of patients who are successfully reperfused are free of disability at 90 days, and why some centres began injecting a clot-busting drug directly into the artery after the procedure. The reasoning assumes that the obstructed capillaries are the reason for the gap, which is plausible and has never been demonstrated in a trial that manipulated them directly.

A phase 2b trial tested the idea in seven stroke centres in Catalonia, randomizing 121 patients who had been treated with thrombectomy within 24 hours and whose angiographic reperfusion was graded 2b50 or 3 to intra-arterial alteplase at 0.225 milligrams per kilogram, up to 22.5 milligrams, or placebo. The paper appeared in *JAMA*, 1 March 2022. The primary outcome was the proportion reaching a modified Rankin score of 0 or 1 at 90 days.

It stopped early, for reasons that had nothing to do with the science. Placebo supply could not be maintained and enrolment collapsed during the pandemic, so 121 of a planned larger number were randomized, 61 to alteplase and 52 to placebo, and the analysis was underpowered for the endpoint it had chosen. A trial that stops early for operational reasons can still contribute a safety result, and the authors present it that way rather than as a test of efficacy.

What the trial does show is safety. Symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage and death were not increased by the intra-arterial drug, which was the concern that had kept the approach experimental. A drug that fails to show benefit and does not show harm leaves the question open rather than closing it.

The mechanism remains plausible. A clot that fragments during thrombectomy embeds in distal branches, and a local infusion reaches those branches at a concentration that a systemic dose cannot achieve without bleeding risk. Trials of adjunct therapy are built on that reasoning, and this one did not refute it. Distal emboli are common after thrombectomy, and their size is below the resolution of angiography, so a negative angiogram says nothing about the microcirculation that the infusion is meant to reach.

Design choices limited what could be learned. The trial was double-blind and placebo-controlled, which is unusual for an intra-arterial procedure, but the sample was small and the endpoint of independence at 90 days requires large numbers to detect a modest shift. A continuous measure of disability would have been more sensitive in a trial this size. The patients were treated within 24 hours and had good reperfusion grades, which is a narrow slice of the stroke population and the slice most likely to benefit from an adjunct.

The practical consequence is that intra-arterial alteplase remains a research question. Centres that adopted it routinely should reconsider, and the next trial will need a larger sample, a feasible placebo, and an endpoint that can detect the small benefit the mechanism predicts.
