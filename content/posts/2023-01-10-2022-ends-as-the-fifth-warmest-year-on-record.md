title: "2022 ends as the fifth warmest year on record"
date: "2023-01-10T09:20:00+08:00"

description: "European monitoring put the global average 0.3 degrees above the recent baseline, and the summer heat in Europe set a continental record."
---

European monitoring published its annual assessment on 10 January and ranked 2022 fifth warmest since records began in the nineteenth century. The global average was about 1.2 degrees Celsius above the pre-industrial level, and 0.3 degrees above the 1991 to 2020 reference period. Every year in the top ten has occurred since 2015. The service uses satellite data and ground stations, and its figures are published within a month of the year's end.

Europe had its hottest summer on record, and the figure was broken by a wide margin. Temperatures in Britain passed 40 degrees for the first time, and the sensor at Heathrow recorded 40.2. France, Spain and Italy all reported excess deaths during the July heat, and the total across the continent was estimated at 20,000. The excess deaths were concentrated among people over 65, and the estimate came from a study published in the summer. The heat wave was followed by a drought that cut the Rhine to a level that stopped barges for two weeks.

Oceans absorbed most of the extra heat. Ocean heat content reached a record for the seventh consecutive year, and the increase was largest in the North Atlantic and the western Pacific. Sea surface temperatures in the Mediterranean were 3 degrees above average in the summer, which contributed to the rainfall that followed in the autumn. The heat content figures are calculated from 3,000 buoys and are considered the most reliable ocean measure.

Sea ice told the same story in the north. Arctic sea ice reached its annual minimum in September at 4.7 million square kilometres, one of the lowest in the satellite record. The Antarctic was closer to average, though the February minimum was the lowest recorded, a detail that became more important a year later. The Antarctic minimum became the subject of a separate study after the sea ice failed to recover the following year. The Arctic figure was 1.2 million square kilometres below the 1980s average, and the trend has continued since.

Concentration of carbon dioxide reached 417 parts per million, up about 2.4 from the year before. Methane rose faster than in any year since measurements began, and the reasons are still being argued: wetlands, agriculture and leaks from gas infrastructure all contribute, and the monitoring services cannot yet separate them. The methane rise was partly attributed to tropical wetlands, though the authors said the attribution was uncertain.

This report makes no forecast. It records what happened, and its authors said the last eight years are the eight warmest in the record, which is a statement about a trend rather than about a single season. The World Meteorological Organization published a similar ranking two days later with the same top five. The organization said the eight warmest years had all occurred since 2015, and that the trend was not in doubt. National services in Britain and Japan ranked the year slightly higher, and the difference comes down to the reference period used.
