By the end of July the library belongs to the people preparing for something. The undergraduates who filled it in April have gone home. What remains is a population with a deadline, and the deadline shows in the way the seats are taken. A bag placed on a chair at half past eight. A bottle of water and a phone charger laid out like the markers of a claim. The claim is honoured until the owner returns, and there is an informal limit of about two hours before another reader will move the bag.

The reading room has its own etiquette, and it is one of the few social contracts in the city that everyone keeps without being asked. Silence is the first rule and it is enforced by looks rather than by staff. Eating is prohibited and takes place anyway, quietly, over a book. A phone that rings once is forgiven. A phone that rings twice is a public event. A person who saves a seat for four hours and does not return will find the seat taken and will not complain, because they know the rule.

Summer heat turns the room into a refuge as much as a workplace. The air conditioning runs all day, the doors open at seven, and by nine the lobby is full of people who have come in for the temperature and stayed for the desk. Some of them are reading, some are preparing for an examination in the autumn, some are between jobs and using the address as a routine. The library does not distinguish between them, which is part of its value.

Staff see all of this from an angle the readers do not. They know which seats are contested, which readers arrive at the same minute every day, and which books are requested in July because a syllabus requires them. They also know that the room will empty in the last week of August, in the space of about four days, when the term begins and the students return to a different building with a different chair.

One other function goes unmentioned in the brochures. It is the only indoor place in many districts where a person can spend an afternoon without being asked to leave, and for a reader who has nowhere else to go in the heat that is a service worth more than the books. There is a version of the library that the last week of July brings out more clearly than any other week. The institution is one of the only places in the city where a person can sit for eight hours without buying anything, and where the only currency is the claim on a chair. That is a small and genuinely egalitarian thing, and it survives because nobody has found a way to charge for it.

The doors close at nine. The last readers leave in a group, blinking, into air that is still thirty degrees. The bags are collected, the chairs are pushed in, and the room is reset for tomorrow, when the same people will arrive at the same time and make the same claim on the same seat.
