Generalized myasthenia gravis is a disease of the neuromuscular junction, and most patients carry antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor or against a muscle-specific kinase. The antibodies do not kill the receptor so much as remove it, and the result is fatigue that worsens through the day, double vision, and in the worst cases a swallow that fails. Steroids and acetylcholinesterase inhibitors control the symptoms, and newer drugs that block complement or the neonatal Fc receptor have changed the trajectory for some patients, at a price.

A different route targets the cell that makes the antibody. Inebilizumab binds CD19 and depletes B cells, including the plasmablasts that sit upstream of the antibody-secreting plasma cells. The phase 3 trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 8 April 2025 randomized 238 participants, 119 to the antibody and 119 to placebo, and followed them for 26 weeks. The primary measure was the change in the myasthenia gravis activities of daily living score, a patient-reported count of eight everyday functions.

The drug moved both scales. The activities of daily living score fell by 4.2 points on inebilizumab and by 2.2 on placebo, an adjusted difference of 1.9 points, with a 95 per cent confidence interval from 2.9 to 1.0 below zero and a *P* value under 0.001. The quantitative myasthenia score, which a clinician measures, fell by 4.8 points against 2.3, a difference of 2.5 points and an interval from 3.8 to 1.2. Both intervals clear zero, which is the least a trial of this size has to show.

Mechanistically the result is plausible and incomplete. Depleting CD19-positive cells removes the precursors that replenish antibody-secreting cells, and total IgG falls over weeks, which is why the trial waited 26 weeks before reading the primary endpoint. Plasma cells in the bone marrow do not express CD19 and survive depletion, so the antibody response that matters may only shrink as those cells turn over. That lag explains the shape of the curve as much as it explains the effect size.

Safety looked predictable for a B-cell drug. Headache, cough, nasopharyngitis, infusion-related reactions and urinary tract infections were the common events, and the pattern is what depletion of a B-cell compartment usually produces. The trial was not powered to detect rare infections, and 26 weeks is short for a drug that patients with a chronic disease might take for years. Whether the antibody can replace steroids, or let patients stop them, is a question the paper raises and does not answer.

The clinical question is placement. Complement inhibitors act within minutes to hours and are used in crises and in refractory disease; Fc receptor blockers lower IgG and are given weekly; inebilizumab is an intermittent infusion that changes the B-cell compartment over months. Each works on a different part of the same loop, and none has been compared head to head. A difference of 1.9 points on a patient-reported scale is modest, and it is the kind of modest that matters when the alternative is an escalating steroid dose.

What would settle the argument is a longer trial with a steroid-tapering arm and a relapse endpoint, and a comparison against the drugs already used in patients who fail first-line treatment. Until then the result adds a mechanism to a small menu and a number to a conversation about cost. A disease that will not settle needs options more than it needs a single best drug, and this is one more option with a defensible effect size.
