description: "Epigenetic plasticity in early progenitor-like cells, coupled to signals from the surrounding tissue, sets pancreatic cells on the path to cancer."
---

Carcinogenesis is usually told as a sequence of mutations. A cell acquires them one at a time, and the ones conferring advantage accumulate until the cell is malignant. The story explains many cancers and fits pancreatic cancer poorly.

In *Science* on 12 May 2023, a different sequence is offered. The authors trace pancreatic tumour evolution across model systems and find that epigenetic plasticity, rather than a single initiating mutation, primes progenitor-like epithelial cells for transformation.

Plasticity is a state. A cell whose chromatin is accessible at many regulatory regions can adopt several identities, and an injury inflaming the tissue pushes such a cell towards a ductal programme, so the state precedes the mutation. Chromatin is the mechanism, because accessible regions near cell-cell signalling genes are remodelled as the tissue progresses, and that remodelling is how a plastic state becomes a stable programme.

Microenvironment participates. The paper maps communication between epithelial cells and immune cells, and identifies an interleukin-33 loop that sustains inflammation and reinforces the plastic state. That loop is a therapeutic handle, since a cytokine linking epithelial stress to immune recruitment can be blocked by an antibody, and perturbing the loop tests its necessity.

Computational inference is the contribution. A model that infers communication modules from gene expression assigns a role to each cell type at each stage, and the trajectory from healthy tissue through inflammation and premalignancy to malignancy becomes visible. Spatial data strengthen it, because a tissue section preserves each cell's neighbourhood, and an analysis keeping that neighbourhood intact can attribute a signal to a cell type rather than to a bulk average.

Interception is the clinical implication. A patient at risk has an inflamed pancreas, and an intervention preventing the plastic state from stabilizing is a strategy a mutation-centred model does not suggest. Mutational burden offers the evolutionary comparison, since pancreatic cancer's burden is low and its lethality high, and a model in which plasticity does the early work explains why such a tumour can be so aggressive. Model systems set the limitation, because engineered mice and organoids approximate a human pancreas and the timing of the stages differs. Causality stays unestablished, since a state preceding cancer is not necessarily one causing it, and the perturbation experiments are what argue that it does.

Cancer biology gains a mechanism before the mutations, because a field looking for the first mutation may be looking at the wrong step. Plasticity appears in several epithelial cancers, and pancreatic cancer's speed makes it the clearest case, where a slower tumour would blur the stages this analysis resolves. A marker of the plastic state would be the clinical instrument this argument implies, since an inflamed duct whose cells have begun to remodel chromatin is a pancreas a surveillance programme could watch, and no such marker yet exists. Whether the same sequence holds for the commonest pancreatic cancer subtype is the question a human tissue atlas would answer.
