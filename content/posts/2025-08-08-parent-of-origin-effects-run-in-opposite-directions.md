A variant's effect can depend on which parent passed it on. Imprinting explains part of that: a small set of genes is expressed from only one of the two copies, according to marks laid down in the germ line. The parental-conflict hypothesis explains why imprinting would exist at all, arguing that a mother's copy and a father's copy can be selected to pull resource allocation to offspring in different directions. Testing the idea needs parental genotypes, which most biobanks do not hold, so the effects have stayed largely unexplored in complex traits.

A paper in *Nature* on 6 August 2025 works around the missing parents. Its authors infer the parent of origin without parental genomes, using interchromosomal phasing, mitochondrial sequence, the X chromosome and the pattern of crossovers in siblings. Applied to the UK Biobank, the method assigned a parent of origin for up to 109,385 individuals out of 236,781 available, which is a large fraction to recover from data that never recorded a mother or a father.

The scan covered 59 complex traits and more than 14,000 protein quantitative trait loci. It identified over 30 parent-of-origin effects, confirmed more than half of the associations already known, and found that more than a third of the new effects ran in opposite directions for the two parental copies. Growth traits carried several of them, with IGF1 and height among the named examples, and metabolic traits such as type 2 diabetes and triglyceride levels carried others.

Replication came from two independent cohorts. Up to 85,050 individuals from the Estonian Biobank and 42,346 offspring from the Norwegian Mother, Father and Child Cohort Study were available, and 87 per cent of the testable associations held in them. That rate is the part of the paper that separates a phasing artefact from a biological effect, since an error in inference would not reproduce in a cohort assembled differently, in another country, with another set of families.

The limits lie in what phasing can resolve. Inference degrades where a parent is homozygous across a wide region, and the method leans on siblings, so an only child contributes less than a family of four. Effect sizes for the opposite-direction variants are small, as they are for most complex-trait variants, and a small effect measured in two cohorts is still a small effect when it reaches a clinic. Whether the same inference holds in cohorts without siblings is an open question, and most cohorts are short of families.

What the result changes is the default reading of a genetic association. A variant that raises risk when inherited from the father and lowers it when inherited from the mother cannot be summarized by one odds ratio, and the parental-conflict hypothesis now has evidence in traits where it had been only a story told about imprinting. The next test is mechanism: which of these sites change expression in a parent-specific way, in which tissue, and at which stage of development.
