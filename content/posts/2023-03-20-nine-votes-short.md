title: "Nine votes short"

description: "The French National Assembly rejected two censure motions on 20 March 2023, the first of them by 278 votes against the 287 required, which left the pension reform adopted without a vote."
---

The assembly debated the two motions for three hours and voted in the evening, and the first one came within nine votes. The motion filed by the centrist LIOT group and supported by the left took 278 votes when 287 were needed for a majority of the chamber, and the arithmetic was reported to the member as it was read out. The second, filed by the National Rally, failed by a much larger margin, and the government was therefore in office and its bill was law. The motion was debated on a Monday and the result was known before midnight, and the members who had promised to support it were asked to explain themselves on television.

Nine votes were the product of a week of pressure applied in public. Several members of the governing party had said they would vote for the motion, and they had been summoned to the prime minister's office and offered arguments about the consequences for their constituencies. A handful of them abstained instead, and the abstentions were the difference, and the members who abstained said afterwards that they had been asked to choose between their voters and their government. Two members of the governing group had said they would vote against, and one of them did, and the other abstained after a conversation that was reported in the press.

Rejection of the motion did not end the dispute. The Constitutional Council would review the text within a month, and the unions called for a day of action, and the prime minister was asked to form a government with a majority that had just refused to support her. The opposition said the law had been imposed rather than adopted, and the government said the constitution provided for exactly this procedure and that it had been used by every government since 1958.

Streets were the part that the vote did not measure. Refinery blockades, uncollected rubbish and cancelled flights continued through the week, and the interior ministry counted the demonstrations in the hundreds of thousands. The violence at the margins of the protests became the story on some days, and the unions' ability to keep a movement going for months without a parliamentary majority was the year's most durable political fact.

On 14 April the council approved the essential parts of the text, including the rise in the retirement age, while striking down a few provisions about the contracts of senior staff. The decision closed the legal question and left the political one, which was whether a reform adopted this way could be implemented by a government that had lost the argument in every forum except the one that counted.

The lesson of the nine votes was about how thin a majority can be and still govern. A government with a relative majority in the chamber, no allies among the opposition and a president who had promised to consult, passed its most contested reform by five per cent of the votes it needed to lose. The margin was the mandate, and everyone in the country knew it.
