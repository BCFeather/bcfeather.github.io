Four years of provenance research ended this month with a crate. A second-century bronze figure, acquired by a Dutch museum in 1972 from a dealer who supplied no excavation record, was packed, insured and flown back to the country whose ministry of antiquities had requested it in 2022. The crate took a week to move. The paperwork took the rest of the time.

Provenance work is archival, not archaeological. The museum's curators began with the dealer's invoice, which named a private collection in Rome dissolved in 1971. From there they followed an auction catalogue, two customs declarations and a photograph in a 1968 exhibition catalogue, and found that the figure appeared in no publication before 1966. What the record did not show mattered as much as what it did.

The legal test differs between jurisdictions, which is why the outcome was negotiated rather than litigated. In the Netherlands, a good-faith acquisition before 1994 carries protection, and a claim must show that the object left its country of origin in breach of that country's law at the time. The requesting state's export law dated from 1939, but the object's departure was undocumented, so the case rested on inference from the absence of a permit.

Negotiation produced a middle path: no court judgment, a transfer of title by agreement, and a joint statement that both sides could sign. The museum keeps a cast and a long-term loan of two related pieces; the receiving museum gets the original and provides research access. Neither side describes the outcome as a victory.

Time is the variable worth watching. Restitution cases that run through courts take eight to twelve years on average and cost more in legal fees than the objects are worth at auction. Cases resolved by agreement average three to five years and depend on a fact that courts find hard to establish: whether the holding institution wants the object or wants the dispute to end.

For smaller museums, the constraint is capacity. Provenance research on one object consumed the equivalent of a curator's year, and the museum has 6,000 objects acquired before 1990 with incomplete records. Most of them are not from anywhere contested, but the audit cannot say which without doing the same work.

The agreement also deals with the possibility of further claims. Title passes to the receiving state, and the museum's insurers insisted on a clause indemnifying it against any third party asserting ownership within ten years. That clause is why the file is closed with a legal opinion rather than a press release: a museum that returns an object to one claimant can still be sued by another.

For the field, the more useful precedent is procedural. The four-year audit produced a document that other institutions can reuse, a template for how to reconstruct an acquisition history from dealer invoices, exhibition catalogues and customs records. Most museums hold thousands of objects with the same gaps, and the bottleneck is not willingness but the cost of the research.

A reader might ask why the return matters when the object's aesthetic value is unchanged. It matters because the record is the object's meaning. A bronze with a documented find spot is a datum for archaeology; without one it is a decorative survivor of an unknown context, and the museum that lost it has lost a source, not a trophy.
