DNA is read in the nucleus and RNA is made, and that work costs energy: nucleotide triphosphates are consumed at rates that rise sharply when a cell switches on a large programme of gene expression. Mitochondria are the power plants, and the question of how the two organelles are coupled has been answered mainly in terms of metabolites diffusing through the cytoplasm. The distance a metabolite has to travel is short either way, but the local concentration at the nuclear envelope can be much higher when the source is tethered.

A paper in *Nature* on 10 June describes a physical tether between mitochondria and the nucleus, with the argument that proximity gives the nucleus a dedicated supply rather than a share of a well-mixed pool. The structure had been observed in images before; what the paper adds is a functional account of what it is for. The tether is a protein bridge rather than a membrane fusion, which matters because fusion would mix the compartments while a bridge only brings them close.

One consequence is that transcription and metabolism become spatially coupled. A nucleus far from the nearest mitochondrion would be at a disadvantage during a burst of gene expression, and a cell that can move mitochondria to the nuclear envelope when it needs them has a control mechanism that diffusion alone does not provide. A nucleus with mitochondria at its envelope can also exchange metabolites directly, which matters for calcium signalling as well as for energy.

That kind of coupling has been reported in other systems as well: mitochondria accumulate around the nucleus during differentiation and during immune activation, and the arrangement reverses when the demand falls. A tether that can be regulated turns a static anatomical curiosity into a dynamic control point. A static structure would not explain the reversibility, and the regulators that assemble and disassemble the link are the handle the field will look for next.

This tether also suggests an explanation for why some nuclear processes fail in mitochondrial disease. If the supply of energy to the nucleus depends on a physical connection, then a defect in the outer membrane or in the proteins that form the link could impair transcription even when total cellular respiration looks adequate. Mutations in the proteins that form the link have not yet been connected to a human disease, and that is the sort of evidence that would make the mechanism clinically relevant.

The open question is whether the tether is a general feature of mammalian cells or a specialization of the ones studied. The next experiments are the ones that would test it: measuring transcription in cells where the tether is disrupted but mitochondrial mass and membrane potential are unchanged, since that is the condition in which proximity is the only variable. Disrupting the tether cleanly is the technical challenge, since removing a bridge protein may also change membrane potential and mitochondrial mass.
