Life is a chemical system that needs a dense fluid in which molecules can meet, so the question of which liquids can serve as solvents is a question about chemistry and not only about temperature and pressure. Water is the standard answer, and some authors argue it is the only answer. Astrobiology has kept the alternative open, since a solvent that is common elsewhere would widen the range of planets worth examining. Which liquid clears every hurdle is the question, and a single failure disqualifies a candidate.

Work posted to arXiv on 14 January 2024 turns that openness into a framework. The authors sort every candidate solvent through four criteria: how often the substance occurs, whether it can dissolve the relevant solutes, whether it keeps those solutes stable, and whether it offers the chemical functionality a metabolism would need. Sorting is useful because the alternative is an argument from analogy, and analogy has carried this field for a long time.

The four criteria are not independent. A liquid that dissolves a compound but destroys it is useless, and a liquid that preserves a compound but cannot dissolve it is equally useless, so a candidate has to pass both tests at once. Functionality is the third hurdle, and it is the one that most candidates fail. Passing three criteria and failing the fourth is the common case, which is why the list shortens quickly.

Protonation is the dividing line. Only solvents that can donate a proton meet all four chemical requirements, and of those only water and concentrated sulfuric acid are likely to be abundant in a rocky planetary context. That leaves the field with two plausible solvents. Sulfuric acid is the surprising entry, and it is surprising because a chemistry in it would have to be built from scratch.

Liquid carbon dioxide is the interesting exception. It fails the protonation test, yet it stands out among non-protonating solvents as a substance that planets plausibly supply in quantity, and the authors argue that its potential deserves serious study. Planetary abundance is what lifts a candidate out of the laboratory, and abundance on a rocky body is the only kind remote observation could confirm.

The paper closes on a harder question, which is whether a biochemistry could change its solvent as a planet's environment changes. A switch of that kind would require a metabolism to rebuild its membranes and its charge carriers, and the authors treat the question as unsettled. An organism that changed solvent would be a new kind of organism, and the paper says as much.

What the framework does not do is measure anything. The criteria are applied semiquantitatively, so a candidate can be ordered against another without being given a score. A reader should take the paper as a way to set priorities for laboratory work, and the paper stops short of a verdict on any single solvent. Scoring would suggest a precision the criteria do not have, and the authors avoid it on purpose.
