Seven cuts in thirteen months ended on 24 July with a decision to do nothing, and the European Central Bank chose the month in which the trade truce was holding and the euro was strong to stop and look around. The deposit rate stays at 2 per cent, the main refinancing rate at 2.15 per cent, and the marginal lending facility at 2.40 per cent.

Guidance changed shape. The council dropped the language about a direction of travel and kept the phrase that decisions would be taken meeting by meeting, which is the formula a central bank uses when it wants to be neither a dove nor a hawk in public and to keep both options in private. Lagarde told the press that the bank is in a good place, a phrase that reads as a pause rather than a pivot.

Inflation is at the target and the components are not. Headline inflation was 2.0 per cent in June, services inflation was above 3 per cent, and negotiated wages were still growing at a pace that implies domestic prices have further to fall before the council can call the job finished. A headline at target with services above it is the standard picture of the last mile, and the council has said as much at every meeting since the spring.

Growth is the case for cutting and the case for waiting at the same time. The euro area grew 0.1 per cent in the first quarter and slightly less in the second, Germany is in its third year of stagnation, and the commission's forecast has the bloc at about 0.9 per cent for the year, which is a rate that would normally justify insurance, and which is why the market had priced a cut before the meeting and had to take it back afterwards.

The exchange rate is the constraint that the council does not say aloud. The euro has risen against the dollar through the year, and a stronger currency tightens conditions by making imports cheaper and exports dearer, so a bank that has already cut seven times can wait and let the currency do part of the work, which is a form of tightening that no vote has to record.

Fiscal policy is the other reason to wait. Germany has passed a large infrastructure and defence package, the Netherlands and the Nordics are loosening, and a council that cuts into a fiscal expansion risks repeating 2022, when demand ran ahead of supply and energy did the rest of the damage, which is a memory that sits in every governing council discussion of the current package.

What would settle the autumn is whether services inflation falls below 3 per cent by the September meeting. A fall would make another cut in the autumn easy to justify, and a stall would leave the bank at 2 per cent into the winter, which is a rate that neither the doves nor the hawks can call a mistake.
