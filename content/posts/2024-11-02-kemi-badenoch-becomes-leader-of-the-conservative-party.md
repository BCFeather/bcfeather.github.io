Conservative Party members chose Kemi Badenoch as their leader on the second of November, beating Robert Jenrick in a ballot of the membership by 53,806 votes to 41,388. Result made her the first Black woman to lead a major British political party, and the sixth woman to lead the Conservatives. She took over a party with 121 members of parliament, down from 365 at the previous election, and a membership that had spent the summer arguing about who was to blame for the defeat in July.

Campaign she ran was built on an argument about the state of the party rather than about policy detail. Badenoch described the Conservatives as having behaved badly in office, spoke of a need to rebuild trust before offering programmes, and said the party had to stop treating the state as a machine for delivering announcements. Jenrick campaigned on a promise of immediate policy change, including a hard line on the European Convention on Human Rights, and on his record as immigration minister.

Contest itself took four months and went through several stages. MPs eliminated candidates in a series of ballots until two remained, and the final vote was left to members, most of whom are older than the electorate the party needs to win back. Turnout was about 60 per cent, and the result was closer than the headlines suggested, which left the new leader with a substantial minority inside her own party who had supported her opponent. Several former ministers announced within days that they would not serve in her shadow cabinet, which is a measure of how much of the parliamentary party she has to win over.

Background of the new leader is unusual for the party. She was born in London to Nigerian parents, grew up partly in Nigeria and the United States, and worked in banking and software before entering politics. She held ministerial posts under three prime ministers, including business secretary and equalities minister, and acquired a reputation for directness in interviews and for arguments about identity and bureaucracy that made her a favourite of the membership.

Task ahead is the hardest the party has faced in a generation. Opposition benches give a leader time, and the party's immediate problem is financial as well as political: donations fell after the election, and the central organization has been cutting staff. Badenoch has said she will spend the first two years rebuilding, which her critics inside the party read as a plan to wait rather than to fight, and she has refused to commit to a date for the next election.

Comparing with the other side of British politics is unavoidable. Labour took office in July with a large majority and has spent its first months announcing tax rises and planning reforms, which gives the Conservatives a target. Badenoch's first tests will be the local elections in May, where the party is defending hundreds of council seats, and the budget statement in the autumn. Her tenure will be judged on whether the party's membership and its electoral coalition can be rebuilt at the same time, which is the problem that has defeated every Conservative leader since 2019.
