---

Green fluorescent protein works because its chromophore is held rigidly inside a barrel of beta sheets, and the rigidity is what converts chemical energy into light rather than into heat. A nucleic acid that does the same job is a surprise.

The structure in *Nature*, 29 June 2023 explains the surprise. The authors determine how an aptamer called Lettuce binds a fluorophore and switches on its fluorescence, and the fold they find is not a duplex or a hairpin but a compact three-dimensional arrangement.

The function is protein-like. A fluorophore in solution loses energy by twisting, and a pocket that holds it flat suppresses the twist, and the suppression is what makes it emit.

The fold is the discovery. A nucleic acid's default structures are helices, and a helix cannot enclose a pocket. The authors' structure shows that a nucleic acid can build one by stacking, by looping and by using non-canonical pairs.

The comparison with GFP is instructive. A protein's barrel is built from a repeated secondary structure, and the aptamer's pocket is built from a fold that has no protein analogue. The two solutions converged on the same physics.

The generality is the claim. If a nucleic acid can form such a pocket, then the division of labour between nucleic acids and proteins is a convention rather than a constraint.

The application is imaging. A fluorescent aptamer is a tag that can be encoded in DNA and expressed in a cell, and a tag that is smaller than a protein is one that perturbs less.

The limitation is the ligand. A fluorophore is not a natural molecule, and the aptamer was selected for it rather than evolved for it.

What the paper does not show is a natural example. A cell does not make Lettuce, and whether biology uses nucleic acid pockets for catalysis is a question the structure raises rather than answers.

For structural biology, the value is a proof of principle. The field's assumption that nucleic acids are scaffolds and proteins are machines is a division that the aptamer's structure crosses.

The selection history is the aptamer's origin. Lettuce came from an in vitro selection, and the sequence that survived was the one that folded well enough to bind. The structure is therefore a product of selection rather than of design.

The fluorescence mechanism is the paper's physical content. A fluorophore's emission competes with a twisted intramolecular transfer, and a pocket that restricts the twist shifts the balance towards light.

The metal question is a detail with consequences. Many nucleic acid folds need a cation to neutralize phosphate repulsion, and the aptamer's compactness depends on the ions present.

The comparison with ribozymes is the deeper frame. A ribozyme is a nucleic acid that catalyses, and an aptamer is one that binds. A fold that does both would be a nucleic acid with a protein's full repertoire.

The imaging use is already established. A tag that is encoded rather than conjugated can be fused to a target gene, and a cell that expresses it labels its own protein.
