Green fluorescent protein works because its chromophore is held rigidly inside a barrel of beta sheets, and that rigidity converts chemical energy into light rather than heat. A nucleic acid doing the same job is a surprise. In *Nature* on 29 June 2023, the surprise is explained. The authors determine how an aptamer called Lettuce binds a fluorophore and switches on its fluorescence, and the fold they find is neither duplex nor hairpin but a compact three-dimensional arrangement.

Protein-like function is what the aptamer achieves. A fluorophore in solution loses energy by twisting, a pocket holding it flat suppresses that twist, and suppression is what makes it emit. The mechanism is the paper's physical content, since emission competes with a twisted intramolecular transfer, and a pocket restricting the twist shifts the balance towards light.

Fold is the discovery. A nucleic acid's default structures are helices, and a helix cannot enclose a pocket, so the structure shows one built by stacking, looping and non-canonical pairs. Metal ions are a detail with consequences, because many nucleic acid folds need a cation to neutralise phosphate repulsion, and the aptamer's compactness depends on which ions are present.

Comparison with GFP is instructive. A protein's barrel comes from repeated secondary structure while the aptamer's pocket comes from a fold with no protein analogue, and both solutions converged on the same physics. Selection history explains the origin, since Lettuce came from an in vitro selection and the surviving sequence was the one folding well enough to bind, which makes the structure a product of selection rather than design.

Generality is the claim. If a nucleic acid can form such a pocket, the division of labour between nucleic acids and proteins is a convention rather than a constraint. Ribozymes deepen the frame, because a ribozyme catalyses and an aptamer binds, and a fold doing both would be a nucleic acid with a protein's full repertoire.

Imaging is the application. A fluorescent aptamer is a tag encodable in DNA and expressible in a cell, and one smaller than a protein perturbs less. That use is already established, since a tag encoded rather than conjugated can be fused to a target gene, and a cell expressing it labels its own protein.

Ligand sets the limitation, as a fluorophore is not a natural molecule and the aptamer was selected for it rather than evolved with it. Natural example is what the paper does not show, because no cell makes Lettuce, and whether biology uses nucleic acid pockets for catalysis is a question the structure raises rather than answers. Structural biology gains a proof of principle, since the assumption that nucleic acids are scaffolds and proteins are machines is a division this structure crosses. A natural RNA that folds the same way would turn the concept into biology, and a search of structured RNA families is the cheapest way to look for one.
