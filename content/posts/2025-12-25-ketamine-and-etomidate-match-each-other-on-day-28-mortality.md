Rapid-sequence intubation is one of the most repeated procedures in critical care, and the sedative used for it has been settled by habit rather than by evidence. Etomidate held the habit because it blunts the fall in blood pressure that induction produces. Ketamine was preferred elsewhere because it supports circulation, and a reputation for opening the airway had grown up around it. Neither claim had been tested against death. The trials that existed were single-centre and powered for surrogates such as peri-intubation hypotension, which is a reading of a monitor, not an outcome a patient has.

Jonathan D. Casey and colleagues at 14 emergency department and intensive care units across six American medical centres randomized 2,365 critically ill adults to ketamine or etomidate before intubation. The trial was open-label, so patients and their clinicians knew which drug had been given. Their report appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 9 December 2025.

Measured as in-hospital death from any cause by day 28, the ketamine group lost 330 of 1,173 patients, 28.1 per cent, and the etomidate group lost 345 of 1,186, 29.1 per cent. Adjusted for trial site, the difference is 0.8 percentage points in ketamine's favour, with a 95 per cent confidence interval running from 4.5 points below to 2.9 points above, and a *P* of 0.65. A prespecified secondary endpoint moved the other way: cardiovascular collapse occurred in 22.1 per cent of ketamine patients against 17.0 per cent of etomidate patients, a difference of 5.1 points with an interval from 1.9 to 8.3.

The interval is the result worth holding onto. A trial of this size, in this population, still cannot separate a 4.5-point mortality benefit from a 2.9-point harm, which is what a null finding of this shape actually says. Design explains the rest. Enrolment at the bedside with a pragmatic protocol meant the patients were the ones who normally arrive at these units, and the absence of a placebo infusion kept the comparison honest about dosing practice, since each anaesthesiologist and emergency physician gave the drug they would ordinarily give. The cardiovascular difference is consistent with the mechanistic argument that favoured etomidate: ketamine raises sympathetic tone, and a collapse immediately around intubation is where that load is paid.

Worth naming is what the trial leaves open. Adults whose primary diagnosis was trauma were excluded, so nothing here reaches the largest group that receives these inductions. The comparison was against etomidate only, and conclusions do not transfer to propofol or to the other agents used in the same minutes. Because assignment was known to the teams, awareness of the group could have shaped co-interventions, including how much fluid and how much vasopressor was given, and the authors say so directly. Death in hospital by day 28 was the endpoint, and a longer horizon would count differently.

Two designs would close the question. A blinded induction trial using matched-volume infusions of both drugs would separate the pharmacology from the knowledge of who received what, which is the confounder this trial carries by construction. A second would recruit trauma patients explicitly instead of screening them out. Practice can move now in one narrow respect: neither drug has a mortality claim on it, so an argument about which is safer cannot rest on these endpoints, and the surrogate that motivated a decade of preference has not been shown to predict the outcome anyone cares about.
