The attack began before dawn on 7 October, when fighters from Hamas and other armed groups crossed the perimeter fence around Gaza at multiple points and moved into the communities beyond it. They attacked kibbutzim, an army base and an outdoor music festival, and the Israeli military took hours to establish control. By the end of the day officials counted about 1,200 dead, most of them civilians, and more than two hundred hostages taken back into the strip, which made it the deadliest single day in Israeli history. The communities that were overrun are small, and the fighting inside them went house to house.

The failure of intelligence has become its own story. Israeli agencies had built their assessment of Hamas around a belief that the group wanted economic relief and quiet, and the warnings that arrived in the weeks before the attack were treated as routine. The barrier that had been sold as a technological solution, with sensors and underground walls, was breached with bulldozers and explosives, and the response was slow enough that the first units to arrive were outnumbered.

Israel declared war that evening and began an air campaign against targets across Gaza. Ministers ordered a total siege of the strip, cutting off water, food, fuel and electricity, and told residents of the northern half to move south. The health ministry in Gaza, which is run by Hamas, reported several hundred deaths in the first days of strikes, and the figures have risen steadily since. Reservists were called up by the tens of thousands, and armour massed along the border while the air campaign ran.

Diplomatic consequences followed within hours. The United States began moving carrier groups to the eastern Mediterranean, and several European governments froze development aid to Palestinian programmes pending review. Saudi Arabia suspended talks on normalization with Israel, which had been presented weeks earlier as the centrepiece of a regional realignment, and the prospect of a corridor linking India to Europe through the Gulf was quietly dropped from the agenda.

Hezbollah opened a second front along the Lebanese border with rocket and anti-tank fire, and exchanges of fire there have continued at a low but steady level. Iran denied involvement in the planning while praising the attack, and the question of how much it knew has become a point of dispute among intelligence services. The risk that the conflict widens has shaped every subsequent decision. Iran has said it will not be drawn into a direct war, and the militias it backs have fired at American positions in Iraq and Syria.

Politics inside Israel changed within a week. The government that had spent the year pushing a judicial overhaul through the Knesset formed an emergency cabinet with the opposition, and the argument about the courts was set aside. Public anger has focused on the failure that allowed the attack, and the question of who will answer for it is deferred rather than answered. Nothing in the emergency cabinet settles who will answer for the intelligence failure, and no commission of inquiry has been appointed.
