The search for organisms that eat plastic has produced a long list of candidates and a short list of useful ones. Most of the work has focused on PET, the polyester used in bottles and clothing, because it has an ester bond that enzymes can attack. A paper published this month describes a bacterium isolated from soil that degrades a polyurethane foam, a polymer whose backbone is a mix of urethane linkages and ether segments, and whose recycling has defeated every mechanical and chemical process tried at scale.

The bacterium was found by enrichment: soil samples were incubated with the polymer as the only carbon source, and the organisms that grew were isolated and tested. The strain that emerged produces an enzyme that cleaves the urethane bond, and the paper reports that a foam sample lost roughly twelve per cent of its mass over eight weeks in a bioreactor. That is slow by any industrial standard, and it is fast enough to be interesting.

The chemistry explains the difficulty. Polyurethane is a block copolymer, and the hard segments that give it strength are held together by hydrogen bonding that also blocks enzymes from reaching the bonds. The soft segments are more accessible, and the bacterium appears to attack those first, which is why the foam becomes brittle before it loses much mass. A material that embrittles is easier to break down mechanically afterwards, which may be the useful application rather than the biodegradation itself.

The paper is careful about its claims. The strain was characterized at the species level, the enzyme was partially purified, and the products were identified by mass spectrometry. What was not shown is degradation in a real environment, in soil or seawater, at a temperature and concentration that would occur outside a bioreactor. The authors say the next step is a pilot at a waste site, and they are explicit that a laboratory result at thirty-seven degrees is not a landfill.

The context is a recycling system that has failed for this material. Polyurethane foam is used in mattresses, insulation and car seats, and almost all of it is landfilled or incinerated, because the thermoset bonds that make it durable also make it impossible to melt and re-form. Chemical recycling routes exist, and they are expensive and energy-intensive. A biological route would be cheaper if it were faster, and the rate is currently measured in weeks rather than hours.

Scale is the other obstacle. A bioreactor that degrades twelve per cent of its input in eight weeks would have to be enormous to handle a waste stream measured in thousands of tonnes a year, and it would have to run at a dilution rate that keeps the enzyme in contact with fresh polymer. The authors estimate that a pre-treatment step, shredding the foam and heating it, raises the rate several-fold, which is the usual pattern in this field.

The broader pattern in this field is that every few years a paper reports an organism that eats a plastic, and the reporting outruns the science. The useful questions are always the same: what is the rate, what are the products, and does it work in the environment where the waste actually is. This paper answers the first two and defers the third, which is honest and not yet a solution.
