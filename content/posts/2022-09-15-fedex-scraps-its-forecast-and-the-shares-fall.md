description: "The company withdrew its full-year guidance on 15 September, and the stock lost 21 per cent the next day."
---

FedEx reported results for the quarter to August on 15 September and withdrew the guidance it had given in June. Revenue was 23.2 billion dollars, above the forecast, and earnings per share were 3.44, below it. The company blamed weak volumes in Europe and Asia, and it said the first quarter had been the hardest of the year.

Investors read the withdrawal as a statement about the economy rather than about the company, because FedEx carries freight for every part of it. The share price fell 21 per cent on 16 September, the largest one-day fall in the company's history. Its chief executive said in an interview that the numbers did not reflect a recession but that he expected one.

Volumes had fallen in every region. Express packages in Asia fell 11 per cent, and in Europe 8, and the company said the declines had begun in the summer. Ground volumes in the United States were flat, which was the only part of the business that held up. The company had raised prices 7 per cent in January and again in June, and it said the price rises were holding.

Costs were the other problem. The company had spent 1.5 billion dollars on automation and on a network redesign, and the benefits had not arrived as quickly as planned. It announced cuts of 170 million dollars in the quarter, including the closure of 90 offices and a freeze on hiring. The chief executive said the company would cut more if volumes did not recover.

Both companies had benefited from the surge in online shopping during the pandemic, and both were now carrying capacity that had been built for a market that had shrunk. The rival parcel service reported results a month later and confirmed the picture. It cut its own guidance and said volumes had fallen for four consecutive quarters. Analysts noted that the withdrawal was unusual because the company had given the guidance only three months earlier and had reaffirmed it in June. The finance chief said the speed of the change had surprised the company as well. Several banks cut their price targets the same day, and the stock ended the year down 33 per cent.

FedEx had also been under pressure from an activist investor who had bought a stake in the spring and wanted the company to separate its express and ground businesses. The board rejected the proposal in July, and the investor said in October that it would keep pressing. The announcement of a new chief financial officer in November was read as part of that argument. The company's results for the following quarter were better, and volumes stabilized in the spring. It restored guidance in March and met it at the end of the year. The episode was remembered as an early indicator, because the freight recession that began that autumn reached the rest of the economy six months later.
