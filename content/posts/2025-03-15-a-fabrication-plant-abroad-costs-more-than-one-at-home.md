TSMC told investors on 3 March that it would add a hundred billion dollars to its American investment, taking the total to a hundred and sixty-five billion. The programme includes three more fabrication plants, two advanced packaging facilities and a research centre, all in Arizona. The first plant there has been producing at four nanometres since late 2024, several years behind the original schedule and well above the original budget. A hundred and sixty-five billion dollars is more than the company has invested in any other geography, and it is being committed in a country where it has no home market.

Cost gap is the central fact. Building and operating a fab in Arizona costs materially more than in Taiwan, by a margin that executives have put at somewhere between thirty and fifty per cent, and the gap comes from construction, from utilities and from the supply chain. A wafer that passes through Arizona pays for those costs, and a customer that can buy the same wafer from Taiwan has to be persuaded to pay them. A customer that designs a chip for an American product line can claim a domestic supply chain, and that claim is worth something to a government buyer.

Construction is the largest line. A fab is built by crews that have done it before, and the American crews are learning a trade that Taiwan has practiced for thirty years. The cleanroom that would take eighteen months in Hsinchu takes three years in Phoenix, and the schedule slip is paid for twice, once in capital and once in revenue forgone. A construction workforce of several thousand people has to be assembled in a metro area that is already short of electricians and of pipefitters.

Materials and chemicals are the second line. A fab consumes ultra-pure chemicals, gases and photoresist, and those supply chains are concentrated in Asia. A plant that imports them pays freight and inventory, and a plant that builds local suppliers waits for the suppliers to reach the required purity. A chemical plant that supplies a fab needs its own permit and its own customers, and one fab is not enough demand to justify the investment.

The subsidies cover part of the gap and not all of it. The American award to TSMC under the chips programme was six and a half billion dollars, against a capital plan that is now twenty-five times that. A grant of that size offsets the first plant and leaves the rest to be justified by strategic value rather than by return. A grant that covers a tenth of the plan is a signal that the government wants the capacity, and a signal is not a subsidy that makes the arithmetic work.

What the expansion buys is insurance. A company with capacity in three geographies can serve customers in a world where one of them is disrupted, and the insurance premium is the cost gap. Whether the premium is worth paying is a question for shareholders and for governments, and both have answered yes. A company that can build in Arizona, in Japan and in Germany is a company that can keep selling in a world where the rules change by country.
