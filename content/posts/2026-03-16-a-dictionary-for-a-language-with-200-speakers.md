A language with 200 speakers is past the point at which formal grammar can be elicited from children, and its documentation has to be done by recording adults. A dictionary of 9,000 entries published this month documents such a language, and the method is as interesting as the content.

The fieldwork ran eleven years with three linguists and six community members who were trained as recorders. Entries came from recordings of conversation, storytelling and cooking rather than from questionnaires, which is where the example sentences come from.

Structure reflects how the material arrived. Each entry has a transcription in a practical orthography, a recording identifier that resolves to an audio file, a gloss, and at least one example sentence with its own translation. The audio link is the difference from a printed dictionary: a learner can hear the word.

Orthography was a political decision. The community had two competing conventions, one from a missionary tradition and one devised by a local teacher, and the dictionary adopts the second and cross-references the first. That choice was made by the community's language committee, not by the linguists.

Grammar is presented as patterns rather than rules. The introduction has 20 pages on verb morphology and no formal paradigms, because the speakers vary and the variation is described rather than resolved. The linguists describe this as a record of a language rather than a standard.

Distribution is a print run of 600 and a digital edition with the audio. The community's schools will use it in a two-hour weekly class, which is the only formal teaching the language has.

What the dictionary does not do is revive the language. It documents it, and documentation is a precondition for any revival attempt. The committee's stated goal is that children should hear the words, which is a smaller ambition than fluency and a more realistic one.

The corpus is the durable asset. The dictionary's 9,000 entries rest on 240 hours of recorded speech, and that corpus is archived with the community's consent and a licence that allows academic use and forbids commercial redistribution.

Orthography politics is not settled. A second committee has formed around the missionary convention, and the dictionary's introduction acknowledges the disagreement without resolving it, which is the only honest position a reference work can take.

The teaching material is the dictionary's practical output. Two primary schools have adopted a reader built from the first 400 entries, and the committee reports that children who use it read the orthography faster than those who learn the missionary spelling.

The archive's format is a decision about the future. Recordings are stored as uncompressed audio with a transcription alignment file, and the committee chose that over a database because a future reader with no software can still listen.

For a reader, the case shows what documenting a small language now looks like. The recordings are the primary object and the text is an index to them, which reverses the order of a traditional dictionary and makes the work robust to transcription error.
