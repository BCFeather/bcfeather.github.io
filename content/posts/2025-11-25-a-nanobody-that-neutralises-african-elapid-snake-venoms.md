description: "A single engineered nanobody neutralised the venoms of 17 of 18 African elapid snakes in mice, a result that points to a universal antivenom made in microbes rather than in horses."
---

Antivenom is made the way it has been made for more than a century: venom is injected into a horse or sheep, the animal's serum is collected and the antibodies are purified. The product is effective against the species that donated the venom, expensive to produce and impossible to reformulate once a regional mix of snakes is known. Sub-Saharan Africa carries a disproportionate share of the world's snakebite deaths, and the antivenoms available there are usually imported and tuned to snakes elsewhere, so a bite from a local species can fall outside the product's coverage entirely.

A small antibody fragment, a nanobody, can be selected from a synthetic library screened directly against venom components rather than harvested from an immunised animal. The team engineered one that binds a conserved site on the three-finger toxins and phospholipases making up most elapid venom, then tested it against the venoms of 18 African elapid species; 17 of the 18 were neutralised in mice, including the black mamba, several spitting cobras and the boomslang, whose toxins differ enough that conventional antivenoms often miss them.

The number to carry away is 17 out of 18. A conventional antivenom is a mixture of polyclonal antibodies and works by binding the exact proteins in the immunising venom; change the species and the coverage falls. A single fragment with a conserved target offers the reverse trade: breadth at the cost of potency against any one species, since one binding site must do the work of a mixture. The experiments used venom challenged with nanobody at measured ratios, so the result is a neutralisation curve rather than a claim of equal efficacy across all 17.

Manufacturing is the second advantage. A nanobody is expressed in bacteria or yeast and requires no animal, no cold chain beyond the usual and no batch-to-batch variation from an immunised herd. It can be sequenced, stored as a digital record and reproduced exactly, which matters in a market where production has repeatedly been abandoned because the demand is too small to justify the facility.

What remains open is whether breadth survives contact with whole venom. Venom is a mixture of dozens of proteins, and a conserved site on the major toxin families may still leave minor components unbound. The 18th species, whose venom escaped neutralisation, is the warning that a universal product is not yet in hand, and the result does not establish how the fragment performs against vipers, whose venoms are built from a different set of proteins.

The study also stops short of human dosing. Mice are not humans in their response to envenomation, and the time window between bite and treatment is wider in the experiments than it often is in the field. Tissue damage at the bite site, which antivenom addresses poorly in any case, is not captured by survival as an endpoint.

A antivenom that is cheap to make, stable without refrigeration and effective across species would change who gets treated rather than how they are treated. The 17-out-of-18 result is the first demonstration that a single fragment can cover a continent's elapids; the remaining species is the reason it is still a demonstration and not a product.
