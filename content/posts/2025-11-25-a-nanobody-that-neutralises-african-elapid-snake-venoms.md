Antivenom has been made the same way for more than a century: venom goes into a horse or a sheep, serum is collected, antibodies are purified. The product covers the species whose venom was used, costs a great deal to produce and cannot be reformulated quickly when the regional mix of snakes changes. Sub-Saharan Africa carries a heavy share of the world's snakebite deaths, and the antivenoms sold there are often imported and matched to snakes from elsewhere, which leaves a local bite outside the label.

A recombinant alternative starts from a smaller unit. Nanobodies, the single-domain binding fragments of camelid antibodies, can be selected from phage libraries and expressed in bacteria, so the whole product can be designed rather than harvested. In work reported in *Nature* on 29 October, a team at the Technical University of Denmark immunised an alpaca and a llama with the venoms of 18 snakes, 13 cobras, four mambas and a rinkhals, and screened the resulting library against the toxins that do the damage.

The product is a mixture of eight nanobodies, chosen for binding to conserved sites on the major toxin families and combined into an experimental antivenom that protected mice against lethal doses of 17 of the 18 venoms when the two were mixed before injection. The exception was the eastern green mamba, Dendroaspis angusticeps, whose venom escaped the mixture entirely. Each fragment is roughly a quarter the size of a conventional antibody, which is what makes a mixture of eight practical to formulate and to express in bacteria.

Breadth is the claim, and the comparison behind it is against the product on the shelf. In pre-incubation tests the cocktail did better than Inoserp PAN-AFRICA across the panel, with the black mamba the one species where the commercial antivenom held its ground. A stricter test, venom injected first and treatment five minutes later, protected mice against eight of 11 venoms, which is the model that resembles a real bite rather than a laboratory convenience.

There is a second benefit that matters clinically. The cocktail reduced dermonecrosis, the tissue destruction around a bite that conventional antivenom barely touches and that leaves many survivors with permanent damage. Survival is the endpoint most antivenom studies report, so a tissue result is unusual in this field and is the part of the paper that clinicians will read first. Because the fragments can be produced in Escherichia coli and sequenced, the product is reproducible batch to batch in a way that an immunised herd is not.

What the work has not shown is durability in the field. Protection in mice is not protection in a person, the timing of treatment in an accident will rarely match a five-minute window, and the eastern green mamba is a reminder that continent-wide coverage still has a hole in it. The paper also stops at preclinical evidence, so the questions that decide whether this reaches a clinic are manufacturing scale, stability in heat and the cost of a course. Whether the missing species reflects the mixture or the immunising venom is a question the authors leave open.
