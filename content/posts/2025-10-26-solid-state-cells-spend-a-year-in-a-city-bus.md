title: "Solid-state cells spend a year in a city bus"

description: "A transit operator has run a 12-metre bus on solid-state cells for a year, and the data are a rare field test of a chemistry whose selling point is safety rather than energy density."
---

A solid-state cell replaces the liquid electrolyte that carries lithium ions with a solid, and the argument for it is that a solid cannot leak or ignite. A transit operator ran a 12-metre bus on such cells for a year, and the report is one of the few accounts of the chemistry outside a laboratory.

The pack holds 180 kilowatt-hours and the bus covers 210 kilometres on a charge, against 260 for the lithium iron phosphate bus it replaced. The energy density penalty is real and the route was planned around it. Temperature is where the chemistry differs. The solid electrolyte's conductivity falls with cold, and the pack's available power at minus 10 degrees is 60 per cent of its rating. The operator's winter schedule added 15 minutes to the recharge stops.

Safety testing was done on the pack rather than on cells. A nail penetration produced a local temperature rise of 40 degrees and no fire, and an overcharge to 150 per cent of the nominal voltage vented gas without ignition. Those are the results the operator needed to park the bus in a depot with 40 others. Scheduling is the operator's other test: a pack that has lost 8 per cent of its capacity still covers a route, and one that has lost 15 does not.

Cost is the barrier. The pack cost 2.4 times a lithium pack of the same energy, and the operator's analysis puts the breakeven at 9 years on fuel and maintenance savings alone, which is beyond the bus's service life of 12 years only if the pack lasts as long.

Maintenance data are the unexpected argument. The pack's capacity loss after a year of daily cycling was 4 per cent, against 6 for the lithium fleet, and the operator attributes the difference to the absence of a liquid electrolyte's side reactions. A slower fade is worth more than a higher energy density for a fleet.

Procurement is where the two buses separate. A hydrogen bus costs 60 per cent more and needs a fuelling station, and a solid-state bus needs a charger the depot already has. For a city with an existing depot, the second is the cheaper path. A bus is a demanding test because its duty cycle is predictable and its duty is hard. A city route runs 14 hours a day with 200 stops, and a stop's regeneration is a charge pulse that a cell must absorb at a high rate. Cell-level data is what the trial produced. The operator logged each module's capacity every month and its internal resistance every week, and the degradation curve is what a fleet manager needs to decide when to replace a pack rather than a bus.

Field performance in a battery is a set of trades rather than a score. Cold weather, energy density and cost all moved against the solid-state bus, and durability moved in its favor, and the operator's decision to keep it is a judgment about which of those a fleet can absorb.
