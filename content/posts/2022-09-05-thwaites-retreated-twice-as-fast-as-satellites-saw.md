A glacier that is losing mass is a glacier whose grounding line is moving inland, and the position of that line is measured by satellite for the last four decades. The seafloor underneath records the positions before the satellites existed, and the record is written in ridges of sediment. Thwaites is the glacier where the question matters most, because its bed is below sea level and slopes inland.

An autonomous underwater vehicle at the ice front of Thwaites Glacier and mapped the ocean-floor imprint of past retreat from a seabed promontory, using geophysical data to identify back-stepping sedimentary ridges. The paper appeared in *Nature Geoscience*, 5 September 2022. A vehicle under an ice shelf reaches water a ship cannot and resolves features a satellite cannot see, and the platform is why the data exist.

Ridges formed daily by tidal lifting and settling at the grounding line, at a time when the glacier was more advanced than it is now, and the spacing of the ridges gives a rate. A daily archive is the kind of time resolution that a sediment core cannot provide. Tidal lifting and settling leave a ridge every cycle, and the cycles are the clock.

Over a duration of 5.5 months the grounding zone retreated at more than 2.1 kilometres per year, which is twice the fastest rate the satellites observed between 2011 and 2019. A historical rate that exceeds the modern one is the result that matters for a projection. Models are calibrated on the satellite era, and the satellite era turns out to be a slow interval.

Sustained pulses of rapid retreat have occurred at Thwaites in the past two centuries, and that similar pulses are likely when the grounding zone migrates back off the high points that stabilize it. A retreat that stops at a bump and restarts when the bump is passed is the behaviour that the bathymetry predicts. The bumps are the pinning points, and pinning points are what make a retreat episodic.

A submarine vehicle can reach water that a ship cannot and can resolve features that a satellite cannot see, and the platform is the reason the data exist. An instrument that works under an ice shelf is the technical achievement behind the result. A rate is only as good as the two numbers that produce it, and the absolute age comes from evidence outside the tidal rhythm. Uncertainty lies in the dating, because the tidal rhythm gives a duration and the absolute age comes from other evidence, and the paper's inference depends on both. A model calibrated on the satellite era may be calibrated on a slow interval, and the interval is what Thwaites is now leaving.

The projection that matters is sea level, and Thwaites holds enough ice to raise it substantially, so a rate that doubles the modern maximum is a number that a model has to accommodate. A number that doubles the modern maximum is one a projection has to absorb, and the absorption is what ice-sheet models are for.
