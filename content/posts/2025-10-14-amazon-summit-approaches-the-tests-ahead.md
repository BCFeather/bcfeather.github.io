Mid-October put the year's climate summit into its last preparatory phase, with the host city's arrangements finished, the negotiating texts assembled and the finance chapter still open. Preparations of that kind are where a summit's outcome is substantially fixed before any minister speaks, so the useful questions concern what the draft commits its parties to and what it leaves to be settled in the room.

The draft text is a procedural instrument as much as a substantive one. Built from bracketed alternatives, it records where governments have converged and where a chair has chosen to keep competing wordings side by side, so a bracketed chapter that survives into the ministerial week signals an absent decision rather than a dispute about language. Reading the brackets is therefore a more reliable forecast than reading the speeches.

Finance is where the arithmetic does the talking. Pledges announced in earlier cycles have been disbursed at rates below what was promised, the largest historical contributor has left the framework, and the distance between the stated need and the money actually available is the number the negotiations inherit, whatever the draft says about mechanisms. A gap of that kind does not get closed by drafting technique; it gets closed by a decision that a government has to take at home first.

Consensus rules shape what any of this can produce. Every delegation holds a veto, and the presidency holds the agenda, which is why a host uses both location and calendar to concentrate attention on a single deliverable. Coalitions of the most exposed states work the same instruments in the other direction, keeping a session open long enough that a bracketed paragraph cannot quietly disappear.

Domestic constraints travel into the room with each delegation. A government whose legislature has not appropriated the money it announced negotiates a different text from one whose funds are already committed, and that asymmetry explains why the finance chapter tends to be settled last and cited first. Delegates with a mandate narrower than their brief can agree to a process and cannot agree to a figure.

Three questions will be settled in the Amazon basin over the weeks the summit schedules. Whether the finance chapter closes with a number or with a process is the first; whether the assembled texts survive contact with ministers is the second; whether a framework operating without its largest contributor still issues decisions that smaller parties treat as binding is the third. None of the three turns on rhetoric, and each turns on procedure laid down months before the opening.

What the week will show, in other words, is the weight of choices already made in preparatory sessions, when brackets were placed and agenda items were ordered. A summit opens with its texts drafted and its finance unresolved, and the shape of the answer is visible in the punctuation of a text that was written before anyone arrived.
