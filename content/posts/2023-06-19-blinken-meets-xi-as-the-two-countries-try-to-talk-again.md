Antony Blinken met Xi Jinping at the Great Hall of the People in Beijing on 19 June, the closing act of a two-day visit that was the first by an American secretary of state in five years. The meeting lasted about half an hour beyond its scheduled time, and both sides described it as a chance to stabilize a relationship that had been drifting towards open confrontation since a suspected Chinese balloon was shot down over the Atlantic in February. The visit came after months of waiting for the two governments to agree on the terms of a sit-down.

The concrete outcomes were modest and were mostly about process. The two countries agreed to resume talks on a working group for economic issues, to reopen channels over military contacts that had been suspended, and to increase flights between them, which the airlines had been seeking for a year. No joint statement was issued, and the phrase that both sides used afterwards was that the meetings were candid and constructive, which is the language of governments that have agreed to disagree and want to keep the channel open.

Washington came with a list of concerns that it said would be raised in a way that had not been the case before: the detention of American citizens, the fentanyl precursors that Chinese companies sell, the treatment of American businesses in China, and China's support for Russia's war machine. The Chinese side came with its own list: the sanctions and export controls that cut Chinese firms off from advanced chips, the military's activities near Taiwan and in the South China Sea, and the restrictions on academic and journalistic exchanges that Washington has tightened since 2018.

Behind the visit sits a strategic question: whether the two governments can manage a competition that both expect to continue. The United States has described the relationship as one of intense competition with guardrails, and China describes the United States as a declining power unwilling to accept that it must share the Pacific. Neither formulation is a basis for trust, and both are adequate for a truce. The visit was intended to keep the truce working at the level of officials rather than leaders, and the next test will be whether the defence and trade channels now reopen.

For China, the visit served an additional purpose that Chinese officials did not hide: it was used to demonstrate that foreign businesses are welcome and that the economy's door remains open. The meetings were scheduled alongside a forum for international investment and followed a series of statements about the private sector, and the timing reflected a government that is worried about capital leaving and about growth that has stalled. Beijing wanted a photograph of the two countries talking, and the visit provided one.

Whether the truce holds depends on something neither side controls. The two economies are entwined in ways that no policy can quickly unwind, and the chips, the fentanyl precursors, the airline slots and the detained citizens are the ordinary matter of a complicated relationship. The leaders who met in Beijing have agreed that officials should keep meeting, which is a real improvement on the months before. The harder question, whether the competition can be kept inside the guardrails, will be answered by the next incident rather than by this meeting.
