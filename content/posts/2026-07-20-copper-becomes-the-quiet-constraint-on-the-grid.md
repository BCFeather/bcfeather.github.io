Grid connection queues have become the binding constraint on new load, and the conversation usually stops at generation. The longer part of the wait is elsewhere: a high-voltage transformer takes years to build and is ordered against a backlog, and the cable that carries power from a substation to a campus is a manufactured object with a lead time of its own. A data centre can be finished in eighteen months and then sit idle waiting for a wire. A queue is a promise about the future, and the promise is only as good as the steel and copper that have been ordered against it.

Manufacturing capacity is thin. Transformer production consolidated for decades, and the skills that make a large power transformer are not the skills that make a chip. Orders placed in 2024 are being delivered in 2027, and the prices reflect that scarcity rather than the cost of copper and steel. A utility that forecasts demand five years ahead is now competing with buyers who can pay a premium to jump the queue.

Copper supply adds a second constraint. Electrification of transport, heating and industry all draw on the same metal, and a new mine takes between ten and twenty years to permit and develop, which is longer than the energy transition's own timetable. Recycling covers part of the gap, and substitution covers another part, but neither scales at the rate the connection queue implies. Substitution is proceeding slowly, because aluminium is lighter and cheaper but needs more volume for the same conductance and a different connector.

Adaptation is already architectural. Operators are moving loads to where the wires already exist, which is why old industrial sites with heavy connections have become valuable, and why some campuses are being built beside hydroelectric dams rather than beside fibre. Behind-the-meter generation, fuel cells and batteries can bridge a delay, but they change the economics of a facility that was planned around cheap bulk power.

Regulatory lag is a third layer. A connection study, an environmental review and a local planning process each take time, and they run in sequence rather than in parallel. Some jurisdictions have begun to batch studies and to allow provisional connections, which is a real improvement, and others have responded by raising the deposit required to hold a place in the queue, which transfers the cost to the developer without shortening the wait. Batching studies helps only if the network data behind them is current, and in many jurisdictions it is not.

Investment follows the bottleneck, and the bottleneck is a business with a moat. Transformer manufacturers, cable makers and switchgear suppliers have pricing power that they have not had in thirty years, and the utilities that own the interconnection process have become gatekeepers of the industry's growth. Until capacity catches up, the constraint will be measured in copper and steel rather than in megawatts.
