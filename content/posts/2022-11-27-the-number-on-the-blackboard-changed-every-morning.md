The board at the front of the classroom had two panels. The left one carried the day's lessons, written in the small careful hand of the duty monitor. The right one carried a number, and that number was changed every morning by whoever arrived first. It had started in September, when the number was still above two hundred and fifty, and by the end of November it had fallen below two hundred.

Nobody had told us to keep it. A boy in the second row had started it with yellow chalk, and when the chalk ran out somebody bought a new box with money collected at the back of the room. Teachers ignored the panel, except for one who looked at it in the middle of a lesson and said that counting the days would make them pass more slowly.

The school day in winter began before the sky was properly light. The first bell rang at ten past seven, and the walk to the gate took fifteen minutes along a road where the streetlights were still on. The shop that sold breakfast opened at six, and the steam from its steamer made a white column that could be seen from the corner. Most of the class arrived with cold hands and something wrapped in foil.

Lessons in the afternoon were harder to follow. The radiators made a knocking sound, the windows had condensation on the inside, and the second period after lunch was the one in which the teacher's voice became a kind of weather that you could fall asleep inside. The chemistry teacher had a habit of drawing diagrams with his back to the room, and the diagrams were the only part of the lesson that anybody copied.

What I remember most clearly from that month is the last twenty minutes of the day, when the sun had gone and the classroom was lit by a strip light that hummed. The teacher would stop the lesson and hand back the previous week's papers, and the sound of the room changed as people read their marks. Somebody would ask a question about a mark, and the answer would be given in a lower voice than the lesson had been taught in.

Then the bell, the corridor, the stairs, the bicycle shed, and the road home with the wind coming off the lake. My mother would have the light on in the kitchen and something warm on the table, and the evening would be a table, a lamp, a book, and a clock that was too loud. The countdown on the right panel was meant to make the year shorter. In practice it made each day longer, because a number that falls is a number you check. Somebody rubbed it out one afternoon by accident with a wet sleeve, and the next morning it was rewritten, a little crooked, one lower; by then the number was low enough that people stopped reading it aloud, and the last weeks passed in a rhythm of bells and papers.
