title: "Psychedelic analogs separate antidepressant action from hallucination"
date: "2022-01-29T13:15:00+08:00"

description: "Structures of the serotonin 2A receptor bound to psilocin, LSD and serotonin revealed a second binding mode that guided analogs with antidepressant-like effects in mice and no hallucination."
---

Psychedelic drugs have returned to psychiatry on the strength of small trials in depression and addiction, and their clinical use is constrained by the experience they produce. A treatment that requires a supervised session, a quiet room and hours of altered perception is difficult to deliver at scale, and the hallucination is widely assumed to be part of the therapeutic mechanism.

Chemistry suggests the two can be separated. The serotonin 2A receptor is the target, and its activation has downstream branches: one coupled to G proteins and one to beta-arrestin. A group solved structures of the receptor bound to psilocin, the active metabolite of psilocybin, to LSD, to serotonin itself and to lisuride, a psychedelic that does not hallucinate. The receptor is among the most studied in neuropharmacology, which makes the absence of a second binding mode in earlier structures surprising. The paper appeared in *Science*, 27 January 2022.

The structures showed that serotonin and psilocin occupy a second binding mode in addition to the canonical one, a pose that had not been resolved in earlier receptor structures. That extra contact suggested a design route, and the authors used it to produce IHCH-7113, a substructure of the antipsychotic lumateperone, together with several beta-arrestin-biased agonists. Lumateperone supplied a fragment with the right shape and no psychedelic liability, and the analogs were built by varying substituents around that scaffold.

In mice the new agonists produced antidepressant-like behaviour without the signs of hallucination that the classical compounds produce. The battery included the head-twitch response, the rodent correlate of hallucination, and the biased agonists did not produce it at doses that changed behaviour. The dissociation is the finding: a molecule can engage the same receptor, avoid the perceptual effect and still change behaviour in a model that responds to known antidepressants.

Rodent behaviour is a weak proxy for human experience. The tests used for antidepressant-like activity measure locomotion, social interaction or response to stress, and none of them captures the subjective states that psychedelic trials report as central. The absence of hallucination in a mouse is inferred from behavioural signs rather than observed directly.

The structural work is the durable part. A second binding mode explains why compounds with similar affinity produce different downstream signalling, and it gives medicinal chemists a handle that affinity screening alone does not. Beta-arrestin bias is a hypothesis about which branch carries the therapeutic effect, and the mouse data support it without proving it. Whether that bias is the right axis remains contested, since some groups attribute the antidepressant effect to G-protein signalling instead.

Human trials will decide. A biased agonist that produces no perceptual effect could be given in a clinic without a therapist in the room, which changes the economics of the treatment. The first safety studies in people are the next step, and they will also test whether the rodent behaviour predicts anything a patient would recognize.
