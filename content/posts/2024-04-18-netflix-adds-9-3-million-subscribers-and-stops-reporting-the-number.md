Netflix added 9.3 million subscribers in the first quarter, more than double what analysts expected, and reported revenue of 9.37 billion dollars, up 15 per cent. The company also said it would stop reporting subscriber numbers by region from next year, and the shares fell after the announcement. The growth came from the markets where the cheapest tier had been introduced most recently, which is the pattern the company has described in every quarter since the price change.

Change of metric is the news inside the news. A subscriber count measures the size of a market that the company has largely saturated, while revenue per member and advertising income measure what it can earn from a base that no longer grows quickly. A company that stops counting heads is a company that has decided to be judged on money. Revenue per member rose in every region, which is the number that the company is asking investors to watch instead of the count it will stop publishing.

Advertising is the new engine. The cheaper tier that carries advertisements now accounts for a growing share of new sign-ups in the markets where it is offered, and the revenue from it arrives before the cost of the content that supports it. A cheaper tier is a bet that volume compensates for price. The tier is priced at a fraction of the standard subscription and the company sells the space between the titles rather than inside them, which the advertisers prefer because the interruption is shorter.

Password crackdown is the arithmetic behind the quarter. Households that had shared an account were converted into paid members or into extra-member fees, which produced a one-off gain that the company has said will fade. A price rise that is dressed as a policy change is still a price rise. The conversion produced a base that is now paying more per household than the account it replaced, and the households that refused were the ones that had been watching least.

Content spending is the constraint. The company has said it will spend less on new titles than it did at the peak, and the studios that supply it have been selling libraries rather than commissioning new production. A smaller slate is cheaper to run and easier to market, and it is also less likely to produce the hit that drives a quarter. The studios have been licensing finished programmes rather than commissioning new ones, which is cheaper for the buyer and produces a schedule that is less distinctive than the one the service built its reputation on.

What the quarter shows is that a subscription business can be run for profit once growth slows, and the measure it chooses to report is a statement about which part of the business it now expects to carry the story. A business that stops reporting a number is a business that has decided the number no longer describes it, and the market will spend the next year testing that claim.
