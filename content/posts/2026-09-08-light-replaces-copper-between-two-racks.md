A silicon-photonics company demonstrated last week a link carrying 1.6 terabits per second between two server racks, over a single strand of fibre, with the optical engines packaged alongside the switch silicon rather than plugged in as separate modules. The demonstration was not a record for a laboratory; comparable speeds have been shown before. What made it worth reporting is that the link ran inside a working data-centre rack, with the thermal and mechanical constraints of a real installation, and that its power consumption per bit was roughly a third of the copper link it replaced over the same distance.

The distance is the key variable. Copper cables carry electrical signals well for a metre or two, and then attenuation and crosstalk begin to eat the signal. As rack densities and switch bandwidths have risen, the length at which copper stops being viable has shrunk, to the point where the cable between two adjacent racks can require retimers that cost more power than the data they carry is worth. Optics solve the problem by moving the signal into light, where loss over a few dozen metres is negligible, but traditional pluggable optical modules are themselves power-hungry and unreliable at the densities modern switches demand.

Co-packaging is the industry's answer to that second problem. Instead of a module at the edge of the board, the optical engine sits on the same package as the switch die, sharing the substrate and the cooling. The electrical path shrinks from tens of centimetres to a few millimetres, which is where most of the power saving comes from, and the fibre attaches directly to the package through a connector that has to survive the thermal expansion of a running rack. That connector is the hard engineering: aligning a dozen fibres to a photonic chip with sub-micron precision, and keeping them aligned through thousands of thermal cycles.

The commercial pressure is straightforward. AI clusters are now built around switches that move enormous amounts of data between accelerators, and the interconnect has become a visible share of the cluster's power budget and a visible source of failures. Operators who used to buy optics as an accessory are now specifying them as part of the system, and the suppliers who can co-package them with the switch are in a stronger position than those who sell modules. Several large switch vendors have announced co-packaged designs this year, and the startup's demonstration is best read as evidence that the smaller suppliers can compete on the same terms.

The obstacles are yield and repairability. A co-packaged switch is a single expensive assembly, and if one fibre connector fails the whole package may need to be replaced, which is a different service model from swapping a pluggable module at the front of the rack. Photonic chips also test differently from electronic ones: a wafer has to be probed optically as well as electrically, which slows the production line and raises cost. Nobody in the industry pretends these problems are solved; they are being solved, at the pace that a market with a genuine bottleneck imposes.

If the approach holds, the consequence is a quieter change than the headline speed suggests. Data centres have spent twenty years arranging themselves around the reach of copper: racks close together, cables short, layout constrained. Optics that are cheap and cool enough to sit on the package loosen that constraint, and the layout of a machine room becomes an engineering choice rather than a consequence of cable physics. That is the sort of shift that shows up in electricity bills years later, long after the demonstration that started it has been forgotten.
