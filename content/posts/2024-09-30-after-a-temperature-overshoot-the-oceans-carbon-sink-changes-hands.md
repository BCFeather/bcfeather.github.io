Overshoot scenarios assume that warming will exceed a target and then be brought back by removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, and the ocean's behaviour under such a trajectory has not been examined closely. A paper in *Nature Geoscience* on 30 September 2024 does that with an Earth system model of intermediate complexity over centennial timescales. The scenarios are idealized, which is appropriate for a first question about mechanism rather than for an assessment of a policy.

Under rising emissions the ocean takes up carbon because the partial pressure in the atmosphere is higher than in the surface water, and that physico-chemical uptake dominates the sink. The question the paper asks is what happens once emissions become net-negative and the gradient reverses. The gradient is what drives the flux, so the sink is a consequence of the atmosphere rather than of any property of the water.

An answer is that the physico-chemical sink becomes fragile. Its fate depends on the atmospheric boundary conditions that follow the overshoot, and some of the carbon that was stored is released back to the atmosphere when the atmosphere's concentration falls. A stored quantity that can return is not a removal in the accounting sense, and the distinction is what the paper makes explicit.

Storage does not disappear, though, because the biological pump takes over. Carbon fixed by photosynthesis and exported to depth is sequestered for longer than the dissolved pool, and in the overshoot runs that export supplies most of the net storage. Export is a flux through the surface layer, and its efficiency depends on the particles reaching depth before they are respired.

Shift is a change in which mechanism holds the carbon. A sink that is chemical responds to the atmosphere within years, while a sink that is biological responds on the timescale of the water masses that carry the particles. Which mechanism holds the carbon determines the timescale on which it can be released, and the two timescales differ by orders of magnitude.

The model's resolution is a limitation, since an intermediate-complexity model cannot represent eddies or the full range of ecosystem responses, and the biological pump is parametrized rather than resolved. An intermediate-complexity model is the right instrument for a centennial question, and it is the wrong instrument for a regional one.

Policy reading is that the durability of a removal strategy depends on which sink is credited. A tonne stored by physico-chemical uptake can return, and a tonne stored by export is more likely to stay. What the paper establishes is that overshoot is not a simple reversal. The ocean that absorbs carbon under one regime is not the ocean that holds it under the next, and the transition is where the accounting has to be done. A removal that is credited as permanent while the sink beneath it is reversible would misstate the benefit. A regional model, forced with the same boundary conditions, would show whether the shift survives eddies and coastal processes.
