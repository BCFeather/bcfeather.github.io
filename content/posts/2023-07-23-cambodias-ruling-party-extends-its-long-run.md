The Cambodian People's Party won the election on 23 July by a margin that no observer described as competitive. With results announced over the following days, the party of Hun Sen took 120 of the 125 seats in the National Assembly, and the turnout reported by the national election committee was 84.6 per cent, higher than in 2018. The Candlelight Party, the only opposition group with a national organization, had been barred from registering months earlier over paperwork that its lawyers said was manufactured.

Exclusion was the election's central fact. Candlelight had won a fifth of the vote in the commune elections of 2022, and its disqualification left the ballot with the ruling party, its royalist ally Funcinpec and a handful of small parties. The European Union and the United States said the vote had been neither free nor fair, and the European Parliament had already begun a review of Cambodia's trade preferences. Sanctions on individuals involved in the exclusion were discussed in Washington and then shelved. The government's position was that its opponents had broken the rules and that foreign criticism was interference.

Hun Sen has governed since 1985, first as a young man installed by the Vietnamese army that removed the Khmer Rouge and then by winning elections that he sometimes lost and always survived. His party controls the army, the police, the courts, the media and most of the provincial administrations, and it has spent a decade removing the institutions that once allowed the opposition to compete. The remaining space for criticism is small, and it has shrunk every year of his rule.

Succession was announced within days of the result. Hun Sen said he would step down as prime minister, a post he had held for thirty-eight years, and hand it to his son Hun Manet, a four-star general educated at West Point and in Britain. The king signed the appointment in early August, and the new government was sworn in on 22 August. Hun Sen remains a member of parliament, the party's president and a man whose family controls several ministries, and his son's cabinet contains several ministers who are the children of the men who ran the country before him.

Scepticism abroad has been loud and mostly ineffective. Cambodia's growth has been built on Chinese investment, garment exports to Europe and American markets, and tourism, and none of those partners has been willing to sacrifice the relationship to press for democracy. The opposition in exile has called the vote a sham, which it was, and has no means of altering the outcome.

A young prime minister will take office with an old party and an unchanged security apparatus. His mandate rests on a ballot that most foreign governments have declined to recognize, which will shape every negotiation he conducts abroad. The next few years will show whether the dynasty can manage the problems that outlast its founder: a garment sector facing higher costs, a youth population looking for work, and a dependency on a single foreign patron.
