Coral bleaching forecasts have used a single temperature anomaly as a proxy for stress, and the proxy misses the differences between species and between reefs. A model that adds a species-specific threshold to the same temperature data reproduced the 2024 event's severity with a three-month lead, and its improvement comes from the corals' own history.

The threshold is a cumulative heat exposure that a species tolerates before bleaching, and it is estimated from a reef's previous events. A reef that bleached in 2016 and recovered has a higher threshold than one that has not bleached in twenty years.

The model's inputs are a sea surface temperature product, a species composition map and an exposure history. The composition map is the newest piece, built from 12,000 survey transects.

Prediction quality improved from a correlation of 0.4 to 0.7 between forecast and observed severity, and the improvement is largest for reefs with a well-documented history. For reefs without one, the model falls back to a default threshold and loses most of its skill. Recovery is the variable the exposure history encodes, and a reef that recovered slowly carries a threshold that a fast recovery would have raised.

The mechanism's implication is uncomfortable. A reef that has bleached repeatedly appears more tolerant and is also more degraded, and a model that treats its threshold as adaptation may understate its risk. A threshold that rises with repeated exposure describes a reef that is adapting and a reef that is thinning, and the model cannot distinguish the two.

Validation used a held-out region. The model was trained on the Pacific and tested on the Indian Ocean's 2024 event, and its forecast matched 8 of 11 reef groups within one severity category.

Management use is the point of a three-month lead. A reef manager with a forecast can shade a nursery, relocate fragments or close a site, and the interventions are cheap compared with restoration.

A cumulative exposure metric integrates temperature above a bleaching threshold over weeks, and its advantage over a single anomaly is that it captures a heat wave's duration. A reef that spends a month at one degree above its threshold bleaches more than one that spends a day at four above.

A species composition map is the model's weakest input. A transect identifies coral by genus and a model needs a species' threshold, and the gap is filled by a genus average that hides the variation within it.

A forecast's use is a decision and its accuracy is a resource. A reef manager with three months can move a nursery's stock; a manager with three weeks can only watch. The model's lead time, rather than its correlation, is what a management agency buys.

Forecast skill depends on a population's history as much as on its temperature. A model that learns each reef's past is a model that can say which reefs are about to lose what they have left.
