A festival's date is a decision about weather, competition, audience and the town that hosts it, and a festival that moved from August to late September this year reported attendance up 14 per cent and complaints down by half.

Weather was the stated reason. The region's August mean maximum is 34 degrees and the site has no shade, and the medical tent treated 180 heat-related cases in the last August edition. The September mean is 27, and the festival reported 40 cases.

Competition was the quieter reason. Three festivals within 300 kilometres now fall in August, and the audience overlap is 40 per cent. Moving to September put the festival in a month with one competitor.

The town's objections were about traffic and noise, and the September date changed both. The August event coincided with the school holidays, when the town's population doubles; the September event falls in term time, and the traffic plan needed half the road closures.

Economics of the move are not trivial. The festival's contract with its site runs annually, and the move required a renegotiation because the site hosts a harvest fair in September. The festival pays 30 per cent more for the site and saves 20 per cent on temporary shade and cooling.

Artists' availability changed in both directions. Two headline acts were available in September and not in August, and one was the reverse. The programme's genre balance shifted slightly towards artists on tour in the autumn.

The comparison with other festivals is a pattern. Four festivals in the region have moved dates in five years, and all four moved away from the hottest month, which suggests the calendar is becoming a climate adaptation decision.

Local employment is the argument the town makes for keeping the festival, whatever the date. The event employs 400 people for a week, mostly casual, and the September date falls in a period when agricultural work is winding down, which makes staffing easier.

The audience data show a shift in composition. Camping fell 20 per cent and hotel bookings rose 35 per cent, because September is cooler and the audience is older. That is a change in the event's character that the programme did not intend.

The ticketing data are the operational proof. Sales in the first week were 40 per cent above the previous edition's, and the refund rate fell from 8 per cent to 3, which the promoter reads as a lower risk of a weather-driven cancellation.

The risk the promoter accepted is a September drought. The region's rainfall is concentrated in July and August, and a dry September would leave the site's grass cover thin and the dust control budget higher. Two September editions in the past decade were cancelled for rain, against four August editions for heat.

For a reader, the case is that a cultural event's date is an operational variable with health, competition and civic consequences. The programme is what audiences talk about, and the date is what determines whether they can attend it comfortably.
