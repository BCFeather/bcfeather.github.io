The prime minister spoke on Monday morning and said the legislation would be suspended until the next parliamentary session, which would begin after the Passover recess in May. He described the pause as a chance for dialogue, and he said he would convene the opposition parties to negotiate a compromise. The announcement came twelve hours after the general strike had been suspended by the court and after the country had spent a night watching crowds on the highways. He said the coalition would return to the legislation in the summer session, and that the suspension was a chance for the opposition to negotiate in good faith.

In substance the pause was a retreat, and in form it was a manoeuvre. Two of the three readings of the main bill had already passed, and the third would now wait, which meant that the coalition had not given up the legislation but had postponed the vote. The opposition leaders said they would negotiate and refused to accept the suspension as a settlement, and the protest organizers announced that the demonstrations would continue.

Coalition arithmetic explained the retreat. The government held sixty-four of a hundred and twenty seats, and several of those members had been shaken by the reservists' refusal and by the warnings from the security establishment. The minister of national security, who had demanded a new national guard as the price of his support, had become the most visible opponent of the pause, and his opposition made the coalition's internal argument public. Of the sixty-four members, at least five had said in private that they would not vote for the third reading, and one of them had said it in public.

Reservists had become the decisive fact. Air force pilots, intelligence officers and special forces veterans had announced that they would not volunteer, and the chief of staff had told the government that the readiness of the army was being damaged. A country that had fought several wars on the assumption of a rapid mobilization discovered that the mobilization depended on the consent of the people who were being mobilized. Their refusal had been announced unit by unit, in letters and in group chats, and the chief of staff had met the prime minister twice in a week.

By then the economy had begun to price the risk. The currency had fallen to its weakest level in three years, the central bank had raised its key rate in an unscheduled meeting, and the technology industry reported that investment was being moved abroad. The finance ministry's own forecast had been revised down, and the revision was the part of the crisis that the government could not describe as a matter of opinion.

The pause held for a month and did not produce an agreement. The negotiations that the president convened ended without a text, the legislation returned in the summer in a modified form, and the protest movement that had filled the streets in March stayed through the year. The country had learned how quickly a legal argument could become a question about the army, and the lesson was still being taught.
