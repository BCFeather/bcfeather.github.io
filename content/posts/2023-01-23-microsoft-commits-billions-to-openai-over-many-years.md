Announcing the deal on 23 January in a blog post and a regulatory filing, the company described the commitment only as multiyear and multibillion. People familiar with the terms put the total at 10 billion dollars, spread over several years and paid partly in Azure computing credits rather than in cash. That structure matters, because it keeps the money inside the company's own cloud business. The post was signed by the chief executive and contained no numbers, which is why the figures came from people rather than from the filing.

That relationship began in 2019 with a 1 billion dollar investment, and it has shaped both firms since. OpenAI uses Azure for training runs that consume thousands of graphics processors for weeks, and Microsoft has used the models in its own products, including a search feature and a coding assistant. The new agreement extended the compute arrangement and gave Microsoft a larger equity stake, reported at 49 per cent on a fully diluted basis. The stake is structured as a profit-sharing agreement, so the reported 49 per cent is not equity in the ordinary sense.

Regulators in Europe and Britain had already begun asking whether the arrangement amounted to a merger. The filing language was careful, and the two companies described the deal as a partnership rather than an acquisition. Microsoft does not consolidate OpenAI's results, and OpenAI remains a separate company with its own board. The competition authority in Britain opened a merger review in December and closed it without action.

Ten billion is large for a research lab and small for the company paying it. Microsoft spends about 28 billion dollars a year on research and development, and its cash position was over 100 billion. Analysts at two banks said the deal was cheap relative to building the capability in house, and one of them described it as an option on the next platform. Capital spending rose 20 per cent in the quarter that followed, mostly on servers for the models.

What the investment does not buy is certainty about the technology. The models are expensive to run, and the products built on them have not yet produced revenue at the scale of the cloud business. Microsoft's gaming and search divisions were both losing share before the partnership, and the search feature that launched in February changed that picture within a quarter. The search feature was used by 100 million people within a month, according to the company, and advertising revenue did not follow at once.

Competitors responded within weeks. Google announced its own assistant upgrades, and Amazon published a language model service for its cloud customers. The race is now about distribution rather than about research, which is why the money went to the company that already had the computers. The partnership has since been extended again, and the terms of the later agreement have not been published.
