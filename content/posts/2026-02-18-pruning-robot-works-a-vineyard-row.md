---Winter pruning decides a vine's yield and its shape, and it is done by hand with secateurs at a rate of about 40 vines an hour. A tracked robot that cuts canes with a reciprocating blade completed a full season across 12 hectares this month, and the comparison with the pruning team is the substance of its report.

Perception is where the difficulty sits. The robot images each vine with stereo cameras, builds a depth map, and classifies canes by thickness and orientation against the spur position. A cane that crosses the vine's trunk must be cut; one that will become a spur must be kept.

Performance is 92 per cent of the cuts a skilled pruner makes, measured by an agronomist who reviewed the same vines. The misses are concentrated where canes overlap the trunk or where a spur is buried under wood, which are the cases a human resolves by feel.

Throughput is 55 vines an hour, against 40 for a person, and the machine runs 14 hours a day with a battery swap every four. That is a real gain in a season that lasts six weeks and a real limitation in a field with irregular trellis.

Cost is 180,000 dollars, leased at 22,000 a season, against a pruning crew of four at 60,000 for the same work. The vineyard's arithmetic depends on labour availability, which is the constraint in the region where the trial ran.

The human in the loop is not a fallback. The robot operates with a supervisor who corrects errors and finishes the vines the machine flags, and the supervisor's productivity rises to 110 vines an hour because the machine has done the heavy cuts.

Agronomic effects are being measured over seasons. Pruning quality affects budbreak and yield in the following year, and the vineyard reports a 4 per cent yield variation between robot-pruned and hand-pruned rows, which is within its normal year-to-year range.

Safety certification is the barrier to a wider trial. A machine with a reciprocating blade working in a row where people also work requires a risk assessment under machinery rules, and the vineyard's assessment took eight months and imposed a two-metre exclusion zone.

Data ownership is the quieter issue. The robot records an image and a cut decision for every vine, which is a map of the vineyard's yield potential. The vineyard's contract with the manufacturer assigns the data to the grower, which two other growers have asked to renegotiate.

The economics are within reach of a mid-sized vineyard. The robot costs 180,000 dollars and replaces 2,000 hours of seasonal labor at 22 dollars an hour, and the payback is 4 years at the vineyard's scale. A smaller holding cannot justify it.

The Comparison with a mechanical pruner is the honest baseline. A conventional machine cuts every spur at the same height and leaves 30 per cent of the decisions to a follow-up pass, while the robot makes each cut individually. The gap is in quality rather than in speed.

For a reader, the pattern is the one that recurs in agricultural robotics. The machine reaches most of the task at a fraction of the labour and stops short at the cases that require judgement, and the working arrangement that results is a supervisor with a machine rather than a machine alone.
