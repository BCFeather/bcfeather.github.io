Alexander Zverev spent five years as the best player never to win a major, a label that hardened after he lost the 2020 US Open final from two sets up and then lost three more finals to opponents who played the big points better. The talent was never in question, since a 198-centimetre frame with a serve that touches 220 kilometres per hour and a backhand that has been the cleanest in the game since he was nineteen does not arrive by accident. The serve had been there for years, and the nerve had not.

On 13 September he closed the argument. Zverev beat Ben Shelton 6-3, 7-6(2), 5-7, 6-2 in the final at Arthur Ashe Stadium, taking his second major of the season after Roland Garros in June and a Wimbledon final in July. He is the fourth man in the Open era to win his first two Grand Slam titles in the same year, and the first to do it at 29 with a career that had already been written off. The final was his sixth meeting with Shelton and his sixth win, and the pattern is one of the clearest matchups on the tour.

The match turned on the smallest margins and Zverev owned them. He faced one break point before the third set, won 85 per cent of points on his first serve, and struck 35 winners with nine aces, keeping rallies long enough that Shelton's power had to be generated twice. Shelton broke serve for the first time in 66 service games to take the third set, and the crowd in New York responded as if a comeback had begun. His returning position was deep and his backhand cross-court was the shot that did most of the damage, which is the pattern that has troubled Shelton all year.

The fourth set answered that. Zverev broke in a 24-point opening game, held without difficulty, and closed out a match that had lasted three and a half hours in a tournament where he had spent more than nineteen hours on court. Shelton, who had beaten him never in six meetings, was left with the compliment he offered at the net: that Zverev may be the best player in the world this year. Shelton's serving numbers fell in the fourth set as the pressure accumulated, and the double fault at 30-40 in the opening game set the tone.

For American tennis the result extends a drought that has become a structural fact. Andy Roddick in 2003 remains the last American man to win a major singles title, and Shelton was only the second to reach a final since. A home final is the best chance the country has had in two decades, and the gap between reaching one and winning one is the gap the next generation has to close. The next American finalist will face the same question, and the answer depends on junior development as much as on any single player.

Zverev's season now reads as the correction of a career rather than a single fortnight. Two majors, a final at Wimbledon, and a head-to-head record against Shelton that stands at six wins and no losses suggest a player who has solved the psychology that used to undo him. Whether that holds into next season is a different question, and it is the one that separates a good year from a great career. A second major in one season also changes how he will be seeded and scheduled next year, which is the reward that compounds.
