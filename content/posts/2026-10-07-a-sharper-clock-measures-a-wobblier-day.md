The second is defined by a caesium transition, and the clocks that realize it have become so stable that the definition is now the weaker link. Optical lattice clocks, which trap atoms in a standing wave of laser light and interrogate a narrow transition, reach fractional stabilities around ten to the eighteenth. At that level, a clock will not drift by a second in the age of the universe. Comparing two such clocks is straightforward if you can get them into the same laboratory, and comparing a clock to the rotating Earth is the harder and more interesting measurement.

A paper published this month reports a comparison of an optical clock against the Earth's rotation over several months, using very-long-baseline interferometry to fix the orientation of the planet and the clock to fix the duration. The measured variation in the length of the day, the difference between the rotation period and twenty-four hours of atomic time, was resolved at a level of tens of microseconds, a precision that used to require weeks of averaging.

The variation itself is not new. The length of the day changes for three reasons. The atmosphere exchanges angular momentum with the solid Earth, and the seasonal migration of the jet streams and the monsoon circulations produce an annual signal of about a millisecond. The oceans and the tides add shorter-period terms. And the core, through electromagnetic coupling at the core-mantle boundary, produces variations over years and decades that are difficult to explain without a model of the fluid flow two thousand kilometres below the surface.

What the new measurement adds is resolution. The atmospheric terms can be modeled from weather reanalyses, and subtracting them from the observed signal leaves a residual that is mostly the core. A sharper measurement makes that subtraction cleaner, which turns a rough inference about the geodynamo into a constraint on it. The authors report that the residual matches the pattern expected from a torsional oscillation in the outer core, a wave that propagates along magnetic field lines and has been theorized for two decades.

There is a practical side as well. Civil timekeeping depends on the difference between atomic time and the rotation of the Earth, and the decision to insert leap seconds or to let the difference accumulate is a decision made with imperfect measurements. The body that coordinates time has already voted to raise the tolerance, effectively ending leap seconds by 2035. Better measurements of the rotation do not change that decision, but they do mean the correction can be made in a planned way rather than a reactive one.

The result is a reminder of how strange the measurement is. The most precise instruments humans have built are now turning the planet itself into a test mass, and the wobble they detect is the signature of an ocean of liquid metal we cannot see. A clock that keeps time better than the Earth does is a useful instrument precisely because the planet does not keep time well.
