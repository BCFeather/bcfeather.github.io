The federal statistics office reported on 28 April that gross domestic product was unchanged in the first three months of 2023 compared with the previous quarter, after adjustment for prices, seasonal effects and the calendar. The fourth quarter of 2022 was revised to a fall of 0.5 per cent, which meant that the two consecutive quarters of decline that define a technical recession did not occur. The office described the result as stagnation, and the word was in every headline by the afternoon. The office revised the fourth quarter down from a fall of 0.4 to 0.5 per cent, which is what put the definition within reach.

Composition was weak in the places that matter. Household spending fell as inflation ate into real incomes, government consumption was flat, and construction was held back by higher interest rates. Industry was the one component that helped, with output rising slightly after a year of energy costs that had pushed some energy-intensive producers to cut production or to move it abroad.

Energy explains the year. Gas prices that peaked at ten times the American level in the summer of 2022 forced chemical and metal producers to reduce output, and the government's support package cost about 200 billion euros. The price has since fallen below the pre-war level, and the relief has not yet reached the order books of the companies that shut furnaces. Industrial production is still about 5 per cent below its level before the war, and the chemical industry, which consumes a third of the country's gas, has moved part of its capacity to Asia and to the United States.

Forecasts have been revised so often that they have become a genre. The government's council of economic advisers, the institutes in Munich and Kiel, and the finance ministry have all published numbers between minus 0.5 and plus 0.5 per cent for the year, with each revision explaining why the last one was wrong. The common ground is that growth will be weak, that inflation will fall slowly, and that the labour shortage will limit any recovery that does arrive.

Politics of the number are awkward for the coalition. The economy minister has described the situation as a pause and the finance minister has said that the country is losing competitiveness, and both are describing the same data. The industrial policy that the government has announced, including subsidies for chip plants and a price cap on electricity for manufacturers, is being argued in a country that has built its reputation on not doing that.

What stagnation means for Europe is a largest economy that contributes nothing to growth. Germany's exports to China have fallen, its car industry is investing elsewhere, and its fiscal room is smaller than it was. A country that avoided a recession by a rounding error is still a country whose households are poorer than they were three years ago, and the difference between the two statements is what the rest of the year will be spent arguing about.
