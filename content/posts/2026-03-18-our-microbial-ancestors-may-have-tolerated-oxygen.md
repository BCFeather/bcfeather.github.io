The origin of the eukaryotic cell is usually told as a story about oxygen. Anaerobic prokaryotes dominated the early biosphere, cyanobacteria invented oxygenic photosynthesis, the atmosphere filled with oxygen, and only then did complex cells appear, using the new oxidant to power a larger metabolism. The story is tidy and it has a weakness: it requires complexity to wait, and the fossil record suggests it did not wait quite as long as the tidy version implies.

Asgard archaea are the closest known prokaryotic relatives of eukaryotes, recovered from sediments and hot vents by genome assembly and not by culture. Their genomes contain genes for a cytoskeleton, for membrane trafficking and for small GTPases, which are the components that make a eukaryotic cell more than a bag of enzymes. A new genomic analysis asked whether they also contain the machinery for using oxygen.

They do. The work, summarized in a research briefing in *Nature* on 18 March and published by Appler and colleagues, reports that Asgard archaea possess the genes required to respire with oxygen, and the genes appear to be ancestral to the group and not recently acquired. The implication is that the common ancestor of Asgard archaea and eukaryotes could have been oxygen-tolerant, which is the opposite of the standard model.

If the ancestor was tolerant, the timeline changes. Oxygen tolerance does not require an oxygenated atmosphere, because local oxygen sources exist wherever photosynthetic microbes form mats, and a microbe living near such a mat experiences oxygen long before the atmosphere does. Complexity could then have begun in a niche, and expanded when the niche became the world.

The claim rests on genomes and not on cultures, and genomes are inferences. A gene for a terminal oxidase is evidence that the capacity exists, and it is not evidence that the organism uses it under the conditions in which it lives. Culturing an Asgard archaeon remains the ambition, and until it is achieved the argument is about what the sequence allows, not about what the cell does.

Reconstruction has a second layer of uncertainty. The ancestral state is inferred by comparing gene trees across lineages, and horizontal gene transfer moves genes between lineages often enough in prokaryotes that no single-gene inference survives contact with a second genome. The authors' case rests on a set of genes moving together, which is stronger evidence than a single locus and weaker evidence than an experiment.

Deeper still, the origin of eukaryotes is being rewritten from below. For fifty years the story was driven by organelles and by the endosymbiotic theory, and the modern version is driven by sequence data from organisms that nobody had seen a decade ago. Each new genome makes the ancestor less like a bacterium and more like something that had already begun to acquire a cell with structure, and the field is now arguing about a lineage that nobody expected to be this complicated.
