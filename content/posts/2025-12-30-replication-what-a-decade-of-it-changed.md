Ten years ago a set of replication projects began reporting that a substantial share of published findings in psychology and cancer biology did not reproduce. The reaction was a crisis narrative, and a decade later the record is more interesting than the narrative.

The reported replication rates settled at between 40 and 60 per cent across fields, and the number depends heavily on what counts as a success. A study that reproduces a direction but not an effect size is a partial success, and the categories were not standardized when the first results landed.

Practices changed in ways that are measurable. Preregistration rose from a rarity to a majority in several journals, sample sizes in the replicated subfields grew by a factor of four, and the reporting of a study's constraints on generality became a section rather than an afterthought.

Incentives moved more slowly. A replication is still worth less to a career than a novel result, and a journal's rejection of a replication remains common. Two funding agencies now have a line for it, which is a start. The categories were standardized in a 2022 consensus statement, and the projects that reported before it are read through a conversion table.

The estimate of the problem's size is the part that improved. Meta-analyses of replication projects put the share of findings with genuine effects at 60 to 70 per cent in psychology, against the earlier impression of a field that was half wrong. Another field ran the same experiment with worse results. Preclinical cancer biology's replication rate was 20 to 25 per cent in the largest project, and the field's response was a set of reporting standards rather than a restructuring of incentives.

Nobody counts the decade's cost. A replication consumes a graduate student's year and produces a paper that few cite, and the balance between checking and exploring is a policy question that a field decides by what it rewards. A replication's outcome is a distribution and not a verdict. An effect that is smaller than the original's but still distinguishable from zero is a partial success, and a field that reports it as failure teaches its students to read every result as binary.

The infrastructure that made the decade possible is still small. Two registries hold most of the preregistered protocols, and a replication's materials are deposited in a repository that a funder pays for. The field's checking capacity is about 2 per cent of its output. A field's checking capacity is set by its training. A graduate program that teaches replication as a method produces researchers who can run one, and a program that teaches it as a controversy produces researchers who argue about it. The decade's lasting change is in the curriculum rather than in the journals.

Neither the crisis narrative nor the complacent one survives the record. The record shows a field that fixed its methods and left its incentives alone, and the second is where the next decade's work sits.
