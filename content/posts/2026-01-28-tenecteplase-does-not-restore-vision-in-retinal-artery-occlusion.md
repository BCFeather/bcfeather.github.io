Central retinal artery occlusion is a stroke of the eye. The same clot that causes a cerebral stroke blocks the artery supplying the retina, and the retina tolerates ischaemia for perhaps ninety minutes before the loss becomes permanent. Because the eye is small and the diagnosis is made in an ophthalmology clinic, patients rarely reach a thrombolysis service inside the window, and no treatment has been shown to work.

TenCRAOS randomised 81 patients with acute non-arteritic central retinal artery occlusion to intravenous tenecteplase at 0.25 milligrams per kilogram or to aspirin 300 milligrams, with 78 patients included in the analysis. The primary outcome was recovery of visual acuity at 30 days, defined as an improvement of at least three lines on the chart or a return to a level that permitted reading. The trial appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 28 January.

The result was neutral and slightly in the wrong direction. Visual recovery occurred in 8 of 40 patients given tenecteplase, twenty per cent, and in 9 of 38 given aspirin, twenty-four per cent, a risk difference of minus 3.7 percentage points with a confidence interval from minus 22.0 to 14.7 and a *P* value of 0.69. No secondary endpoint favoured the drug. The numbers are small in absolute terms because the condition is rare in trial-eligible form, and the arithmetic of 8 against 9 is presented with confidence intervals and not percentages alone.

Safety was the finding that will change practice. Adverse events were more frequent in the tenecteplase group, including one fatal intracranial haemorrhage, and the trial's population was small enough that a single event dominates the safety picture. Thrombolysis carries a known risk of bleeding, and that risk is justified when the expected benefit is large and harder to justify when the benefit is absent.

The trial's limitation is its size and its timing. Eighty-one patients cannot exclude a modest benefit, and the median time from symptom onset to treatment was not short enough to test the hypothesis that early lysis saves the retina. A trial enriched for patients treated within two hours would answer a different question, and this one cannot.

Design difficulty explains why the question has stayed open. The diagnosis requires an ophthalmologist, the treatment requires a stroke team, and the two are rarely in the same building at the same time. Recruitment therefore depends on a referral pathway that most hospitals do not have, which is why a condition that affects thousands of people a year has been studied in trials of fewer than a hundred. A referral pathway is an intervention in itself, and several centres are now building one to prepare for the next trial rather than for this one.

Practice will not change quickly, because some centres have used intra-arterial thrombolysis on the basis of case series and will read this trial as being about the intravenous route at a dose that may be too low. The honest summary is that the best available evidence now shows no benefit and some harm, and that the burden of proof has shifted to those who continue to treat.
