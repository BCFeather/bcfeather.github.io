Recurrent urinary tract infection is managed with daily low-dose antibiotics for months, and the strategy works well enough that it has been standard for decades. It also selects for resistant organisms in the gut and the bladder, and a prevention that does not rely on antibiotics has been sought for as long as the strategy has existed. Every course of prophylaxis is a course of selection, and a woman who takes a daily antibiotic for a year carries organisms that no longer respond to the drug she is taking.

Methenamine hippurate is an old drug that releases formaldehyde in acidic urine, which is bactericidal without being a antibiotic in the conventional sense. A UK trial compared it with standard prophylaxis in women with recurrent infection, randomizing 240 to twelve months of either daily antibiotic or methenamine hippurate. The results appeared in *The BMJ*, 9 March 2022.

The primary outcome was the incidence of symptomatic, antibiotic-treated urinary infection during treatment, and the non-inferiority margin was set at one episode per person year by a patient and public involvement group. That margin is generous by statistical convention and was chosen to reflect what patients would accept. Choosing a margin before the trial begins is the correct way to run a non-inferiority study, because a margin chosen afterwards can always be set to accommodate the result.

In the modified intention-to-treat population of 205 women, the antibiotic group had 0.89 episodes per person year, with an interval from 0.65 to 1.12, and the methenamine group had 1.38, with an interval from 1.05 to 1.72. The difference fell within the prespecified margin, so the trial met its non-inferiority criterion.

The design was open-label and allowed crossover between arms, which is a weakness for a symptom-based endpoint. Women who knew they were taking a non-antibiotic might have tolerated symptoms differently, and the treating clinician was not masked either, so the decision to prescribe an antibiotic was made with knowledge of the allocation. An open-label design is defensible for a drug with a different mechanism and a different side-effect profile, and it weakens the claim that the two treatments are equivalent in the eyes of a patient.

Adherence and adverse events need to be read alongside the primary result. Methenamine requires a acidic urine and is less effective when the urine is alkaline, and the trial's population was drawn from UK centres where the drug is licensed and familiar.

For patients who have had repeated courses of antibiotics and who worry about resistance, the result offers a reasonable alternative with a small increase in episodes. For a health service, it offers a way to reduce antibiotic consumption without abandoning prophylaxis, and that is the argument that will drive adoption. A proportion of women will prefer the antibiotic because it produced fewer episodes, and a proportion will prefer the alternative because it does not disturb the gut community, so the choice is one for shared decision.
