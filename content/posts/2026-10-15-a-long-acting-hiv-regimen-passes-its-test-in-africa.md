An injected long-acting regimen held HIV suppression as well as daily pills in African adults who had trouble taking pills, according to a trial in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 15 October. At 48 weeks, 91.0 per cent of participants on the long-acting therapy had a viral load below 50 copies per millilitre, against 89.2 per cent on oral therapy, a difference that met the trial's criterion for non-inferiority.

Adherence is the quiet failure mode of HIV treatment. A regimen that works when taken is worthless to a patient who cannot take it reliably, and the reasons range from stigma and travel to the simple difficulty of a daily appointment with a bottle. Long-acting injectables were developed for exactly this population, and the trials that matter are the ones run where the problem is worst rather than where the clinics are best.

Trial participants were adults with a documented history of adherence difficulty, randomized to the injectable schedule or to continue oral therapy, with 271 and 269 people in the two arms. Virologic suppression at 48 weeks was the primary measure, and the confidence interval around the 1.9 percentage point difference, from 3.1 points below to 6.9 above, sat inside the margin the investigators had pre-specified. On the main question, the injection is not worse than the pill.

One number complicates the headline. Confirmed virologic failure occurred in 5 participants, 1.9 per cent of the long-acting group, and in none of the oral group, a difference of 1.9 percentage points with a confidence interval that excludes zero. A failure rate that small is not a reason to reject a regimen, but it is a reason to keep viral load monitoring in place, because a failure on a long-acting injection can persist for months after the last dose.

Serious adverse events were similar between arms, 4.1 per cent against 3.0 per cent, which is the safety result a programme needs before it can offer injections outside a specialist clinic. The practical questions are different: cold chain, injection training, and the willingness of health systems to pay for a visit every two months instead of a bottle every month. A regimen that removes daily adherence moves the adherence problem to the clinic, where it becomes a question of appointments, stock and staff time rather than of a patient's discipline.

Long-acting HIV therapy has been available in rich countries for several years and has been slow to reach the settings where adherence is hardest, for reasons of price and infrastructure rather than of evidence. This trial is the evidence. What it cannot show is durability beyond a year, or what happens when a patient misses an injection and returns late, and those are the operational failures that decide whether a programme works. Price is the other, and it is set by a manufacturer rather than by the health system that would run the clinics.
