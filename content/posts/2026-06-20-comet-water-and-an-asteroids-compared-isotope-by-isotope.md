Earth's water is older than Earth, and its origin is a question about which reservoir the oceans came from. A paper published this month compared the deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio in water from three comets with that in a carbonaceous asteroid's hydrated minerals, and the asteroid's water matches Earth's more closely than any comet measured.

The ratio is the tracer. Deuterium is a heavy isotope of hydrogen, and the ratio in a reservoir records the temperature at which its water formed: colder conditions produce a higher ratio because the heavier isotope condenses more readily. Earth's oceans have a ratio of 156 parts per million.

Comet measurements come from spectroscopy. A comet's coma contains water vapour, and a ground-based instrument measures the ratio in the emission lines at 3.7 micrometres. The three comets in this study have ratios between 180 and 320, which is consistent with formation in the cold outer disc.

The asteroid measurement is new. A sample returned by a mission contains hydrated clay minerals, and the team extracted the water by heating the sample and measuring the released vapour with a mass spectrometer. The ratio is 148 parts per million, within 5 per cent of Earth's oceans.

The comparison changes the inventory arithmetic. A comet population with high ratios could contribute at most 10 per cent of Earth's water without pushing the ocean's ratio above its measured value. An asteroid population with the measured ratio could contribute all of it.

Mechanism for delivery is dynamical. Carbonaceous asteroids formed in the outer part of the main belt and were scattered inward during the giant planet migration, and the number that hit Earth in the first 100 million years is estimated from the crater record and from the lunar samples.

Limits are sample size and representativeness. Three comets and one asteroid is a small sample, and the asteroid belongs to a class whose members vary in their water content. The paper's claim is about a class rather than about every body in it.

The measurement's precision is what gives the comparison weight. The mass spectrometer's ratio is reproducible to 3 parts per million on a 2-milligram sample, and the extraction's blank was measured in every batch. A sample contaminated by terrestrial water would show a ratio near 156, which is the value the team reports, so the blank control matters more than usual here.

Nitrogen isotopes provide a second line. The sample's nitrogen ratio is consistent with a cold formation, which supports the dynamical model that placed these bodies beyond the snow line before scattering them inward.

The inventory model is the test the measurement enables. A contribution of 10 per cent comets and 90 per cent asteroids matches both the ratio and the total mass required, and a cometary contribution above 30 per cent would require an ocean whose ratio was later raised by an unknown process.

For a reader, the significance is that a single isotope ratio ties a measured sample to a planetary-scale inventory. The question of where Earth's water came from has been argued on models for decades; a returned sample turns it into a comparison between two numbers.
