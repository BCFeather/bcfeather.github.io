description: "Among 2,449 people followed from age 65 for up to 18 years, a favourable lifestyle was associated with 3.1 more years of life in women and 5.7 in men, with Alzheimer's dementia occupying a smaller share of those years."

Dementia research has focused on delaying onset, and a delay that lengthens life without shortening the period of illness simply moves the burden. The right question is whether a healthy lifestyle compresses the years with dementia or merely postpones them. The compression hypothesis has been debated for three decades, with some studies reporting an expansion of morbidity as life expectancy rises and others reporting the opposite.

A cohort called the Chicago Health and Aging Project followed 2,449 people from age 65 for up to 18 years, scoring five lifestyle factors and estimating life expectancy with and without Alzheimer's dementia using a multistate model. The study appeared in *The BMJ*, 13 April 2022.

Five factors made up the score score in the upper 40 per cent, late-life cognitive activity in the upper 40 per cent, at least 150 minutes of moderate or vigorous activity per week, no smoking, and light to moderate alcohol consumption. The MIND diet is a hybrid of the Mediterranean and DASH patterns, and the score in the upper 40 per cent is a threshold rather than a target that a person either meets or misses. A person who meets four of the five does not necessarily differ from one who meets three in any way that the analysis can see.

Women aged 65 with four or five favourable factors had a life expectancy of 24.2 years, against 21.1 years for those with none or one, a difference of 3.1 years. They spent 10.8 per cent of those years with Alzheimer's dementia, against 19.3 per cent for the least favourable group, so the gain in dementia-free years was larger than the gain in total years.

Men showed a larger difference, 23.1 years against 17.4 years, and spent 6.1 per cent against 12.0 per cent of their remaining years with dementia. The sex difference is partly a matter of baseline life expectancy and partly a matter of how the factors distribute in the cohort. A difference of two and a half dementia-free years is large in population terms, and it is the kind of estimate that a public health campaign can use as its headline.

This study is observational, and the lifestyle score was measured once at baseline, so a person who changed their habits at 75 is classified by what they did at 65. Reverse causation is possible, since early cognitive decline reduces activity and diet quality before it is diagnosed. The model assumes that the transition rates between states are constant after age 65, which is a simplification that a cohort of this length cannot fully test.

A compression of morbidity is the outcome that health services want, because it reduces the number of years in which a person needs care. The result supports advice that is already standard and quantifies what it buys, which is the kind of number that makes prevention worth funding. The cohort is from one American city and is predominantly urban and older, so the estimates describe a population rather than a universal law of ageing.
