NVIDIA reported revenue of 39.3 billion dollars for the quarter ended in late January, up 78 per cent from a year earlier, and 130.5 billion for the fiscal year. Data centre sales accounted for 35.6 billion of the quarter. The numbers beat the consensus, and the share price moved less than one per cent the next day, which is what happens when a company has spent two years teaching the market to expect a beat. The company also guided to a gross margin near seventy-one per cent for the current quarter, which is the line analysts use to judge pricing power.

The guide is what the market trades. Management told investors that the next quarter would be constrained by supply rather than by demand, which has been the company's answer for six quarters. A supplier that cannot meet orders is a supplier whose revenue is limited by the number of wafers it can buy, and the constraint has moved from the accelerator die to the memory and the packaging. A wafer that takes four months to process cannot be ordered into a quarter, so the supply chain responds with a lag of a year.

Packaging is the current bottleneck. The most advanced accelerators are built with stacked memory and with a silicon interposer that connects them, and the capacity to assemble that package is concentrated in a small number of facilities. Building a packaging line takes longer than building a wafer line, because the process is new and the yields are learned on the tool. The packaging bottleneck has produced a secondary market, with capacity reserved months in advance and paid for whether it is used or not.

Customers are concentrating. A handful of hyperscale buyers account for a large share of data centre revenue, and each of them is designing its own accelerator as a hedge. A custom chip is expensive to develop and cheap to run at scale, and the largest buyers have the volume to justify it. Custom accelerators run the same models at lower cost per token, and the buyers who can afford one are exactly the buyers with the most volume.

Margins are the number that analysts watch. Gross margin has stayed above seventy per cent through the cycle, which is unusual for a hardware company, and it reflects both pricing power and the fact that the company sells a system rather than a component. A margin that high invites competition, and the competitors are both merchant silicon firms and the customers themselves. A merchant competitor needs both a competitive chip and a software stack, and the stack is the harder half.

The risk in the report is concentration of a different kind. A company that earns most of its revenue from one product line in one end market is a company whose valuation depends on a single assumption, which is that AI capital spending continues to grow. The report did not challenge that assumption, and it did not test it either. The report was a statement about the past, and the share price was a statement about the future.
