description: "A rail corridor between two customs territories has cut its transit time from 18 days to 11 by moving inspection to the origin, and the case shows how a border's cost is mostly paperwork rather than distance."
---

A freight train between two customs territories crosses one border and takes eighteen days from terminal to terminal, of which two are actual running time on the slowest section. A corridor authority reported this month that it has cut the total to eleven by moving inspection to the origin and accepting a digital guarantee.

Pre-clearance is the mechanism. A shipper files a declaration at loading, the origin customs authority inspects and seals the container, and the border post accepts the seal rather than reopening the box. That removes a stop that averaged 30 hours.

A digital guarantee replaced a cash deposit. A bank's guarantee used to be lodged per consignment, which took three days to arrange. The corridor's system lets a registered forwarder lodge a single guarantee against a rolling balance.

Transit time is now dominated by the railyard rather than by the border. The corridor's data show 4 days of terminal handling at each end and 3 days of running, and the remaining time is the interchange between two national networks with different gauges.

The economics are the argument for the rail. At 11 days, a container from an inland factory reaches a European distribution centre in the same week as an airfreight shipment at a quarter of the cost, and the corridor's volume has risen 40 per cent in a year.

Road haulage explains the last leg. A road truck crosses the same border in 4 hours because it is inspected at a different post with a different mandate, and the corridor's authority has asked for the two posts to be harmonized.

Risk is concentrated at one point. A single corridor with one border crossing is vulnerable to a strike or a systems failure, and the corridor's contingency plan is a diversion that adds five days. That vulnerability is the price of a fast route.

A border's delay is mostly paperwork and not inspection. A container that arrives with a declaration already filed and a guarantee already posted is released in hours, and one that arrives with neither waits for a broker. Pre-clearance moves the paperwork to the origin.

Rail's economics improve with reliability more than with speed. A shipper planning a 30-day inventory can plan around 11 days with a variance of one, and cannot plan around 18 with a variance of six. The second number is the one that sends cargo to a truck.

A corridor's capacity is the second constraint. A border post that clears 300 containers a day is a post whose throughput sets the corridor's ceiling, and pre-clearance raises the post's rate rather than the rail's. A corridor at its train-path limit gains nothing from a faster border.

Infrastructure is a set of procedures as much as a set of tracks. The distance did not change and the transit time fell by 40 per cent, because the paperwork moved to where the goods were loaded.
