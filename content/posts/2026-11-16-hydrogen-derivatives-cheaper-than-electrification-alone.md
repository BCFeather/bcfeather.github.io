title: "Hydrogen derivatives cut the cost of China's whole transition"

description: "A national energy model that links electricity, hydrogen and fuels made from it finds that turning curtailed wind and solar power into ammonia and methanol lowers the cost of China's whole transition by 10.7 trillion dollars."
---

China has built renewable capacity faster than it can use it. Wind and solar installations passed 1.8 billion kilowatts against a stated target of 3.6 billion by 2035, and curtailed power grows because the grid cannot absorb everything at the hours it arrives. Demand that electricity cannot reach is the harder half. Heavy road freight, ocean shipping and the process heat behind steel and cement stay on imported fuel, and imports covered 72.7 per cent of China's crude oil use in 2025. Most transition studies optimize the power system and treat the rest separately, so the question of how surplus electrons reach a furnace or a ship hull has stayed open.

Xinyu Chen and colleagues at Huazhong University of Science and Technology, working with Harvard and Shandong University, have put both halves into one optimization. Their paper was published in *Nature*. The model, HeD-MISSION, solves 29 provincial regions hour by hour, moves energy carriers along more than 46,000 pipeline, rail, road and sea routes, and lets each end-use sector choose its fuel process by process.

Measured against a pathway built on renewables and direct electrification, cumulative transition cost falls by US$10.7 trillion. Carbon abatement arrives at negative cost, 6.15 dollars a ton, which is the model's way of saying the switch pays for itself. Crude purchases no longer needed come to more than half of China's 2023 trade surplus. For the date, the team's own account of the work puts freedom from oil imports around 2045 in its best scenario.

Ammonia and methanol carry the saving. Hydrogen as a molecule is awkward: it wants purpose-built pipelines and dedicated storage, and fuel cells stay expensive to adopt at scale. Turning curtailed power into green ammonia or methanol instead puts energy onto routes and into burners that already exist, since both are bulk chemical trades by pipeline, rail and vessel, and engines and boilers that run on them are in service today. Model inputs include four decades of hourly weather, the operating characteristics of more than 3,000 thermal power units and tracks for some 78,000 vessels. That breadth is why the result reads as cost arithmetic rather than advocacy for hydrogen.

Worth naming are the assumptions underneath a figure of that size. The comparison runs between two modelled pathways, so the saving rides on technology cost curves that no counterfactual can test, and a negative abatement cost presumes the learning rates and the carbon accounting the model takes as given. Fleet renewal is slower than either. A tanker or a heavy truck delivered this year burns its design fuel until it is scrapped, so a 2045 crossover needs the replacement cycle to cooperate. Cost also prices only what carries a market, which leaves water at the electrolysers and nitrogen oxides from burning ammonia outside the arithmetic.

Two checks would settle what the result is worth. Reproduction by a group outside the team, working from parameters laid out across 52 supplementary figures and 160 tables, would show whether the saving survives another hand on the code. Then two observables: how much power China actually curtails once installed capacity nears the 2035 target, and how fast ammonia-capable tonnage and refuelling ports arrive. The planning habit that changes first is the pairing. A provincial wind and solar quota gains a companion requirement, a chemical sink sized for the surplus, and the team places the cheapest of those sinks in the three northern regions, where wind and sun are cheapest to build on.
