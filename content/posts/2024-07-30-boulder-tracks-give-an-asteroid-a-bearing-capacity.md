Soil mechanics has a vocabulary that planetary scientists have borrowed cautiously. Bearing capacity is the load a surface can support before it fails, and it has been estimated for the Moon from the tracks that rolling boulders leave behind, since a track records how far a boulder sank as it travelled. The method assumes that a rolling boulder leaves a record of the surface it crossed, which is true only where the regolith is loose.

A paper in *Nature Communications* on 30 July 2024 applies the method to an asteroid for the first time. The images come from DRACO, the camera on the DART spacecraft, which photographed the surface of (65,803) Didymos before it struck the smaller moonlet Dimorphos. DART's camera was built to navigate to a target, and its images turned out to be good enough for geotechnics.

Nine tracks were resolved well enough to measure. Their average width is 8.9 metres, with a spread of 1.5 metres, and their average length is 51.6 metres, with a spread of 13.3 metres. The dimensions are what a geotechnical calculation needs, since width records the boulder and length records the distance it rolled while sinking. Widths and lengths were measured from shadows as well as outlines, since the illumination was oblique and the relief shallow.

Gravity on Didymos is orders of magnitude below terrestrial, so the standard assumptions do not transfer unchanged. The authors give attention to cohesion, to the angle of repose of the regolith, and to whether a boulder's motion ends by stopping or by lifting off the surface into a ballistic arc. At such low gravity a boulder's weight is small, so cohesion rather than friction may dominate how far it sinks.

Direction is informative. The boulders travelled from high gravitational potential to low, which is what a loose surface would allow and what a consolidated one would resist, and the tracks therefore say something about the regolith's thickness as well as its strength. A track that ends abruptly may mark the point where the boulder left the ground rather than the point where it stopped.

The tracks also place a limit on trafficability. A surface that cannot bear a boulder cannot bear a lander leg, and the paper's numbers feed directly into the design of a spacecraft that might touch down rather than impact. Landing gear designed for the Moon would be overbuilt here, and gear designed for a comet might be too light.

The limits are inherent to the method. Nine tracks from a single encounter sample one region of one body, and the tracks may be recent or ancient, which matters if the surface has been reworked since. Even so, the measurement is a first. Bearing capacity has become a quantity that can be estimated for an asteroid from a photograph. A second encounter with a different asteroid would test whether the method generalizes, and none is planned yet.
