On the nineteenth of August doctors are thanked, and the day is marked with flowers, a banner in the lobby and a short ceremony that the hospital director attends. The people being thanked are mostly at work. A ward does not stop for a ceremony, and the ones who could attend are the ones whose shift happens to allow it, which is usually the consultants and rarely the residents.

Junior doctors' working day in a large hospital is documented in a way that most jobs are not. The hours are regulated on paper and the paper is frequently exceeded. The night shift is the part of the job that carries the most risk per hour, because the staffing is thin, the decisions are made with less information, and the person making them has been awake for sixteen hours or has just been woken from two hours of sleep.

The evidence on this is old and unambiguous. Performance on cognitive tasks declines with sleep deprivation in a way that resembles alcohol intoxication, and the effect is worse at four in the morning than at midnight. Studies of medical errors consistently find a higher rate at night, and the studies are hard to run and have been run anyway. The profession has known this for thirty years and has changed its schedules slowly.

Ignorance is not the reason the schedules have not changed faster. A hospital that reduces the length of a shift has to hire more doctors, and a hospital that hires more doctors spends money it does not have, and a health system that spends more on staffing spends it away from something else. The decisions are made by administrators who understand the evidence and are constrained by a budget, and the constraint is the actual policy.

What has changed in the last few years is the public conversation. Patients and families have become more willing to ask whether the person treating them has slept, and hospitals have become more willing to say. Some provinces have introduced caps on consecutive hours and there is a monitoring system for the worst cases, and the caps are enforced unevenly because enforcement requires staff who are not on a shift.

The doctors' day ceremony is a small thing. It is also the only scheduled moment in which a hospital says out loud that the work is hard, and the card that carries the message is printed by the hospital itself. The gratitude is genuine and it is directed at individuals who chose a profession that is difficult in ways the public only sees in a corridor at night, and whose families mostly hear about it afterwards.

What would change the night shift is not gratitude. It is a rota with more people on it, and that is a line in a budget that somebody has to sign. The flowers are a fine gesture and the schedule is the substance, and the distance between the two is the distance this holiday has not yet closed.
