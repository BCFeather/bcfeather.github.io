description: "Cave-dwelling fish have lost their eyes and their skin colour, and a study published this month shows they have also rewired the molecular clock that sets their metabolism. The clock still runs; it no longer listens to light."
---

Circadian clocks are built from a loop of genes that switch each other on and off with a period close to twenty-four hours. In most animals the loop is synchronized every day by light, which resets it through a photoreceptor in the retina or, in some tissues, in the skin. The clock is a timekeeping device with a daily correction, and the correction is what makes it useful.

A study of cave fish published this month describes what happens when the correction is removed for a million years. The fish, a species that has lived in dark caves long enough to lose its eyes and its pigmentation, retains a working molecular clock. The genes that make up the loop are still expressed, and their expression still oscillates. What has changed is the input: the light-sensing pathway that resets the clock in surface fish is degraded, and the clock's period has drifted.

Measurement is the interesting part. The researchers sampled gene expression from several tissues over two days in constant darkness, and compared the phase of the oscillation across tissues in cave fish and in their surface-dwelling relatives. In surface fish, the tissues are synchronized: liver, muscle and brain all peak at the same phase, because light resets them together. In cave fish, the tissues drift apart, with some peaking hours before others. The clock still runs in each tissue; the tissues no longer agree on what time it is.

That decoupling has consequences for metabolism. An animal whose liver and muscle are on different schedules digests food inefficiently, and the cave fish show the signature: a lower metabolic rate, a different pattern of fat storage, and a response to feeding that depends on when the food arrives rather than on an internal expectation. The authors argue that this is an adaptation rather than a defect, since a cave has no day, and a metabolism that does not commit to a schedule may be more flexible in an environment where food arrives unpredictably.

Whether the clock is a timekeeper at all, or a coordinator, is the broader question. The evidence here points to the second: the loop keeps running without input, but its value comes from keeping the body's tissues in step, and that value depends on a signal that tells them all the same time. Remove the signal and the loop persists as a set of local oscillators, each doing its own thing.

What the fish cannot answer is how much of the human clock's function depends on the same synchronization. Studies of shift workers find metabolic signatures that resemble the decoupling the cave fish show, and the fish offer a way to ask what that decoupling does over a lifetime rather than across a shift roster. The experiments that would answer the question are the ones the field is now designing.

The result also has a practical edge for anyone studying circadian rhythms in humans. Shift work and artificial light disrupt the same synchronization, and the cave fish provide a clean natural experiment in what happens when it is absent for a long time, without the confounding of a laboratory protocol. The fish are not a model of a night-shift worker. They are a model of what the clock does when nobody sets it.
