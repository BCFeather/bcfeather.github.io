Chushu, the fourteenth solar term, begins on 23 August this year. The name means the end of heat, and the calendar is careful to place it after the hottest part of the summer rather than at the moment the weather changes, which is a distinction that anybody walking outside at four in the afternoon will appreciate.

What actually changes in the last week of August is the light. Sunrise comes about twenty minutes later than it did at the start of the month, and the shadow of the buildings reaches the far side of the road earlier each evening. The temperature follows slowly, in the way that a heavy pot cools after the gas is turned off: the air above the city stays warm for weeks after the sun has begun to behave differently. Meteorologists call the result a continental air mass that has been heated from below for three months and has nowhere to go; everybody else calls it the tiger of autumn.

The market notices first. Peaches give way to grapes and then to pears, and the price of tomatoes falls because the ones from the higher fields arrive at the same time as the ones from the plain. Vendors start selling roasted chestnuts at the end of the month, which is a claim about autumn made with sugar rather than with a thermometer.

Households have their own calendar. The electric fan is still on the bedside table, but the winter quilts have been taken out of the cupboard and hung on the balcony for an afternoon, which is an act of optimism that the following week will usually justify. Blankets aired in the sun smell of soap and hot cotton, and that smell is the closest thing the season has to an announcement. School uniforms are bought in the last ten days of the month, and the shops that sell them are crowded in a way that the shops selling swimwear were crowded in June.

The agricultural version of the term is more precise. Chushu is when the grain fills and the heat begins to break, and the farmers around the basin use it to decide the last irrigation of the rice and the first picking of the early maize. A week of rain at this point is worth more than a week of sun, and a week of sun is worth more than a week of either in July. The rice does not care about the calendar; it cares about the difference between day and night temperatures, and that difference is finally widening.

Nothing in the city is obliged to follow the term, and the term is not obliged to follow the city. Both of them proceed at their own pace, one by the calendar and the other by the habits of people who have learned that the way to survive August is to behave as though it were still July, and to notice, one evening, that it is not.
