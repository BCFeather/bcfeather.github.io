Federal borrowing reached its statutory limit on 19 January at 31.381 trillion dollars. Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen wrote to the congressional leadership that afternoon to say the department had begun extraordinary measures, the set of accounting shifts that free room under the cap. Those measures buy time rather than money, and the letter put the exhaustion date somewhere in early June. The letter was copied to the four congressional leaders and published within an hour of being sent.

Extraordinary measures are familiar machinery. They suspend reinvestment in a pension fund for civil servants, redeem some securities early and stop issuing certain instruments. Each has been used in previous standoffs, and each has limits. The department can also buy a few weeks by delaying payments to some trust funds, which it did in 2011 and again in 2013. The pension fund involved holds about 600 billion dollars, and the maneuver is reversed as soon as the limit is raised.

Politics was already set before the limit was reached. Republicans in the House had passed a bill tying any increase to spending caps, and the White House said it would not negotiate over the full faith and credit of the country. Neither side had a plan for what happens if the talks run past the summer, and both said the other side would be blamed. The House bill passed on a party line and had no chance in the Senate, which made it a position rather than a proposal.

Markets treated the news as routine for the first weeks. Yields on one-month bills rose above the policy rate in February, a sign that some investors were avoiding paper that might be caught in a delay. Credit default swaps on American debt traded at levels last seen in 2011, when the country came within days of missing a payment. Yields on bills maturing in March rose 0.4 points above the overnight rate by the middle of February.

Counting the cost of a default is simple and unpleasant. About 60 per cent of federal spending goes out as checks each month, and the department would have to choose which to stop. Interest on the debt would still be paid first, because a missed coupon would damage the market for everything else. Analysts at three banks published estimates of the job losses that would follow a two-week delay, and all of them ran into the millions. The department also postponed an auction of state and local securities, a step it had taken in 2013.

Investors spent the spring watching the calendar. The limit itself is a number that Congress sets, and the only question was whether the two chambers could agree before the measures ran out. Every previous standoff ended with a deal, usually at the last hour, and the cost each time was a downgrade of the country's credit rating or a rise in borrowing costs that outlasted the episode. The downgrade of 2011 cost the Treasury about 1.3 billion dollars in extra interest over the following decade.
