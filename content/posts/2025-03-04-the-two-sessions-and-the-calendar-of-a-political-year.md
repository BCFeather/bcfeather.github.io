The advisory body opened its annual session on the fourth of March and the legislature follows on the fifth, and for the fortnight that follows the political year of the country has its one scheduled moment of public accounting. The procedure is fixed to the point of ritual, and the fixedness is the part that matters, because a calendar that everyone can plan around is a form of predictability that markets and ministries both use.

The sessions are large. Several thousand delegates and advisers gather in the capital, the hotels around the venue fill, and the city's traffic is managed for two weeks with a set of restrictions that residents have learned to expect. The delegates bring proposals that have been drafted over the previous year, many of them technical and local, a proposal about a bridge, a proposal about a subsidy formula, a proposal about the accreditation of a vocational school. The proposals that reach the agenda are the ones that a ministry has already found useful, and the rest are filed.

The public face of the fortnight is the work report, delivered on the morning of the fifth, which sets out the previous year's results and the coming year's targets. The report is long, it is read aloud in full, and the parts that the coverage extracts are the numbers, which is why the numbers are the parts that are drafted most carefully. The rest of the document, several dozen pages on industrial policy, on rural health, on the management of a river basin, is the part that the specialists read.

The advisory body does something different and the difference is easy to miss. Its members are not elected and its proposals are not binding, and its function is to put in front of the government a set of opinions from people who are not in the government, an entrepreneur, a professor, a doctor, a village teacher. Whether those opinions change anything is a question that cannot be answered from outside, and the institution's own account is that a proposal which is repeated across several years eventually finds a ministry willing to act on it.

What the fortnight produces, beyond the documents, is a vocabulary. The phrases that appear in the report become the phrases that appear in the provincial plans, the bank research notes and the local news for the rest of the year. A phrase like high-quality development that arrives in March will be in every county's work summary by December, and a phrase that is dropped from the report disappears from those documents within a year. The language is the transmission mechanism.

The sessions end in the middle of the month and the city returns to its ordinary traffic. The targets that were announced are then disaggregated into provincial quotas, the quotas into municipal ones, and the numbers that were read aloud in a hall in the capital become the numbers that a county official is measured against in the following January. The ceremony is the visible part, and the invisible part is the machinery that starts on the day it ends.
