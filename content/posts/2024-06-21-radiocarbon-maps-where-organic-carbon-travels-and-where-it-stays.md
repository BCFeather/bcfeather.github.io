Radiocarbon is a clock that runs on decay, and its abundance in organic matter records where carbon came from. A paper in Nature Geoscience on 21 June 2024 maps global patterns of organic carbon transfer using that clock. Mapping is what turns a tracer into a picture of a cycle. Transfer is the movement of carbon from one reservoir to another, and reservoirs differ in age. A tracer is only as good as its measurement, and measurement of radiocarbon has improved enough to make a global map possible. A map is an argument about pattern, and pattern is what a reader can carry away from a compilation.

Age is what radiocarbon reports, and reporting age is how a tracer separates a fresh input from a recycled one. Separation is the analytical gain. Accumulation is the other half, since carbon that arrives somewhere and stays is carbon that a sediment records. Sediments are the archive, and archives are what a global pattern has to be built from. Possible is recent, and recent methods are what explain the timing of a synthesis.

Building from archives is what makes a synthesis possible. Patterns differ between rivers and shelves, and difference is what the map shows. Rivers deliver fresh carbon, and shelves accumulate older material, and the contrast is the finding. Contrasts are what a reader can remember. Timing of a synthesis depends on coverage, and coverage is what a decade of surveys supplied.

Human influence appears as a change in the age of what is delivered, and change is what a tracer can detect. Detection of change is what turns a descriptive map into a contribution to policy. Policy interest is in burial, since burial removes carbon from the atmosphere for long timescales. Timescales are what make a reservoir useful, and useful is the word a carbon accountant uses. Supply is the invisible contribution, and invisible work is what makes a map worth publishing.

Accountants need numbers, and numbers come from surveys such as this. Surveys are expensive and unevenly distributed, and unevenness is the bias a global map carries. Carrying a bias is different from hiding it, and the paper states its coverage. Coverage statements are how a synthesis stays honest, and honesty is what makes a map reusable.

Reuse across studies is the fate of a good compilation, and compilation is what the paper really is. Really is the operative word, since the analysis is an assembly of measurements rather than an experiment. Experiments test hypotheses and compilations test coherence. Coherence is what a global cycle claim needs, and coherence is what the paper delivers.

Delivering coherence is a service to the field, and service is the kind of contribution that does not attract headlines. Headlines go to discoveries, and discoveries rest on compilations that no one cites individually. Individually unremarkable measurements become a map, and a map is what a modeller needs to bound a cycle.
