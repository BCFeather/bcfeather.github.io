Starship lifted off from Boca Chica in south Texas at 8.33 in the morning local time on 20 April and flew for just under four minutes. The vehicle is about 120 metres tall with the booster beneath and the ship above, and it carries 33 engines on the first stage, the most ever fired at once on a rocket. It cleared the tower, passed the point of maximum aerodynamic pressure, and then stopped behaving as the flight plan required. Two engines had failed before the vehicle cleared the pad, and the countdown had resumed after a hold caused by a frozen valve.

Video from the vehicle showed engines failing in the outer ring of the booster and the plume turning orange as the thrust dropped. Stage separation was never attempted, the stack began to tumble, and the autonomous flight termination system fired about 40 seconds later. SpaceX's commentator described the outcome on the live feed as a rapid unscheduled disassembly, and the debris fell into the Gulf of Mexico. The booster and the ship were both lost, and the tracking cameras followed the fragments for about a minute before they dropped below the horizon.

The launch mount took damage that was visible by the afternoon. The concrete beneath the pad was torn up and chunks of it were thrown hundreds of metres, and the blast left a crater under the steel plate that the company had installed instead of a flame trench. Photographs taken from a distance showed debris on the surrounding mudflats, and the company said that the pad would need a redesign before the next attempt.

Regulators grounded the vehicle the same week and required an investigation before another flight. The Federal Aviation Administration said that no damage or injury had been reported to the public and that the anomaly would be reviewed under the licensing conditions attached to the site. The review took several months, and the second flight in November ended in another loss, this time after stage separation.

The purpose of the flight was to test a design rather than to reach orbit, and by that standard the company counted the day as a success. The vehicle flew longer than on any previous test, several of the risks that had worried engineers did not appear, and the data returned was enough to justify changes. What the flight also showed is that the largest rocket in history is being developed by a company that can afford to lose one. Its valuation, its contracts and its launch schedule all assume that failures are learning events rather than verdicts.

Stakes extend past the company. NASA has contracted Starship to land astronauts on the Moon in the second half of the decade, and the vehicle is the only one that can carry the mass that a lunar base and a return journey require. The agency's schedule assumes a cadence that the rocket has not achieved, and each flight that ends early pushes the landing further into a calendar that has already slipped once.
