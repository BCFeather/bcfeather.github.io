Salman Rushdie was stabbed on Friday evening while he was preparing to speak at the Chautauqua Institution in western New York, and he was taken to hospital by helicopter. The attacker ran onto the stage and struck him about ten times in the neck and the abdomen before the audience and the staff held him down. The writer, who is seventy-five, was on a ventilator after surgery and the doctors said the damage included a severed nerve in his arm and injuries to his liver. A moderator who was introducing him was also hurt.

The institution is a summer school and arts centre that has hosted lectures for a hundred and fifty years, and the hall was full. The programme for the day was a discussion about America as a refuge for writers, and Rushdie had been invited because he has lived in the country since 2000. There was no security at the entrance to the amphitheatre, which is a feature of the place, and the police said the attacker had bought a ticket. The county sheriff described the response as a matter of seconds.

The attacker was identified as a twenty-four-year-old man from New Jersey, and he was charged with attempted murder. His motive has not been established, and the prosecutors said they were examining his history and his reading. The writer has lived under a threat since 1989, when the Iranian leader issued a religious decree calling for his death over the novel that was published the year before. The decree was never formally withdrawn, and the writer had spent nine years in hiding in the 1990s.

At the same time, the response from the literary world was immediate. Publishers, editors and novelists published statements within hours, and the head of the writers' association said that a room full of people had watched a man attacked for a book. The French president, who had given Rushdie an honour in 2016, said the attack was a crime against freedom of expression. The Iranian government did not comment, and the semi-official media in the country described the writer without sympathy.

Meanwhile, the security question is the one that the institutions will have to answer. The Chautauqua campus has been open for a century and a half, and its lectures are attended by people who expect to walk in without a bag check. Several writers said that they would not appear in public without protection again, and the organizers said they would review their arrangements. The attack also raised the question of how a threat that is thirty-three years old is handled by the police in a small town.

What the attack does is make an old argument new. The novel is still banned in some countries and the decree is still cited by people who have never read it, and the writer has spent his later career arguing that the answer to a bad idea is a better one. He was on stage to make that argument when he was attacked, and the audience that had come to hear it saw the opposite demonstrated. He is expected to survive, and the trial of the man who stabbed him will begin in a court that will have to decide what he intended.
