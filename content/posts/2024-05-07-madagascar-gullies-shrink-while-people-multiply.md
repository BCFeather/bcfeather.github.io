Madagascar's highlands are scarred by lavaka, the deep amphitheatre-shaped gullies that open in the laterite and cut back into hillsides. They are usually read as a record of human land use, a story in which grazing and clearing stripped the cover and the monsoon did the rest. The attribution is repeated in textbooks more often than it is tested, because testing it needs photographs taken before the satellites.

A study in *Science Advances* on 7 May 2024 supplies that test for one region. The authors assembled historical aerial photography and recent orthoimagery for the catchment of Lac Alaotra, the country's largest lake and wetland and its main rice-growing district, and classified lavaka by how actively they were retreating. The headline result runs against expectation. Since the middle of the twentieth century the proportion of highly active lavaka has decreased. Over the same interval the human population, the intensity of farming and the density of livestock have all risen sharply, in a trend the paper describes as exponential.

The classification rested on repeat photography. Each lavaka was scored for the freshness of its headwall and the exposure of bare laterite, and the same criteria were applied to the mid-century frames and the modern ones, so the two eras could be compared on a single scale. Ground checks in the field calibrated what the photographs showed.

That divergence does not prove that people are innocent of erosion, and the authors do not claim it does. Their argument is narrower and more useful. Background factors, seismicity among them, are plausible primary drivers of lavaka initiation, and the steep, fault-bounded hills around the lake are exactly where such forcing would express itself. Land use may add to the sediment budget without controlling the gully cycle.

Policy follows from the distinction. A programme that treats farmers as the cause of gullying will spend its money on fences and grazing bans, and will not address the process that actually opens the gullies. The authors note that the same misreading appears outside Madagascar, wherever a dramatic landform is attributed to the nearest human activity.

Sediment cores from the lake would test that hypothesis directly, and the paper points to them as the next measurement. Climate is a candidate as well. Rainfall intensity in the highlands has shifted over the same decades, and a storm pattern that delivers less water in fewer, harder events would change how a headwall retreats without changing how many people live below it.

Two limits are worth keeping in view. The proxy is the proportion of active gullies, not total sediment export, and a landscape can lose its active lavaka while still delivering soil through sheet erosion. Photographic coverage is also uneven in time, so the trend is reconstructed from snapshots rather than a continuous record. Even with those limits, the paper is a rare case of a long-held environmental narrative being checked against the archive that could refute it, and finding the narrative wanting.
