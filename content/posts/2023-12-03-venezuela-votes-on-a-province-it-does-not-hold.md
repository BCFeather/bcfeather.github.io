Venezuela held a consultative referendum on 3 December on the claim to Guayana Esequiba, a territory of 160,000 square kilometres that Guyana administers and that Venezuela has described as its own since the nineteenth century. The national electoral council reported more than ten million voters and a yes share above ninety-five per cent, on five questions that ranged from rejecting a boundary drawn in 1899 to supporting the creation of a new state in the disputed area. Turnout figures were disputed by independent observers, and the result carried no legal weight outside Venezuela.

The dispute is older than both republics. Britain and Venezuela signed an award in 1899 that fixed a boundary along the Cuyuni and Essequibo rivers, and Venezuela denounced it in 1962 as the product of a rigged arbitration. A 1966 agreement froze the quarrel while promising to negotiate, and the case has sat at the International Court of Justice since Guyana asked it to rule in 2018. The court accepted jurisdiction in 2020, heard preliminary objections in 2023, and Venezuela has said it will ignore any judgment that goes against it, which leaves the dispute in the unusual position of being litigated by one party.

Oil changed the temperature. ExxonMobil discovered commercial reserves offshore in 2015 and now produces several hundred thousand barrels a day from fields that Guyana licensed, and Guyana's economy has grown faster than any other in the hemisphere for three years running. Venezuela's government, meanwhile, has been under sanctions since 2019 and has watched a neighbour with a fraction of its population become an oil exporter again.

The referendum campaign was run by the government and the armed forces, with billboards in Caracas and a military parade in the border state of Bolivar. Guyana's president called the vote a step towards annexation and put the armed forces on alert, while Brazil moved armoured vehicles and troops to its own border with the disputed area and told both governments that it would not accept a military solution. The United States announced joint exercises with Guyana and repeated its recognition of the 1899 boundary.

Latin American governments split along familiar lines. Colombia and Chile urged negotiation through the court, Brazil offered to mediate, and the organizations that group the region produced statements vague enough to be signed by everyone. Guyana's foreign ministry, which is small and has no diplomatic weight of its own, spent the week asking larger states to say the word annexation out loud, and got the word from Britain and from the European Union.

Nothing about the vote changed the ground under either capital. The territory remains administered by Georgetown, the court calendar remains in place, and the referendum's practical effect has been to convert a legal file into a domestic political asset for a government facing an election in 2024. What it also did was make a border that had been quiet for fifty years into a place where two armies now look at each other across a river.
