A glacier is not a solid block. It sits on a bed of rock and till, and between the two runs a network of channels that carries meltwater from the surface to the margin. The water matters because it lubricates the base: more water at high pressure means less friction, and less friction means the ice slides faster. That is the mechanism behind the summer speed-up that most glaciers show, and it is the reason the plumbing is worth mapping.

Mapping it has usually meant drilling. A borehole through a hundred metres of ice can be instrumented to measure water pressure at the bed, and a few dozen boreholes give a picture of a small area. A study published this month takes a different approach: it listens to the ground.

The method uses ambient seismic noise, the constant low-level vibration produced by wind, waves and human activity. When that noise passes through a subglacial channel filled with water, the water resonates and the ground above it vibrates at a characteristic frequency. A seismometer on the ice surface records the spectrum, and the peaks in it correspond to channels of different sizes and depths. By deploying an array of sensors across the glacier and analysing the spectra, the team reconstructed the channel network without drilling.

The results are coarse, with a resolution of tens of metres, and that is enough to answer the questions that matter. The study found that the channels migrate during the melt season, moving from a distributed sheet at the start of summer to a few efficient conduits by August, which is the same transition that borehole studies have found at individual points. The advantage of the seismic method is coverage: it can map a whole glacier in a season rather than a dozen points in a decade.

The physics of the speed-up is where the result connects to sea level. As the conduits become efficient, the water drains quickly, the pressure at the bed falls and the glacier slows down. A glacier with an inefficient plumbing system holds water at high pressure and slides fast throughout the summer. Which regime a glacier is in depends on its bed geometry and its melt rate, and both are changing as the climate warms.

The method also has an obvious application to hazards. A glacier that drains suddenly can release a flood, and the seismic signature of a filling or emptying cavity is detectable weeks before the flood arrives. Several mountain regions have warning systems built on borehole pressure sensors, which are expensive and sparse. A seismic array is cheaper and wider, and it can be deployed for a season.

The paper is careful about its limits: the method needs a noise source, so it works better where there are waves or wind than in a dead calm, and it cannot see channels that are not water-filled. That leaves a gap in winter, when the plumbing is empty, and the authors suggest combining the method with radar surveys. A tool that sees an invisible network for a season is a useful addition, and it is the sort of addition that changes what a field campaign can ask.
