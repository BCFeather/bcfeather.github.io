Kamala Harris accepted the Democratic nomination on 22 August in Chicago, in a speech that presented her career as a prosecutor as the qualification for the office and framed the election as a choice about freedom. The speech was the largest audience she had addressed as a candidate. She had been the candidate for eight weeks and had not yet sat for a long interview, which the campaign promised to arrange.

Biography was the argument. A career that began in a prosecutor's office and moved through a state attorney general's office and the Senate was presented as a record of taking on institutions, and the contrast with an opponent who is a businessman was implicit throughout. A biography that is a contrast is a biography that does not need a policy. The prosecutor's record includes cases that the party's left wing dislikes, and the speech presented them as evidence of independence rather than as a liability.

Policy content was thin by design. The campaign had published an economic plan in the previous week and used the speech to repeat its themes rather than to add detail, because a convention speech is a summary rather than a programme. A summary that is repeated is a summary that voters can remember, which is the point. The economic plan proposed subsidies for first-time buyers and a ban on price gouging, and neither was described in detail on the night.

Foreign policy passage was the most concrete. The speech defended the alliance system and the support for Ukraine, and it described the opponent's approach as a gift to the countries that the party's coalition had spent decades deterring. A passage about alliances is a passage aimed at the donors and the diplomats rather than the swing voter. The passage was aimed at donors alarmed by the previous candidate's position on the war, and their money returned within a week.

Crowd was the measure of the change. A convention that had been organized around a president who had been in office for four years ended with a nominee who had been in the Senate for three, and the delegates' enthusiasm was for the newness as much as for the programme. A party that has replaced its candidate is a party that is also replacing its generation. The delegates were younger and more diverse than the ones elected in the spring, which is the physical evidence of the transfer.

What the speech shows is that a nomination can be won without a primary and still be presented as a mandate. The mandate is the thing the campaign now has to earn in the states that decide the result. The mandate is the thing the campaign now has to earn, and the states that decide the result have not yet been asked. The campaign's next task is to define the opponent before he defines the nominee, and the advertising that will do it has been reserved for September.
