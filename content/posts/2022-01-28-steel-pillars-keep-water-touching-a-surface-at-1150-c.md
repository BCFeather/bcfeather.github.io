Drop water onto a hot plate and, past a certain temperature, the drop stops boiling rapidly and starts skittering. A vapour layer separates liquid from solid, heat transfer collapses, and the surface stops cooling. The effect is named for Leidenfrost, and in metallurgy and in reactor safety it is a practical ceiling: the hotter the surface, the worse water cools it. In a loss-of-coolant accident the effect can strand heat in a fuel rod just when removal matters most, and in die casting it sets the cycle time.

Textured surfaces and surface channels had raised the Leidenfrost point, but only at the cost of cooling performance, because the features that keep liquid in contact also trap vapour. A group in Hong Kong and Shenzhen designed around that trade-off with what they call a structured thermal armour, reported in *Nature*, 26 January 2022.

The armour combines three elements. Steel pillars act as thermal bridges that conduct heat to the liquid, an embedded insulating membrane wicks and spreads water across the surface, and U-shaped channels evacuate the vapour. The contrasting thermal and geometric properties of the three materials turn an otherwise uniform temperature profile into a non-uniform one that keeps liquid in contact. Pillars conduct, the membrane spreads and channels vent, so each function is assigned to a material chosen for it rather than to a single surface asked to do everything.

Measured performance put the Leidenfrost point at 1,150 °C, which is 600 °C above the previous best. Cooling was preserved across the range, and the authors describe the limit as the melting point of the armour rather than a failure of the design. The structure can be made flexible and attached to substrates that cannot themselves be patterned. Flexibility also means the armour can wrap a pipe, which is the geometry most industrial cooling actually uses.

The mechanism is a division of labour at the scale of a few hundred micrometres. Pillars supply a path for heat, the membrane supplies a path for liquid, and channels supply a path for vapour, so no single feature has to serve two incompatible functions. That separation is what earlier texturing lacked.

A laboratory demonstration of sustained cooling is not yet a component. The experiments used water at atmospheric pressure, the armour was tested on a heated stage rather than in a real heat exchanger, and the durability of the bond between steel, insulator and channel under thermal cycling is unmeasured. Molten salts and cryogenic fluids, which are the working fluids of interest in concentrated solar and in superconductors, have different wetting behaviour.

Applications follow from the temperature: efficient water cooling at 1,000 °C would change the design of high-temperature reactors, of die-casting tools and of thermal storage. The immediate next step is a test in a flow loop, where the armour must survive pressure, fouling and thousands of cycles. Fouling is the specific worry, since a membrane that wicks water will also wick salts, and the pores that spread liquid can clog.
