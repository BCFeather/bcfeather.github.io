title: "An earthquake off Fukushima shakes the shinkansen off its tracks"
date: "2022-03-16T23:40:00+08:00"

description: "A minute of shaking at 11.36 at night, a train that came off the rails without killing anyone aboard, and a warning about a wave that arrived smaller than feared."
---

At 11.36 on the night of 16 March an earthquake of magnitude 7.4 struck about 60 kilometres off the coast of Fukushima, at a depth of 57 kilometres, and the shaking reached the upper level of the Japanese scale in parts of Miyagi, Fukushima and Iwate. The Japan Meteorological Agency had first published the magnitude as 7.3 and raised it after reviewing the records, and it described the event as an aftershock of the earthquake of 2011. More than 3,000 people in the region felt the strongest shaking, according to the intensity maps that the agency published.

Casualties were few for an event of that size. One man in Soma, in Fukushima prefecture, was killed when furniture fell on him in his home, and 92 people were injured, most of them by falling objects or in the confusion after the lights went out. The count of the dead rose to four in the following days as bodies were found in collapsed buildings, and the total stayed in single figures, which the agency attributed to the building code that had been rewritten after 2011.

A Tohoku shinkansen train came off the tracks near Shiroishi, between Fukushima and Sendai, and the photographs of the carriages leaning into the embankment became the image of the night. The train was carrying 78 passengers, and none of them was killed, because the automatic braking system had slowed it when the first tremors reached the track. The line was closed for the following days, and the section took weeks to repair.

Electricity failed across a wide area. More than 2 million households lost power in Tokyo and in the northern prefectures, the bullet trains stopped, and the elevators in tall buildings locked. The meteorological agency issued a tsunami advisory for a wave of up to one metre along the coast of Fukushima and Miyagi, ordered residents in low-lying areas to move to higher ground, and lifted the advisory three hours later when the sea rose by less than half of that.

The nuclear plants on the coast reported no release of radiation. The operator of Fukushima Daiichi said the cooling systems for the spent fuel pools had stopped briefly and been restarted, and the plant at Onagawa reported a fire in a turbine building that was put out. The regulator asked for inspections of all the plants in the region, and the reports that came back described damage to equipment rather than to containment.

What the night tested was the machinery that Japan had built after 2011: the seawalls, the early warning system that stops the trains, and the drills that send people to higher ground. The machinery worked, and the warning about the wave was issued for a metre that arrived at half that, which is the normal ratio between a forecast and a flood. The country spent the following week repairing a railway and counting four dead, and the argument about whether the seawalls are high enough went on as it had since the previous disaster.
