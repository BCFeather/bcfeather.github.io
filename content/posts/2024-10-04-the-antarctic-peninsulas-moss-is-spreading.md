The Antarctic Peninsula has warmed faster than most of the continent, and the cryospheric response is well documented in retreating glaciers and shrinking sea ice. A paper in *Nature Geoscience* on 4 October 2024 measures the response of the land itself, which had not been quantified at the scale of the peninsula. A peninsula that greens is one whose ice retreat has exposed ground, so the vegetation record and the cryospheric record describe the same process from two sides.

The method is an archive rather than a field campaign. Landsat imagery from 1986 to 2021 was processed in a cloud workflow that classifies vegetated ground, and the classification was used to track the area of likely vegetation cover across the peninsula. Cloud processing is what makes a thirty-five year archive tractable, since a manual classification of that many scenes would be neither repeatable nor affordable.

For the same reason, the numbers are small in absolute terms and large in relative ones. Vegetation covered 0.863 square kilometres in 1986 and 11.947 square kilometres in 2021, which is a fourteen-fold increase over thirty-five years on a continent where plants grow slowly. Fourteen-fold growth from a small base is the kind of number that looks dramatic and means little without the absolute areas beside it.

In the clinic, the rate is the part that matters. Between 2016 and 2021 the increase ran at 0.424 square kilometres per year, against 0.317 square kilometres per year across the whole period, so the expansion is accelerating rather than proceeding at a constant pace. The acceleration is the substantive finding, because a linear trend can be explained by recovery from a past disturbance and a rising one cannot.

Moss is what the classification is detecting. The peninsula's terrestrial flora is dominated by mosses and lichens, and a moss bank that expands is one that has found more growing season, more liquid water and less scouring by ice. Moss expands by growing at its margins, so the mapped area is a proxy for biomass rather than a direct measurement of it.

On the modelling side, the trend is local evidence for a pattern that is being reported across cold-climate ecosystems, where warming lengthens the season in which photosynthesis is possible and where a small change in temperature can move a site across a threshold. A threshold effect is plausible here, since a moss bank that gains a few weeks of liquid water can grow where it previously could not survive.

Attribution is the weak point. A single archive cannot separate the effect of temperature from the effect of changing precipitation or of retreating ice that exposes new ground, and the classification itself has errors that are hard to quantify on a surface this heterogeneous. The consequence for conservation is that a protected area defined by its ice may need to be redefined by its plants, since a peninsula that greens is one whose terrestrial ecology is no longer marginal. Ground surveys would be the check, and the peninsula has few of them, which is why satellite archives carry so much of the argument.
