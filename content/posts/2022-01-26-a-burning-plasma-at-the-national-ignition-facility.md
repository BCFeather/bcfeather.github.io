Fusion research has lived with a specific gap for decades. Machines can heat a plasma until it fuses, but a burning plasma, one in which the fusion reactions supply most of the heating, had not been produced in the laboratory. Without that state there is no way to study the physics that governs a self-heating fuel, and no path to a reactor that returns more energy than it consumes.

The National Ignition Facility approached the condition from the direction of inertial confinement. Its 192 laser beams deliver up to 1.9 megajoules in pulses with peak powers up to 500 terawatts, and the energy is converted to X-rays inside a radiation cavity that then ablates and implodes a fuel capsule. Indirect drive is used because illuminating the capsule directly seeds hydrodynamic instabilities that destroy the symmetry of the implosion. Two implosion designs, both scaling the capsule upward in size, were tested in experiments reported in *Nature*, 26 January 2022.

The burning-plasma criterion is an energy comparison rather than a temperature. Fusion self-heating had to exceed the mechanical work injected into the implosion by the imploding shell, and several metrics built on that comparison were satisfied. A subset of shots appear to have crossed the static self-heating boundary, where fusion heating surpasses the losses from radiation and conduction, which is the threshold that separates a plasma that burns from one that merely reacts. Two implosion concepts were tested and both satisfied the criteria, which reduces the chance that the result is an artefact of a single design. Energy reaches the capsule only after the hohlraum converts the laser pulse to X-rays, and the conversion is itself a lossy step that the balance accounts for.

Diagnostics carry the claim. The yield, the ion temperature and the shape of the hot spot are inferred from X-ray and neutron emission measured with instruments calibrated against known sources, and the energy balance is reconstructed from the laser pulse and the implosion trajectory. The uncertainty in each term is large enough that the paper reports the criteria as satisfied across a family of shots rather than as a single number.

Inertial fusion has its own arithmetic, and it is not the arithmetic of a power station. A target that burns is destroyed, the repetition rate of the driver is far below what a reactor needs, and the laser energy quoted is delivered to the hohlraum rather than to the grid. The result establishes a physics regime, and the engineering questions of gain, repetition and cost remain untouched.

What the state enables is experiment rather than promise: alpha-particle transport, burn propagation and the stability of a self-heating hot spot can now be measured instead of simulated. Whether those measurements lead to a reactor depends on driver efficiency that no laser has yet demonstrated. The next experiments vary capsule scale and pulse shape to see which term of the energy balance moves first.
