Voters in Solomon Islands went to the polls on the seventeenth of April in a general election for fifty seats in a single national parliament. It was the first election since the government signed a security agreement with China in 2022, and it was watched by Australia, New Zealand, the United States and China as a test of influence in the Pacific. Turnout was high, and counting took more than a week because results had to be moved from remote islands by boat and by air.

Political system that produced the result is built on local alliances rather than on national parties. Most candidates run with their own organizations or as independents, and the parties that exist are small and fluid. After the count, no group had anything like a majority, and the largest blocs were the party of the outgoing prime minister and a group of independents, which meant that the next government would be decided in negotiations rather than at the ballot.

Outgoing prime minister, Manasseh Sogavare, had governed since 2019 and had made the relationship with China the centre of his foreign policy. He switched recognition from Taiwan to Beijing in 2019, signed the security pact in 2022 and defended the arrangement against criticism from the opposition and from Australia. He lost his own seat in the election, which removed the most prominent advocate of the Chinese relationship from the parliament.

Foreign policy was the subject that outside governments cared about. Australia and the United States had increased their aid and their diplomatic presence since 2022, and the American government reopened an embassy in Honiara in 2023 for the first time in thirty years. Australia funded a police training programme and an undersea cable, and both governments warned that a Chinese naval presence in the islands would change the strategic balance of the region.

Concerns raised during the campaign were about domestic services as much as about strategy. Voters in the provinces said the roads, clinics and schools that the government had promised were not delivered, and that the Chinese investment that had been announced had produced few jobs. Candidates from both camps promised to improve health care and to fix the inter-island shipping that most communities depend on.

What followed from the seventeenth of April is a government that will be formed by numbers in the parliament, and the choice of prime minister will be read in Canberra, Washington and Beijing as a signal. Jeremiah Manele, a former foreign minister from the outgoing party, was elected on the second of May with the support of independents and of small parties, and he said he would keep the relationship with China while improving ties with traditional partners. What the election showed was that voters were willing to remove a leader who had become the symbol of a foreign policy and that the country's alignment will depend on whoever can assemble a majority. The commission reported a turnout of about seventy per cent, with counting completed in most constituencies within four days.
