The bamboo shoot and the toon arrive in the market in the second half of February at a price that falls by half within three weeks. The first crates that reach a city wholesaler are the most expensive vegetables of the year, and the same crate in the middle of March costs a third of what it cost in February.

The reason is the shape of the supply. A bamboo shoot is cut from a grove that produces for about eight weeks, and the first shoots that emerge are few and the harvest is small. As the season advances the grove produces more, the growers in the lower valleys begin to cut, and the volume that reaches the market doubles and then triples. The price follows the volume down, and the fall is steep because the early supply is so thin.

The transport is the other variable. A shoot begins to lose its quality within a day of the cut, and the fibre that makes it crisp becomes woody as it ages. The trade therefore moves the early crop by air and the later crop by road, and the cost of the air freight is a large share of the price of the first crate.

The toon is a smaller market with a sharper peak. The young leaves of the tree are edible for about two weeks, they are bitter unless they are blanched, and the price of a bundle in the first days is high enough that the market sells it by the hundred grams. The season is short enough that the restaurants build a menu around it and withdraw the menu when it ends.

The buyer's strategy is a matter of patience. The vegetable that is bought in the third week of March is as good as the one bought in February and it costs much less, and the cook who waits is rewarded for the wait. The exception is the household that wants the first taste of the year, and the premium that it pays is the price of being early.

The seasonal vegetable also carries a rural economy. The groves that produce the shoots are managed by households that own a small plot, the harvest is done by hand, and the income of the season is a meaningful share of the year for the families in the hills. The market that pays a high price for the first crate is paying those households, and the fall in the price in March is the fall in their income.

What the vegetable shows is that a price can be a calendar. The same object costs three different amounts in three weeks, and the difference is the supply, the freight and the patience of the buyer. The market in February is a market for the impatient, and the market in March is a market for everyone else. The gap between the two prices is the value that a household places on being first, and it is a small demonstration of how a season is priced at every stage of its supply.
