Alexander Stubb won Finland's presidential election on the eleventh of February, taking 51.6 per cent of the vote against 48.4 per cent for Pekka Haavisto in a second round that was closer than the first round had suggested. Turnout was about seventy per cent, in a country where voting is not compulsory and where eighty per cent is unusual without being rare. Stubb, a former prime minister and foreign minister who stood for the National Coalition Party, takes office on the first of March for a six-year term. Concession came from Haavisto, who ran as an independent backed by the Greens, within an hour of the count being published.

Campaign turned on the presidency's foreign and security role rather than on domestic questions. Finland abandoned three decades of military non-alignment and joined NATO in April 2023, and the new president will be the first to serve in the alliance. Membership was supported by both candidates, so the argument was about tone. Stubb campaigned as an enthusiastic ally who would anchor Finland in European and transatlantic structures, and Haavisto campaigned as a careful diplomat who had spent years in the foreign ministry and who had negotiated with Russia as foreign minister. That difference was visible in the debates and decisive in the eastern districts, where the vote split over how quickly to move.

First round on the twenty-eighth of January had nine candidates and produced a clear result: Stubb took 27.2 per cent, Haavisto 25.8, and the rest of the field was far behind. Runoff campaign was therefore a contest between two Foreign Ministry professionals with overlapping careers, and it became a referendum on style. Supporters of Stubb described a candidate who could speak to the alliance's capitals and who had spent a decade in Brussels. Supporters of Haavisto described a diplomat who had actually resolved things. Both claims are true, and the vote decided which one the country preferred at this moment.

Gap between the two rounds in turnout was small, and the geographical pattern was the familiar Finnish one. Stubb carried the cities and the Swedish-speaking coastal districts, and Haavisto carried parts of the east and the north. A margin of about seventy thousand votes came from the capital region and from voters who had supported the centre-right in the first round and moved to the more international candidate in the second. Apathy that has hurt Finnish turnout in other elections was not visible this time, and the campaign was conducted almost entirely on the issues the office actually handles.

Presidency in Finland has less domestic power than the title suggests and more foreign weight than it had a decade ago. The office leads on foreign policy in coordination with the government, represents the country in NATO and has traditionally carried the relationship with Russia. Stubb inherits a border that was closed in stages during 2023 and 2024, a security environment that has changed more than at any time since 1945, and a public still adjusting to being inside the alliance rather than beside it.

Transition itself is short. Presidents take office in March, and the outgoing president, Sauli Niinisto, leaves after two terms in which the country's foreign policy was rebuilt around the alliance. Stubb's first task will be to establish his relationship with the government of Petteri Orpo, which retains the domestic agenda, and his second to represent Finland at the Washington summit in July. Neither requires a majority in parliament. Both require the personal authority that the office confers and that the campaign was fought over.
