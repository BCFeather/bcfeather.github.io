Retinal degeneration destroys photoreceptors in an order that a patient experiences as a shrinking field, and no animal model reproduces the human sequence. A tissue-engineering group reported this month a retina-on-a-chip built from human stem cells that survived 120 days and responded to light.

The construct is a layered organoid on a membrane, with a channel beneath for nutrients and a transparent window above for illumination. The layers self-organized from induced pluripotent cells into a pigment epithelium, a photoreceptor layer and a neural retina. One hundred and twenty days is the achievement this construct reports. Earlier organoids lost their outer segments after 40 days because the inner layers consumed the nutrients and the outer ones starved. The membrane's perfusion channel delivers nutrients from below.

The function is tested by the electrical response. A patch electrode on a ganglion cell recorded a spike train when the construct was illuminated with a 500-millisecond pulse, and the response's latency matched a photoreceptor's. Outer segments are the light-sensitive part, and a construct that keeps them for four months keeps the part a drug would act on.

Disease modeling is the application. The team introduced a mutation that causes retinitis pigmentosa and observed the degeneration's sequence over 60 days, which showed the pigment epithelium failing before the photoreceptors. Animal models are the standard this construct is measured against. A mouse's retina has a different layer thickness and a different oxygen supply, and a drug that rescues a mouse's photoreceptors has failed in patients four times. A human construct is a better predictor. A chip's cost per experiment is a tenth of an animal study's, which is the reason a laboratory would adopt it even before a regulator accepts it.

The limit is vascularization. A 120-day construct has no blood vessels, and the retina's outer layer in a person sits 200 microns from a capillary. A longer-lived construct needs a vasculature, and one group has begun perfusing with endothelial cells. Survival is the first milestone and function is the second. A photoreceptor that lives but does not respond to light is a cell culture. The construct's electrical response to a light pulse is what makes it a model of a retina rather than of a tissue.

Vascularization is the limit. A retina's outer layers sit within 100 micrometres of a capillary, and a chip's channel delivers nutrients by diffusion. A layer thicker than that dies in the center, which is why the construct is a patch and not a whole retina. A model's value is the experiment it makes possible. A chip that keeps a human retina alive for four months lets a laboratory test a drug's retinal toxicity on human tissue rather than on a rodent's, and the difference is the reason a pharmaceutical company would pay for the platform.

A model earns its place in the parts of a disease it reproduces. A dish that keeps human photoreceptors alive long enough to degenerate is useful precisely because the thing being studied is the degeneration and not the dish.
