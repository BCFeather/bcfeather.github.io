---

Output grew at an annual rate of 2.1 per cent in the second quarter, the Bureau of Economic Analysis said in its third estimate on 28 September, unchanged from the previous reading. The revision moved the composition rather than the total. The first quarter grew at 2.2 per cent, and the two together are the strongest half year since 2021, which the economists had not expected when the rates began to rise. The figure was in line with the forecast, and the revision left the annual average for the first half at about 2.2 per cent.

Investment was revised up. The spending on the equipment and the software rose by about 7 per cent, and the structures by about 16 as the factories for the chips and the batteries were built. A factory that is built is a demand that arrives before the output does. The spending on the computer equipment rose by about 12 per cent, which the analysts connect to the demand from the AI developers for the data centers.

Inventories were revised down. The firms added less to their stocks than the earlier estimate showed, which means the final sales were stronger than the headline, and the economists watch that line because the stocks cannot keep adding. A warehouse that is not filled is a demand that is real. The real final sales to the domestic buyers rose at about 2.4 per cent, a measure that the economists prefer because it strips out the inventories and the trade.

Consumer spending grew by about 0.8 per cent, the weakest of the year, and the services did the work while the goods fell. A household that buys the services and not the goods is a household that has shifted the budget as the prices changed. The goods spending fell by about 0.5 per cent as the prices of the durable goods rose, and the restaurants and the travel took the share that the goods lost. The spending on the financial services and the insurance rose as the premiums were raised, which is a price that the household pays rather than a service it chooses.

Trade was a drag. The imports rose faster than the exports, and the deficit subtracted about half a point from the growth, which is the arithmetic of a strong dollar and a slow world. A deficit that widens is a growth that depends on the domestic demand. The imports rose by about 7 per cent and the exports by about 1, and the deficit has been a drag for four quarters, which is the pattern of a strong currency.

What the report shows is that the economy of the spring was driven by the business investment and the consumer, and the two are the parts that the rates are meant to slow. A growth that is driven by the investment is a growth that adds capacity, and the capacity is what decides whether the slowdown is a recession.
