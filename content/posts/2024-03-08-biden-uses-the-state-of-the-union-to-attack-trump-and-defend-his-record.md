President Joe Biden delivered his third State of the Union address to a joint session of Congress on the seventh of March, and it was a campaign speech in the setting of a constitutional report. He spoke for more than an hour, named Donald Trump thirteen times, and described the election as a choice between a country that looks forward and one that looks back. Speech was watched by about thirty-two million people, a smaller audience than in previous years.

Substance of the address was a list of proposals that had little chance of passing the House. He asked Congress to restore the expanded child tax credit, to raise the minimum wage, to codify the right to abortion, to pass a border bill that the Senate had negotiated and that Republicans had rejected at Trump's request, and to increase taxes on corporations and on the wealthiest households. Each proposal was framed as a contrast with what he said the opposition wanted to do.

Age was the question the speech was designed to answer. Biden's performance was loud and fast, and he challenged Republicans to a debate on entitlements, an offer that drew applause from his own side and jeers from the other. He also addressed the report of the special counsel who had investigated his handling of classified documents, which described him as a well-meaning elderly man with a poor memory, and he said the description was wrong and that he was angry about it.

Immigration was the issue on which the two parties came closest to a physical confrontation. A Republican congresswoman from Georgia, Marjorie Taylor Greene, interrupted him and refused to stop, and Biden picked up a pin she had offered him and held it while he talked about a nursing student who had been killed. Exchange was the most memorable moment of the night, and it was the one that both campaigns used in advertising the following day.

Reaction was split along predictable lines. Democratic commentators said the speech had done what the campaign needed and had put the age question to rest for a week. Republican commentators said the volume and the interruptions were evidence of a president who had lost command of the chamber, and they pointed to the parts of the address in which he confused names and dates. Independent assessments said the speech was effective as political communication and did little to change the structure of the race.

Republican answer to the speech was delivered by Senator Katie Britt of Alabama, who spoke from her kitchen table in a style that was widely mocked and who drew criticism for a passage about a trafficking victim that described an event from a different administration. Party leaders defended her performance and said the setting was intended to present a younger generation of the party. Response was watched by far fewer people than the address itself, which has been true of every opposition reply in recent years.

Consequence of the address was limited and specific. It did not produce legislation, since the House was controlled by the opposition and the Senate was evenly divided. It did set the themes for the campaign, which were abortion, democracy and the economy, and it gave the incumbent a moment in which he could speak to an audience larger than any he would reach again before the autumn. Whether it answered the doubts about his age was a question that the debate in June answered differently.
