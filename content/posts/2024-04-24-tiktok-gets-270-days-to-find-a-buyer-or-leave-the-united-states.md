A law signed on 24 April gives the Chinese company that owns TikTok 270 days to sell the application or see it removed from the American market. The president can extend the deadline by up to ninety days if he judges that a sale is in progress, and the company has said it will challenge the statute in court. The deadline falls after the election, which means the enforcement will be decided by an administration that has not yet been formed.

Ownership is what the law targets rather than the content. The concern is that a foreign owner could be compelled to hand over data on American users or to shape what they see, and a sale to a domestic buyer is the remedy that the statute prescribes. A rule about who owns an application is a rule that is hard to enforce after the application is already installed. A sale would also have to satisfy the regulators in the country where the seller is based, and that government has said it will block a forced divestiture.

A sale is harder than it sounds. The application is worth tens of billions of dollars, the buyer would have to be acceptable to the seller's government as well as to the American one, and the code that recommends videos depends on infrastructure and data that cannot simply be copied. An asset whose value rests on an algorithm and a user graph is an asset that is difficult to move across a border. The recommendation system is trained on the behaviour of the users themselves, and a buyer that starts again from the existing data would need the users to stay while the model is rebuilt.

Court challenge is the next step. Company argues that the statute singles out one firm and one owner, which the constitution forbids, and the government argues that the risk is national security rather than speech. A case about a single company is a case that will define how far the state can reach into a platform. The courts have generally allowed the state to restrict foreign ownership of communications infrastructure, and they have generally been reluctant to accept a rule that names a single firm.

Users are the constituency nobody counted. More than a hundred million Americans use the application, and a large share of them earn money from it, which is why the proposal has produced a lobbying campaign that is unusual for a foreign-owned firm. A ban that removes an audience is a ban that removes a livelihood. The creators who earn from the platform have been the most effective lobbyists, because their argument is about income rather than about security, and it is easier for a legislator to answer.

What the law shows is that the ownership of a platform has become a matter of statecraft, and the deadline it sets is a clock that both the company and the government are now watching. A law that is written for one company is a law that the next company will read as a precedent, and the drafting of the statute is now part of the case.
