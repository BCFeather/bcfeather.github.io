title: "Freight rates fall while the route grows 30 per cent longer"

description: "Container rates on the Asia-Europe route have fallen back to their pre-diversion level while the distance sailed is still 30 per cent longer, and the gap between those two facts is the story of a market that has absorbed a detour."
---

A shipping route that avoids a choke point adds 30 per cent to the distance and 12 days to the voyage, and the arithmetic says rates should rise. They did, for a year, and then they fell back to roughly the level before the diversion, which is the interesting part of the market's behavior.

Capacity absorbed the detour. The world fleet grew 9 per cent in the same period, and slow steaming was reversed, which released 6 per cent of effective capacity. The two together covered the distance's demand for ships.

Bunker costs moved the other way. Fuel prices fell 18 per cent over the year, and the longer voyage's fuel bill per box is 30 per cent higher than before but 5 per cent lower than the peak, because the price fell faster than the distance grew.

Schedule reliability improved as the diversions settled. A carrier that had padded its schedules by 10 days cut the padding to 6, and shippers value predictability more than a shorter transit at the margin. A carrier's published rate and its realized rate differ by the surcharges a route's delay invites, and the second is the number a shipper plans around.

The rate index is a weighted average and hides a spread. Contract rates, which cover 70 per cent of the volume, fell 22 per cent, while spot rates on the route fell 40 per cent, and the spread is where a small forwarder's margin lives.

Insurance and inventory carry the residual cost. A longer transit ties up inventory for 12 more days, and a shipper's working capital cost is the part of the bill that no rate index captures. That cost has not fallen, and it is why some manufacturers have shifted sourcing rather than accept the transit.

Distance and rate are not the same market. A longer route burns more fuel per box, and a carrier's cost per box rises by 8 per cent on the detour. The rate fell anyway because the fleet that had been ordered in the boom arrived at the same time, and capacity grew faster than the detour's cost.

Schedules absorbed the detour better than expected. A carrier that adds two days to a rotation can hold its weekly frequency by adding one ship to a loop of nine, and the extra ship's cost is 25,000 dollars a day against a rate that fell 15 per cent.

A detour's cost lands on different parties depending on the contract. A spot rate carries it in the price; a contract with a bunker adjustment clause carries it in a surcharge; a fixed-rate contract carries it on the carrier. The three mechanisms produce three different freight markets at the same time, which is why two shippers on one route can report different costs.

Freight rates behave as a residual rather than as a price for distance. It moves with capacity, fuel and schedule discipline, and a longer route can be cheaper than a shorter one if the fleet has grown enough to fill it.
