Open Dialogue came out of Finnish psychiatry in the 1980s with a promise that sounded implausible and, to its critics, unscientific: treat a psychotic crisis with the patient's own network in the room and relapse becomes rarer. A trial published in *The Lancet Psychiatry* in October tested the model in England, and the promise has split cleanly in two.

The trial, called ODDESSI, randomized mental health teams rather than patients, because the intervention is a way of running a service and cannot be allocated one person at a time. Teams in the Open Dialogue arm were trained and supervised in the model, which gathers the patient's family and friends into treatment meetings and keeps the meetings going through the crisis. The comparison was ordinary care in comparable teams, and the trial ran long enough to observe what happened after the first episode rather than only during it.

On the primary outcome, time to relapse after an initial recovery, the two arms were indistinguishable: a marginal hazard ratio of 0.95, with a confidence interval from 0.67 to 1.32. Of those who recovered far enough to be assessed, 174 in the usual-care group and 225 in the Open Dialogue group had the data needed to determine relapse, which means the analysis rests on a subset that the trial did not fully control, and the relapse clock starts from a point that differs between patients.

On the secondary outcomes the model did better. Open Dialogue was associated with significantly lower probabilities of psychiatric inpatient admission and of re-referral to crisis care or secondary services, and with improvements in self-rated recovery, health-related quality of life and satisfaction with services. Social network size and quality did not change, which undercuts one of the model's stated mechanisms.

Admission is the outcome that health systems pay for, and A model that keeps people out of hospital without shortening the illness is a plausible bargain for a health system, and a bad bargain for anyone who read the original claim. Serious adverse events point the same way: 386 in total, 281 in the usual-care arm and 105 in the Open Dialogue arm, and 376 of them, 97 per cent, were judged unrelated to the intervention, so the difference reflects the care setting rather than the therapy.

The honest reading is that Open Dialogue is a service design that reduces the use of acute beds, not a treatment that changes the course of psychosis. Its advocates have often claimed both, and the trial supports only the first. What the second would require is a trial powered on relapse with fuller follow-up, and the field has now spent a decade arguing about a model on the strength of results that did not measure the thing they claimed. Trials of service models are expensive and slow, which is why so few exist, and why the ones that do deserve to be read for what they measured rather than for what their supporters hoped.
