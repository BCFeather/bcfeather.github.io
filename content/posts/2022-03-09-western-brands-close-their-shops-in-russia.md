McDonald's said on 8 March that it would close its 847 restaurants in Russia, and Coca-Cola, PepsiCo and Starbucks suspended their sales in the days around it. The chains had traded in the country for three decades, and their departure was read as a statement about the war rather than as a commercial decision. The company said it would keep paying its 62,000 Russian employees while the restaurants were shut, and it took a charge of about 50 million dollars in the quarter.

Symbolism attached itself to one address. The first McDonald's in the Soviet Union opened on Pushkin Square in Moscow in January 1990, and 30,000 people queued on the first day in a line that ran around the block. The restaurant was cited in every account of the country's opening to the world, and the arches on that corner became a landmark of the kind that a cathedral is, which is to say a place people used to find each other. Closing them said something that a sanctions list could not.

The arithmetic was smaller than the symbolism. Russia and Ukraine together accounted for about 2 per cent of McDonald's system-wide sales, and 84 per cent of the Russian restaurants were run by a franchise partner rather than by the company. Coca-Cola's Russian volume was a similar fraction of its total, and Starbucks operated through a licensee. The cost of leaving was survivable, which is what made the decision available to companies that had spent years arguing that engagement was a better instrument than exit.

The wave that followed covered most of the shelf. Apple had stopped selling products in Russia on 1 March and limited its payment service; IKEA closed its stores and factories; H&M, Nike, Zara, Chanel and Hermes suspended trading; BP and Shell wrote off their stakes in Russian oil. Payment networks, shipping lines, airlines and audit firms joined within two weeks, until the list of brands that stayed had become a shorter and more conspicuous one.

Moscow answered with a bill rather than a boycott. The public chamber proposed nationalizing the assets of companies that left, and a draft law gave the prosecutors the power to appoint an external administration. The threat was used selectively, and it produced a compromise in which some companies sold their Russian operations to local buyers at a price they would not have chosen. McDonald's sold to a licensee in June, and the restaurants reopened under a new name with a new logo, on the same sites, with the same menu.

What the exits established was the limit of the instrument. A brand can withdraw in a week, and a factory, a workforce and a supply chain cannot, and the gap between the two is what a government that wants to keep its economy running exploits for as long as it can. The arches came down from Pushkin Square, and the queue that had stood there in 1990 was replaced by a queue of a different kind in the autumn, when the first partial mobilization sent men to the recruiting offices.
