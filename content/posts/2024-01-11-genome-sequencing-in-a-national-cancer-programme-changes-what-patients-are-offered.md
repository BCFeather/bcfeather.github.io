Precision oncology promises that a tumour's genome will decide its treatment, and the promise has been tested mostly in single-centre studies. A paper in Nature Medicine on 11 January 2024 reports a national programme. A molecular tumour board is the institution that turns a variant list into a recommendation, and a recommendation requires a clinician to weigh evidence that is often thin. Consent for genomic data is a governance question, and governance decides whether a programme can be repeated elsewhere.

The Cancer Programme of the 100,000 Genomes Project sequenced tumours at scale, and scale is what a health system needs before it adopts a test. Actionable variants are the output that matters clinically, and actionability is a judgement that a molecular tumour board makes. Actionability is a spectrum rather than a binary, since a variant may have a trial, an off-label drug or no option at all. A repeated programme is what a result needs before it becomes a policy, and policy is where sequencing is heading.

A variant that no drug targets is information rather than a decision. The programme pairs sequencing with a board, and the pairing is what converts data into care. Auditing management changes is the only way a health system can value a genomic test, and valuing is what a budget requires.

The fraction of patients whose management changed is the measure of value. A test that finds variants but changes nothing is a test that a payer will question. Rare variants are where sequencing earns its cost, since a common variant is usually found by a cheaper test. A cheaper test finds the common variant, and the sequencing budget is justified by the rare ones that the cheaper test misses.

Changing management is the endpoint that a health system can audit. Rare variants accumulate when enough tumours are sequenced, and accumulation is the argument for a national rather than a local programme. Distribution of a test is a decision about equity, and equity is the argument that a national programme makes against a market one. Rare variants accumulate only when a programme sequences enough tumours, and accumulation at that scale is what a national cohort supplies. A test that changes management for a minority is still a test a health system may buy, and buying depends on the price paid per change.

A rare variant seen once is a case report, and seen twenty times it is a target. Equity is the policy question, since sequencing a whole population costs more than sequencing the patients who can pay. Outcomes require follow-up that a sequencing programme does not control, and the gap between action and outcome is the gap the paper leaves.

A national programme distributes the test, and distribution is a decision about who benefits. The follow-up is what the paper cannot supply, since an action taken is not an outcome achieved. A national cohort also supplies a research resource, and the resource outlives the clinical question that motivated it.
