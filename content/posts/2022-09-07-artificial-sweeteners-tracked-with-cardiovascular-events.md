A sweetener that replaces sugar is a food additive that most people consume without thinking about it, and the epidemiology of its long-term effects is thin because the exposure is hard to measure. A cohort with repeated 24-hour dietary records and brand names is the best measurement available.

The study followed 103,388 participants of the NutriNet-Sante cohort from 2009 to 2021, with dietary intake assessed by repeated 24-hour records that included the brand names of industrial products, and modelled cardiovascular outcomes with multivariable adjusted Cox models. The paper appeared in *The BMJ*, 7 September 2022. A repeated 24-hour record with brand names is the measurement that separates one sweetener from another, and the separation is what the analysis needs.

Total artificial sweetener intake was associated with a hazard ratio of 1.09 for cardiovascular disease over 1,502 events, and 1.18 for cerebrovascular disease over 777 events, with absolute incidence rates of 346 against 314 per 100,000 person years for higher consumers and non-consumers. An absolute rate of 346 against 314 per 100,000 person years is a difference small enough to be a rounding error and large enough to matter at population scale.

Aspartame was associated with cerebrovascular events at 1.17, and acesulfame potassium at 1.40 and sucralose at 1.31 with coronary heart disease. A difference between sweeteners is the pattern that a class-level analysis would hide, and the pattern is what suggests a mechanism rather than a lifestyle. Aspartame, acesulfame potassium and sucralose each produced a different outcome, and the differences are the signal.

The absolute rates differ by about 10 per cent, which is small enough that an individual's decision should not turn on it and large enough that a population recommendation might. A small relative risk applied to a large population is a large number of events, and the arithmetic is the policy argument. Thirty events per 100,000 is the arithmetic, and the arithmetic at national scale is a public health question.

The authors call for replication in other cohorts and for experimental work on the mechanisms, because a plausible pathway through the gut microbiome or through insulin responses has not been demonstrated. A signal from one cohort is a signal that a second cohort should test. Replication in another cohort is the request, and the request is the correct one.
