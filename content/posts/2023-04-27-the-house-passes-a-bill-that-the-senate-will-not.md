title: "The House passes a bill that the Senate will not"
date: "2023-04-27T22:40:00+08:00"

description: "House Republicans passed a bill on 26 April 2023 that would raise the debt limit in exchange for spending cuts, by 217 votes to 215, opening a negotiation with the White House that the Senate has no intention of joining."
---

The House passed the Limit, Save, Grow Act on 26 April by 217 votes to 215, with four Republicans joining every Democrat in opposition. The bill would raise the debt limit by 1.5 trillion dollars or suspend it until March 2024, whichever comes first, in exchange for about 4.5 trillion dollars of savings over a decade. It would also repeal parts of the previous year's energy and tax legislation and add work requirements to several benefit programmes. The bill would also block a planned expansion of the internal revenue service and would attach the whole package to a single vote that the Senate must take or leave.

The margin tells the story. The speaker had spent a week assembling a coalition that could lose no more than four votes, and he lost exactly four, all from the right of his conference. The bill was amended twice to satisfy them, including a change that would move the work requirements forward, and the final vote was held with a member absent on each side and the result read out twice.

Senate passage is out of the question. The Democratic majority has said the bill is dead on arrival, and the White House has said the debt limit should be raised without conditions, which is the position it has held since January. The negotiation that follows will be about a smaller package, and the shape of it was visible in the weeks afterwards, when the speaker and the president began meeting and both sides described the meetings as productive. Those meetings produced no text, and the positions each side had taken in public made a compromise look like surrender to both.

Contested as the economics are, the arithmetic is plain. A cut of 4.5 trillion dollars over ten years is about 2 per cent of projected spending over that period, and most of it comes from caps on discretionary spending rather than from entitlements. The Congressional Budget Office said the bill would reduce deficits by 4.8 trillion over ten years on one basis and by much less on another, and both numbers were quoted by both sides.

A deadline forces a deal. The government reached the limit in January and has been using extraordinary measures since, and the treasury has said those measures will be exhausted in the summer. The Congressional Budget Office projected in February that the limit would bind between July and September, and the estimate moved forward as tax receipts came in weaker than forecast. The treasury has been moving money between accounts to buy time, and its monthly statement has become the calendar the markets follow.

What the vote established was that the speaker could pass a partisan bill and that the president would have to negotiate. It also established the price of the confrontation, which is a downgrade risk, a rise in borrowing costs and a repetition of the 2011 episode in which a similar standoff cost the government its top credit rating and about 1.3 billion dollars in extra interest that year alone.
