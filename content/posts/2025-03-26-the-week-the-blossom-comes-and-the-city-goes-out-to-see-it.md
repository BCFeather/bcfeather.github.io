The cherry trees flower for about a week at the end of March, and for that week a city with a few thousand of them reorganizes itself around the forecast. The parks that have the oldest trees publish a prediction, the prediction is revised daily, the roads around them are closed to cars, and a visitor who arrives on the wrong day sees a queue and a great deal of bare branch. The blossom is not a festival with a date. It is a fortnight of crowds that follows a plant.

The tradition of going out to look at flowers is older than the trees. The practice appears in the poetry of the Tang dynasty, it was formalized in the festivals of the Song, and it has survived every change of the last century because it costs nothing and requires only a walk. What the modern version adds is the camera. A tree in flower is the most photographed object in the city for two weeks, and the photographs are the reason the crowds are larger than the tradition alone would produce.

The university campuses are the places where the crowds are hardest to manage. A campus with a famous avenue of trees is a campus with an open gate, and the visitors who come to photograph it are the visitors who walk past the laboratories and the libraries. Several universities have introduced a booking system in the last few years, a quota per day, an entry through a single gate, and the system has been criticized as an attempt to fence off something that belongs to the public and defended as the only way to keep the campus usable for the students who live on it.

The agronomy of the flowering is what makes the prediction possible. A cherry tree requires a period of cold and then a period of warmth, and the date of the flowering is a function of the accumulated warmth since the start of the year, which is why an unusually warm February brings the blossom forward by a week. The records that the parks keep go back decades, and the trend in them is early, in the same way and for the same reason as the records of the apricots and the willows.

What the week produces, beyond the photographs, is a piece of shared calendar. A city of ten million people knows that for seven days the north gate of a particular park will be crowded, that the subway will be full at ten in the morning, and that a picnic on the grass will be refused by a guard with a whistle. The knowledge is trivial and it is the kind of knowledge that makes a place feel lived in.

The trees themselves do not care. They flower when the warmth has accumulated, the petals fall within a week, and the leaves that follow last until November. What the city does with those seven days is its own business, and the business is mostly standing under a tree with a phone held up, which is a reasonable use of a spring afternoon.
