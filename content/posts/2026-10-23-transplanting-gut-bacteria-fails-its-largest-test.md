title: "Transplanting gut bacteria fails its largest test"

description: "A phase 3 trial in The Lancet finds donor faecal transplants no better than placebo for irritable bowel syndrome, although the donor microbes did take hold."
---

Faecal microbiota transplantation has been the most oversold procedure in gastroenterology, and the largest trial of it in irritable bowel syndrome has now reported. The phase 3 study, published in *The Lancet* in October, randomised 448 people with moderate to severe IBS to donor transplants or placebo and found no difference in symptoms at 90 days: 40 per cent of the transplant group met the improvement threshold against 38 per cent of the placebo group. It is the largest trial of the procedure in the condition, and it arrives after years in which clinics have offered the treatment on the strength of much smaller studies.

The premise was reasonable. People with IBS have gut communities that look different from those of people without it, and transferring a healthy community is the most direct way to test whether the difference causes the symptoms. Small trials had suggested benefit, which is why a phase 3 trial was funded, and the field has been waiting for a result that could settle the question rather than add to the noise. Donor screening rules, which exist to keep pathogens out of a vulnerable gut, also constrain how much material can be given and how often.

Engraftment was not the problem. The donor strains did establish themselves in recipients, which the investigators verified, so the trial tested the right hypothesis with a live intervention rather than a dead one. Symptoms simply did not follow. At 365 days, 44 per cent of the transplant group and 45 per cent of the placebo group had improved by at least 75 points on the symptom severity scale, and the quality-of-life measure showed the same pattern.

A placebo response of that size is not an artefact. IBS is a condition in which symptoms and expectations interact, and a procedure as elaborate as a transplant produces a strong expectation. Trials of drugs in the same condition face the same problem, which is why the field insists on sham controls and why an open-label transplant study, however promising, cannot carry the argument.

The authors draw a narrow conclusion: replacing the community without changing anything else is unlikely to provide consistent benefit, though the microbiota may still matter in the disorder's pathophysiology. That leaves the field with a harder problem, since the plausible next steps are selective: defined strains, dietary substrates, or a subset of patients whose community has a specific defect rather than a general dysbiosis.

For patients, the result is a caution against paying for the procedure outside a trial. For researchers, it is a reminder that an ecological intervention can succeed at colonizing and fail at treating, and that the gap between the two is where the biology lives. The next trial will have to specify which organisms, in whom, and by what mechanism, rather than whether a donor's community is better than a patient's. That is a slower kind of trial, and it is the one the result now demands.
