Sea spray begins where a wave breaks, and the droplets that carry salt into the air are made by a small number of events inside the breaker. Modelling those events is difficult because a laboratory wave breaks for a fraction of a second and the droplets are small, fast and numerous.

Measurements reported in a preprint posted to arXiv on 4 October 2022 take the problem apart with in-line holography. Three plunging breakers of weak, moderate and strong intensity are generated by dispersively focused wave packets that differ mainly in the amplitude of the wave-maker motion. Two cinematic holographic systems record 650 holograms per second across the width of the tank, and droplets of at least 100 micrometres are tracked as they cross a plane 1.2 centimetres above the highest point the crest reaches.

Four mechanisms account for the droplets. The first is the closure of the indentation between the top of the plunging jet and the splash it throws up. The second is the bursting of large bubbles entrapped beneath the jet at impact. The third is splashing in the turbulent zone on the wave's front face, and the fourth is the bursting of the large bubbles that the same zone carries. Each mechanism leaves its signature in the height, size and velocity of the droplets it emits, which is what allows the four to be separated in a single record.

What the separation buys is a way to attribute spray to a stage of the breaking event. A model that treats a breaker as one aerosol source has to average over the four, and the averaging hides the dependence on intensity: a strong breaker entrains more large bubbles, so the bubble-bursting routes gain weight while the indentation route saturates.

Sea-salt flux estimates are where the result would land. A parameterization that scales spray with wind speed assumes one production mechanism with one size distribution, and the four routes here have different distributions: the bubble routes favour smaller droplets at lower heights, the jet routes favour larger ones higher up. Splitting the flux by route would let a model predict the size spectrum as well as the total mass, and the spectrum is what decides how far the salt travels before it settles, and a model with the wrong spectrum can match the total mass while misplacing the deposition.

Limits are those of the apparatus. The measurement plane sits above the crest, so droplets that never reach it are invisible, and the size floor at 100 micrometres excludes the small droplets that dominate number counts but carry little mass. A tank wave is also not a sea, and the three intensities span a narrower range than a storm does.

Whether the four routes keep their proportions in a wind-driven sea is the open question, and it is the one a spray parameterization needs answered before it can be used in a climate model.
