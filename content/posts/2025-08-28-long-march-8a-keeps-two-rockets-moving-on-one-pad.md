A Long March 8A lifted off from the Hainan commercial space launch site at 03:08 on 26 August and put the tenth group of low-orbit internet satellites into orbit. The mission was the 591st flight of the Long March family, and the fourth for this vehicle from the new commercial site on the island's north-east coast.

Long March 8A is the largest rocket designed for the commercial market. The vehicle is about 50.5 metres tall and weighs around 371 tonnes at liftoff, with a 3.35-metre kerosene core stage, a 3.35-metre hydrogen upper stage and two liquid boosters, and it can put about seven tonnes into a 700-kilometre sun-synchronous orbit. The five-point-two-metre fairing is wider than the core, which allows it to carry a stack of flat satellites without a dispenser taking up half the volume. That fairing size is a quiet advantage for constellation work, because a batch of flat satellites can be stacked directly rather than arranged around a central column.

The mission also tested a change in how the pad is used. The launch team ran what it calls a professional test-and-launch mode, in which a small crew finishes the final assembly of one vehicle in the integration building while most of the team moves to another rocket on the pad. The company says the arrangement saves about five days of waiting compared with the traditional sequence, where the same people do one rocket from start to finish.

That matters more than it sounds. A launch site is expensive to build and cheap to operate only if it is busy, and the bottleneck is usually the people who do the integration and testing rather than the concrete. If two vehicles can be processed in parallel on one pad, the effective capacity of the site roughly doubles without a second pad. The company calls the arrangement a pulsating workflow, and it depends on the integration building being able to hold two vehicles at once.

The satellites themselves belong to the low-orbit internet constellation, which has now been launched in ten batches this year. Each batch goes to a slightly different altitude and inclination, and the upper stage does the distribution: it relights after the main stages have separated and releases the satellites in sequence, then performs a deorbit burn so that it does not stay in orbit as debris.

Hainan's commercial site opened in 2024 and is the first Chinese launch facility built for private and commercial customers rather than for a state programme. It has two pads, one for the Long March 8 series and one for smaller commercial vehicles, and its position at 19 degrees north gives an energy advantage for geostationary transfers and a disadvantage for polar orbits, which is why the sun-synchronous missions still go to inland sites. The commercial site is also close to the coast, which means that a failure early in flight lands in the sea rather than on a town.
