Hungary's election on 3 April returned the Fidesz-KDNP alliance to power with 136 of the 199 seats in parliament, a margin wide enough to preserve the two-thirds majority that the governing party has used to rewrite rules on courts, education and the media. Turnout was high by Hungarian standards, and the governing list took close to half of the votes counted under a mixed system that converts a plurality into a supermajority. The race was called within two hours of polls closing, and the governing share of the party-list vote came close to what it won four years earlier.

The opposition had tried to solve its fragmentation before the vote. Six parties cleared the threshold and backed a single list led by Peter Marki-Zay, a small-town mayor and former management consultant who had won a primary against better-known rivals. The campaign framed the contest as a referendum on corruption rather than a choice of programmes, and the alliance lost every age group except the youngest, winning only a handful of districts in Budapest.

Observers from the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe monitored the vote and described an absence of a level playing field, pointing to state advertising routed to friendly outlets, opaque campaign financing and the overlap between government and party. Their report did not allege fraud on election day, and the result itself never came into doubt once counting began, which is the usual shape of a contest that is competitive in form and tilted in practice. Domestic observers reported similar findings on access to advertising and on the use of public institutions during the campaign.

What the supermajority buys is institutional rather than legislative power. Since 2010 the governing party has used its seats to enlarge and reshape the constitutional court, to place universities and public broadcasters under friendly boards, and to write an electoral system that rewards the largest list. A parliament that can amend the constitution without partners can continue that work, and the checks that such majorities normally meet inside a coalition exist only inside a single party. Two-thirds also lets the majority change the electoral law itself, which the opposition says makes the next contest harder to win.

Brussels and Budapest have argued for a decade over the rule of law, and the argument has hardened as money from the recovery fund has been withheld pending judicial reforms. The election does not settle it, since the conditions attached to that money concern the same court appointments the supermajority protects, and a government that has just been re-elected on its own terms has little reason to concede anything quickly.

The result is no surprise, and that is what deserves attention. Hungary has now held four consecutive elections in which one party could legislate alone, and each was certified as free while observers judged the field uneven. A democracy can pass both tests and still drift, and the mechanism is usually the same: the rules are legal, and the people who write them keep winning.
