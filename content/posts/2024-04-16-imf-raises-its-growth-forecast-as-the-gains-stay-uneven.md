title: "IMF raises its growth forecast as the gains stay uneven"

description: "The fund now expects the world economy to expand 3.2 per cent this year, with the American figure raised and the European one cut."
---

International Monetary Fund raised its forecast for global growth in 2024 to 3.2 per cent in the report published on 16 April, a modest upgrade from its January estimate, and left next year's figure unchanged. The headline conceals a widening gap between regions. The upgrade was small enough that several private forecasters had already gone further, which is the pattern the fund has followed since the pandemic. The fund's own economists described the revision as a rounding rather than a turn, which is a description that finance ministries read as a warning about the next report.

America is the upgrade. Strong consumption and a labour market that has not loosened pushed the forecast for the United States up to 2.7 per cent, faster than the fund expected in January and faster than any other large advanced economy. A consumer that keeps spending is a forecast that keeps being revised upward. The fund's economists attributed the American strength to productivity growth that has surprised them, and they conceded that they cannot yet explain where it comes from.

Europe is the cut. Germany is close to stagnation and its industry pays energy costs its competitors do not, and the forecast for the euro area was reduced again. An economy built on energy-intensive manufacturing is an economy that a price shock hits twice, once through the input cost and once through the demand that follows. The German figure was cut partly because its industry has been slow to replace the cheap gas it lost, and the replacement it has bought is liquefied gas shipped from the other side of the Atlantic.

Gap is the theme of the report. The fund notes that the spread between the fastest and slowest performers is the widest in decades, and that the countries growing fastest are generally those with the least debt and the most room to spend. A forecast built on averages hides the range that matters to investors. The report's own projection for the medium term is lower than the average of the decade before the pandemic, which is the number that finance ministries read first.

Risks are named rather than quantified. The report lists a widening conflict in the Middle East, a prolonged slump in Chinese property, and the possibility that inflation proves sticky and forces central banks to keep rates high for longer. A list of risks is an admission about the limits of the model. The fund's advice to the countries with the least room is to rebuild fiscal buffers rather than to spend, which is advice that few governments in an election year accept.

What the report shows is that the post-pandemic recovery has become a set of national stories, and the institutions that forecast in averages are being asked to explain a distribution instead. A forecast that is revised upward twice in six months is a forecast that the institutions are still learning from, and the learning is happening in public.
