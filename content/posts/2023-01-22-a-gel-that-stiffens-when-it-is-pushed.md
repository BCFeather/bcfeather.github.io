Most soft materials soften when they are deformed. A rubber band that is stretched past its elastic limit thins and then breaks, and a foam that is compressed collapses its cells one after another. A class of hydrogels does the opposite, and the contrast is what makes them interesting as replacements for load-bearing tissue. A gel that stiffens as it is strained can be soft enough to sit in a joint and firm enough to carry the load that the joint sees when a person stands up.

The architecture that produces the effect is a network with two kinds of connection. Long flexible chains carry most of the water and give the material its softness, and a sparse set of stronger bonds holds the network together and sets the strain at which the response changes. Below that strain the chains are coiled and the network is governed by entropy: pulling on a coil reduces the number of shapes it can take, which costs little energy. Above it the coils are pulled taut, the chains align along the direction of the pull, and the resistance rises sharply.

Alignment is the mechanism, and it is cooperative. A chain that is already straight transmits force to its neighbours through the crosslinks, and those neighbours straighten in turn, so the stiffening spreads through the network instead of staying local. The effect is therefore a property of the network and not of a single molecule, and a material made from shorter chains reaches the stiffening point at a higher strain, which is the handle a designer uses.

Measurement is done with a rheometer that imposes an oscillating strain and reads the stress, and the signature of the effect is a storage modulus that climbs as the strain amplitude rises. The same experiment shows whether the response is reversible, since a network that stiffened by breaking bonds will not recover its softness when the strain is removed. A material that has hysteresis between the loading and the unloading curve is one that has paid for its stiffness with damage.

Applications follow the mechanics. Cartilage carries a load in compression and needs to be soft at rest, and a gel that stiffens under load has the profile that a meniscus implant needs. A spinal disc is loaded by body weight in a way that varies through the day, and a material whose stiffness tracks the load would distribute the stress more evenly than a homogeneous rubber. Soft robotics is the third application, since a gripper that firms its grip as it closes can hold an object without crushing it.

Limits are the same ones that any hydrogel faces. Water is lost to evaporation unless the sample is sealed, the network fatigues after many cycles, and the strong bonds that set the strain threshold are the ones that fail first. A material that stiffens once is a material with a single story, and the versions that have been demonstrated cycle a few thousand times instead of a few million. The question that remains is whether the alignment can be made to reverse without damage, and that is a question about chemistry rather than about mechanics.
