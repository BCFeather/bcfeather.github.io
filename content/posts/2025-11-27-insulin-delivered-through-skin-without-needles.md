Skin rejects insulin on grounds of size rather than fragility. At about 5,800 daltons the molecule sits an order of magnitude above the cutoff of the stratum corneum, and the barrier makes no distinction between useful molecules and harmful ones. Ultrasound has been investigated as a way through for decades, but the cavitation that makes the method work is the same effect that causes tissue damage, so too little pressure delivers nothing and too much damages the skin.

The device in the new study uses low-frequency ultrasound at a pressure chosen to stay below the threshold for tissue damage, applied for a set interval while insulin is held against the skin in a small reservoir. In pigs, the approach delivered 60 to 80 per cent of the loaded dose into circulation, and the resulting pharmacokinetic profile resembled that of a standard subcutaneous injection rather than the irregular absorption typical of topical patches. Blood glucose fell within the target range and stayed there for roughly six hours.

What separates this result from earlier work is the reproducibility rather than the principle. Previous ultrasound-mediated delivery varied sharply with skin thickness, hair, contact pressure and the geometry of the transducer, which made a clinical device difficult to specify. The study reports consistent delivery across sites on the same animal and across animals, and measures skin recovery at the application site; the barrier properties returned to baseline within 24 hours with no visible damage.

The remaining obstacle is the device itself. An ultrasound transducer small enough to hold, powered for long enough and manufactured at a price that undercuts a disposable syringe is a substantial engineering problem, and the study does not present one. A second dose on the same site, which patients would need daily, was not part of the experiment, so the question of cumulative irritation is open.

The comparator is worth keeping in mind. A subcutaneous injection is cheap, familiar and accurate to the unit, and it works through clothing and in field conditions; it is burdensome rather than unreliable. A needle-free route has to beat that standard on adherence, not on novelty, and the evidence for adherence is still a survey result rather than a trial.

The pharmacokinetic profile also has to match the patient's day. A six-hour window may suit a meal but not an overnight basal need, and the study does not test whether the delivered dose can be titrated finely enough for the ranges that insulin regimens require. Dose precision is the property that needle-based delivery gets for free, and it is the one a new device must demonstrate rather than assume.

Skin is a barrier by design, and any method that crosses it has to show that the crossing was permitted rather than inflicted. The 24-hour recovery and the consistent pharmacokinetic profile are the evidence that this method stayed on the permitted side of that line; without them, the result would be a permeability experiment rather than a delivery route.
