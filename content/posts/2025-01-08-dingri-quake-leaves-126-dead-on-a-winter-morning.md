Seismographs recorded a magnitude 6.8 event at 09:05 on 7 January, with the epicentre in Tingri county, Shigatse prefecture, at a depth of ten kilometres. By the following morning the count stood at 126 dead and 188 injured, with 3,609 houses collapsed and 27,248 damaged across five townships. More than 30,000 people were moved into tents and heated halls, and the search phase ended faster than the recovery of services. Road access was the second constraint, because the highway to the epicentre runs through passes that close in heavy snow.

Altitude is the constraint that shapes everything else. Tingri sits above 4,000 metres, the January night temperature falls well below freezing, and the oxygen is thin enough that rescue teams work in short rotations. Diesel generators, quilts and coal stoves arrived before field hospitals did, and the first priority the local government named was warm shelter rather than medical capacity. Tents were pitched in school yards and in the courtyards of monasteries, and the county moved its heating fuel to those sites first.

Building practice explains the damage pattern. Village houses in the county are largely single-storey structures of rammed earth and timber, with heavy roofs of mud and stone laid for insulation against the cold. That roof is a large mass sitting on walls that were never designed for lateral shaking, and adobe fails without warning once the corners crack.

The tectonic setting is a collision zone. India moves north into Eurasia at roughly four to five centimetres a year, and the stress is released along faults that run through the Himalaya and the Gangdise range. Tingri is close to the fault system that produced the 2015 Nepal earthquake, and the region has a documented history of large events. Faults of this kind produce shallow events, and a shallow event of magnitude 6.8 releases its energy close to the surface, where villages stand.

Early warning is the technology that changed the casualty arithmetic, and only partially. Sensors near the epicentre sent an alert that reached Lhasa and Shigatse before the surface waves did, giving schools and hospitals between ten and thirty seconds. A warning of that length helps people reach a doorway and stops lifts, and it does nothing for a house that falls in on itself. Earthquake early warning has been deployed across the region since 2019, and the alerts reach phones through a service that costs the user nothing.

Reconstruction will run for several years, and the design question is already open. Rebuilding with the same heavy roof and the same earth walls would restore a settlement that fails again; rebuilding with a light steel frame and a different roof would change how a house looks and how it keeps heat. Both options cost money that the county does not have, and the winter after the rebuild is the test that matters. Materials are the practical choice, and a light roof of metal sheet loses heat that a mud roof keeps, so the household pays for the engineer's decision every winter.
