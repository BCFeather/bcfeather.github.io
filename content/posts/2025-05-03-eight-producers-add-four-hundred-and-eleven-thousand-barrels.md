A cartel is a group of producers who agree not to compete, and the agreement holds only while the members believe that the price is worth more than the volume. The eight countries that had made the deepest voluntary cuts met on 3 May 2025 and decided to raise output by 411,000 barrels a day in June, measured against the level required in May, which is three monthly increments delivered at once.

Arithmetic of the decision is a speed-up and not a new policy. The group had agreed in December 2024 to return 2.2 million barrels a day of voluntary cuts gradually from April 2025, and it had already tripled the May increment in an earlier surprise. June's 411,000 barrels therefore pulls forward production that had been scheduled for later in the year, which is why the International Energy Agency described it as advancing the bloc to levels previously planned for October.

Quotas show who is meant to absorb the increase. Saudi Arabia's required production was set at 9,367 thousand barrels a day, Russia at 9,161, Iraq at 4,086, the United Arab Emirates at 3,092, Kuwait at 2,466, Kazakhstan at 1,500, Algeria at 928 and Oman at 775. A country that is already producing above its quota gains nothing from a higher quota except a smaller violation.

That is the political content of the decision. Several members, Kazakhstan and Iraq among them, had been producing above their targets for months, and a unilateral increase by the group's largest producers imposes a price low enough to make overproduction expensive. The statement cited healthy market fundamentals and low inventories; delegates told reporters that discipline was the real subject.

Prices fell on the announcement and forecasts followed. Brent had been sliding since the tariff escalation in April, and several banks cut their oil price forecasts within a week, because a producer group that is willing to accept a lower price is a group whose floor has moved. A floor that moves is the single most important variable for a shale producer deciding whether to drill.

Demand side was already soft. The International Energy Agency projected demand growth of 740,000 barrels a day in 2025, down from 990,000 in the first quarter, and supply rising 1.6 million barrels a day to 104.6 million, with non-OPEC producers adding 1.3 million. In that balance, 411,000 barrels from the group with the lowest cost is a decision to take market share.

What would settle the strategy is whether the price fall stops at a level that hurts the overproducers more than it hurts the group's treasury. A price that stays above 60 dollars leaves everyone solvent and the discipline unresolved; a price below 55 does the work that a quota meeting cannot, and it also tests how long a finance ministry will accept a budget that no longer balances. The group meets monthly now, which means the question is not whether the floor moves but how many meetings it takes to find it.
