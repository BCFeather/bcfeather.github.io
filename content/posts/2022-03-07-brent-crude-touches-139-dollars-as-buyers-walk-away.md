Brent crude touched 139 dollars a barrel in London on 7 March, its highest price since 2008, and the move came from a market in which nobody wanted to be the last buyer of Russian oil. The benchmark had closed near 118 dollars on the previous Friday, and it opened the week with a gap that traders described as disorderly. West Texas Intermediate settled above 119 dollars, and the products that refineries make from crude rose further than the crude itself.

The immediate cause was a conversation about an embargo. American officials said the United States and its allies were discussing a ban on imports of Russian crude, and the British government signalled that it would support one. Shell, which had bought a cargo of Russian crude at a discount on 4 March and been criticized for it, said it would stop buying and would close its service stations in Russia. Traders read the statements as a signal that the private market would withdraw before any government order arrived.

Physical supply tightened at the same time. Shipowners refused to load at Russian ports without war-risk insurance, insurers withdrew cover for the Black Sea, and the discount on Urals crude widened to about 30 dollars a barrel against Brent. That discount is the price of finding a buyer, a ship, a bank and an insurer willing to touch a cargo, and the list of those willing was shrinking through the spring.

Comparisons with earlier spikes came easily that week. The record of July 2008 was 147 dollars, set in a market driven by demand and by the weak dollar, and the spike of 1990 followed the invasion of Kuwait. Neither started with a buyer's strike against the exports of a single producer, and neither had a central bank preparing to raise rates into it. Analysts who had spent a decade writing about the shale revolution had to revise the assumption that supply would respond within months.

Consumers met the price at the pump. Petrol in the United States passed four dollars a gallon for the first time since 2008, and the average in Britain passed 1.60 pounds a litre. Airlines added fuel surcharges, hauliers passed costs to shops, and the price of diesel, which moves freight and farming, rose above crude for weeks because refining capacity had closed during the pandemic. The strategic reserves that the American and European governments released in March were measured in tens of millions of barrels against a market that consumes a hundred million barrels a day, which is the arithmetic that kept the price high through the spring.

Relief came slowly and from the demand side. By the end of March Brent had fallen back towards 105 dollars as traders priced the destruction of demand that a war and a lockdown in China would bring. What the month established was the mechanism: a commodity can lose its price discovery before it loses its supply, and a discount that pays for the difficulty of a transaction is the first place where a sanction shows up.
