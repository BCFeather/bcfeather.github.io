title: "Living near oil and gas wells raises mortality for the elderly"
date: "2022-01-30T15:40:00+08:00"

description: "A cohort of 15.2 million Medicare beneficiaries found higher mortality associated with living near or downwind of unconventional oil and gas wells."
---

Unconventional oil and gas extraction moved closer to homes as horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing spread, and the health question followed the wells. Studies of birth outcomes and asthma had suggested effects, but mortality in the elderly, the group with the least reserve, had not been examined across the whole industry. Hydraulic fracturing arrived in counties with little prior industrial activity, which makes the comparison both more informative and more confounded.

A Stanford-led analysis assembled 15,198,496 Medicare beneficiaries contributing 136,215,059 person-years from 2001 to 2015, across all major American unconventional exploration regions, and linked them to records from more than 2.5 million oil and gas wells. Exposure was computed two ways: by proximity to the beneficiary's ZIP code and by whether the ZIP code lay downwind of a well. The paper appeared in *Nature Energy*, 27 January 2022.

Both methods found statistically significant higher mortality associated with living in proximity to and downwind of unconventional wells. The association was strongest in the downwind specification, a pattern that points to airborne transport rather than to water or noise. The result held under a Cox proportional hazards model and under a difference-in-differences design, which compares changes within a region over time rather than differences between regions at a moment.

Two designs agreeing is the paper's main defence. The Cox model controls for measured covariates but can be confounded by anything unmeasured that leads people to live near wells, and the difference-in-differences design removes fixed differences between places while remaining vulnerable to trends that coincide with drilling. Neither is decisive alone, and together they narrow the range of explanations.

The exposure measure is coarse. Wind direction comes from meteorological records, and the dose falls with distance in a way the paper reports as a gradient rather than as a threshold. A ZIP code averages over several square kilometres, so proximity is a proxy for the air a person breathes, and downwind is calculated from prevailing winds rather than from measured concentrations. The authors suggest that primary air pollutants from the wells are a plausible pathway, which remains an inference.

Residents near wells differ from other Medicare beneficiaries in income, occupation and access to care, and the records do not capture smoking or body mass. Claims data also lack residential history before enrolment, so a person who moved near a well after retirement contributes exposure that cannot be dated precisely. The mortality finding is an association, and the size of the effect is not reported in a form that supports a clinical recommendation.

What would settle it is measurement rather than modelling: monitoring at the fence line, paired with health records at the household level, would replace a ZIP-code proxy with an exposure gradient. Until then the result adds mortality to a list of outcomes that regulators have been slow to attribute to the industry. The cohort is large enough that a small relative risk becomes a large count of deaths.
