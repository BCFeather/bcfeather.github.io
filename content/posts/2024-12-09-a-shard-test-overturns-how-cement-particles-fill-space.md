Cement sets by growing calcium silicate hydrate, and the volume that the hydrate occupies decides how dense the paste becomes. A paper in *PNAS* on 9 December 2024 re-examines an assumption that has stood since the nineteenth century. A concrete engineer can model a packing rearrangement, and modelling is what a correction has to enable. A century of inference is not evidence, and the paper replaces it with a measurement that can be repeated.

Le Chatelier in 1887 and Powers in 1947 both described hydrate particles as smaller in volume than the reactants they came from. The claim was accepted because it explained the shrinkage that a setting paste shows. Direct measurement is the corrective the paper offers, and a measurement that contradicts a classical inference is worth more than a refinement of it. Service life is predicted from porosity, and a porosity that is wrong by a few per cent shifts the prediction by years. Packing is the mechanism the corrected volume implies, and packing is what a paste model actually simulates.

The shard test measures individual particles rather than an ensemble, and nanoporomechanics supplies the deformation behaviour that a single particle shows. Together they give a volume for the hydrate that the classical picture does not. A packing rearrangement is a mechanical explanation for shrinkage, and a mechanical explanation is what a concrete engineer can model. Low-clinker blends are where the error is largest, and largest errors sit where the models are most used.

The measured particles are larger than the classical account allows, which inverts the explanation of shrinkage. A paste that shrinks is not a paste whose particles contract but one whose packing rearranges. Porosity is the number that a durability model consumes, and a model fed with the wrong porosity predicts the wrong service life. Repeatability on other hydrates is what turns a correction into a method, and the method is the durable contribution.

Porosity is the variable that the correction touches, since the space left between particles is what controls strength, transport and durability. A volume estimate that is wrong by a few per cent changes the predicted porosity by more. Low-clinker blends are where the models are used most, and a volume error is largest where the clinker fraction is smallest.

On the modelling side, the result matters for a material that is produced in billions of tonnes annually, and it matters most where the models are used to design low-clinker blends. A century of agreement is not evidence, since the agreement was about an inference rather than about a measurement. A method is more durable than a correction, and durability is what a materials paper should aim at.

Two classical measurements are not overturned by one test, and the paper's claim is that the volume should be measured directly rather than inferred from shrinkage. A test that can be repeated on other hydrates is the general contribution, and repeatability is what makes the correction usable.
