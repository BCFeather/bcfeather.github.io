A jump of nine tenths of a point in a single month is the kind of number that ends a rate-cutting cycle, and in April it arrived from the calendar and not from demand. Consumer prices rose 1.2 per cent on the month and 3.5 per cent on the year, the highest annual rate since January 2024, according to the Office for National Statistics on 21 May.

Detail explains the move. Housing and household services rose 7.8 per cent on the year after a monthly increase of 3.3 per cent, as energy tariffs, water bills and television licences were adjusted at the start of the financial year. Ofwat's price review raised water bills by an average of about 26 per cent from April, which is a regulated decision, not a market one.

Core inflation rose too. The measure excluding energy, food, alcohol and tobacco went from 3.4 to 3.8 per cent, and services inflation from 4.7 to 5.4 per cent, with airfares and package holidays accounting for much of the monthly increase because Easter fell in April. Goods inflation was 1.7 per cent, up from 0.6, which is where imported costs begin to appear.

Bank of England had forecast this. Its May report projected CPI at about 3.5 per cent in the third quarter, and the April figure arrived a quarter early. A central bank that cuts rates while inflation rises has to argue that the rise is mechanical, and the argument is easier when the components are regulated prices and airfares than when they are wages.

Wage data support the argument. Private sector regular pay growth had slowed, vacancies had fallen, and the committee judged that a margin of slack had opened. Services inflation at 5.4 per cent is still too high for comfort, and it is the number that decides whether the next cut comes in August or later.

Politics sits on top of the arithmetic. The government had raised employer National Insurance contributions and the minimum wage in April, both of which raise costs for the firms that set service prices, and the same month's inflation report contains the effect. A finance ministry that needs growth and a central bank that needs 2 per cent can read the same number differently.

What would settle the path is whether the regulated effect drops out as fast as it arrived. Energy prices are set by a cap that changes quarterly, so a mechanical rise in April can be a mechanical fall in July, and the committee will look through the level to the direction. A core rate that stays near 3.8 into the autumn would end the argument. A committee that cuts into a rising headline and is proved right is a committee with credibility; one that cuts and is proved wrong has spent it. The July cap, the next services print and the August meeting are the three dates that decide whether the easing resumes this year or waits for next.
