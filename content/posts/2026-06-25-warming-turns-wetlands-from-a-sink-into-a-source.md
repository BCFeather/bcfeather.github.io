Wetlands have been counted as a brake on climate because peat accumulates carbon over millennia, drawing carbon dioxide out of the air faster than decay returns it. That accounting has always depended on the water table staying high, since saturated peat decays slowly and methane-producing microbes thrive only in the absence of oxygen. The accounting assumes the water table stays near the surface, and drainage for agriculture breaks that assumption directly, which is why drained peatlands already emit heavily in many temperate regions. Restoration has therefore to be judged against the emissions the site would produce if left alone, which is a different question from the one most monitoring programmes ask.

A study in *Nature* on 25 June examined what warming does to both sides of the ledger at once. Methane emissions rose, and carbon dioxide sequestration declined, so the two effects push in the same direction rather than offsetting each other.

The projection matters more than the present-day measurement. Under high-forcing scenarios, SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5, the models show widespread conversion of temperate wetlands into persistent net sources of carbon dioxide equivalent by the end of the century. A persistent source is different from a temporary one, because it keeps adding to the atmospheric burden rather than paying it back later. The scenarios are the high end of the emissions range, and the authors are careful to say that the conversion is projected rather than observed, since a persistent source is a statement about the next seventy years.

Positive feedback is the mechanism the authors identify. Warming dries the surface layer, which allows oxygen in and accelerates peat decomposition, releasing carbon that took centuries to accumulate; it also warms the anoxic layers that remain, which increases methane production. Both responses scale with temperature, and neither has an obvious brake. Peatland carbon has accumulated over thousands of years at rates of millimetres per year, and a decomposition rate that doubles with a few degrees removes that balance quickly.

Methane's short lifetime is the counter-argument that has been used to play down wetland emissions: it is a powerful gas, but it leaves the atmosphere within a decade, so a sustained methane flux produces a stable rather than growing forcing. That argument holds for the methane alone and not for the carbon dioxide that the same warming releases from the peat.

Conservation reading is straightforward and uncomfortable. Restoring water tables protects the carbon store, and drainage does the opposite, which is a familiar argument. The new element is that a warmer climate works against the restoration: a rewetted wetland in a warmer world may still lose carbon, and the management target has to be defined against a moving baseline rather than against the state of a site in 1990. The realistic target is therefore a water table kept as high as the land use allows, with the accounting adjusted for the warming that is already committed.
