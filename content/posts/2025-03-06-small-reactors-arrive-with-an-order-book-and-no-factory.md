Small modular reactors have become the most ordered power plant in the world without becoming the most built one. Companies in the United States, Britain, Poland and Japan have signed agreements for hundreds of units, and the buyers are increasingly data centre operators who need firm power close to a site. The order books are real in the sense that deposits have been paid; they are not real in the sense that a factory exists to fill them. The deposits are large enough to fund design work and small enough that a cancellation costs the buyer a fraction of a plant.

Economics rest on serial production. A large reactor is built once at a site with a bespoke design, and the cost overruns of the past two decades came from that model. A small reactor is designed to be built in a factory and shipped, which moves labour from a construction site to a controlled shop floor and turns an engineering project into a manufacturing one. A factory that builds four units a year has to sell four units a year, and the order book is what keeps the line alive between orders.

First units are the test of that logic. Every design now being marketed is still in its first-of-a-kind phase, and first units always cost more than the average that follows. A vendor that quotes a price before the first unit is complete is quoting a number it cannot yet know, and the contracts that utilities have signed contain escalation clauses for that reason. A utility that signs for a first unit is buying an option on the second, and the option is worth more if the vendor publishes its costs.

Regulation is the second constraint. Licensing a new design takes years, and the regulators are short of people who can review a design they have not seen before. Several countries have created frameworks for advanced reactors, and the frameworks are faster on paper than in practice. A regulator that has reviewed light-water designs for fifty years is being asked to assess a design with passive safety features it has no framework for.

Fuel is the third. Most designs use low-enriched uranium, which is available, and some rely on high-assay low-enriched uranium, which is produced in very small quantities. A reactor that needs fuel nobody can buy is a reactor that will not run, and the enrichment capacity is being expanded at a pace that reflects the uncertainty in demand. A fuel fabrication line costs hundreds of millions and needs a customer before it is built, which is a chicken-and-egg problem that governments are solving with subsidies.

Data centre buyers change the calculation. A hyperscale operator that needs two gigawatts of firm power in five years cannot wait for a large reactor, and it can pay a premium for a smaller unit that arrives earlier. That customer is also the one with the balance sheet to absorb a first-of-a-kind risk, which is why the order books are being filled by companies that are not traditional utilities.
