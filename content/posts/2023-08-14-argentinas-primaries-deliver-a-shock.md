Javier Milei campaigned with a chainsaw, a wig and a promise to blow up the central bank, and on 13 August he won the largest share of the vote in Argentina's primaries. His party, La Libertad Avanza, took about thirty per cent, ahead of the governing coalition's candidate Sergio Massa on twenty-one and of the traditional opposition coalition on twenty-eight between its two competing lists. Turnout was seventy per cent, in a country where voting is compulsory and frequently resented.

The result was a rejection of the two blocs that have governed Argentina since 2015. Patricia Bullrich, who represents the hawkish half of the main opposition coalition Juntos por el Cambio, defeated the more conciliatory Horacio Rodriguez Larreta and will face Milei in October. Massa, the economy minister, carries the responsibility for inflation running above a hundred per cent and for a currency that has lost most of its value in four years. Both of them now compete for votes from a public that has stopped believing either can fix the money.

Milei's programme is an argument rather than a policy. He wants to replace the peso with the dollar, abolish the central bank, cut public spending by fifteen per cent of output and close several ministries. Economists describe the dollarization proposal as technically possible and socially ruinous, because the country has few dollars and would have to acquire them by selling assets at whatever price the market offered. His appeal is to younger voters and to people who have watched savings evaporate twice in a decade. Pollsters found his support strongest among men under thirty and weakest among public employees, which describes a coalition rather than a majority.

The market voted before the voters. On 14 August the government devalued the official exchange rate by nearly a fifth overnight, and the central bank raised its policy rate to 118 per cent. The parallel rate, which is the one that most Argentines actually use, had already gone far beyond that. The devaluation was a condition attached to the review of the country's programme with the International Monetary Fund, and the timing, one day after the primaries, looked deliberate.

Campaigning has resumed with the currency at the centre of every speech. Elections in Argentina are now about the currency. Massa has spent the year managing a crawling peg, price controls and a series of tax measures that have kept the fiscal deficit from exploding, and the results are visible in shop windows. Bullrich promises shock therapy with the backing of the business establishment. Milei promises something more radical and less specified, and his rise has already changed what the other two say.

Voting in the first round takes place on 22 October, with a runoff in November if nobody reaches forty-five per cent. Argentina has been here before, with a crisis, a radical outsider and a choice that the establishment describes as unthinkable, and it has usually chosen the outsider later than it expected. This time the outsider arrived first, and the two old blocs spent the following week arguing about which of them had let him in.
