The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation carries warm surface water north and cold deep water south, and its stability is one of the larger open questions in climate science. Palaeoclimate records suggest the circulation has had more than one equilibrium, and a bistable system can be pushed from one state to the other by noise as well as by a change in forcing. What that push looks like in time is harder to compute than whether it is possible.

Large deviation theory supplies the tool. Given a stochastic model, it identifies the most probable path between two attractors, which is the trajectory a rare transition will most likely follow, and the preprint posted on 21 November 2023 applies it to a stochastic box model of the World Ocean. A box model is not a general circulation model, and it is small enough that the rare-event calculation can be done properly.

The collapse pathway is counterintuitive. The most probable route begins with a strengthening of the circulation, not a weakening, and the strengthening is followed by a drop within a couple of years, triggered by a short but relatively strong pulse of freshwater. The initial surge is what leaves the overturning vulnerable, since the same freshwater input then removes the buoyancy that was holding the circulation up, and the sequence runs opposite to the gradual weakening that a forcing-led view would predict.

Recovery is a different shape entirely. The return path requires the North Atlantic to be gradually salinified over roughly twenty years, with no comparably fast route available. An asymmetry of that kind matters for any discussion of reversibility, because a system that tips quickly and recovers slowly will spend most of its history in the collapsed state once it has tipped.

Where the freshwater arrives also changes the odds. Comparing noise scenarios, the calculation produces probability ratios for collapse, and the circulation comes out most vulnerable to forcing applied in the thermocline region of the Atlantic and not at the surface or at high latitudes. That localization is a physical result about which observations would be most informative.

A comparison with a quasi-equilibrium approach sharpens the picture. A bifurcation-induced collapse, produced by slowly changing a control parameter, looks different from a noise-induced one, and the two are often conflated in discussion, which leads to runs being read as evidence for a mechanism they were not built to test. Separating the two makes the existing simulations easier to interpret.

A model of this kind carries its own limits. A box model resolves basins and not eddies, the noise is prescribed rather than derived, and the most probable path is one of many that the rare-event calculation ranks. The result is a statement about mechanism and relative probability inside that model, and its transfer to a fully resolved ocean has to be argued rather than assumed, which is where a reader should be sceptical.
