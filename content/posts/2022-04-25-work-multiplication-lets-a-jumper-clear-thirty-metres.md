A flea jumps a hundred times its body length and a galago leaps two metres, and both are limited by the same constraint: a muscle can produce a fixed amount of work in a single stroke, and the energy stored in an elastic element cannot exceed what the muscle put there. A muscle can be made to store energy over several seconds through a tendon, and the storage is still limited by the work the muscle did, which is why a biological jumper cannot exceed a few metres.

An engineered device is not bound by that constraint. A motor can rotate, a ratchet can reset, and work can be added over many strokes before release. A study built a jumper on that principle and reached more than 30 metres, higher than any previous engineered jumper and an order of magnitude above the best biological ones. The paper appeared in *Nature*, 27 April 2022. A latch that stores energy slowly and releases it quickly is a mechanism that appears in clockwork and in catapults, and the study's contribution is the scaling analysis that shows how far it can be pushed.

The design separates the motor from the spring. The motor winds a latch through repeated cycles, the latch stores the accumulated energy in an elastic element, and the release converts it into a jump. The energy is therefore not limited by a single contraction. A latch that can be reset without releasing the spring is the component that makes the multiplication possible, and the design of that latch is the engineering contribution.

The scaling argument is the interesting part, because it predicts that engineered and biological jumpers should diverge in design and not converge. Biology optimizes for a single explosive stroke, and engineering optimizes for energy storage per unit mass, and the two optima produce different geometries.

A jumper of 30 metres needs a landing system, a structure that survives the impact and a launcher that can be aimed, and the paper reports those as engineering problems and not as afterthoughts. The mass and the power required scale with the height, which limits how much further the approach can go. A structure that survives a 30-metre landing has to absorb the energy of the fall, and the mass of that structure then subtracts from the height the motor can buy.

An application is planetary exploration, where a robot that can hop over a boulder field is more useful than one that must drive around it, and where the low gravity of a small body makes a jump far cheaper than it is on Earth.

A demonstration is not a field instrument, and the durability of the latch over thousands of cycles is the property that a mission would test. The contribution is the principle, which is that a machine need not respect the energy budget of a muscle. A hopping robot on a small body is a mission concept that has been studied for two decades, and the limiting factor has been the energy density of the actuator and not the idea.
