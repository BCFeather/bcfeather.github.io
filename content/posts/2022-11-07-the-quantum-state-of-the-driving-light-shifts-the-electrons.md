Strong-field physics treats a laser pulse as a classical field and computes what its oscillating vector potential does to an electron. The approximation has been good enough to explain high harmonic generation, above-threshold ionization and the attosecond bursts that come out of both, and the quantum nature of the light is left out because a bright laser looks classical. A coherent state has a well-defined phase, a large photon number and a variance that shrinks relative to the mean as the intensity grows, which is why the classical treatment usually suffices.

Work posted to arXiv on 6 November 2022 puts the light's quantum state back into the calculation. The authors extend the strong-field approximation to drivers that are not in a coherent state, and they find that the state contributes a force of its own, which they name the photon-statistics force.

The force acts on the same trajectories the classical theory computes. In high harmonic generation, squeezing the driver, which is experimentally available, shifts and reshapes the electronic paths and moves the emitted attosecond pulses by hundreds of attoseconds. A hundred attoseconds is a large fraction of a pulse that is itself a few hundred attoseconds long, so the shift is not a correction at the edge of measurement.

Why the effect appears is visible in the algebra. Squeezing changes the variance of the field quadrature while keeping the mean, so a trajectory driven by the squeezed vacuum feels a different distribution of kick strengths even at fixed intensity. The electron samples the field many times during a pulse, and how those samples are distributed is a property the mean alone does not record. The classical limit keeps the mean and discards the variance, and discarding it is what removes the force.

What the paper leaves open is the size of the effect in a real experiment. Bright pulses used in attosecond science are far from ideal squeezing, and the authors work with parameters that are proposed rather than demonstrated. A reader also wants a statement about how the force scales with intensity, since a small effect at low intensity could still matter at the focus of a current laser. The paper gives the scaling in its formalism, and the question is whether a source bright enough to show it can be built.

On the theory side the extension is promising because it turns the driver into a control knob. If the quantum state of the light shapes the emission, then squeezing becomes a way to tune a source without changing its energy, and that is a degree of freedom no classical calculation can offer.

The next test is a measurement of the shift in a harmonic spectrum under a controlled amount of squeezing, and the authors state the conditions under which it should be visible. A measurement of that kind would also test whether the quantum correction behaves as the extended theory predicts, which is a check on the formalism and on the experiment at once. In a field where attosecond timing is already the currency, a shift of that size is the kind of number an experimental group can aim at.
