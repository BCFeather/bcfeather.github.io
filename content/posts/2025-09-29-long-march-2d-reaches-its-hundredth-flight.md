A Long March 2D carried two satellites of the Shiyan-30 group into orbit from Xichang at eleven in the morning on 29 September, and the launch was notable for a number rather than a payload: it was the hundredth flight of the model. The Long March 2D is the second Chinese rocket type to reach that total, after the Long March 3B, and the launch was the 598th flight of the Long March family as a whole, a count that includes every vehicle the family has flown since 1970.

Satellites are described as technology test platforms for Earth observation, a category designation rather than a specific mission, and one that the programme has used for twenty years. Shiyan, meaning experiment, has been used for a series of spacecraft since 2004 whose purposes are stated vaguely and whose orbits suggest imaging, electronic monitoring or new sensor development. Two satellites on one flight is the usual arrangement for this series, which flies in pairs, and the pair is then checked out jointly before being handed to the user.

History of the rocket explains the number. The Long March 2D first flew in 1992, a two-stage vehicle derived from the Long March 4 series and built mainly by the Shanghai Academy of Spaceflight Technology. It carries a modest payload, around a tonne and a half to sun-synchronous orbit, and it has been used for remote sensing, for the Yaogan and Gaofen series, for scientific satellites and for the earlier batches of what later became the Guowang constellation.

A hundred flights of a single model is an achievement of manufacturing as much as of engineering. Rockets are built in small numbers, and the ones that fly often are the ones whose production line has been kept open, whose components have been standardized and whose launch procedures have been written down often enough to be routine. The Long March 2D's rate has risen to several flights a year, which is what produces a hundred in thirty-three, and the last fifty have come in less than a decade, most of them since the constellation work began.

Current programme is framed by the count as well. Chinese launches in 2025 have run at a pace of roughly one a week across all vehicles and all launch sites, and the last hundred flights of the Long March family took place in a fraction of the time the first hundred did. Reusable vehicles, still in testing, are intended to change the arithmetic again, and the expendable workhorses are the base against which their economics will be judged, flight for flight and kilogram for kilogram.

Checkout of the satellites will take the coming weeks, and the orbit they went to has not been published in detail. The rocket, by contrast, has now flown more times than any other Chinese vehicle of its class, and the next one is already on the pad at another site, waiting for a window.
