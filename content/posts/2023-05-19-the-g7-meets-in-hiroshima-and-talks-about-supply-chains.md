Leaders of the Group of Seven met in Hiroshima from 19 to 21 May, in a city chosen for the obvious reason and used, by the Japanese hosts, for a less obvious one. The summit's communique ran to dozens of pages and covered the war in Ukraine, the state of the global economy, the risks of artificial intelligence, and a subject that has become the organizing theme of the group's economic work: the security of supply chains and the tools that states use to control them.

Ukraine was the first item and the one with the most visible result. President Volodymyr Zelensky flew in on the last day, having secured a promise of training for Ukrainian pilots on American-made fighters, which is the first step towards the delivery of aircraft that his government has requested since the beginning of the war. The group also agreed to tighten the enforcement of the price cap on Russian oil, with a new system to track tankers and to punish the insurers and shippers who help the sanctioned trade.

China was the second item, and the language was careful. The communique said that the group would resist economic coercion and would work to reduce critical dependencies, without naming the country that both phrases describe. The leaders agreed to coordinate export controls on semiconductors and on equipment for their manufacture, an area in which the Netherlands and Japan have already imposed restrictions, and to build a mechanism for screening investment in critical minerals.

The economic section of the statement was a description of an odd moment. Inflation in the group's economies has been falling from its peak but remains well above target, growth has been near zero in Europe and is slowing in the United States, and the fiscal space that governments built up during the pandemic has been spent. The leaders recommitted to fiscal sustainability and to a rules-based trading system, in a year in which both are under strain and neither has an enforcement mechanism.

Hiroshima supplied the summit's symbolic centre. The leaders visited the peace memorial and met a survivor of the atomic bombing, and the Japanese prime minister used the setting to argue that nuclear deterrence and disarmament can coexist. The statement on nuclear weapons repeated the group's support for the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons and noted, without enthusiasm, that the New START treaty is the last arms control agreement standing between the two largest arsenals.

The summit's conclusions will be judged by what is delivered in the months after it. Pledges of air-defence systems and of ammunition for Ukraine have a way of arriving late and short, and the enforcement of a price cap depends on the willingness of shippers in third countries to cooperate. The group's members agree more easily on principles than on costs. Hiroshima produced a document that says the right things about a world in which the supply of chips, energy and weapons has become the substance of foreign policy, and it produced little that binds anyone to act.
