Whether mathematical ability is innate or taught has a long and unhelpful history, and imaging has made it empirical. Adults show a distinct network for number, centred on the intraparietal sulcus and connected to frontal regions, and the developmental question is when that network appears and what teaching does to it. Cross-sectional studies cannot separate age from instruction, and the field has needed a longitudinal design.

A study in *PNAS* on 7 July 2026 followed 58 children from preschool through first and second grade, scanning them as they heard mathematical sentences and comparing the response with their progress in school. The design is what makes the result useful: the same children were imaged before formal instruction and again after two years of it, so the change in the network can be attributed to development and to teaching together. The sentences were simple arithmetic statements rather than symbolic problems, which keeps the task close to what a preschooler hears at home.

A specialized cortical network for mathematics was present before schooling began. The regions that responded in preschoolers were the regions that responded in adults, and the strength of the early response predicted later performance on arithmetic tests. Instruction did not create the network; it appeared to sharpen the representation and to strengthen the connections between parietal and frontal components. The parietal response was present in every child, which is itself notable, since a network that appears only after instruction would look different. The frontal contribution grew over the two years, which suggests that instruction adds control rather than creating the representation.

Claim needs care. A response to mathematical sentences in a preschooler is a response to language about number as much as to number itself, and the paradigm cannot fully separate the two. The correlation between early response and later performance is also modest, and a predictor that explains a small share of variance is a research finding rather than a screening tool.

What the study does establish is a baseline. Children arrive at school with a number network already wired, which means that a curriculum built on the assumption of a blank slate is starting from the wrong model. It also means that difficulty in arithmetic can have a neural signature that predates instruction, which is the beginning of an explanation for why some children struggle despite good teaching. Curricula that assume no prior representation may spend time teaching what children already have, rather than building on it.

The practical question is whether the network can be trained. The study shows it responds to teaching, but not whether a particular teaching method changes it more than another. A trial that randomizes classrooms to different approaches and images the children before and after would answer that, and it would be the first evidence that curriculum design can be aimed at a specific circuit. Classroom trials are also the only design that can separate a curriculum effect from a teacher effect, which observational studies cannot.
