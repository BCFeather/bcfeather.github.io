Modi opened the meeting with a short speech about terrorism and connectivity, and then handed the floor to presidents who do not usually share a table. The Shanghai Cooperation Organization held its twenty-third summit on 4 July, hosted by India in the chair's role and conducted entirely by video link, with Xi Jinping, Vladimir Putin and Shehbaz Sharif among the heads of delegation. The business of the day was enlargement. Iran was confirmed as a full member, and Belarus signed the memorandum that commits it to the group's obligations before admission.

Iran's accession has been in preparation since 2021, when the organization accepted it as a dialogue partner and began the paperwork that its charter requires. Membership gives Tehran a seat in a body that describes itself as covering forty per cent of the world's population and a quarter of its land, and it gives the group a member under heavy Western sanctions who is also a candidate for closer ties with China and Russia. Iranian officials presented the step as evidence that the country cannot be isolated, and they are right in the narrow sense that a club of nine has now accepted it.

The organization began in 2001 as a confidence-building arrangement between China, Russia and four Central Asian states, mostly about border security and separatism. Its scope widened after 2017, when India and Pakistan joined, and again after 2022, when the group began to describe itself as the core of a multipolar order. China has used it to argue that regional security should be managed by regional states, which in practice means without American participation, and Russia has used it as evidence that it still has friends.

India's chairmanship has tried to make the group serve Indian purposes. New Delhi pushed language on terrorism that pointed at Pakistan without naming it, promoted its own port and rail projects in Central Asia, and used the platform to argue that connectivity should respect sovereignty, a phrase aimed at China's Belt and Road. The tension is real: India is a member of the Quad with the United States and Japan, and a founding member of a club that Moscow and Beijing would like to see as their alternative to Western institutions.

Whether the group can do anything with its new weight is a different question. Members disagree about Afghanistan, about the Taliban, about pipelines and about the terms of trade, and the body issues communiques rather than orders. Enlargement has made it more representative and less coherent at the same time, which is a familiar fate for clubs built around a shared objection rather than a shared plan.

The Tehran government called the day historic. The next meeting will be held in Kazakhstan, and by then the group will have to say what membership has bought its newest member beyond a photograph and a seat. Belarus, which has been waiting longer than Iran and draws fewer objections from its neighbours, will be watching closely for the answer, since its own admission depends on the same paperwork being judged sufficient twice.
