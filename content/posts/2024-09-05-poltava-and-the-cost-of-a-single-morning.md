On the morning of 3 September, two Russian ballistic missiles struck the city of Poltava, in central Ukraine, well east of the Dnieper and far from the front line that has defined the war since 2022. Targets, according to both sides' accounts, were the buildings of a military communications institute, where cadets had gathered for a ceremony, and a nearby hospital. The strikes killed more than fifty people and wounded several hundred, and within hours they produced the images that have become the war's visual grammar. Gaping walls of a building that was full of young people, rescue teams working through the dust, relatives waiting outside for names to be read. President Zelenskyy called it one of the deadliest attacks of the entire war, and the phrase, measured against a conflict that has killed tens of thousands, was not hyperbole.

Dead included cadets who had not yet seen combat, and the choice of target was the thing that made it feel different even to a country inured to the daily toll. Poltava is a training city, not a combat city; its institute trained the specialists who keep the army's communications running, and the two missiles that arrived at the moment of a morning gathering were not aimed at soldiers in the field but at the system that produces them. Russian defence ministry, with the formula it has used all year, described the strike as a precise hit on a training centre that hosted foreign instructors. Ukrainian officials rejected the claim, and the site itself, a civilian-adjacent campus in a city of 280,000, made it hard to square with any reading of 'precision'.

Strategic timing was not accidental. It came in the week after Ukraine's incursion into Kursk, which Moscow answered by intensifying pressure on the front it already holds rather than by diverting forces. Poltava is the kind of deep-rear target that the Russian military uses its limited supply of ballistic missiles for, choosing moments of maximum human concentration. It was also, unmistakably, a message. The border may have been crossed in Kursk, but the war's centre of gravity remains Ukrainian territory, and the Russian command retains the ability to strike anywhere in the country, at a time of its choosing, against a target of its choosing. It was received, in the form of funerals.

Numbers deserve to be read without the numbness they invite. Fifty-one dead, two hundred and seventy-one wounded, the majority young, the majority in their twenties: a cohort that a normal country would be sending to university, counted out in a single morning because two missiles found a building at the wrong hour. Ukraine has lived through so many such mornings that the global attention paid to each one has decayed to a reflex, a headline, a statement of condemnation, and the decay is itself part of the strategy that makes attacks like Poltava possible: the more ordinary they become, the less they cost the attacker in the court of world opinion, and the cheaper it is to repeat them. War's arithmetic works that way, and the arithmetic is the crime.

There is no way to mourn fifty-one cadets that would restore the attention they deserve, and no way to report the attack that would keep it fresh, and that is the trap the conflict has built for everyone watching it. What can be said, and should be said, is the thing that the war's repetition makes easy to forget: that each of the numbers in the daily reports is a person, that the persons in Poltava were killed on purpose, and that the purposes they were killed for have not changed. Reports will carry new numbers and the same shape, and the shape has not improved.
