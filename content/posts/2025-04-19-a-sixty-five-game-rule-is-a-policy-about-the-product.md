Professional basketball has an eighty-two game regular season and a playoff that decides the champion, and the two are in conflict. A team that rests a star for twelve games improves his chances of being healthy in April, and it sells a ticket to a paying customer who came to see him. That conflict became a policy problem when the resting spread from veterans to players in their twenties. A team that plays a back-to-back on the second night of a road trip is a team that has already decided which game matters.

A rule negotiated with the players' association requires a player to appear in sixty-five games to be eligible for the individual awards that determine contract bonuses and legacy. It also restricts teams from resting two healthy stars in the same game and gives the league office the power to investigate. The mechanism is a financial incentive rather than a ban.

Its members agreed because the awards matter to them. A player who makes an all-league team qualifies for a higher maximum contract, and a rule that ties that money to attendance gives the player an interest in playing. A player who is genuinely injured is protected, and the boundary between injured and managing a chronic condition is where the disputes happen. A player who misses twenty games and still makes an all-league team is a player who has been judged on a sample.

Medical argument is legitimate. Soft-tissue injuries accumulate with fatigue, and a schedule with four games in five nights is a schedule that produces them. The teams that have invested in sports science want to manage minutes, and the rule pushes them to manage them inside a game rather than by absence. A chronic knee condition is a condition that a player can play through and should not, and the rule does not distinguish.

Commercial argument is equally legitimate. A national broadcast that features a team without its best player loses audience, and the league's media contracts are priced on the assumption that the best players appear. A rule that protects the product is a rule that protects the revenue that pays the players. A broadcast that pays for a package of games is paying for the expectation that the stars appear in most of them.

What the rule does not fix is the calendar. An eighty-two game season is a length that was set by the number of arenas and the number of dates, and a shorter season would reduce both the injury risk and the revenue. The league has discussed a tournament that replaces some regular-season games, and the tournament would be a way to add meaning to games that currently have little. A shorter season would need the arenas to accept fewer dates, and the arenas are owned by the same people who own the teams. A season that is shorter is a season that is worth more per game, and the league has not yet decided whether to test that.
