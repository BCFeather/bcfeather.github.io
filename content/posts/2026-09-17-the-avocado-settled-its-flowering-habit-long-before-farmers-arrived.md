An avocado tree bears male and female flowers on the same plant, which sounds convenient and is not, because the two sexes open at different times of day. Some varieties open female in the morning and male in the afternoon, others reverse the order, and a tree cannot pollinate itself unless the timing overlaps. Growers have known this for a century and have planted complementary varieties in every orchard, and the genetics behind the two schedules was worked out only recently.

A study of 374 avocado plants has now traced the difference to a region on chromosome 10, where two haplotypes of the transcription factor gene SDMYB separate the flowering types. The authors also examined closely related species and found that both haplotypes have deep evolutionary histories, which is the signature of long-term balancing selection. The work appeared in *PNAS* and was summarized in *Science* on 17 September. Every commercial orchard is therefore a hybrid of two breeding strategies that evolved to keep a wild population cross-pollinating.

Balancing selection is the unusual case in which two variants are both maintained because each does better in some circumstances. In a population of self-incompatible plants, the circumstances are the presence of a partner with the opposite schedule, and a rare variant has an advantage simply by being rare. The avocado haplotypes look old enough to have persisted through many generations of wild trees before anyone planted an orchard.

For breeders the finding is immediately practical. Seedlings take years to flower, and a grower cannot tell which schedule a young tree will follow until it does, which delays the design of an orchard by half a decade. A genetic test that reads the haplotype at chromosome 10 identifies flowering type at the seedling stage, and the paper notes that the characterized variants allow exactly that. That interval is long enough that growers plant complementary varieties on faith and discover the mismatch when the first harvest disappoints.

Reading the evolutionary argument is harder to pin down. Deep divergence between haplotypes can also arise from a hybridization event that brought a distant copy of the gene into the population, and distinguishing ancient polymorphism from introgression requires more sequence from wild relatives. The authors make the case for balancing selection with the pattern of variation around the locus, and the alternative will be raised by anyone who works on tree genomes.

Commercial agriculture has spent a century shaping crops to be uniform, and avocados are a reminder that some traits resist uniformity because the plant's mating system depends on diversity. An orchard is a designed population, and the genetics that make it work are the same genetics that kept the species viable in a wild forest where every tree depended on a partner flowering on the opposite schedule. Reading that history from a single locus is a small and satisfying result. Sequence from wild avocado relatives in Mexico and Central America would test whether the haplotypes predate domestication or arrived with it.
