Thirteen years after he first took office, Mark Rutte handed in a resignation that he had been edging towards for months. The four parties of his coalition argued through the night of 6 July over family reunification for asylum seekers, failed to reach a compromise, and the next day the government fell. Rutte told the country that the parties had disagreed about migration, that the disagreement was serious, and that his cabinet would become a caretaker administration until an election. Three days later he said he would not stand again.

The dispute sounds technical and runs deeper than its wording. The coalition's junior partner, the Christian Union, wanted to keep a rule that let refugees bring children and spouses within a set period, while Rutte's People's Party for Freedom and Democracy and the Christian Democrats wanted to tighten it. The figures behind the argument are modest by the standards of the region: the Netherlands received around forty-six thousand first asylum applications in 2022, a fraction of what Germany absorbed. Housing shortages and a stretched reception system have made the numbers politically explosive even so.

Rutte's departure will change Dutch politics more than his resignation does. He has run the country since 2010, won four elections, and acquired a reputation for surviving crises that other leaders did not: the euro crisis, the MH17 downing, the childcare benefits scandal, the pandemic, and a nitrogen ruling that forced the government to cut livestock farming. His style was to leave the details to ministers and to keep the coalition together with a combination of patience and threat. Few of his successors will find that easier.

The caretaker period has its own rules. Ministers stay on to run the country but may not take decisions that the outgoing parliament would consider controversial, which limits what can be done about migration, housing and the nitrogen file until a new coalition exists. Election day is 22 November, and the campaign will be fought while a caretaker government administers the files that caused the collapse.

Prospects for the vote are unsettled. The Farmer-Citizen Movement, which swept the provincial elections in March on the back of the nitrogen protests, has never fought a national campaign. The far-right Forum for Democracy and the Freedom Party will compete for the same voters, and the left is fragmented between Labour and the Greens. Rutte's party must find a candidate who can hold together a coalition that has just demonstrated it cannot hold together on its own.

A caretaker cabinet will keep the lights on, and an election in November will decide who turns them off. Until then the files that broke the coalition, from asylum reception to nitrogen permits, sit with ministers who lack the authority to settle them. Rutte leaves behind a country that has been governed for a generation by a man who was very good at keeping governments alive, and a political system that has just shown how quickly one can die. His party will spend the summer looking for someone who can do half as well.
