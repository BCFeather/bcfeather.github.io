Leaders of the alliance met in Vilnius on 11 and 12 July and produced a communique that changed the mechanics of enlargement without changing the timetable. Ukraine's path to membership, which had run through a two-step process beginning with a Membership Action Plan, was shortened to one step. The text repeated the promise made at Bucharest in 2008 that Ukraine would join, added that its future lies in NATO, and attached a condition: an invitation would come when allies agree that conditions are met.

Zelenskyy had spent the weeks before the summit arguing for a date, and he described the draft language as vague and unprecedented on the eve of his arrival. He then attended the inaugural meeting of the new NATO-Ukraine Council, stood beside Stoltenberg and other leaders, and left with a shorter path and a package of multi-year assistance rather than with a calendar. The shift in tone was visible in the communique's other clauses: the alliance promised to keep supplying weapons, training and intelligence for as long as it takes, and the Group of Seven issued a separate declaration of long-term security commitments the same afternoon.

Military planning got most of the attention inside the hall. Allies agreed new regional defence plans that assign specific forces and responsibilities to specific countries for the first time since the Cold War, and they turned the target of spending two per cent of GDP on defence from an aspiration into a floor. Stoltenberg told the closing press conference that the plans required more troops on higher readiness and more ammunition stocks, and that the money would have to follow. Allies also agreed to simplify how Ukraine's requests are assessed and to fund a multi-year programme of training and equipment in place of the ad hoc packages of the past year.

Sweden's application moved at the same meeting. Erdogan had blocked ratification for a year, citing Kurdish groups he considers terrorists and demanding that Stockholm extradite people he names. On the eve of the summit he agreed to send the protocol to the Turkish parliament, and Sweden's prime minister called it a good day. Hungary's ratification followed the Turkish signature in January, and the alliance gained its thirty-second member six months later.

The gap between the promise and the invitation is the substance of the bargain. Members will not commit to Article 5 for a country at war, because that would mean going to war, and Ukraine will not accept a membership that can be postponed indefinitely without other guarantees. The G7 declaration is the compromise: bilateral pledges of weapons, money and intelligence over years, negotiated country by country, which will be tested at the next election in every capital that signed it.

Vilnius answered the question of how Ukraine might join and left open the question of when. That the answer satisfied nobody at the summit is a fair description of a compromise between thirty-one governments that each face their own voters. Ukraine's defence minister will now sit in some allied meetings, a small step that matters in a system built on consensus.
