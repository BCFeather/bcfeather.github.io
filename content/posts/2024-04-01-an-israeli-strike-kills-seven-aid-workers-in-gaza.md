description: "Seven employees of World Central Kitchen were killed when their marked convoy was struck in Gaza on 1 April, and the charity suspended its operations while Britain, Australia and Poland demanded an explanation from Israel."
---

Seven employees of World Central Kitchen were killed in Gaza on the evening of the first of April when the three vehicles of their convoy were struck as they left a warehouse in Deir al-Balah after unloading aid. Charity said the vehicles were clearly marked, were travelling on a route that had been coordinated with the Israeli military, and that the Israeli military had been told of their movements. Several of the dead were foreign nationals, which made the incident a diplomatic matter within hours.

Names of the dead were published by the charity. They included Zomi Frankcom, an Australian chef who had managed kitchens in several countries, Damian Soból of Poland, Jacob Flickinger, a dual citizen of Canada and the United States, and three former British soldiers, John Chapman, James Henderson and James Kirby. The seventh was Saif Abu Taha, a Palestinian driver who had worked for the organization since its earliest operations in Gaza.

Account of the strike was disputed from the start. The military said its forces had identified a vehicle carrying what an officer described as a threat and had struck it, and it said the convoy had been hit at night in an area where its troops were operating. The charity said it had received no warning and that the vehicles had been moving along a route the army had approved. Survivors of other aid convoys said markings were no protection against misidentification after dark.

Investigation published a week later found that the army had violated its own procedures. Military said the officers who authorized the strike had acted on a mistaken assessment and had failed to confirm that the vehicles belonged to a humanitarian organization. Two officers were dismissed and several others were reprimanded, and the army said the case would be examined by its advocate general. The charity said it would not accept an inquiry conducted by the same institution that had carried out the strike.

Reaction from governments was unusually direct. The British prime minister said the dead had been doing work that the world needed, and he demanded a full and transparent explanation. Australia's prime minister made a similar demand and said his country would examine the findings. Poland's foreign minister said the incident would test whether Israel treated the lives of foreign aid workers as it treated the lives of its own citizens.

Consequence for the aid operation was immediate and lasting. World Central Kitchen suspended its work in Gaza and said it would pause operations in other countries while it reviewed its security arrangements. It resumed deliveries at the end of April with new procedures and a smaller footprint, and several other agencies said the strike had made them more cautious about coordinating with the military.

Scale of the loss is part of a larger figure. The United Nations says more than two hundred aid workers have been killed in Gaza since October, most of them Palestinian employees of the agency that provides most of the territory's food and health services. That number is the highest recorded in a single conflict in the organization's history, and it has made the delivery of aid dependent on a system whose participants cannot be protected.
