description: "A printing route that sinters a nanoparticle ink at low temperature produces optical-grade glass structures, according to work published in *Science* on 2 June 2023."
---

Glass is made by melting, and melting is hot. A process that prints glass therefore seems to require a furnace, which is why printed glass has been opaque or coarse.

The route in *Science*, 2 June 2023 avoids the furnace. The authors print a colloidal ink of silica nanoparticles, dry it, and consolidate it at a temperature low enough that the structure survives, producing a transparent solid.

The consolidation is the chemistry. A nanoparticle's surface has silanol groups, and a catalyst promotes their condensation into siloxane bridges at a temperature far below the bulk glass transition.

The shrinkage is the problem. A printed body loses volume as the particles fuse, and a structure designed at the ink's scale will be smaller after consolidation. The authors' compensation is built into the design.

The optics are the test. A transparent body scatters little light, and the authors measure transmission across the visible range to show that the residual porosity is below the level that would scatter.

The resolution is the second metric. A printed feature's edge is set by the nozzle and by the ink's spreading, and the authors' smallest features are at the scale that micro-optics needs.

The application is micro-optics. A lens or a waveguide can be printed directly on a chip, and a process that does not need a furnace can be integrated with electronics that a furnace would destroy.

The mechanical property is the caveat. A consolidated nanoparticle body is not a melt-formed glass, and its strength and its index of refraction differ from the bulk material's.

The scalability is unknown. A laboratory print is a small object, and a process that must fill a wafer is a process with a different set of problems.

What the paper does not show is long-term stability. A porous body can adsorb water, and an optical component that changes with humidity is a component with a drift.

For photonics, the value is a fabrication route that fits a clean room. A furnace is incompatible with a finished wafer, and a low-temperature route is compatible with one.

The ink's formulation is the enabling detail. A colloid that is stable enough to print and reactive enough to consolidate is a narrow window, and the authors' solvent and stabilizer choices define it.

The sintering's uniformity is the quality issue. A body that consolidates unevenly develops density gradients, and a gradient in density is a gradient in refractive index, which distorts a wavefront.

The comparison with polymer printing is the useful frame. A polymer resin cures at room temperature and has poor thermal stability, and glass has the stability and not the process. The paper closes that gap.

The optical design freedom is the payoff. A printed lens can be a freeform surface, and a freeform surface corrects aberrations that a spherical one cannot.

The cost is the open question. A colloidal ink and a low-temperature cure may be cheaper than a furnace process, and the economics depend on the ink's price and the yield.
