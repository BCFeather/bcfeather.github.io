The idea that forests will absorb more carbon as the atmosphere grows richer in carbon dioxide rests on experiments in young plantations, where trees are small, fast-growing and unconstrained. Whether a mature forest, with tall trees and closed canopies, still responds has been the uncertainty that models could not resolve. Every carbon budget carries an assumption about how much of the emitted carbon land will take back, and forests are the largest uncertain term.

A paper in *Nature Climate Change* on 12 August 2024 reports a test in a 180-year-old woodland of *Quercus robur* in central England. The stand was exposed to free-air carbon dioxide enrichment for seven years, which raises the concentration around the canopy without enclosing the trees in a chamber. Free-air enrichment avoids the chamber effect, in which enclosure changes temperature, humidity and wind, so the response measured is closer to a real forest.

Woody biomass production increased. The authors combined tree-ring analysis, which gives annual growth resolved to the year, with canopy laser scanning, which gives the three-dimensional distribution of leaves and stems, so that the increase could be attributed to wood rather than to foliage. Tree rings give the history, and the laser scan gives the current structure, so the gain can be checked against both records.

Carbon also moved downward. Elevated carbon dioxide increased exudation from fine roots into the soil, a flux that feeds microbial communities and that can change nutrient availability. A forest that allocates more carbon below ground is a forest that alters its own soil chemistry. Root exudates are a small flux compared with wood, and their effect on soil carbon depends on what the microbes do with them.

That finding supports the fertilization effect in a stand that models had treated as saturated. If old forests continue to respond, then the terrestrial sink is larger than the assumptions built into some carbon budgets, and the projected rise in atmospheric concentration is correspondingly slower. If mature stands respond, then the sink strengthens as concentration rises, which is a positive feedback on uptake rather than a fixed capacity.

Limits are real. One species in one soil type over seven years cannot represent the boreal and tropical forests where most of the world's biomass sits, and nutrient limitation, especially of nitrogen and phosphorus, may cap the response elsewhere. Tropical forests hold more carbon but also more phosphorus-poor soils, so the response there may be smaller than this experiment suggests.

The below-ground flux also cuts both ways. Exudation can stimulate microbes that release carbon from old organic matter, so a gain in wood may be partly offset by a loss from the soil, and the paper's measurements do not settle that balance. What the experiment establishes is that maturity is not a barrier. A woodland a century and a half old still answers an increase in carbon dioxide with more wood. Microbial priming is the mechanism that could undo the gain, and it is measured in few forests and modelled poorly.
