Gazprom said on Friday that the Nord Stream pipeline would stay closed beyond the three days of maintenance that were due to end that evening, and it gave the reason as an oil leak in a turbine at a compressor station. The line had been running at about a fifth of its capacity since July and had been shut altogether for ten days in the summer. The announcement came hours after the group of seven finance ministers agreed a price cap on Russian crude, which the Kremlin described as a mistake. Gas prices in Europe rose by about thirty per cent on the day.

The technical explanation is contested. Siemens, which makes the turbines, said the equipment it had inspected gave no grounds for a shutdown of that length, and the German government said the leak was a pretext. Gazprom had earlier refused to take delivery of a turbine that had been serviced in Canada and shipped back through Germany, citing the sanctions regime. The pipeline's operators published no pressure data after the announcement, and the flow meter on the German side showed zero.

Europe had spent the summer filling storage, and the reservoirs were at about eighty per cent of capacity when the news arrived. That is enough for a normal winter and not enough for a cold one with no Russian gas at all. Governments had already agreed to cut demand by fifteen per cent, and the agreements allow them to ration industry if the winter turns severe. The price of electricity in France and Germany, which is set by the cost of the last plant that runs, rose with the gas price within an hour.

The politics around the pipeline have hardened with each closure. Nord Stream 2 had been completed and never certified, and the German government suspended it days before the war. The Nord Stream 1 route through the Baltic had been the main artery for Russian gas since 2011, and its loss leaves Ukraine's transit route and the pipelines through Turkey as the remaining ways in. Poland and the Baltic states had argued for years that the project was a political instrument, and they now say the events have proved it.

Nord Stream belongs to no private quarrel between a supplier and a customer. The Russian budget depends on the revenue, and the European industry depends on the molecules, and each side has been trying to make the other absorb the loss first. Gazprom's profits rose in the first half of the year because the price rose faster than the volume fell, which is the arithmetic that makes a partial shutdown rational for the seller. The European response has been to buy elsewhere at a higher price and to build terminals for liquefied gas as fast as the permits allow.

What happens next depends on the weather and on whether the line reopens at all. Storage in Germany would last about two months at average consumption if the taps stayed closed, and the government has said it will keep two plants running past their planned closure dates. The price on the Dutch benchmark settled above two hundred euros per megawatt hour, which is ten times the level of a year earlier. Households have been told to expect bills three times larger than last winter, and the bills arrive in October.
