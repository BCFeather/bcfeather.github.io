Core consumer prices in Japan rose 2.3 per cent in the year to December, the slowest pace in eighteen months, according to the report published on 19 January. The headline measure rose 2.6 per cent. The figure was a tenth below the forecast, and the fall came from the energy line rather than from the services, which the board watches more closely. The figure was a tenth lower than the month before and in line with the forecast, and the board is expected to end the policy of negative rates in the spring.

Prices have been above the target for nearly two years, the longest stretch since the 1990s, and the board's argument is that the inflation is now driven by the domestic wages rather than by the imported energy. A measure that falls slowly while the wages rise is a measure that the bank treats as healthy. Services prices rose 2.3 per cent, which is the fastest since 2015, and the increase came from the restaurants, the hotels and the transport.

Wages are the evidence that the board has been waiting for. The negotiations that begin in the spring will set the pay for the year, and the early indications from the large employers suggest increases of about five per cent. A wage round that is generous is a signal that the deflationary mindset has ended. The largest employers have agreed to increases of more than five per cent in the first round, and the smaller firms usually follow within a month. The negotiations between the unions and the large employers begin in March, and the settlements are the evidence the board has said it needs.

Currency is the complication. The yen has weakened against the dollar as the American rates have stayed high, and the imported prices that follow are the reason the bank has been willing to move even while the growth is sluggish. A currency that falls is a tightening that the bank did not choose. The authorities have not intervened in the currency market this year, and the officials have said they are watching the speed of the moves rather than the level.

Government debt is the constraint on the pace. The bank holds a large share of the government debt, and a rise in the policy rate would raise the cost of servicing it, which the finance ministry has described as the main risk to the budget. A balance sheet that is the market cannot be reduced quickly. The bank has said it will end the negative rate before it begins to reduce the bond holdings, and the order of the two steps is the subject of the debate inside the board.

What the month shows is that the conditions for the first rate rise in seventeen years are nearly in place, and the decision now depends on the wage data that arrive in the spring. A country that has spent a generation expecting prices to fall is a country whose households and firms have to learn how to behave when they rise, and that learning is the risk in the exit.
