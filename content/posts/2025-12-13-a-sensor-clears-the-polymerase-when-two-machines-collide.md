DNA carries two processes at once. The replisome copies the double helix while RNA polymerase transcribes it, and the two machines travel along the same track. Head-on collisions are the well-studied case: the polymerase and the fork approach each other, and the fork stalls. Co-directional conflicts are the quieter version, where the polymerase moves ahead of the fork in the same direction and the fork catches up from behind.

The study in *Nature*, 8 October 2025 identifies the sensor that handles the second case. Jake Kloeber, Bin Chen, Zhenkun Lou and colleagues find that KCTD10 binds both the replication machinery and the transcription complex, and that it marks the polymerase for ubiquitination and removal when the two are in conflict.

The geometry explains why a sensor is needed. A head-on collision is visible as a stall; a co-directional one looks, from the fork's perspective, like slow traffic. The fork cannot distinguish a polymerase that is transcribing normally from one that is about to become an obstacle, and the cell needs a way to clear the track before the fork arrives.

KCTD10 is a member of a family of proteins that assemble cullin-based ubiquitin ligase complexes, and its role as a sensor is consistent with that biochemistry. It recognizes a state rather than a molecule: the conflict itself is the signal, and the response is the removal of the transcription machinery rather than the removal of the fork.

Removal is the gentler option. Stopping the fork would leave a lesion that has to be repaired; removing the polymerase lets the fork pass and the gene be re-transcribed afterwards. The cost is a transcript that was cut short, and the cell tolerates that cost in exchange for finishing replication on time.

The consequences of losing the sensor are what the paper measures. Cells without KCTD10 accumulate replication stress, and the accumulation is worse in regions that are heavily transcribed, which is the pattern the model predicts. Cancer cells, which transcribe aggressively and replicate without checkpoints, are a plausible place where the loss matters clinically.

What the study does not settle is the range of conflicts it covers. Transcription and replication can collide in several configurations, and the sensor may handle only one of them; the others would need their own machinery. The paper's experiments are in cell lines, and the tissue-level consequences of the loss have not been followed.

The field's interest is in the general principle. A cell that coordinates two machines on one track needs sensors, and the ones identified so far are few. Each new sensor is a target for a drug that would make a cancer cell's own transcription an obstacle to its own replication, which is a way of attacking a tumour without attacking the process itself.

Therapeutic interest is indirect but real. A tumour that depends on removing polymerases to finish replication is a tumour with a vulnerability, and an inhibitor of the sensor would convert that dependency into a stall. Whether such a molecule exists is a chemistry question that the structural work now makes askable.
