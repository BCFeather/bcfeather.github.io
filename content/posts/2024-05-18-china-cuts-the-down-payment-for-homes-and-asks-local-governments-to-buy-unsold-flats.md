China lowered the minimum down payment for a first home to fifteen per cent and removed the national floor on mortgage rates on 17 May, and it set up a three-hundred-billion-yuan facility for local governments to buy unsold flats. It was the largest single easing since the property slump began three years ago. The facility is meant to be used by the state-owned enterprises that the local governments control, and the pace at which they buy depends on the price that the banks are willing to lend against.

Arithmetic explains the urgency. Sales of new homes have fallen by more than a fifth this year, the developers that borrowed abroad have defaulted, and the local governments that sold land to fund themselves have lost the revenue that paid for their services. A market that provides a third of the economy's growth is a market that cannot be left to find its own floor. The developers that are still solvent have been selling at a discount to raise cash, and a buyer who expects that discount to widen has no reason to sign this month.

Down payment is the lever the authorities can pull without spending money. A household that needed thirty per cent now needs fifteen, which is a subsidy delivered through the banking system rather than through the budget. A rule that is relaxed is a stimulus that costs nothing on the day it is announced. The banking system carries the risk, because a loan with a smaller deposit is a loan that loses more if the price falls, and the lenders have been told to keep their standards.

Buying facility is the harder part. Three hundred billion yuan is small against the stock of unsold housing, and the local governments that would run the programme are the ones whose finances are already stretched. A fund that is financed by the same institutions that are under stress is a fund that moves slowly. The programme also requires the local governments to find tenants for the flats they buy, which in the cities that have lost population is a problem that the facility does not solve.

Confidence is the question the measures do not answer. Households have watched prices fall for two years and have learned that waiting is cheaper than buying, and a cheaper deposit does not change that calculation unless the price stops falling. A market that expects declines is a market where credit is cheap and unused. The savings rate has risen as the households have repaid mortgages early, and the money that would have gone into a deposit is now sitting in a bank account that pays almost nothing.

What the package shows is that the authorities have moved from supporting the developers to supporting the buyers, and the test is whether a household that has lost faith in the price can be persuaded by a lower deposit. A policy that lowers the cost of buying is a policy that assumes the buyers are waiting, and the evidence from the last two years is that many of them are leaving instead.
