title: "Pain neurons instruct the immune cells that patrol the skin"

description: "Sensory neurons that signal pain also direct dendritic cells, attracting them with a chemokine, tuning their inflammatory programme with a neuropeptide and coupling to them electrically, according to work published in *Science* on 31 March 2023."
---

Nerves and immunity meet in the skin, and the meeting has been treated as a coincidence of location. A nerve ending and a dendritic cell occupy the same tissue, and the assumption has been that they act in parallel rather than in conversation.

The work in *Science*, 31 March 2023 documents the conversation. The authors show that nociceptors, the neurons that carry pain signals, control dendritic cells in at least three ways, each with its own molecular channel.

The first is recruitment. A nociceptor releases CCL21, a chemokine that attracts dendritic cells to the tissue and holds them there. A nerve that is active therefore sets the density of the immune cells around it.

The second is programming. When triggered, the neuron releases CGRP, a neuropeptide that induces a poised gene programme in the dendritic cell, a state in which the cell is ready to respond but not yet responding.

The third is electrical. The neuron can drive calcium flux and membrane depolarization in the dendritic cell, so that the immune cell's activity is coupled to the neuron's. A direct electrical link between a neuron and an immune cell is an unusual finding.

The three mechanisms are context-dependent. The paper shows that the route used depends on the state of the tissue, which is what distinguishes a physiological conversation from a fixed wiring diagram.

The immunological consequence is a pain signal that shapes inflammation. An injury that activates nociceptors recruits and programs the cells that present antigen, and the result is an inflammatory response that is tuned by the nervous system.

The clinical implication is a target. If pain neurons set the tone of local immunity, then blocking a neuropeptide is a way to change an inflammatory disease, and several such drugs already exist for other indications.

The limitation is the model. Skin in a mouse is not skin in a person, and the density of innervation and the subtypes of dendritic cells differ.

What the paper does not measure is the consequence for infection. A dendritic cell that is poised may present antigen more effectively or less, and the outcome for a pathogen is the test that the mechanism needs.

For neuroimmunology, the value is a vocabulary. Recruitment, programming and coupling are three distinct verbs, and a field that had one word for the interaction now has three.

The anatomy supports the claim. Nerve endings in the dermis lie close to the dendritic cells that patrol it, and the paper's imaging shows the contact. Proximity is not proof, and the three mechanisms are what turn proximity into interaction.

The neuropeptide's receptor is the handle. CGRP acts through a G-protein-coupled receptor on the immune cell, and a receptor that is expressed on a cell type is a target that a drug can reach without touching the neuron.

The electrical coupling is the most surprising element. Immune cells are not excitable in the way neurons are, and a depolarization that changes their behaviour implies a channel that the paper identifies. That channel is the mechanistic core of the third route.
