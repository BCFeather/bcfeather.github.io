A title defended is harder than a title won, and England defended theirs in the least comfortable way available: a 1-1 draw after extra time in Basel, a shoot-out that they won 3-1, and a winning penalty taken by a substitute who had come on in the last minutes of the match.

Spain were the better team for long stretches. They had more of the ball, more shots and more territory, and they equalized within ten minutes of conceding, which is what a side with a coherent system does when it goes behind in a final. A shoot-out that ends 3-1 is a shoot-out decided by two saves and not by two misses, and the goalkeeper who made them had been the second choice at the start of the tournament, which is the depth that a defending champion needs and rarely has.

England's plan was to survive and to strike. The midfield sat deep, the wingers waited for transitions, and the goal came from a break that started in their own half, which is a plan that looks passive until it produces a lead and looks exhausted once the lead is gone.

The shoot-out was decided by preparation. England's goalkeeper saved two penalties, the takers had rehearsed their routines for two years, and Spain missed the kind of penalty that a team misses when it has spent the tournament winning comfortably and has not been tested from twelve yards. A plan that depends on transitions is a plan that needs a lead, and once Spain equalized the English midfield had to play in a shape it had not rehearsed for sixty minutes, which is why the last half hour was the least controlled of their tournament. A substitute who takes the decisive penalty is a substitute who was chosen for that job, and the selection sheet that named her in the eightieth minute was prepared before the tournament began.

The tournament itself grew. Sixteen teams played across eight Swiss venues, attendances rose at every stage, and the final drew a full stadium and a television audience that the organisers will use to argue for a larger format again, which is the argument that every successful women's tournament now produces.

Legacy is the part that is harder to measure. A second consecutive title puts the team in the small group that has defended a major championship, and it puts the domestic league in a position to sell its rights at a higher price, which is how a national success becomes a club revenue. A full stadium in Basel and a television audience that beat the group stage record is the commercial evidence that the expansion worked, and the next format decision will be taken on that evidence and not on the quality of the football.

What would settle the next cycle is whether the squad rebuilds before the World Cup. Four starters are over thirty, the goalkeeper is 34, and a manager who has won twice will be given the room to replace them slowly, which is the advantage that a title buys.
