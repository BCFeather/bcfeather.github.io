Adobe announced the acquisition on 15 September, and the price was 20 billion dollars in cash and shares. Figma was founded in 2012 and had raised 333 million dollars before the deal, so the return to its investors was several times the money they had put in. The price was about 50 times the company's annual recurring revenue. The announcement came two weeks before Adobe reported results, and the shares fell 17 per cent on the day.

Figma makes a browser-based design tool that several people can use at once. It had grown because designers could share a link rather than a file, and because it worked on any machine. Its customers included 4 million registered users and most of the large technology companies, and its revenue was reported at 400 million dollars. Its free tier had brought in students, and the paid conversion rate was reported at 8 per cent.

Adobe's own design software is installed on the desktop and licensed by subscription. The company had tried to move into collaborative editing with a product launched in 2020, and it had not caught on. Buying the competitor was the alternative, and the company said it would keep Figma as a separate product rather than folding it into the suite. Adobe's subscription revenue had grown 12 per cent in the quarter, the slowest in three years.

Figma's chief executive would receive a payment of 800 million dollars if the deal closed, and the filing disclosed that the two companies had discussed the price for four months. The terms included a break fee of 1 billion dollars, which is large for a deal of this kind. The premium over the last private valuation was about double. The companies said they expected the deal to close in 2023, and that date slipped twice.

Regulators were the risk. The Department of Justice began a review in 2023, and it sued to block the deal in 2024 on the ground that the merger would remove a competitor from a market Adobe already dominated. The companies contested the theory and said the market for design tools was crowded. Customers were worried about the opposite problem. Figma's users feared that the product would be absorbed into Adobe's suite and that the price would rise. Several large design teams said publicly that they were evaluating alternatives, and one competitor reported a 30 per cent rise in sign-ups in the week after the announcement.

Economists who studied the market said the overlap was in the segment that mattered, which is professional design inside large organizations. The companies defended the deal on the ground that they served different customers and that the overlap was small. The review took two years and cost both companies several hundred million dollars. The deal was abandoned in December 2023 after the regulators in Britain and the European Union raised objections. Adobe paid the 1 billion dollar break fee, and Figma remained independent. Its valuation in the private market was marked down in 2023 and recovered in 2024. The chief executives of both companies testified before the Senate in 2023, and the hearing changed no votes.
