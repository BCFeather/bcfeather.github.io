Precision spectroscopy compares the spectrum of a star against a reference, and the reference has to be a ruler with lines spaced so regularly that a shift of one part in a million can be read. Laser frequency combs supply that ruler in the infrared, where oscillators are mature, and the ultraviolet has remained out of reach. Without a reference whose lines are known to a fraction of a linewidth, a measured shift is an instrumental artefact as much as a Doppler signal, and the difference decides whether a detection is believed.

A paper in *Nature Communications* on 2 September 2024 extends the ruler into that range. The authors generate an astrocomb below 400 nanometres by driving a chip-integrated, periodically poled lithium niobate waveguide, a device that converts infrared light into its harmonics through a highly nonlinear optical response. Harmonic generation in a nonlinear crystal is the standard route between spectral regions, and a waveguide concentrates the light so that the conversion happens at modest pump power.

Two obstacles had stood in the way. Material dispersion broadens the pulses that a comb depends on, and the spectral distance between an established infrared oscillator and the ultraviolet output is large, so a single conversion stage cannot bridge it efficiently. The waveguide geometry addresses both at once. A comb converted directly from an infrared oscillator lands where the phase relation between modes has already drifted, so the conversion has to be engineered rather than simply attempted.

Chip integration is the practical advance. A device that fits on a photonic circuit can be stabilized and deployed without the free-space optics that laboratory combs require, and a spectrograph at a telescope needs a source that survives transport and temperature swings. A photonic circuit also allows several functions to be combined on one substrate, so that filtering, amplification and conversion no longer occupy separate optical benches.

The calibration itself is demonstrated against an astronomical spectrograph, which is the test that matters, since a comb's spacing has to survive the instrument's own optics and detectors before it can be used. The measurement compares comb lines against known features of the spectrograph, and the residuals are what an observer would use to trust the wavelength scale on a real exposure.

Ultraviolet lines are valuable because many atomic transitions of interest sit there, including lines used to measure the variability of fundamental constants and to trace the expansion history of the universe. A calibration source in that band makes those measurements possible with the same technique already used in the infrared. They also carry transitions of atoms and ions that trace the chemistry of stars and the state of the interstellar medium, which is why the band has been requested for decades.

The paper does not report a wavelength solution across the whole ultraviolet band, and efficiency falls at the shortest wavelengths, where the material absorbs. Whether the device holds its calibration over a night of observing is the next question. What would settle the question is a run at a telescope, where the source has to survive a night and the spectrograph has to hold its own calibration.
