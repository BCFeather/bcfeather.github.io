description: "Freight rail volumes fell 4 per cent last year and the decline is almost entirely coal. Removing coal from the series leaves a network whose volumes are flat, whose revenue per tonne-kilometre is rising, and whose capacity is suddenly adequate."
---

Rail freight is a bulk business measured in tonne-kilometres, and coal has been its largest single commodity for a century. A network's annual results this month show volumes down 4 per cent, and the decomposition shows that coal accounts for 5.2 of those points.

The arithmetic matters for how the result is read. Coal moved 180 million tonnes last year, against 210 five years earlier, as plants closed. Container traffic rose 3 per cent, grain rose 1, and intermodal now exceeds coal in revenue for the first time.

Revenue per tonne-kilometre is the offsetting trend. Coal travels long distances at a low rate; containers travel shorter distances at three times the rate. A network that loses coal and gains containers loses volume and gains revenue, which is what the results show.

Capacity is the structural gain. A coal train is 100 wagons of one commodity on a fixed route; a container train is 80 wagons with variable destinations and a sorting requirement. The network's yards were built for coal, and their utilization has fallen from 88 to 71 per cent.

Costs are sticky. A coal corridor's maintenance is proportional to axle loadings, and the trains that remain are heavier per wagon than the ones that left. The network reports a 6 per cent rise in track maintenance cost per tonne-kilometre, which is the physical consequence of running fewer, heavier trains.

Labour is the adjustment that the unions contest. Coal terminals employed 4,000 people and container terminals employ 1,200 for the same tonnage, and the network has offered retraining in two locations. The transition is a regional employment question rather than a national one.

The comparison with road is the policy frame. A container that moves by rail instead of road emits a fifth of the carbon per tonne-kilometre, and the network's intermodal growth is partly a response to a carbon price on road haulage in one jurisdiction.

Terminal investment shows the direction. The network has committed 600 million dollars to two intermodal terminals and nothing to coal loading, and its capital plan describes the coal assets as maintained rather than expanded. A terminal that handles containers needs 12 hectares and a reach stacker; a coal loader needs a rotary dumper and a stockpile.

The regulatory frame is the other. Two jurisdictions now price carbon on road haulage and not on rail, which shifts the comparison in rail's favour by 8 per cent per tonne-kilometre. The network's intermodal growth is concentrated in those jurisdictions.

The coal that remains is a contracted flow with a take-or-pay clause. Two utilities have 15-year agreements that require the network to move a minimum tonnage, and those contracts, not the market, set the floor under coal volumes for the next decade.

For a reader, the lesson is that a volume statistic is not a demand statistic. Rail's decline is a commodity leaving the network, and the parts of the business that remain are growing at a rate that would have been invisible beneath the coal number.
