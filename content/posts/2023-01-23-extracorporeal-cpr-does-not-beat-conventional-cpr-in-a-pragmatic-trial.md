description: "A multicentre pragmatic trial finds extracorporeal CPR and conventional CPR give similar survival with a favourable neurologic outcome at 30 days in refractory out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, the trial reports in *The New England Journal of Medicine*."
---

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation can restore circulation when compressions cannot, and single-centre series have reported survival rates that seemed to justify building programmes around it. The question a health system has to answer is whether those rates survive the move from a specialized unit to a set of ordinary hospitals. The trial in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 26 January 2023 randomized patients with refractory out-of-hospital cardiac arrest caused by an initial ventricular arrhythmia to extracorporeal CPR or to conventional advanced life support. It was pragmatic, run across centres and with the logistics each centre could actually provide.

The outcome was survival with a favourable neurologic result at 30 days, and the two arms were similar. The paper's own reading is that reproduction of the earlier single-centre results is not self-evident when the intervention is implemented in practice.

The explanation is time. Extracorporeal CPR works if cannulation happens before the brain and the organs have taken too much ischaemia, and the interval from collapse to flow is the variable that single-centre programmes control and a pragmatic trial cannot. A trial that includes the realistic interval measures the realistic benefit.

Logistics are the hidden intervention. A centre needs a primed circuit, a team that can cannulate at any hour, and a catheterization laboratory ready for the coronary intervention that often follows. Where those exist, the therapy has a chance; where they do not, the trial records the difference.

None of this closes the door. It says that building a programme is not enough, and that the programme has to be organized around the time from collapse to cannulation. Regional systems that route patients directly to a centre with a team waiting are a different intervention from a hospital that calls the team in. The selection question is the next one. Patients with a shockable rhythm and a witnessed collapse are the group with the best chance, and the trial enrolled them. A wider indication, such as an unwitnessed arrest, would dilute the effect further, and a narrower one would raise the ethical difficulty of randomization.

What the trial cannot measure is the cost per survivor. Extracorporeal support consumes staff, circuits and intensive care beds, and a therapy that does not improve survival in the average centre is expensive. The health-economic analysis that follows will decide whether programmes expand or contract. For the family at the bedside, the practical advice is unchanged. Bystander compressions and early defibrillation remain the interventions that matter most, and everything after them is a system's problem.

Neurologic outcome was scored at 30 days, a short window for a brain that may recover slowly after prolonged arrest. A trial that measured function at six months would include the patients who woke late, and their number could change the comparison. The trial was stopped early for futility, which means the confidence interval is wider than the planned one and a small benefit cannot be excluded. Small benefits are what a costly therapy can least afford.
