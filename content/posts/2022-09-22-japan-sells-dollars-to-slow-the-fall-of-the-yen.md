Officials confirmed the intervention the same evening, and they said they would repeat it if the moves continued. The yen had fallen to 145.9 to the dollar in the Tokyo afternoon, a level last reached in 1998. The finance ministry instructed the central bank to sell dollars, and the rate moved to 140.3 within minutes.

Japan held 1.2 trillion dollars of reserves, which gave it room to repeat the operation many times. The authorities had spent 2.84 trillion yen, about 20 billion dollars, according to figures published at the end of the month. That was the largest single-day intervention on record, and it was the first since 2011 for a sale of dollars. The intervention was unilateral. Officials had asked the American Treasury for support and had not received it, according to reports at the time. Without coordination, the operation changes the level for a few days rather than the trend. The yen was back at 145 within three weeks and at 150 by October.

Investors borrowed in yen and bought dollars, and that flow was the trend the ministry was fighting. The cause was the rate gap. The Bank of Japan was the only large central bank still holding its policy rate near zero, and it was buying bonds to cap the ten-year yield. The Federal Reserve had raised its rate to 3 per cent and was signalling more. The dollar had risen 25 per cent against the yen since the start of the year, the fastest in two decades.

Households were the losers, because food and fuel are imported, and the government was preparing a subsidy package to offset the cost. The governor defended the policy in a speech in September, and he said the weak yen was good for some parts of the economy and bad for others. Companies that export were the winners, and their profits reached a record in the year. The bank held 1.2 trillion dollars of reserves, and 20 billion of that was spent in an afternoon.

Importers accelerated their dollar purchases before the operation, and they came back after it, which is the usual pattern. The intervention worked for a week. The ministry had spent 20 billion dollars for a 5 per cent move that lasted ten days. A second covert intervention followed in October, and a third in October after the rate passed 151. The ministry did not confirm those operations until the end of the year, when the totals showed 9 trillion yen spent in the autumn. The yen ended the year at 132 after the American inflation figures softened. Core inflation in Japan was 2.8 per cent in August, the highest in eight years.

December brought a policy change, when the bank widened the band around its yield target. The yen rose 4 per cent in a day, and the ministry said it had not intervened. The band was widened again in January 2023, and the negative rate ended in March 2024.
