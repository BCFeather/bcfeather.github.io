description: "Observations taken fourteen years after a massive star vanished show that every earlier image of the object was a blend of at least three sources, and its remaining luminosity is a tenth to a quarter of the original."

A failed supernova is what happens when a massive star collapses without the bright explosion that theory expects. The star simply leaves the optical sky, and a black hole takes its place, which is hard to prove because the absence of light is also what a dust screen produces. The alternative is a star that dimmed behind dust it had itself expelled, and the two explanations differ in what remains at the centre.

N6946-BH1 became the first plausible candidate for such an event. It had a luminous outburst in 2009, declined sharply in optical brightness, and kept a persistent infrared presence that a dust-obscured star could explain. Follow-up observations have been arguing about that presence ever since. The outburst was bright enough to be seen in a nearby spiral galaxy, and its decline took a few years.

Fourteen years after the disappearance, JWST observed the position with NIRCam and MIRI, reaching a part of the spectral energy distribution that had never been measured, according to a preprint posted on 28 September 2023. The result is not a point source. The near-infrared and mid-infrared instruments together cover the range where a cool, dusty remnant would radiate.

At least three sources are blended in that position, and every earlier observation, before and after the disappearance, is a blend of them. In the near-infrared the object is notably fainter than the progenitor but similar to its brightness in 2017; in the mid-infrared it has brightened relative to the best-fitting progenitor model. Total luminosity now stands at 13 to 25 per cent of the progenitor's.

Dust illuminated by near-ultraviolet radiation would produce the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon features seen in the infrared spectrum, and that is what the spectral energy distribution resembles. Dust explains the infrared without requiring a surviving star, though it does not by itself decide the history. Those comparisons with the progenitor model are the only way to ask what has survived, since no spectrum of the star itself exists.

Stellar merger is what the observations match most naturally, since two stars spiralling together can produce an outburst, a dusty shroud and a fading remnant. The failed supernova hypothesis carries enough theoretical ambiguity that the authors decline to dismiss it either. Both scenarios leave dust, and the difference lies in the mass that remains and in the energy that was released.

Blending is the weakness in the argument, because three sources at one position can be separated only as far as the beam and the model allow, and a single epoch cannot show how the components evolve. A merger would also leave a stellar remnant, which variability could in principle reveal. Spectroscopy at higher resolution is the obvious next step, and continued monitoring would show whether the infrared brightening is a transient. A second epoch would also test the claim that three components are present, which at the moment rests on one image.
