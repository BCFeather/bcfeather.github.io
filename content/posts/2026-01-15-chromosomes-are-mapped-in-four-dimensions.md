A human genome is two metres of DNA packed into a nucleus six micrometres across, and the packing is not random. Regions of the genome that are switched on tend to sit near the nuclear interior, regions that are silenced tend to sit at the periphery, and loops bring distant regulatory elements into contact with the genes they control. Sequencing methods that read contacts between distant stretches of DNA turned this into a measurable structure about fifteen years ago.

The 4D nucleome programme was set up to turn those measurements into a coherent picture, with the fourth dimension being time. The collection published in *Nature* on 15 January describes systematic maps of how chromosomes are organised, how the organisation differs between cell types and how it changes as cells differentiate. The individual papers address different scales, and the integrated view is the collection's collective contribution.

Several findings recur across the papers. Chromosomes occupy territories that are stable enough to be mapped and flexible enough to change during differentiation, and the boundaries of those territories correlate with the boundaries of gene regulation in the cell types examined. Compartments of active and inactive chromatin alternate along a chromosome in patterns that differ between cell types, which is what makes the map useful rather than decorative. The comparison is only as good as the shared standards, and those took three years to agree.

The technical advance is in resolution and in consistency. Earlier maps were made in a few cell lines with methods that produced coarse bins, and comparing them was difficult because the bin sizes and the normalisation schemes differed from laboratory to laboratory. A coordinated set of datasets with shared standards allows a question such as whether a change in folding precedes or follows a change in transcription to be asked across many systems.

Interpretation has a persistent ambiguity. A contact map is a population average, so a contact that appears in every cell is indistinguishable from one that appears in half the cells with high probability. Single-cell versions of the method address that and pay for it with sparsity, and reconciling the two views is where the field's arguments currently live.

Medical relevance is indirect and real. Structural variants that rearrange the genome can move a gene next to a regulatory element that drives it in the wrong tissue, and understanding those effects requires knowing which elements contact which genes in which cell type. Maps of this kind are the reference against which a patient's rearrangement can be interpreted.

Its own summary is careful to describe the maps as a resource. What has been assembled is a coordinate system for the genome's physical organisation, and the biology will be done by people who use it to ask why a particular fold appears when a particular gene switches on. Reference resources earn their keep when a clinician or a geneticist can point to a fold and say what it explains, and that stage has not arrived.
