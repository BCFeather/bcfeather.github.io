---

Malaria kills roughly six hundred thousand people a year, most of them children under five, and a vaccine against a parasite is a harder problem than a vaccine against a virus. The parasite has a life cycle with several stages and thousands of antigens, and the two products now in use target a single protein on the surface of the stage that enters the liver. Efficacy is partial and the schedule is complicated, and the rollout is nonetheless the largest change in malaria control in a decade. A vaccine that reduces severe episodes by three quarters in the first year is a vaccine that changes a paediatric ward.

The first vaccine, RTS,S, was recommended by the World Health Organization in 2021 after a pilot in three countries that followed children for four years. The second, R21, was recommended in 2023 and is cheaper to make and available in larger volumes. Both require four doses in the first two years and a booster later, and both reduce severe disease by a large fraction rather than preventing infection.

Adding a vaccine to a routine schedule is an operational problem. A child who receives four doses has to be brought to a clinic four times, and the clinics that serve rural areas are the ones with the least capacity. Countries that already run immunization programmes have absorbed the new antigen without adding staff, and the ones with weaker systems have struggled. A dose that has to be kept cold is a dose that needs a cold chain, and the cold chain in a rural district is a motorcycle and a cooler box.

Supply was the first constraint and it is easing. Serum Institute of India makes R21 at a scale that can supply the demand, and Gavi has financed the doses for low-income countries. A vaccine that costs a few dollars per dose is affordable at the national level and expensive at the household level, because the visit to the clinic costs a family a day of work.

The combination with existing tools is where the effect will be measured. Bed nets, indoor spraying and seasonal chemotherapy have cut mortality substantially since 2000, and the gains stalled around 2015 as resistance to drugs and to insecticides spread. A vaccine that reduces severe disease by seventy per cent adds to a package that had stopped improving, and the arithmetic of that addition is the reason donors funded it. A district that has bed nets at seventy per cent coverage gains less from a vaccine than a district at thirty, because the two tools overlap.

What the rollout does not do is eliminate the disease. A parasite with a partial vaccine and a mosquito vector will keep circulating, and the coverage that matters is the coverage in the districts where transmission is highest. A clinic that reaches eighty per cent of children in a low-transmission district and fifty per cent in a high one has protected the children who needed it least. A target of elimination requires a vaccine that blocks transmission, and neither of the current products does that well.
