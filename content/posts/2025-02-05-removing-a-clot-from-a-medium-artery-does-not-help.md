description: "A randomized trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* found no benefit from endovascular thrombectomy in strokes caused by occlusion of a medium-sized vessel, with a good outcome in 41.6 per cent against 43.1 per cent with usual care."
---

Stroke treatment was transformed by the demonstration that a clot in a large artery can be pulled out, and the trials that established it changed guidelines within a year. The success raised the question of the next size down: a clot in a medium vessel produces a smaller deficit and a smaller territory, and the procedure is harder because the vessels are thinner, further from the catheter and more likely to be reached only after the clot has organized.

Two trials reported in 2025 tested the question, and the one in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 5 February enrolled patients with occlusion of a medium vessel and randomized them to endovascular treatment plus usual care or to usual care alone. The primary outcome was a modified Rankin score of 0 or 1 at 90 days, which means independence in daily life.

The rates were 41.6 per cent in the treatment group, or 106 of 255 patients, and 43.1 per cent in the usual-care group, or 118 of 274. The adjusted rate ratio was 0.95, with a confidence interval from 0.79 to 1.15, and the *P* value was 0.61, which is as clear a null result as a trial of this size can produce.

Mortality went the other way. Death at 90 days was 13.3 per cent with the procedure and 8.4 per cent with usual care, and the difference appeared in the safety analysis, where symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage was more common after treatment. That asymmetry is the reason a null trial in stroke is not a neutral result: an ineffective procedure that carries a risk of bleeding makes patients worse.

The explanation is anatomical. A medium vessel supplies a smaller territory, and the tissue it supplies may be less eloquent, so the deficit that the clot produces is smaller and the room for improvement is narrower. A procedure that opens the vessel cannot improve a deficit that the patient would have recovered from anyway, and the trial population included many such patients, which is the selection problem that the design could not avoid.

Patient selection is the question that remains. Enrolment was on the basis of imaging that showed an occlusion, and the subgroup analyses suggested that patients with a larger deficit or with a proximal occlusion within the medium range might benefit. A subgroup in a null trial is a hypothesis, and the trials that will test it are already enrolling with stricter imaging criteria and with perfusion imaging that identifies tissue still at risk.

What the result does for practice is to restrain an enthusiasm. Thrombectomy for large-vessel occlusion is one of the most effective interventions in medicine, and the wish to extend it is understandable. A trial that says no to the extension is the kind of evidence that keeps a technique in the population where it works.
