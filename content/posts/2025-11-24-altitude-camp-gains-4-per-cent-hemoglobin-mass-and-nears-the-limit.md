Altitude training raises hemoglobin mass, and the sport's rule sets an upper limit on the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity. A team that spent three weeks at 2,400 metres published its monitoring data this month, and the numbers show how narrow the window between a legitimate gain and a disqualification is.

The camp raised hemoglobin mass by 4 per cent across eight riders, measured by a carbon monoxide rebreathing method that is more reliable than a fingerprick. Two riders reached a hematocrit of 49 per cent, against a threshold of 50.

The rule's threshold is a health measure and not a fairness measure. A hematocrit above 50 carries a risk of thrombosis, and the sport adopted it in the 1990s after a series of deaths. A rider below the threshold is not automatically clean.

Monitoring is the team's defense. Each rider's blood profile is recorded five times a season, and the biological passport compares the profile's variation rather than its absolute value. A camp's rise is expected and a rise outside the model's range is flagged. A camp's altitude, its duration and the rider's training load all enter the result, and the study's single site fixes three of those variables.

Four riders who stayed at sea level supply the control. Four riders stayed at sea level and their hemoglobin mass rose 0.6 per cent, which is the noise level the method has to beat.

The cost is the practical constraint. A three-week camp for eight riders costs 120,000 dollars including accommodation and staff, and a smaller team rents an altitude tent, which produces a third of the effect.

Individual variation is the finding that matters for the rule. The 4 per cent average hides a range from 1 to 7, and the riders who respond most are the ones who approach the threshold. A rule that applies to everyone penalizes the responders.

A rule that caps hemoglobin mass is a health measure, and its threshold sits below the population's upper range. A rider at the limit has a hematocrit that a laboratory would flag in a patient, and the rule's purpose is to keep a sport's participants out of that range.

The measurement is indirect. A carbon monoxide rebreathing test estimates total hemoglobin mass from the dilution of a known dose, and its error is 2 per cent. A 4 per cent gain is twice the error, which is why the study's result survives scrutiny.

A camp's gain decays and the timing of a race is what the calendar is for. A hemoglobin mass gained at 2,500 metres falls by half within three weeks at sea level, and a rider who returns the day before a race carries a mass that a rider who returns two weeks earlier has lost.

One threshold is serving two purposes, and a physiological measurement is what shows the strain. A health limit and a fairness limit are different numbers, and the sport's single threshold is a compromise that a physiological measurement makes visible.
