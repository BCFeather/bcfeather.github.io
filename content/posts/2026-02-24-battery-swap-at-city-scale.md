---

Battery swapping failed in the car market and works in the two-wheeler market, and the reason is the ratio between battery cost and vehicle cost. A car battery is a third of the vehicle's price, which makes it a financial asset the owner will not surrender; a scooter battery is 400 dollars against a 1,200 dollar vehicle, and a rider who does not own the cell can pay per kilometre instead of per purchase.

The network that passed 5,000 stations this month operates on that arithmetic. A rider subscribes for a monthly fee, swaps a depleted pack for a charged one in under a minute at a cabinet outside a convenience store, and the operator retains ownership of every cell. Utilization is the number that decides viability: a cabinet with eight slots needs about 60 swaps per day to pay for itself, and urban delivery fleets provide that density.

Grid connection is the bottleneck. A cabinet with eight chargers draws about 12 kilowatts, and a site needs a three-phase supply, a lease and a fire-safety approval, which in dense Chinese cities takes months. Operators report that permitting, not hardware, sets the expansion rate.

Cell degradation is managed centrally. Because the operator owns the cells, it can retire a pack at a state of health that a consumer would tolerate far longer, and it can charge slowly overnight to extend life. A fleet of packs cycled 500 times a year degrades faster than a consumer phone battery, and the operator's model assumes a four-year replacement cycle.

Standardization is the unresolved issue. Packs differ between vehicle makers in shape, voltage and connector, and an operator that supports two models needs two cabinet types. The national standard published in 2024 defines a connector for light vehicles and leaves the pack form factor to the manufacturer, which preserves competition and prevents a single network from serving every brand.

The comparison with charging is instructive. A charging station serves one vehicle at a time and needs 30 minutes; a swap cabinet serves eight packs continuously and needs one minute per customer. For a delivery rider who earns per order, the time saving is the product.

Financial structure matters more than technology. The operator's capital is in the cells, which are a depreciating asset with a known life, and the revenue is a subscription that a rider can cancel. Networks that have failed did so because utilization fell below the break-even, not because the swapping machinery broke.

Fire safety is the regulatory friction. A cabinet holding eight lithium packs in a shopfront is a fire load that building codes were not written for, and cities have responded with spacing requirements and mandatory suppression. Two operators report that a cabinet approval takes four to seven months, which is longer than the cabinet's installation.

For cities, the policy question is where the cabinets go. A cabinet needs a parking space, a power connection and a landlord willing to sign, and the operators that have grown fastest are those that negotiated with convenience-store chains rather than with property developers.
