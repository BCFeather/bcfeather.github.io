Ig Nobel prizes were awarded on 12 September in a theatre at a university in Massachusetts, in a ceremony that has been held annually since 1991 and that rewards research which first makes people laugh and then makes them think. The prizes are handed out by actual Nobel laureates. The prizes are awarded by a magazine that has published the proceedings for three decades, and the ceremony is financed by the sale of tickets rather than by a foundation.

This year's physics prize went to work on the swimming ability of a dead fish, which sounds absurd until the finding is stated: a dead trout can be pulled through water in a way that produces thrust, which tells engineers something about how a flexible body interacts with a fluid. An experiment that looks silly is often an experiment about a real mechanism. The point of the experiment is that the fish is a model for a flexible body, and a body that is pushed through water without control still produces a wake that engineers can measure.

Biology prize went to a study of the left side of a cow's body, and the chemistry prize to an analysis of the process by which a worm explodes when it is placed in a microwave. The pattern across the winners is that each one takes a question that nobody would fund and answers it with a method that nobody would teach. The award for the cow went to a team that had measured which side of the animal the milk comes from, and the answer was that it depends on the cow rather than on the farmer.

Ceremony has its own rituals. The audience throws paper aeroplanes, a speaker who exceeds a time limit is interrupted by a child who repeats that the speaker is boring, and the winners are expected to explain their work in two sentences. A format that mocks the ceremony is a format that protects the seriousness underneath. The winners pay their own travel, and several of them have said that the prize has done more for their citations than the papers themselves.

Point of the prizes is the defence of curiosity. Research budgets are allocated to problems that have stated outcomes, and the Ig Nobels are the one stage where a result that has no application is celebrated. A field that cannot fund the silly question is a field that will miss the useful answer that comes from it. A laboratory that can afford to test a silly hypothesis is a laboratory that has slack, and slack is the resource that a grant system does not measure.

What the evening shows is that the boundary between the ridiculous and the profound is a matter of what is measured rather than what is asked. The dead trout, the exploding worm and the coin flip are all systems in which the ordinary intuition turns out to be wrong. The prizes will be remembered next year when the same stage is used again, and the memory is the only archive that the ceremony keeps.
