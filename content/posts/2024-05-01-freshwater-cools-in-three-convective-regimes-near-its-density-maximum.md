Water is densest at about four degrees Celsius, so a lake that cools towards that temperature behaves unlike any other fluid. Above it, cooling makes the surface water heavier and the layer overturns; below it, further cooling makes the surface lighter and the convection changes character. It is also why a lake cools fastest near four degrees, since the density maximum sets the point at which the overturning stops.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 1 May 2024 resolves that transition with three-dimensional direct numerical simulations. The authors couple the heat flux through the surface to the convection beneath it, so the surface temperature is not imposed but emerges from a balance between cooling and mixing. The boundary condition is the part most models simplify, and the paper's point is that the simplification changes the sequence of regimes rather than only the rate.

Three regimes appear. Free convection holds while the surface is warmer than the density maximum, penetrative convection holds below it while the plume is still actively mixing the layer, and decaying convection holds once the plume weakens, and the authors predict the temperatures at which each gives way. Each regime has its own scaling of heat flux with the temperature difference, and the transitions are where two scalings meet.

The coupling is what makes the sequence physical. With a fixed surface temperature the fluid would settle into a steady state, while a flux boundary condition lets the surface cool until convection can carry the heat away, which is the balance a real lake strikes at night. The flux condition is also what makes the problem time-dependent, because the surface temperature falls as the layer cools.

Penetrative convection is the interesting middle regime. The plume is colder than the fluid below it and therefore lighter, so it rises into the layer and mixes it while the deeper water stays stable, and the depth it reaches sets how much heat the layer can lose. The plume depth grows with the square root of time in the classical theory, and the simulation reproduces that growth before decay sets in.

The transition to decay is abrupt in the simulations. Once the plume can no longer reach the surface, the cooling rate falls and the layer stratifies, and the time at which that happens depends on the initial temperature and the imposed flux rather than on the depth alone. The stratification that follows decay is what lets a lake hold a warm layer under ice, a fact about winter limnology as much as about convection.

What this bears on is ice. A lake freezes when its surface reaches zero, and the time taken to get there is set by which regime the water is in, so a prediction of the freezing date needs the sequence and not only the cooling rate. A model that ignored the regimes would predict freezing too early in deep water and too late in shallow water, which is the error the paper sets out to correct.
