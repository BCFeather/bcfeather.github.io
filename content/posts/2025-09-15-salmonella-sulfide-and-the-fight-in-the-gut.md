The gut is a crowded place, with several hundred species in the colon and a density that reaches a trillion cells per millilitre, and the competition inside it is chemical as much as spatial. Work published in *PNAS* on 12 September describes how *Salmonella enterica* uses hydrogen sulfide, a gas most cells treat as toxic waste, to suppress *E. coli* in the anaerobic sections of the intestine and so improve its own chances of colonizing.

Two enzymes matter here, and both are ancient ones that predate the animal gut by a wide margin. One reduces thiosulfate to sulfide, the other reduces sulfite, and *Salmonella* carries both while many competing strains carry fewer or none. The authors show that the gas is produced in the lumen, that its concentration rises where oxygen is scarce, and that strains unable to make it lose ground in mixed culture and in animal models, in one case by more than an order of magnitude over a day of growth.

The mechanism is not a poison in the crude sense, and the authors are explicit that they are describing inhibition rather than killing. Sulfide at the concentrations measured interferes with the respiratory chain of competitors that lack the same defences, which slows their growth without killing the population outright. *Salmonella* itself carries protection against its own product, so the same molecule that handicaps a rival leaves the producer unharmed, an arrangement familiar from antibiotics and from the tactics of soil bacteria, both of which specialize in making molecules that hurt neighbours and not themselves.

What makes the result interesting is the setting. Sulfide is usually discussed as a signal in host physiology, in small amounts, and as a hazard in larger ones. Here it is a tool of microbial politics, produced on purpose and calibrated to a niche, and the concentration the authors measure sits in a range that is harmful to competitors and tolerable to the producer. The authors argue that this helps explain why *Salmonella* can establish itself in a gut that already has residents, which is the step that precedes illness, and which public health usually addresses after the fact.

The clinical distance is considerable. Nothing in the paper suggests a treatment, and blocking sulfide production would be as likely to disturb the host as the invader, since the same gas is produced by much of the resident community and used by the cells lining the colon. What it does suggest is that the anaerobic gut is a chemically contested space, and that the metabolites usually described as waste are part of how species sort themselves out, quietly and without anything resembling a fight.

Work of this kind also reframes a familiar observation: the composition of the gut community is stable in healthy people and resistant to newcomers. Resistance is often attributed to space and to nutrient competition, both real. Sulfide adds a third route, one that runs through chemistry and does not require the resident to be bigger or faster, only better defended against a gas it makes itself.
