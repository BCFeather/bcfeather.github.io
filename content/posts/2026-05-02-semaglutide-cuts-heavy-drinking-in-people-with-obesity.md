Alcohol use disorder and obesity travel together, and the treatments have lived in separate clinics. A trial reported in *The Lancet* on 2 May tested whether a drug developed for one condition helps with the other, on the reasoning that both involve reward circuitry and compulsive consumption.

The study, led by Anders Fink-Jensen at Copenhagen University Hospital with NIH collaborators, randomized 108 people seeking treatment for alcohol use disorder who also had obesity. Participants received weekly semaglutide or placebo for 26 weeks, and everyone was offered standard cognitive behavioural therapy, so the drug was tested as an add-on rather than as a replacement for counselling. Enrolment was also restricted to people who wanted treatment, which means the trial speaks to the clinic rather than to the population of people with alcohol problems who never seek care.

Eighty-eight participants completed the trial. Heavy drinking days fell in both groups over the 26 weeks, and the fall was significantly larger with semaglutide. Total monthly alcohol consumption, drinks per drinking day, self-reported craving and scores on measures of harmful use moved in the same direction. Heavy drinking days are the outcome regulators accept in this field, because they track harm more closely than total volume does, and the difference between groups was largest there.

Blood biomarkers of alcohol consumption and liver damage declined more in the semaglutide group. That matters because self-report is the weakest endpoint in addiction trials, and a laboratory measure that moves with the behaviour makes the self-report more credible rather than less. Craving is the mechanism the drug is supposed to touch, and a self-reported measure that moves in the same direction as the drinking days makes the story coherent.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptors are expressed in brain regions that regulate reward, and the class has already shown reduced craving for alcohol and nicotine in observational and small experimental work. What this trial adds is randomization in a clinical population, with a behavioural co-intervention that makes the result closer to practice. Nausea and other gastrointestinal effects were the expected burden, and in a population with obesity they overlap with the symptoms the drug is also meant to improve.

The caveats are size and duration. A hundred and eight patients over six months is enough to establish a direction and not enough to characterize who responds, and alcohol use disorder is a relapsing condition in which six months is early. The trial also enrolled people with obesity, so the result does not speak to patients without it, and the next study needs a longer horizon and a stratified population before semaglutide becomes an alcoholism drug rather than an obesity drug with a side benefit. Prescribing will follow the label rather than the trial, since semaglutide is approved for diabetes and obesity rather than for alcohol use disorder, and off-label use carries its own access problems. That remains the realistic use case for the next few years.
