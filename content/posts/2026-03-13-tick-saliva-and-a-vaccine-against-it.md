Vaccines against tick-borne disease have been difficult because the pathogens vary and because the tick feeds for days before transmission. A study published this month in *Science Translational Medicine* took a different route: it immunized against proteins in the tick's saliva, and reduced transmission of a bacterium by 78 per cent in mice.

The biology of the target is why it works. A tick secretes saliva throughout feeding to suppress the host's haemostasis and immune response, and those proteins are the same for every tick of the species. An immune response against them disrupts feeding and produces a reaction at the bite site.

Protein selection came from a transcriptome of the salivary glands, narrowed to twelve candidates that are expressed early and are conserved across populations. Four were combined in the vaccine, and the combination was more effective than any single antigen.

Results in mice are strong and specific. Immunized animals had 78 per cent fewer infections after a challenge with infected nymphs, and the ticks that fed on them gained 40 per cent less weight, which suggests feeding was impaired.

Field relevance depends on the reservoir. The pathogen cycles between ticks, mice and larger mammals, and a vaccine given to mice reduces the reservoir's infectivity. A human vaccine would need a trial in a region with high exposure, and the endpoint would be infection rather than disease.

Safety is early. The trial in mice showed a local reaction at the injection site and no systemic effect, and a veterinary product based on the same principle has been licensed for use in cattle in two countries.

The comparison with pathogen vaccines is the strategic point. A vaccine against the vector does not need to match a strain, and it works against any pathogen the tick carries. That is an argument for a One Health approach: vaccinating the animal reservoir reduces human exposure.

Limits are the tick's counter-adaptation. Ticks develop resistance to host immunity, and a vaccinated host may select for ticks whose saliva evades the response. The study ran three generations of tick and found no change, which is encouraging and short.

The antigen's conservation is what makes a vaccine durable. The four proteins are encoded by genes that vary by less than 2 per cent across the species' range, which means a vaccine formulated from one population's sequence should work against another.

Production is conventional. The proteins are expressed in a yeast system, which is the same platform used for several licensed veterinary vaccines, and the dose is 50 micrograms in an adjuvant that is already approved.

The field trial's design accounts for exposure. It enrolls people who work outdoors in two regions with different tick densities, and it measures infection by a blood test at the end of each season rather than by reported illness, which catches asymptomatic cases.

For a reader, the useful framing is that vector control has been chemical for seventy years and is failing as resistance spreads. An immunological approach to the vector is a different instrument, and its first evidence is in mice rather than in people.
