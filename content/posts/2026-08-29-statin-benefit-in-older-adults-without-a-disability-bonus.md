Primary prevention in people over seventy is a genuine clinical dilemma, because the trials that established statins enrolled middle-aged populations and the absolute benefit shifts as competing risks accumulate. A drug that prevents a heart attack at sixty-eight may prevent nothing that the patient would have noticed at eighty-five, and the registration endpoint, major adverse cardiovascular events, does not distinguish between those outcomes.

Investigators recruited community-dwelling older adults without clinical cardiovascular disease through Australian general practices and randomised them to high-dose atorvastatin or placebo, following participants for a median of 5.9 years. The trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 29 August, and its two endpoints were designed to answer the practical question: events prevented, and time lived without disability. Recruitment through general practice rather than through specialist clinics is what makes the population representative of the people who are actually offered a statin at seventy-five.

Treatment reduced major cardiovascular events, and the lipid data show the drug was taken and was active, with mean LDL cholesterol falling about 38 per cent against 13 per cent on placebo. Disability-free survival, the composite of death, dementia and persistent physical disability, did not differ between groups. That combination is the informative result, since it separates what the drug does to arteries from what it does to the years a patient experiences. Dementia was included in that composite because it is the outcome older patients fear most, and its inclusion raises the bar that a cardiovascular drug has to clear.

Adherence is the finding that will shape how the result is read in clinic. Discontinuation was higher than in previous statin trials, and the most common reason was that the participant was unwilling rather than that an adverse event occurred, cited in 15.6 per cent of the atorvastatin group and 16.2 per cent of the placebo group. Adverse events led to discontinuation in 7.2 and 6.1 per cent respectively. Willingness to continue is itself an outcome in a prevention strategy that depends on daily adherence for a decade.

A trial run through general practice during a pandemic carries its own limitations, and the authors name them: participants were predominantly white and English-speaking, many assessments were remote, and recruitment was extended. The population was also selected for fitness to participate, which excludes the frail older adults for whom the prevention question is hardest.

The result does not tell a seventy-five-year-old whether to start a statin, and it tells a clinician that the conversation should be about events rather than about mortality. A drug that lowers heart attacks without adding disability-free years is worth taking for some patients and worth skipping for others, and the trial supplies the numbers to have that conversation rather than a recommendation to end it. Shared decision making is the term for this conversation, and the trial's contribution is to make it quantitative rather than aspirational.
