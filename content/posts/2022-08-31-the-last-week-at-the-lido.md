A municipal lido closes in the last week of August, and it closes on a Sunday, and the last session is the one that the regulars attend. The water has been heated by three months of sun and it is warmer than the sea, and the air above it is already cool enough to show the breath of the people who stand at the edge. The light goes at about half past eight, and the last hour is swum in the colour of the tiles rather than in daylight. Nobody who goes there says it is the best swim of the year, and everybody goes.

A lido is a piece of civic engineering from the 1930s, built to give a city the seaside that it could not reach. The one in this town has a main pool and a smaller one, a fountain that has not worked since the refurbishment, and a café that sells chips and tea in paper cups. The tiles are the same colour as the water when the sun is low, which is the reason that the photographs taken there look like they were taken in another decade. The paint on the lanes has worn into stripes that no longer divide anything.

The people who swim in it are the ones who come every week, and they know each other by their caps. There is a woman who swims a mile before work and a man who has been coming since 1974 and who tells the young ones that the water used to be colder. The lifeguards are students who go back to university in September, and the manager has been there for twenty years and will be there next year. The friendships are of the kind that exist only in one place and one season, and they are strong for exactly that reason.

The closing was decided by nobody in the pool. The council runs it at a loss and the repairs that it needs are the kind that are deferred for a decade and then cost more than the pool is worth. The friends group has raised money for the filters and the heating, and it has applied for the grants that pay for the rest. The list of lidos that have closed in the last thirty years is longer than the list of the ones that are open, and the people who swim here know the list.

At the same time, the last session ends at nine and the whistle goes and nobody wants to get out. The water is still warm and the sky above the town is the colour of the water, and the people who are leaving say see you next year in a tone that has a question in it. The manager locks the gate and the fountain is switched off and the pool is covered, and the cover is the same colour as the tiles. The winter is long and the pool will be there in the spring if the budget allows it, and nobody knows yet whether it will.

What the last week of a lido shows is that a public thing is kept by the people who use it. The council pays for the water and the chlorine, and the swimmers pay for the rest with their attendance and with their fundraising and with the habit of turning up. The last swim of the year is a small event and it is the one that the town will notice when it stops happening. The water will be drained and the tiles will be cleaned and the gate will be locked, and the argument about next summer will begin before the leaves have fallen.
