NASA said on Tuesday that its spacecraft had changed the orbit of an asteroid, and that the change was larger than the mission had needed. The DART probe struck Dimorphos on 26 September, and telescopes have since measured how long the small moon takes to go round its parent. The period has fallen from eleven hours and fifty-five minutes to eleven hours and twenty-three, a change of thirty-two minutes. The team had set a threshold of seventy-three seconds to call the test a success.

It was a box the size of a vending machine, and it weighed about six hundred kilograms. It hit the hundred-and-sixty-metre moonlet at twenty-two thousand kilometres an hour, and the impact was recorded by a camera that was destroyed on arrival. The measurement came afterwards, from the light curve of the pair as they dimmed and brightened in a repeating cycle. Thirty-two minutes is about four per cent of the original period, and the telescopes could see it within weeks.

The surprise was the debris. The impact threw out a cloud of rock and dust that pushed the moonlet further than the spacecraft's own momentum would have done. Engineers call that the momentum enhancement factor, and the observations put it somewhere around three, which means the ejecta did about twice the work of the probe itself. That is useful for planning, because a real deflection would want the same effect and would need to know how much of it to expect.

Aim of the test was a question that had been theoretical for decades. An asteroid on a course for Earth would need to be moved years before it arrived, and the earlier the push, the smaller the push can be. Dimorphos was chosen because it orbits a larger asteroid, which gave the measurement a clock that telescopes could read. The parent, Didymos, is about seven hundred and eighty metres across, and the pair pass close to Earth every few years. Their orbits offer no threat for at least a century, which is why the mission could be flown without anyone worrying about the outcome.

The European Space Agency will send Hera to the pair in 2024, and the spacecraft will arrive in 2026 to weigh the moonlet and map the crater. China has announced a mission of its own to a near-Earth object, and several agencies have begun to fund the telescopes that would give the warning in the first place. The United Nations has a network of warning centres, and the one thing it has never had is a test of a deflection that worked.

None of this means that the planet is defended. The body that was moved was small, the warning time was years and the parent asteroid gave the measurement a convenient clock. A larger object would need a heavier impactor, more warning or a different method, and none of those has been tried. What the mission has established is that the arithmetic of a kinetic impact can be done in advance and that the answer arrives on schedule.
