Dopamine signalling depends on how quickly the neurotransmitter is cleared, and the transporter that clears it is the target of stimulants, of antidepressants and of drugs developed for Parkinson's disease. Structures of bacterial homologues had been solved, and the human protein had resisted the same treatment. The transporter is a small protein with a large conformational repertoire, and structures of bacterial relatives had set expectations that the human protein did not meet.

A paper in *Nature* on 7 August 2024 reports the structure of the human dopamine transporter in a tripartite complex. Three ligands are bound at once: β-CFT, a cocaine analogue and a competitive inhibitor, MRS7292, a non-competitive inhibitor, and a zinc ion. Cryo-electron microscopy was the method that made it tractable, since the complex is too small and too flexible for crystallography.

A competitive ligand sits where the substrate would. β-CFT occupies the central site about halfway across the membrane and holds the transporter in an outward-open conformation, which is the state in which the protein faces the extracellular space and can accept a molecule. An outward-open state is the conformation a substrate encounters, and trapping it explains why the analogue behaves as a blocker.

A non-competitive ligand binds somewhere else. MRS7292 occupies an allosteric pocket adjacent to the extracellular vestibule, underneath the loop that gates access to the central site, and that position explains how a compound can slow transport without competing for the substrate site. A pocket under the vestibule is an unusual site, and its position suggests that the inhibitor holds the gate rather than plugs the pore.

Zinc binds at a third site, and its presence in the structure is informative because the ion modulates transport in vivo. A structure that captures all three simultaneously shows how the protein integrates a substrate analogue, an allosteric blocker and a modulator in one conformational state. Modulation by a metal ion is common in transporters, and a structural site for it makes the effect concrete rather than phenomenological. A structure solved with the ion bound leaves the physiological relevance to be tested by mutagenesis.

For drug design the payoff is specificity. The central site is conserved across the transporter family, so a compound that binds there is liable to hit related proteins, while the vestibule pocket is less conserved, which makes it a better place to look for selectivity. Bupropion and methylphenidate both act at this protein, so a structure that distinguishes sites is useful beyond the stimulant class.

One conformation is captured here, and transporters cycle through several. Capturing the inward-open state with the same ligands bound would complete the picture, and it is the obvious next experiment. Structures of the same protein with a substrate bound would show the transition, and that is the experiment the field will want next. That transition is what a transport mechanism is made of, and a snapshot can only mark one station on the route.
