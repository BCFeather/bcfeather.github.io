Retail sales rose 5.1 per cent in April from a year earlier to 3.72 trillion yuan, the National Bureau of Statistics reported on 19 May, and the figure is stronger than it looks because a subsidy did much of the lifting. The same release covered industrial output, investment and services, and the four numbers together describe an economy that is being pushed rather than pulled.

Industrial output grew 6.1 per cent, faster than the first quarter, with equipment manufacturing up 9.8 per cent and high-tech manufacturing up 10.0 per cent. The categories behind that strength were the ones the trade-in programme subsidizes: new energy vehicles, household appliances, and industrial robots. Appliances rose 28.7 per cent, furniture 20.6 per cent and communications equipment 14.9 per cent, and all three are categories where a scrappage payment applies.

The trade-in subsidies contributed about 1.4 percentage points to retail growth, according to the bureau, which means that without them the figure would have been closer to 3.7 per cent. The programme pays a fraction of the price of a new appliance or car when an old one is scrapped, and it has been running since 2024 with a budget that is renewed each year. Building materials fell 2.9 per cent, a number that follows the property market rather than the subsidy.

Fixed investment grew 4 per cent in the first four months, with manufacturing investment up 6.2 per cent and infrastructure up 3.4 per cent, while property investment fell 10.3 per cent. The divergence is the story of the Chinese economy in 2025: the industrial and infrastructure side is being supported, and the property side is still shrinking. Construction starts fell again, and the inventory of unsold housing in the smaller cities is the part of the balance sheet that the stimulus has not touched.

Services grew more slowly than goods. Service retail rose 5.1 per cent in the first four months, and the categories that depend on travel and dining were weaker than the categories that depend on a subsidized purchase. A household that replaces a fridge is consuming; a household that eats out less is also consuming, and the second is harder to subsidize.

What the numbers describe is an economy held up by policy rather than by demand. That is not a criticism: it is what a government does when private demand is cautious. The question for the second half is whether the subsidies can be renewed at the same scale, and whether the export side, which grew in April before the tariff truce, holds up when the truce expires. A subsidy needs a receipt and a serial number; a dinner does not produce either. A retail sector that grows at 5.1 per cent with a subsidy behind it is a sector that is being bought rather than sold to. The bureau publishes the same wording every month, and the wording is the part that changes least.
