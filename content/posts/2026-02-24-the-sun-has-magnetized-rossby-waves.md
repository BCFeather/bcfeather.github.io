The solar cycle is the largest regularly observed magnetic phenomenon in the solar system and its mechanism is not understood. A dynamo operating in the convection zone has to convert the kinetic energy of convective motions into a large-scale field that reverses every eleven years, and the theoretical ingredients are a differential rotation, a helical flow and a magnetic field that the flow can stretch. Observing the waves that are supposed to mediate the process has been the missing measurement.

Rossby waves are the large-scale planetary waves that appear in any rotating fluid, and their solar analogues were detected about a decade ago as patterns in the surface flows. The new analysis reports something different: global-scale inertial modes that are modified by magnetism, recovered by applying helioseismology to observations from the Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager. The paper appeared in *Nature Astronomy* on 24 February.

A slow mode and a weaker retrograde feature that may correspond to the fast mode were identified, confined to layers below \(0.98\) of the solar radius, with amplitudes weaker than the hydrodynamic Rossby waves. The frequencies are consistent with the presence of a large-scale toroidal field, and the implied field strength depends on where the field sits, reaching roughly \(5	imes10^{3}\) gauss if it is located at the base of the convection zone.

That number matters because it is what dynamo models require. A toroidal field of several thousand gauss at the tachocline is an ingredient that models assume and that observations had not confirmed. If the waves are magnetically modified in the way the analysis suggests, the assumption gains an observational footing, and the solar cycle gains a measurable intermediate variable.

Hanasoge is careful about the strength of the claim, and the paper's wording reflects it. Detection is described as possible, the mode identification rests on frequency matching, and a weaker retrograde feature is offered as a candidate rather than as an established mode. Helioseismology at these amplitudes is a signal-processing problem as much as an astronomical one.

Forecast application is the long-term goal, and it is why the measurement matters to anyone outside the field. Space weather forecasting currently relies on empirical relations between the polar field, the sunspot number and the next cycle's amplitude, and those relations degrade at cycle boundaries. A measurable internal wave that modulates the dynamo would be a physical predictor, and the gap between a possible detection and a predictor is several years of work. Extracting a coherent oscillation from a rotating, turbulent, differentially rotating fluid requires months of data and careful removal of the rotation signal itself.

This is the kind of measurement that reorganizes a field by supplying the observable that theory lacked, and it will be tested by whether the modes persist through the next solar minimum, when the field configuration is different and the modes should shift in frequency if the magnetic interpretation holds.
