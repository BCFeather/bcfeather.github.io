Lung cancer that is found early enough to be removed still comes back often, and the reason is biology rather than surgery. Micrometastases that no scan can see are already present at operation in many patients, and adjuvant chemotherapy buys a modest absolute gain. For tumours driven by a fusion, the logic of targeted therapy applies after surgery as well: if the driver is still the thing keeping residual cells alive, blocking it should delay or prevent recurrence.

*ALK*-positive disease is the clearest case. Ensartinib is a next-generation inhibitor that was tested in the adjuvant setting in a randomised trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 8 July 2026. Two hundred and seventy-four patients with resected stage IB to IIIA disease were assigned to two years of ensartinib or placebo, 137 in each group, with disease-free survival as the primary endpoint.

At 24 months, 86.4 per cent of patients with stage II to IIIB disease were alive and disease-free on ensartinib against 53.5 per cent on placebo, a hazard ratio of 0.20 with a confidence interval from 0.11 to 0.38. Across the whole population the figures were 87.3 per cent and 57.2 per cent, the same hazard ratio of 0.20. Overall survival data were immature, which is expected at this follow-up, and grade 3 or higher adverse events occurred in 35.8 per cent of treated patients. The benefit appeared early rather than accumulating slowly, which is what a driver-blocking drug should do if residual disease is driver-dependent.

The size of the effect is what stands out. A hazard ratio of 0.20 is larger than adjuvant osimertinib produced in *EGFR*-mutant disease, and it suggests that the residual cells in *ALK*-positive tumours depend on the fusion almost completely. The toxicity profile is the price: a third of patients had a serious adverse event, and the trial's discontinuation rate is the number to read before judging whether two years of treatment is tolerable. Adverse events were mostly the class effects of an ALK inhibitor, and the rate at which patients stopped treatment is reported in the paper rather than in the abstract.

Several limits narrow the claim. The trial is small for an adjuvant study, follow-up is short relative to the natural history of the disease, and overall survival has not yet been shown to improve. The stage II to IIIB subgroup that drives the headline is also the group with the highest baseline risk, so the absolute benefit in stage IB disease is smaller than the relative risk reduction implies.

What the result changes is the standard of care for a defined group, and what it does not change is the cost of getting there. The next questions are duration, since two years is a convention rather than a finding, and whether circulating tumour DNA can identify the patients who need treatment at all. A trial that randomises by molecular residual disease would answer both.
