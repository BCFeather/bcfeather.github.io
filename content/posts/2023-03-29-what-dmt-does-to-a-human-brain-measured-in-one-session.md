Psychedelics are studied with imaging, and imaging a drug that acts within minutes is a technical problem. A scanner takes seconds per volume and an electroencephalogram samples in milliseconds, and a drug whose effects rise and fall in twenty minutes needs both.

The study in *PNAS*, 28 March 2023 combines them. The authors record EEG and functional MRI simultaneously during a controlled infusion of dimethyltryptamine in human volunteers, and the two recordings are aligned in time so that the fast electrical changes and the slower haemodynamic ones can be compared.

The pharmacology is short. DMT is a serotonergic agonist with a rapid onset and a short duration, which makes it suitable for a within-session design: a participant is measured before, during and after, and the comparison is within the same brain.

The EEG contribution is the temporal detail. Electrical activity shows the oscillatory changes that accompany the subjective effects, and the frequency bands that shift are the ones that a network-level description would use.

The fMRI contribution is the spatial detail. Blood-oxygenation maps locate the regions where activity changes, and the combination of the two gives a change that is both timed and placed.

The connectivity analysis is the interpretative step. A psychedelic alters the correlation between regions, and the paper's analysis asks which connections strengthen and which weaken during the infusion.

The subjective reports are the third strand. Participants describe their experience on standardized scales, and relating a report to a signal is the step that makes an imaging result a statement about consciousness rather than about blood flow.

The limitations are the setting and the sample. A laboratory infusion with a participant lying in a scanner is not a naturalistic use, and a small sample of volunteers is not a population.

The safety framing matters. DMT is a controlled substance in most jurisdictions, and the study is conducted under clinical protocols with medical supervision. The result is a physiological description and not an endorsement.

What the paper cannot resolve is the mechanism's specificity. Serotonergic agonism produces many downstream effects, and the signal changes that the paper reports are the sum of them.

For the field, the value is a method. Simultaneous recording is what a short-acting drug requires, and a protocol that works for DMT can be applied to other compounds with fast kinetics.

The infusion's control is what makes the comparison possible. A fixed dose delivered over a defined interval produces a rise and a fall that the recordings can bracket, and a self-administered dose would not.

The analysis's alignment is the technical crux. EEG and fMRI have different clocks, and the paper's method maps the two onto a common timeline. Without that alignment the claim that an electrical change accompanies a haemodynamic one would not hold.

The interpretation is contested in the literature. One reading treats the changes as a disruption of the brain's default organization, and another treats them as an amplification of particular networks. The paper's data support parts of both.
