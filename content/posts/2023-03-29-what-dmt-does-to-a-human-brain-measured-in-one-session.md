description: "Simultaneous EEG and fMRI recordings during DMT infusion show how the psychedelic changes cortical activity and connectivity in ways that the two techniques can only capture together."
---

Psychedelics are studied with imaging, and imaging a drug that acts within minutes is a technical problem. A scanner takes seconds per volume while an electroencephalogram samples in milliseconds, and a drug whose effects rise and fall within twenty minutes needs both.

In *PNAS* on 28 March 2023, the two are combined. The authors record EEG and functional MRI simultaneously during a controlled infusion of dimethyltryptamine in human volunteers, and the recordings are aligned in time so the fast electrical changes and the slower haemodynamic ones can be compared.

Pharmacology makes that design possible. DMT is a serotonergic agonist with a rapid onset and a short duration, so a participant can be measured before, during and after, and the comparison stays within one brain. Infusion adds control, because a fixed dose delivered over a defined interval produces a rise and a fall the recordings can bracket, which a self-administered dose would not.

Because electrical activity shows the oscillatory changes accompanying the subjective effects and blood-oxygenation maps locate the regions where activity changes, the two techniques contribute in different registers, and the frequency bands that shift are the ones a network-level description would use. Alignment is the technical crux, since EEG and fMRI run on different clocks, and the method maps them onto a common timeline without which the claim that an electrical change accompanies a haemodynamic one would not hold.

Connectivity analysis is the interpretative step. A psychedelic alters the correlation between regions, and the analysis asks which connections strengthen and which weaken during the infusion. Subjective reports supply a third strand, since participants describe their experience on standardized scales, and relating a report to a signal is what turns an imaging result into a statement about consciousness rather than about blood flow.

Setting and sample are the limits. A laboratory infusion with a participant lying in a scanner is not a naturalistic use, and a small group of volunteers is not a population. Safety framing matters as well, since DMT is a controlled substance in most jurisdictions and the work runs under clinical protocols with medical supervision, which makes the result a physiological description rather than an endorsement.

Specificity stays unresolved, because serotonergic agonism produces many downstream effects and the reported signal changes are their sum. The field gains a method instead, since simultaneous recording is what a short-acting drug requires and a protocol that works for DMT can serve compounds with similar kinetics. Interpretation stays contested, with one reading treating the changes as a disruption of the brain's default organization and another treating them as an amplification of particular networks, and the data support parts of both. Replication across subjects is the next requirement, since an effect that appears in a small group is a hypothesis about a population, and a second cohort with the same protocol is the cheapest test of it. A cleaner design would hold the dose constant and vary only the expectation of the participant, since the setting contributes to the experience as much as the molecule does.
