---

Two releases on the same afternoon told opposite stories. American output fell at an annual rate of 0.3 per cent in the first quarter, and euro-area output rose 0.4 per cent on the quarter and 1.2 per cent on the year. The comparison is not like for like, since one is annualized and the other is not, but the direction is the same: Europe grew, and the United States did not.

The euro-area figure came from the preliminary flash estimate published by Eurostat on 30 April. It covers output only, is built from incomplete data, and will be revised. The European Union as a whole grew 0.3 per cent on the quarter and 1.4 per cent on the year, so the euro area underperformed the wider union, a pattern that has held since several eastern members joined the single market without the currency.

Country detail explains most of the aggregate. Germany grew 0.2 per cent, France 0.1, Italy 0.3 and Spain 0.6, so the four largest economies all expanded and none expanded much. Ireland grew 3.2 per cent, a figure that reflects the accounting of multinational pharmaceutical production more than the experience of Irish households, and Hungary contracted 0.2 per cent. A growth rate of 0.1 or 0.2 per cent is within the revision band, so the ranking of the large economies is less informative than the fact that none of them fell.

Drivers were familiar. Household consumption held up as real wages recovered, government investment continued under the recovery funds, and exports were helped by a weak currency and by American importers pulling orders forward. The last of those is temporary: goods that arrived in the first quarter will not arrive again in the second, which is why the flash estimate and the April surveys point in different directions.

Industry is the weak part. Manufacturing output has been contracting for two years in Germany, energy costs remain above American levels, and the tariff announcements of the spring landed on an export sector that had already lost its Russian market. A service economy can grow while its factories shrink, and that is roughly what the aggregate shows. Construction, by contrast, has been weak in Germany and strong in Spain, which is another reason the same number means different things in different capitals.

Rate-cut logic followed three weeks later. The European Central Bank lowered its deposit rate to 2.25 per cent on 17 April, and the number vindicates the decision without explaining it. A central bank that cuts into 0.4 per cent growth thinks the risks are skewed downward, and the statement said as much about trade. Rate decisions react to the next four quarters and not to the last one, and the bank was looking at a tariff schedule and an export order book and not at the first-quarter figure.

What would settle the year is the second quarter, when the pull-forward of exports reverses and the tariff regime is clearer. A second quarter of 0.4 per cent would suggest the economy absorbed the shock; a negative quarter would suggest the first borrowed from the second.
