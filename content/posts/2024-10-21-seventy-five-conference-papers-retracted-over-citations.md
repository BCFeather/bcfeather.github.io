Citation counts are the currency of academic promotion, and the case reported on 21 October 2024 in *Science* shows what happens when a currency is printed privately. Springer Nature retracted 75 conference papers connected to Juan Manuel Corchado, a computer scientist and the rector of the University of Salamanca. The retraction notices cite unusual citation behaviour, editorial handling, and undisclosed competing interests.

Arithmetic makes the case stark. Across the batch, the papers cite Corchado 1,772 times, the journal he edits, *Advances in Distributed Computing and Artificial Intelligence Journal*, 559 times, and members of his research group 329 times. Corchado wrote 14 of the papers himself, and most of the batch included at least one author from the university.

The audit began outside the publisher. Two bibliometricians, Alberto Martín Martín of the University of Granada and Emilio Delgado López-Cózar, published a report on the citation patterns, and the newspaper El País took the findings to the publisher in May. Chris Graf, director of research integrity at Springer Nature, told *Science* that direct professional or personal connections had been responsible for the editorial review of colleagues' work.

Corchado's group disputes the characterisation. In a statement to *Science*, a member described the papers as a very small share of the group's output and the accusations as malicious and aimed at discrediting it, adding that the group had been working with the publisher to correct editorial errors when the decision to retract was taken. Structure, not intention, is the point, and the venue is where the structure lives. Many of the papers appeared in proceedings of conferences organised by Corchado's own group, where peer review sits at arm's length from the publisher and the organisers choose the reviewers. Ludo Waltman of Leiden University called the citation pattern very suspicious, and a Spanish bibliometrician called the scale unprecedented in the country.

That asymmetry is why the case drew attention beyond bibliometrics, and why a citation audit became a governance question, since the same practices that inflate a citation count also decide who sits on the programme committee. Retraction cleans the record and leaves the incentive intact. A conference proceeding that a research group can convene, staff and cite is a machine for manufacturing reputation, and the 75 papers are the output of that machine rather than an aberration within it. Until a programme committee is appointed by someone with no stake in the speakers' CVs, the same pattern will recur with a different name on the cover.
