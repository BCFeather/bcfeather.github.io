date: "2023-09-05T22:15:00+08:00"

description: "France banned the abaya, the long robe worn by some Muslim girls, in state schools from the start of the school year on 4 September 2023, reopening a French argument about secularism and about how a dress can express a religion."
---

The education minister announced the ban at the end of August and it took effect on 4 September, when pupils returned to school after the summer. Gabriel Attal said the abaya and the qamis, its male equivalent, were religious symbols and therefore barred under the law of 2004 that keeps state schools free of conspicuous signs of faith. Nearly three hundred pupils were sent home in the first week for wearing the garments, and officials said most returned wearing something else after a conversation with their families. The minister reported the figures to parliament, which had asked for them.

Argument here concerns the meaning of a garment. The 2004 law bans signs that are ostentatiously religious, including headscarves and kippas and large crosses, and the practice since then has been to judge each case on whether the item can be read as displaying a faith. Critics of the ban say the abaya is a cultural garment that many women in France wear without intending a statement, and that a rule applied to a loose robe relies on the judgement of headteachers about what a girl's clothes mean.

Supporters of the ban have a different worry. Teachers and headteachers had reported a rise in religious pressure in some neighbourhoods, and the government presented the measure as part of a set of actions against sectarianism. A headteacher who tried to enforce the law in a previous dispute was attacked online, and the minister said schools needed a rule that was clear enough to apply without a case-by-case argument.

Politics surrounds the timing. The ban was announced weeks before a debate about an immigration bill, at a moment when the far right has been gaining, and Marine Le Pen had herself proposed a ban on the abaya in schools. Commentators on the left described the decision as a signal in an electoral competition, and commentators on the right described it as overdue. The pupils affected are mostly young, mostly French citizens, and rarely the people the debate is about, and their families have been left to explain a rule they did not write.

The secular tradition behind the rule is specific to France. Laicite was written into the republic's institutions in 1905, when the state separated itself from the Catholic church, and it has since been used to keep religion out of schools, public services and the civil service. The same principle has been criticized for falling hardest on Muslim women, and successive governments have applied it in ways that critics describe as inconsistent, allowing some exceptions and refusing others.

A rule about clothing has opened a larger argument about what it means to belong in French schools, and the pupils sent home are the persons least able to explain themselves in the terms the argument uses. The measure will be tested in the courts and in the classrooms, and its meaning will depend on which test happens first. Appeals against exclusions have already been filed by two families, and a teachers' union has asked for written guidance on what counts as an abaya.
