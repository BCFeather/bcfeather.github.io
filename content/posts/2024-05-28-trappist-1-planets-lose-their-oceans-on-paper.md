title: "How much water the TRAPPIST-1 planets can keep"

description: "A probabilistic treatment of hydrodynamic escape, using uncertainties in stellar mass, luminosity history and planetary parameters, puts water loss at up to eight Earth oceans on TRAPPIST-1e and leaves the inner planets dry."

---

Seven terrestrial planets orbit a small, cool star forty light-years away, and three of them sit where liquid water could exist on a surface. Whether they have surfaces at all depends on what the star did to their atmospheres early on, and atmospheric escape is the process that decides.

A preprint on arXiv on 3 May 2024 replaces the usual practice of assuming a single luminosity history with a statistical treatment. The authors sample stellar mass, the luminosity evolution the star might have followed, its age and the planetary parameters, and run hydrodynamic escape for each combination, so the output is a distribution rather than a number.

Deliberate simplicity is the design. Each planet starts with a pure water envelope and no exchange between atmosphere and interior, and the models track how much hydrogen escapes and how much oxygen is left behind as the water is destroyed. Inner planets come out desiccated for any starting inventory below about fifty Earth oceans. Water is lost faster than it can be resupplied, and the oxygen that remains accumulates in an atmosphere heavy enough to be measurable.

For the outer four, the numbers are more hopeful. Maximum water loss reaches $8.0^{+1.3}_{-0.9}$ Earth oceans on TRAPPIST-1e, $4.8^{+0.6}_{-0.4}$ on f, $3.4^{+0.3}_{-0.3}$ on g and $0.8^{+0.2}_{-0.1}$ on h, with corresponding oxygen retention of about 1,290, 800, 560 and 90 bars. A planet that started with a few oceans could therefore keep some of them.

A atmosphere built from stripped water would also be extremely dry, and the absence of water vapour in a spectrum would be consistent with the escape story rather than with a temperate ocean. A planet that keeps an ocean is also the one worth pointing a telescope at, and the outer four are the candidates, and their modelled inventories differ by a factor of ten. Oxygen left over from water that has been split and stripped is a false positive for life, and a spectrum that shows oxygen on one of these planets would need a companion measurement to rule out the escape route.

Limits the authors name are real. Interior exchange is switched off, which real planets certainly have, and a magma ocean would absorb the oxygen rather than release it. The stellar history is also sampled over a prior that the observations constrain only loosely.

Stellar activity is the other, and the same star that drives escape also erodes a planet's atmosphere with coronal mass ejections, an effect these models leave out. Tidal heating is one reason the outer planets look better than their irradiation implies. Locked orbits keep them warm from the inside, and the paper's assumptions about that heat are among the parameters the sampling varies. What the paper supplies is a range for the observers. JWST can measure the atmospheres of these planets in the next few years, and knowing which water inventories survive which histories is what turns a detection into an interpretation.
