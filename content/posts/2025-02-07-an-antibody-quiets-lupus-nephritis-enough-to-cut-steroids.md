Lupus nephritis is the complication of systemic lupus erythematosus that damages the kidney, and it affects a third of patients with the disease. The treatment has been high-dose corticosteroids with mycophenolate or cyclophosphamide for decades, and the regimen works well enough to preserve kidney function in most patients while producing the infections, weight gain, bone loss and diabetes that a long course of steroids causes.

Obinutuzumab is an anti-CD20 antibody that depletes B cells, and the rationale is the one that made rituximab attractive in lupus: if the autoantibodies come from B cells, removing the cells removes the source. Earlier trials of rituximab in lupus nephritis failed, and the reading of those failures was that the depletion was incomplete or that the trial design was wrong rather than that the target was wrong.

REGENCY, published in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 7 February, randomised patients with active lupus nephritis to obinutuzumab or placebo on top of mycophenolate and corticosteroids. The primary endpoint was a complete renal response at week 76, which requires the proteinuria to fall below a threshold, the kidney function to stay stable and the steroid dose to be low.

Rates were 46.4 per cent with obinutuzumab and 33.1 per cent with placebo, an adjusted difference of 13.4 percentage points with a confidence interval from 2.0 to 24.8 and a *P* value of 0.02. The steroid endpoint did the work that the investigators hoped: 42.7 per cent of the obinutuzumab group reached a complete response while taking 7.5 milligrams of prednisone or less, against 30.9 per cent of the placebo group.

Safety was the one that B-cell depletion produces. Infections were more frequent with the antibody, and the serious ones included pneumonia and urinary tract infections, which is the price of removing the cells that make the antibodies against encapsulated bacteria. Infusion reactions appeared on the first dose and were managed with premedication, and no anaphylaxis was reported in the trial.

What the result changes is the steroid budget. A patient who reaches a complete response on a low dose avoids the cumulative exposure that produces avascular necrosis of the hip, cataracts and osteoporosis, and those complications are the ones that determine the quality of a decade of life with lupus. A drug that raises the response rate by thirteen points and lowers the steroid dose is a drug that changes that decade.

Two questions remain. The first is durability, because a B-cell depleting antibody is given as a course and the response after two years is not yet reported. The second is selection, because the trial enrolled patients with a specific class of biopsy-proven nephritis and the response in the membranous class, which behaves differently, is unknown. Both are the subjects of extension studies, and the answers will decide whether the course is repeated once or given at a fixed interval.
