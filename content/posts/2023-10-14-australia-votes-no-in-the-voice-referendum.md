---

Australians voted against the constitutional amendment on 14 October by a margin of roughly 60 to 40 per cent, and every state recorded a majority against it, which the law requires in addition to a national majority. The proposal would have created a body of First Nations representatives to advise the federal parliament on policies affecting Indigenous communities, and it would have been the first recognition of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in the constitution. The question asked voters to approve an alteration that would have created the body and set out its powers.

The campaign ran for months and turned on questions of detail. Supporters argued that the voice would give communities a route into decisions made in Canberra and that the model had been designed in consultation with Indigenous leaders. Opponents argued that the body would add a layer of advice with no accountability, that its scope was undefined, and that recognition could be delivered without changing the structure of government. The 'yes' campaign had the backing of the largest Indigenous organizations and of most of the corporate sector, and it spent heavily on television.

Defeat is a setback for the Labor government, which made the referendum a first-term commitment and campaigned on it personally. It also ends a period in which Australian politics seemed willing to test constitutional change, after the same-sex marriage postal survey in 2017, and it leaves the recognition question without a vehicle. The prime minister said he respected the decision and that his government would keep working on the gaps the voice was meant to close.

Indigenous leaders responded with disappointment and with a warning about what the vote revealed. Surveys before polling found that a majority of non-Indigenous voters worried about unintended consequences, and that many could not describe what the voice would do, which suggests the campaign failed at explanation as much as at persuasion. Aboriginal organizations had asked for the mechanism rather than symbolic recognition, and the defeat leaves them without the lever they sought. The government has said it will legislate instead, which is the route the referendum was meant to avoid.

Comparisons with other settler societies are instructive. New Zealand has reserved seats for Maori in parliament and a treaty settlement process, and Canada has constitutionally protected Aboriginal and treaty rights. Australia has neither a treaty nor a national representative structure, and the referendum was an attempt to create one through the constitution rather than through legislation, which made it harder to pass. The Canadian treaty process took decades to build, and the New Zealand seats came from a separate political settlement.

Practical consequences will be felt in health, education and justice, where the gaps between Indigenous and other Australians have narrowed slowly. Supporters of the voice argued that consultation would improve programmes that are designed in cities and delivered in remote communities. That argument now has to be made through the existing system, and the defeat gives the government little room to claim a mandate for anything more ambitious.
