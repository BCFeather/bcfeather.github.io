French investigators detained the founder of a messaging service at an airport north of Paris on 24 August and charged him four days later with offences connected to the moderation of content on his platform. He was released on bail with a requirement to remain in the country. The detention was carried out under a warrant that allowed four days of questioning before a decision on charges.

Charges are the substance. They concern the failure to act on illegal content and the refusal to supply information to investigators, which are obligations that French law imposes on platforms of a certain size. A founder who is charged as an individual is a founder who cannot answer with a company's lawyers. The obligations include answering requests from the regulator within a fixed period, which the company's filings had described as impossible for its design.

Company's response was a statement that its owner had done nothing wrong and that the case was an attempt to hold a chief executive responsible for the behaviour of users. A platform that is run by one person is a platform whose governance is itself the issue, because the person carries the responsibility that a board would otherwise share. A board would have had to answer for the same facts, and the absence of one is what made a personal case possible.

Size of the service is the background. It hosts channels with hundreds of millions of subscribers, and its moderation is deliberately light, which is a business model as much as a philosophy. A model that sells privacy and resists requests for data is a model that a prosecutor will test. Revenue comes from subscriptions and from advertising in some markets, and neither depends on the data the investigators wanted.

Encryption is the technical question. A service that cannot read its own messages is a service that cannot comply with an order to remove content, and the argument about whether that is a defence or an evasion is now before a court. A law that assumes access is a law that encryption was designed to defeat. The same argument was made in Europe, where a proposal to scan private messages was withdrawn after technical objections. The company has said it will appeal, and an appeal will take longer than the investigation that produced the charges.

What the case shows is that a platform can become global and remain a personal project, and that the two are becoming incompatible. A service with a hundred million users and one owner is a service that the state will treat as an institution, and institutions are expected to answer. A founder who cannot leave the country is a founder whose company makes its decisions under a constraint its users did not choose. The service will keep running from servers outside the country, and the case will keep its founder inside one.
