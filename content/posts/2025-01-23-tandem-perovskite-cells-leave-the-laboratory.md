Silicon has been the substrate of the solar industry for forty years, and its efficiency record has barely moved since 2018. The reason is thermodynamic: a single junction can only convert a slice of the solar spectrum, and the theoretical ceiling for silicon sits near 29 per cent. Stacking a second absorber on top, tuned to the blue end of the spectrum, raises that ceiling above 40 per cent, and the perovskite family of materials has turned out to be the cheapest way to do it.

Certified results now sit above 33 per cent for a perovskite-silicon tandem, against a commercial silicon module that delivers around 22 per cent in the field. The gap between a laboratory cell of one square centimetre and a module of two square metres is where most of the engineering lives, and that gap has narrowed to a few percentage points on mini-modules of a hundred square centimetres. Silicon passivation layers and contact schemes have been optimized for a single junction, and a tandem forces both sub-cells to share one current.

Stability is the question that decides whether the technology ships. A perovskite absorbs moisture, degrades under ultraviolet light, and migrates ions under an electric field, and the encapsulation that protects it has to survive twenty-five years of thermal cycling. Accelerated tests now report encapsulated tandems retaining most of their initial output after several hundred hours at elevated temperature, which is encouraging and short of the industry's standard. Field data from the first commercial installations will matter more than any accelerated test, because real modules see heat, humidity and voltage stress together.

Lead is the other objection. The best perovskites contain lead, at roughly a gram per square metre of module, and a fire or a broken panel puts that lead into the environment. Alternatives based on tin lose efficiency and stability, and the compromise that most groups are pursuing is a lead-based absorber with a barrier layer designed to contain it.

Manufacture is where the economics will be settled. A tandem line adds two or three deposition steps to a silicon line, and the capital cost per watt of added capacity is what decides whether an existing factory is retrofitted or replaced. Texturing the silicon, depositing the transport layers and capping the stack all have to happen without breaking the wafers, and the yield of a full-size module is the number the industry watches. Printing a perovskite layer costs less per square metre than the silicon wafer beneath it, so the added expense sits mostly in the barrier films.

What has changed in the past two years is that the question is no longer whether a tandem can be efficient. A laboratory result that once looked like a curiosity now appears on commercial modules, and the argument has moved to warranty terms and to the price of the transparent electrode. Physics was the easy part. Grid operators care about the degradation curve, because a module that loses a fifth of its output in ten years changes the economics of a project.
