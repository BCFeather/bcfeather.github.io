Keir Starmer became prime minister on 5 July and spent the following weekend assembling a cabinet in which the most senior posts went to figures who had held them in opposition for years. The appointments were read as a signal that the government intends to run a disciplined administration rather than a factional one. A cabinet drawn from the shadow benches is a cabinet whose members have already argued their case in public.

The promises were five, and they were deliberately modest. Growth, energy, the health service, crime and education were named as the missions, and none of them was attached to a spending number that a chancellor could be held to. A promise without a number is a promise that buys time. A mission that is stated as a direction is a mission that a civil service can organize around without a new law. A promise that is repeated for five years is a promise that the voters have already discounted.

Fiscal inheritance is the constraint. The deficit is large, the tax burden is at its highest in decades, and the outgoing government had already pencilled in spending plans that the new one described as unfunded. A government that says it has no money is a government that has to grow the economy or raise taxes, and it has ruled out the second. A tax burden at a record is a burden that a government cannot raise further without losing the voters it just won.

First decisions were symbolic and cheap. Planning restrictions on onshore wind were lifted, a rail reform was announced, and the health service was given a new winter plan. A cheap decision is a decision that tests whether the machinery of government responds. A planning reform is the cheapest lever a government has for growth, and it is also the one that angers the most voters.

Opposition is not the constraint. A majority of more than 170 means that the government can pass what it wants, and the constraint is inside the party, where the left and the centre have different views about how fast to spend. A large majority is a majority that contains its own arguments. A party with a large majority and a small fiscal margin is a party whose arguments are about priorities rather than power.

What the first week shows is that the government has chosen competence as its brand. A cabinet of people who have shadowed their briefs for years is a cabinet that will be judged on delivery, and delivery in a low-growth economy is the hardest test in politics. A government that is judged on delivery is a government that needs the civil service to be better than it was, and reform of the state is the hardest promise. A government that inherits a state that has been cut for a decade is a government that has to rebuild capacity before it can deliver.
