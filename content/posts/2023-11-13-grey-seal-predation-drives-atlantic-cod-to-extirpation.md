title: "Grey seal predation drives Atlantic cod to extirpation"
date: "2023-11-13T09:05:00+08:00"

description: "A multispecies model of the southern Gulf of St. Lawrence finds that seal predation accounts for most recent cod mortality and that the stock will disappear without a strong and rapid reduction in grey seals."
---

Atlantic cod in the southern Gulf of St. Lawrence collapsed in the early 1990s, and the closure of the fishery did not bring the stock back. The standard account of a failed recovery points to high natural mortality, and the candidate behind that mortality has been the grey seal. Culling seals to save cod is a policy proposal with a long history and very little quantitative backing, because the food web does not cooperate with simple stories.

A multispecies model of intermediate complexity was fitted to scientific and fisheries data for the region, and its projections were used to ask what happens to cod under different seal abundances, according to a preprint posted on 11 November 2023. The model includes the triangular relation that makes the question difficult: small pelagic fishes are eaten by both cod and seals, and the same fishes eat young cod.

Seal predation came out as the majority of recent cod mortality. Under the fitted parameters the stock declines towards extirpation unless grey seal abundance falls strongly and soon, and a slow reduction does not rescue it. The model also tested the counter-argument, that culling seals would release pelagic fishes which would then eat more young cod, and found no evidence for a large enough increase in herring biomass to offset the gain.

The asymmetry between the two effects is what decides the policy question. Direct predation removes adult fish every year, while the indirect effect acts on prerecruits through a pathway that the model resolves but does not find strong. A food web of that shape makes the net effect of culling positive in the model, which is a claim about magnitudes rather than about sign, and the margin is what a manager would need before acting.

Timing matters as much as magnitude. A stock already reduced to low abundance has little spawning capacity left, so a reduction in mortality that arrives after the spawning stock has collapsed cannot rebuild it within a management horizon. The paper's projections therefore separate scenarios by speed as well as by seal numbers, and the fast scenarios are the only ones that keep cod present.

Limits belong to the model class. Intermediate complexity means that many biological details are parameterized rather than resolved, seal diets are inferred, and the fitted data cannot discriminate every competing structure. Fishing mortality, temperature and prey availability enter as assumptions, and a model cannot test a culling policy the way a field experiment would.

What the work does supply is a quantitative prior for a decision that has been argued from anecdotes. If the projections are right, the choice is between a rapid reduction in seal abundance and the loss of cod from the region, and the cost of waiting is not linear. A decision of that kind deserves a second model built independently, and the paper's structure gives the next group something to disagree with.
