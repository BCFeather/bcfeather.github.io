A subtype of avian influenza known as H5N1 has been circulating in American dairy herds since March 2024, when the virus was confirmed in Texas and Kansas. The strain belongs to the clade that has killed hundreds of millions of poultry worldwide since 2020, and its appearance in cattle was unexpected because the receptors it prefers are rare in the bovine respiratory tract. It has since been found in more than nine hundred herds across sixteen states. The first signs were a drop in milk yield and a herd that would not eat, which is why the diagnosis took weeks rather than days.

Transmission inside a herd appears to run through the milking parlour. The virus concentrates in milk and in the cells that line the udder, and equipment that touches those cells carries it from cow to cow. That is why the outbreak has been concentrated in large operations with shared milking machines, and why smallholder herds have reported fewer cases than their share of the national herd would predict.

The human tally is small and the severity is uneven. Most confirmed infections have been conjunctivitis in dairy workers, with no respiratory symptoms, and one death has been recorded in Louisiana in a person over sixty-five who kept a backyard flock. The absence of severe disease in dairy workers is the most reassuring fact in the outbreak, and it rests on a receptor distribution that could change with a single mutation. Sequencing of the first human samples found no changes associated with airborne spread, and every month without them is a month the virus has spent adapting to something else.

Surveillance is the part of the response that has drawn criticism. Testing of bulk milk tanks began nationwide only in December, and the programme is voluntary in several states. A virus that spreads through milk can be tracked by sampling the tank rather than the cow, which is cheap and fast, and a voluntary system leaves the herds that decline to test as blind spots. Farmers have economic reasons to avoid a positive test, because a confirmed herd faces movement restrictions and lost contracts.

Raw milk is the exposure route that public health agencies have underlined. Pasteurization inactivates influenza, and the commercial milk supply has tested clean, but farms that sell unpasteurized milk directly to consumers have reported cases among cats that drank it. Cats are a sentinel species here, because they develop severe neurological disease and die quickly.

The wider question is what ten months of cattle infection means for the virus. Influenza reassorts readily, and a pathogen that has access to both mammalian and avian hosts has more opportunities to exchange segments than one confined to birds. Nothing in the January data suggests that has happened, and the surveillance that would detect it is the same surveillance that has been slow to scale. Vaccines for poultry exist and are not widely used, and a cattle vaccine would take years to license even if it worked.
