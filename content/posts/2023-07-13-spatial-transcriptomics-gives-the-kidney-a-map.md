description: "Spatial transcriptomics resolves gene expression across a section of human kidney, revealing zonation and cell states that a dissociated sample would blur."
---

A kidney is organised by function: filtration at the cortex, concentration in the medulla. That organisation is visible in a microscope and invisible in a tube of dissociated cells. In *Nature* on 13 July 2023, it is recovered. The authors measure gene expression at positions across a kidney section, and the result is a grid in which every spot carries a transcriptome.

Zonation is the result. Cortex and medulla differ in the expression of transporters and metabolic genes, and the difference is continuous rather than abrupt, which a section shows and a bulk sample cannot. Geometry explains why the map matters, since a tubule is a long tube with a gradient along it and a section cuts across many tubules at once, so a reconstruction is what recovers that gradient.

Cell states form the finer structure. A proximal tubule cell's transcriptome changes along the tubule's length, and a spatial measurement resolves a change a dissociated sample reports as an average. The immune population is a separate layer, because macrophages and resident lymphocytes follow the vasculature rather than the tubules.

Resolution sets the method's limit. A spot contains several cells, so deconvolution assigns the mixture to cell types, and that assignment is an inference whose uncertainty grows as the spot shrinks. Laser capture is the technical alternative, since a microdissected region gives a clean transcriptome at the cost of throughput while a spatial array trades resolution for coverage.

Donors add a second axis. A transplant donor's kidney and a nephrectomy specimen differ in their injury signature, and the comparison shows where the difference sits. Development follows, because a fetal kidney has a nephrogenic zone an adult lacks, and a map spanning both ages shows where that zone was. Disease supplies the motive. A diabetic kidney's injury is regional, and a map locates the region a biopsy's random sample might miss, although a clinical sample is small and often superficial, so a whole-organ map is not a map of what a needle retrieves.

Mechanism is what the paper does not establish, because a spatial pattern describes and the causal chain from expression to tubular failure runs through physiology the map does not measure. Nephrology gains an anatomy of expression, since the kidney has been described in terms of cells and of function, and a map joining the two is a reference for both. Transplantation reads it speculatively, because a graft biopsy compared with a reference might distinguish rejection from injury by a pattern no single marker provides, and the released interactive resource is as much a product as the paper.

A map built from many donors would turn the single-organ reference into a population, and the variation between healthy kidneys is the baseline any disease comparison needs before a pattern can be called abnormal. A section from a transplant kidney before and after engraftment would let the same organ serve as its own control, which no cross-donor comparison can do, and the biopsy material for such a pair is already archived in most centres.
