---

A kidney is organized by function: filtration at the cortex, concentration in the medulla. The organization is visible in a microscope, and invisible in a tube of dissociated cells.

The map in *Nature*, 13 July 2023 recovers it. The authors measure gene expression at positions across a kidney section, and the result is a grid in which each spot has a transcriptome.

The zonation is the result. The cortex and the medulla differ in the expression of transporters and of metabolic genes, and the difference is continuous rather than abrupt, which a section can show and a bulk sample cannot.

The cell states are the finer structure. A proximal tubule cell's transcriptome changes along the tubule's length, and a spatial measurement resolves the change that a dissociated sample reports as an average.

The resolution is the method's limit. A spot contains several cells, and a deconvolution assigns the mixture to cell types. The assignment is an inference, and its uncertainty grows as the spot shrinks.

The comparison across donors is the second axis. A kidney from a transplant donor and one from a nephrectomy differ in their injury signature, and the map's comparison shows where the difference sits.

The developmental question follows. A fetal kidney has a nephrogenic zone that an adult lacks, and a map that includes both ages shows where the zone was.

The disease application is the motive. A diabetic kidney's injury is regional, and a map locates the region that a biopsy's random sample might miss.

The limitation is the biopsy. A clinical sample is small and often superficial, and a map of a whole organ is not a map of what a needle retrieves.

What the paper does not establish is a mechanism. A spatial pattern is a description, and the causal chain from a gene's expression to a tubule's failure runs through physiology that the map does not measure.

For nephrology, the value is an anatomy of expression. The kidney's structure has been described in terms of cells and of function, and a map that joins the two is a reference for both.

The tubule's geometry is the reason the map matters. A tubule is a long tube with a gradient along it, and a section cuts across many tubules at once. The reconstruction is what recovers the gradient.

The immune population is a separate layer. A kidney contains macrophages and resident lymphocytes, and their distribution follows the vasculature rather than the tubules.

The technical alternative is laser capture. A microdissected region gives a clean transcriptome at the cost of throughput, and a spatial array trades resolution for coverage.

The atlas's use in transplantation is speculative. A graft's biopsy could be compared with a reference, and the comparison might distinguish rejection from injury with a pattern that a single marker cannot.

The data's release is the legacy. A map is useful only if others can query it, and the authors' interactive resource is as much a product as the paper.
