title: "Seabed full of metals with a rulebook that is not finished"
date: "2025-03-22T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "Polymetallic nodules cover parts of the Pacific floor, and the international body that would license their extraction has not agreed on the code that would govern it."
---

Nodules the size of potatoes cover parts of the Clarion-Clipperton Zone, an area of the Pacific larger than the European Union, and they carry manganese, nickel, copper and cobalt in concentrations that land-based ore rarely reaches. The nodules grow by accreting metal from seawater over millions of years, and the abyssal plain they sit on is one of the least studied habitats on earth. A mining operation would vacuum them from the sediment and separate them on a ship.

A legal framework comes from the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, which created the International Seabed Authority to administer the area beyond national jurisdiction. The authority has issued exploration contracts to a group of sponsored contractors, and it has spent a decade negotiating the exploitation code that would set environmental standards, benefit sharing and liability. A contractor's sponsorship comes from a state, which means a company cannot apply on its own behalf.

A procedural trigger moved the timetable. In 2021 a small island state sponsored an application that started a two-year clock, after which the authority was obliged to consider a provisional licence even without a code. The deadline passed, the code was not finished, and the council has continued to negotiate without issuing a commercial licence. A provisional licence would be issued with conditions that the council invents in the absence of a code, which is why the negotiators have avoided that outcome.

Environmental objections rest on recovery times. A nodule field that took millions of years to form is not a renewable resource on any human timescale, and the sediment plume that a collector raises can travel for tens of kilometres. A study of a trial in the 1970s found that the tracks were still visible four decades later and that the benthic community had not recovered. A plume that travels tens of kilometres is a plume that crosses the boundary of the contract area, which is the regulatory problem the code has to solve.

Arguments for extraction rest on demand. Nickel and cobalt are inputs for batteries, and the land-based mines that supply them are concentrated in a few countries, one of which is Indonesia. A supply that comes from the seabed would diversify that concentration, and the companies involved argue that the environmental cost is lower than the alternative. A land-based mine that opens in Indonesia takes five years and a permit, and a ship that is already built can start in months.

What has changed in 2025 is that some contractors have lost patience with the international process. A company has said it will apply for a licence under American law if the authority stalls, which would create a parallel regime and a legal dispute that no one wants. A rulebook that is not finished is a rulebook that will be written by whoever moves first. A parallel regime would give one country the power to license an activity that the rest of the world has spent a decade trying to govern.
