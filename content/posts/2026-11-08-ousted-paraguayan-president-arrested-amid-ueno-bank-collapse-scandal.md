Paraguay's former president Santiago Peña was taken into custody by national police on the evening of 6 November at his private residence in Asunción, hours after the Senate voted overwhelmingly to impeach and remove him from office over a major financial scandal involving Ueno Bank, the country's largest digital lender.

The Senate impeachment vote was nearly unanimous: 44 out of 45 senators present backed the motion, finding Peña guilty of misconduct. Once stripped of the presidency, he lost constitutional immunity from prosecution, clearing the way for the public prosecutor's office to issue an arrest warrant. Authorities initially placed him under house arrest, before moving him to a security compound overnight due to unrest near his home. He is scheduled to appear before prosecutors specialized in economic crime and anti-corruption investigations on Saturday for formal questioning.

The crisis erupted after Paraguay's central bank intervened and sealed Ueno Bank on 3 November. The institution served roughly three million customers, close to half of Paraguay's population, and its sudden closure locked millions of ordinary savers, pensioners and small business owners out of their funds. Street protests quickly broke out across Asunción, with demonstrators demanding full reimbursement of frozen savings. Clashes erupted between frustrated depositors and police as crowds marched towards the national congress building.

Prosecutors allege that Peña, a former major shareholder of Ueno Bank, engaged in conflict of interest, breach of trust, improper handling of pension funds and financial manipulation while in office. Investigators claim he leveraged political influence to benefit the bank and failed to protect ordinary depositors as the lender's financial health deteriorated. Peña first tried to submit his resignation on 5 November to avoid impeachment, but the congressional session to accept his resignation collapsed over a lack of quorum, forcing lawmakers to proceed with the impeachment process.

Vice President Pedro Alliana was sworn in as Paraguay's new president immediately after Peña's removal. In his inaugural address, Alliana pledged to work to restore depositors' lost savings and rebuild public trust in financial oversight. He will complete the remaining one year and nine months of the presidential term. Lawmakers also elected a new vice president to stabilize the executive branch amid the turmoil shaking the South American nation.

The case marks one of the most explosive political and financial shocks in Paraguay's recent history. Analysts note that the scandal has exposed weak corporate governance and loose financial regulation, while testing the independence of the judiciary. Supporters of Peña maintain that the accusations are politically motivated, while opponents frame the arrest as a landmark victory for anti-corruption efforts. Legal proceedings are expected to stretch for months, as prosecutors gather evidence and build a full criminal case against the former head of state and other implicated figures.
