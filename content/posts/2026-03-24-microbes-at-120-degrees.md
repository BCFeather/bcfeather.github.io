The upper temperature limit for life has been a moving boundary since the first hyperthermophiles were cultured in the 1980s. A drilling expedition reported this month in *Nature Microbiology* recovered viable microbial communities from a hydrothermal reservoir at 120 degrees Celsius, 8 degrees above the previous confirmed limit, and the cells grow in culture at a doubling time measured in days.

Sampling is what makes the claim credible. The expedition used a coring tool that seals the sample under pressure at the seafloor, and the microbiologists measured the tracer concentration of drilling fluid in each core to establish contamination. Samples with tracer above a threshold were discarded, which removed 60 per cent of the cores.

Growth in culture is the confirmation. The team maintained the enrichment at 120 degrees and 250 atmospheres for 14 months, and measured cell counts rising from 10^4 to 10^7 per millilitre. The doubling time is 21 days, which is slow even for a hyperthermophile and consistent with a metabolism operating close to the limit.

Molecular adaptations are the substance. The organism's proteins carry a higher proportion of charged amino acids on their surfaces, which stabilizes folding at high temperature, and its DNA is protected by a reverse gyrase that introduces positive supercoiling. Both adaptations are known; what is new is that they work at 120 rather than 105.

Metabolism is hydrogen-based. The organism oxidizes hydrogen and reduces sulfate, obtaining little energy per reaction, which explains the slow growth. The community includes a partner that produces hydrogen from organic matter, and the pair is a minimal ecosystem of two metabolisms.

Implications for astrobiology are specific. The subsurface of Mars and the oceans of the icy moons are candidate habitats, and the temperature and pressure at which life can operate determine where to drill. A limit at 120 degrees, rather than 105, widens the depth range that a mission could target.

Limits of the study are the ones inherent to culturing. One organism, one site, one enrichment condition: the community in situ may include members that the culture does not support, and the paper's diversity analysis of the environmental DNA shows sequences from at least four phyla that did not grow.

Enzyme stability is the applied angle. Proteins from a 120-degree organism are stable at temperatures where industrial processes operate, and two of the enzymes in the paper retain activity after four hours at 100 degrees. Biocatalysis has wanted such enzymes for twenty years, and the supply has been limited by what could be cultured.

Pressure is a variable the study could not separate. The cultures were maintained at 250 atmospheres, and some hyperthermophiles are known to require pressure to grow at their upper temperature limit, since pressure stabilizes proteins and membranes. Whether the organism would grow at 120 degrees at atmospheric pressure is untested.

Reading this against earlier claims, the field has a history of temperature records that did not survive scrutiny. The contamination controls in this paper are stricter than most, and the cultivation result is what separates it from a report of DNA sequences alone.
