Public broadcasters in three European countries have commissioned radio drama this season, and all three are translating: a Catalan play into German, a Korean series into French, a Portuguese adaptation into Italian. The commissioning notes cite audience data, not heritage. Audio drama has returned because it is cheap, and it is translated because the source material is cheap in the same way.

Cost is the argument. A 45-minute audio episode with a cast of six, a studio, a sound designer and a composer costs a few tens of thousands of euros. The same script filmed costs millions, and the audience for a niche literary drama is too small to justify it. Broadcasters that abandoned the form in the 1990s are returning because the digital platform made distribution free.

Translation changes what is produced. A translated play keeps its structure and loses its idiom, and the adapter's job is to find a register in the target language that carries the same social information. Spanish formal address, French tutoiement, Korean honorifics and German du/Sie encode relationships that a plot can depend on, and a translation that flattens them flattens the drama.

Performance is where audio earns its keep. A voice can carry a class marker, an accent and an age without a set or a costume, and casting a translated play across regional accents is the equivalent of location shooting. Two of the three series cast actors from the source language's diaspora, which solves pronunciation and produces a texture that a dubbed film cannot.

Audience measurement for the form is awkward. Podcast platforms report downloads, broadcasters report listening hours, and the two are not comparable. The Catalan original reached a domestic audience of about 40,000 per episode on broadcast and 120,000 downloads; the German translation's first three episodes have drawn 90,000 downloads, which the commissioning editor describes as sufficient to continue.

What translation does to the canon is the interesting long-run effect. A national dramatic repertoire that was closed to outside work when production was expensive opens when it is cheap, and the flow is not from large languages to small ones but between small ones, since a Catalan play travelling to Germany travels further than an American series would.

Rights are the friction that decides which plays travel. A translation requires the original author's permission, a translator's contract and a separate licence for the audio performance, and the three are negotiated with different parties in different jurisdictions. Public broadcasters have blanket agreements for domestic performance that do not extend to translation, and the resulting delay can exceed the production schedule.

Small-language traffic is where the model works best. A Catalan play licensed to a German broadcaster costs a fraction of an original commission and arrives with a production history that a commissioning editor can cite. Whether the flow reverses, with German plays reaching Catalan audiences, depends on the relative size of the two markets, and the pattern so far is one-way.

Might the form overreach? The economics that make it viable also make it easy to fill a schedule with adaptations rather than originals, and the ratio in the three commissions is two translations to one original. A tradition rebuilt on someone else's scripts is a distribution channel, not a literature, and the commissioning notes do not address it.
