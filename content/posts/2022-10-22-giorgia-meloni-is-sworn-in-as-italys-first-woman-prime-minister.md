Giorgia Meloni took the oath of office at the Quirinal Palace on Saturday morning, and with it became the first woman to lead an Italian government. She is forty-five, and she leads the most right-wing coalition to hold the office since 1945. The cabinet has twenty-four members, drawn from her Brothers of Italy, from Matteo Salvini's League and from Silvio Berlusconi's Forza Italia. The three parties won a majority in both houses in the election of 25 September.

The two deputy prime ministers are Salvini and Antonio Tajani, and the economy minister is Giancarlo Giorgetti, a League politician who has spent most of his career on industrial policy. The coalition had argued for weeks about the foreign ministry and about the undersecretary who would handle the intelligence services. Berlusconi had said publicly that Meloni was arrogant and domineering, and she had replied that a coalition and a friendship are different things. The dispute was settled before the oath, and the settlement was visible in the allocation of the ministries.

Her party took about twenty-six per cent of the vote, more than twice what it had won four years earlier. The party's origins are in the youth wing of the neo-fascist Italian Social Movement, and she joined it at fifteen and rose through its successor groups. She has spent the years since attacking the European Union's budget rules, immigration policy and what she calls the culture of the left. In the campaign she promised a naval blockade of the Mediterranean and a review of the sanctions on Russia, and she has since said the sanctions will stay.

A result like this ends a period in which Italy was governed by a technocrat. Mario Draghi had run the country since February 2021 with a cabinet of ministers from most parties, and his government fell in July when three of them withdrew. The president, Sergio Mattarella, dissolved the parliament the same day, and the election was held in the last week of September. Draghi handed over the office on Sunday morning and was photographed leaving with his papers in a plastic bag.

The European Commission's first reaction was cautious, and its president said she expected Italy to remain at the centre of the union. The markets were calmer than they had been in the summer, when the spread between Italian and German bonds widened after the election was called. That spread widened again on the morning of the vote and narrowed after the result, which suggests that investors had already priced the outcome.

What the government will do first is the budget, which has to be sent to Brussels by the end of the year. The programme promises tax cuts for the self-employed, a flat rate for higher incomes and a rise in pensions, and the arithmetic will be decided in the next six weeks. Italy's public debt is about a hundred and forty-five per cent of national output, and the interest on it rises with every point the European Central Bank adds. The first woman prime minister will be judged on that, and not on the ceremony.
