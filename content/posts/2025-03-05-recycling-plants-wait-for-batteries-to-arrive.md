Battery recycling has been announced at a scale that the feedstock does not yet support. Companies in Europe, China and North America have announced plants that together could process several hundred thousand tonnes of cells a year, and the volume of end-of-life batteries available in 2025 is a fraction of that. A plant that runs below capacity is a plant with a cost per tonne that no commodity price can cover. A plant that is announced is a plant that has a site, a permit application and a customer letter of intent, and none of those is a tonne of cells.

Recycling chemistry splits into two routes. Pyrometallurgy smelts the whole cell and recovers cobalt, nickel and copper as an alloy, and it is robust to mixed chemistries and to contamination. Hydrometallurgy dissolves the black mass that results from mechanical shredding and recovers metals separately, and it can recover lithium at a higher rate. The second route costs less energy and yields more, and it requires a feedstock that is sorted.

Collection is the bottleneck before the chemistry. A pack that is removed from a vehicle is a hazardous waste, and the rules that govern its transport differ by country. A recycler that pays for a collection network is competing with informal processors that pay the owner cash and skip the paperwork, which is a familiar pattern in every waste stream. A collection network that covers a country costs money per kilogram, and the recycler that builds one is competing with a scrap dealer who pays cash.

A regulatory push is the reason the capacity exists. Europe's battery regulation sets recovery targets and, from 2031, minimum recycled content for new cells, and those requirements create demand for recycled material regardless of price. A car maker that has to prove recycled content will pay a premium for a documented supply, and the premium is the subsidy the industry is running on. A car maker that has to document recycled content will sign a long-term supply agreement, and the agreement is the collateral a recycler needs to build.

Design is the question of whether packs are built to be disassembled. A cell that is glued into a structural pack is cheaper to make and harder to take apart, and the trend towards cell-to-chassis construction works against recycling. Several manufacturers have begun to publish dismantling manuals, and a manual is not the same as a design. A pack that can be opened with standard tools is a pack that a repair shop can also service, which is a reason manufacturers resist the design.

Economics will improve as the first generation of packs retires. A vehicle sold in 2015 has a battery that is now twelve years old, and the packs that will arrive in volume between 2030 and 2035 are the ones being sold today. A recycler that builds now is making a bet on a feedstock that arrives later, and the bet is rational only if the alternative is losing the permit. A recycler that waits for volume loses the permit to a competitor, and the permit is the scarce asset rather than the plant.
