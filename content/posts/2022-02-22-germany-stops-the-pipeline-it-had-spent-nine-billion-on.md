---

Germany halted the certification of the Nord Stream 2 pipeline on 22 February, and the decision removed the last obstacle that had kept the project in limbo for four years. The pipeline was finished in September 2021, and it had never carried commercial gas, because the regulator had suspended its approval in November while it checked whether the operator met the rules on unbundling. Two pipes, each 1,224 kilometres long, run under the Baltic Sea from Russia to Lubmin, near the Polish border.

Money had gone into it for a decade. The total cost was about 9.5 billion euros, half of it paid by Gazprom and half by five European companies, Shell, OMV, Engie, Uniper and Wintershall, which each put in up to 950 million. The project was designed to carry 55 billion cubic metres a year, roughly the volume that Germany imported from Russia, and it would have bypassed the transit route through Ukraine on which the existing pipeline depended.

For the project, the case was that Germany needed gas and that the direct route was cheaper and more reliable than the pipelines that crossed a country at war. The argument against it was that it increased the dependence it claimed to reduce and that it made Ukraine, which earned about 2 billion dollars a year in transit fees, more vulnerable to a Russian squeeze. The American government had sanctioned the companies that built it, and the German government had spent years saying the decision was commercial.

Announcing the halt the morning after the recognition was a deliberate choice. The chancellor spoke on the morning after the Russian president recognized the two separatist republics, and he said the circumstances had changed in a way that made the certification impossible. The project had not yet been certified, so the decision did not cancel a contract; it stopped a process. The company that owned the pipeline said it would review the decision, and its shares fell by half.

Prices rose at once. The European benchmark for gas, which had traded around 70 euros a megawatt-hour in the previous month, moved above 80 on the news, and the cost of German wholesale electricity followed. The German government had been filling storage through the winter and had reached about 30 per cent of capacity, below the level it wanted, and it began to buy on the spot market and to look for terminals where ships of liquefied gas could be unloaded.

The decision set the direction of German energy policy for the following years. Two floating terminals at Wilhelmshaven and Brunsbuttel were approved within weeks and opened within a year, and the government legislated a mandatory level for storage. The pipeline itself was never certified, and one of its two strings was damaged by an explosion in the Baltic in September 2022, along with both strings of Nord Stream 1, which ended the debate about whether it could be restarted. What the halt established was that a commercial project had become a political instrument, and that a state could decide to destroy its own investment rather than let it become leverage. The nine billion euros did not disappear in February, because they had been spent already, and the loss fell on the companies and on the consumers who paid for the alternative.
