Economists had expected no change, and the franc rose 3 per cent against the euro on the day. The Swiss National Bank raised its rate to minus 0.25 per cent from minus 0.75 on 16 June, and the move was the first increase since 2007. The bank had kept rates negative for seven years, and it had been the most dovish in Europe.

Inflation was the reason, and it reached 2.9 per cent in May. The figure is low by European standards, but it was above the bank's target of 2 per cent, and it was the highest since 2008. The bank said the rise was needed to prevent inflation from becoming entrenched, and that it would watch the data. The rate had been minus 0.75 per cent since January 2015, when the bank first went negative.

Reluctance had come from the currency, because the franc had been strong and the bank had sold francs to hold it down. The rise made the currency stronger, and the bank said it would accept that, because the imported inflation was the problem. The decision was taken a week before the European Central Bank raised rates for the first time in 11 years. The two moves together signalled the end of the negative rate era, and the market repriced the region's rates. The euro fell 1 per cent against the franc. The bank said the franc's strength had cut imported inflation by 1 point.

Its balance sheet was 700 billion francs, larger than the economy. It had bought foreign currency to hold the franc down, and it said it would reduce the holdings slowly. Its chairman said the balance sheet was a tool, and that the bank would use it if needed. The government had cut its growth forecast to 2.6 per cent from 3, and it said the war had raised the price of energy. The bank said the economy would grow 2.5 per cent, and that unemployment would stay at 2 per cent. The country imports most of its energy. Its assets were 120 per cent of output, and it had bought currency in every month since 2015.

Another 75 points came in September, and the rate reached 0.5 per cent. It raised in December to 1 per cent and in March 2023 to 1.5, and the peak was 1.75 in June 2023. Its chairman said the bank would keep going until inflation fell. The bank raised three times in 2022, and the rate reached 1 per cent in December.

Inflation peaked at 3.5 per cent in August and fell to 2.8 in December. The bank said the franc's strength had helped, and that the imported inflation had peaked. The rate stayed at 1.75 per cent until March 2024, when the bank cut by 25 points, the first cut among the large central banks.
