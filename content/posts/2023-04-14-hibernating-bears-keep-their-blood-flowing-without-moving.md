After surgery, a patient who lies still faces a rising risk of thrombosis, as does a paralysed patient and an astronaut in a capsule, and the risk climbs with the duration of immobility. Clinicians answer with anticoagulants that carry their own bleeding risk.

A comparative study in *Science* on 14 April 2023 asks why a hibernating bear escapes that risk. The animal lies still for months, eats and drinks nothing, and its circulation should be a textbook case of stasis. It does not clot.

The answer is a coordinated reduction. Platelets and the coagulation system move into a state in which clot formation is suppressed, and the suppression reverses when the animal wakes, so the system is switched rather than damaged. Platelets initiate the clot, and their quieting is a change in cell behaviour rather than in plasma chemistry, which is why arousal restores the effect so quickly.

Matching that trajectory to human physiology supplies the second finding. Patients with spinal cord injury, immobile for long periods, show a comparable change, and it protects them from the clots their immobility would otherwise produce. The mechanism has a molecular handle as well: the authors identify a factor whose activity falls during hibernation, and inhibiting it in mice reproduces the protection.

Because hibernation lowers heart rate and body temperature, a clot formed in a slow circulation would travel slowly and lodge where it could do the most damage, which is why a system that suppresses clotting protects an animal that cannot afford a stroke in midwinter. Samples taken at entry, in midwinter and at arousal trace a trajectory rather than a single state, and the trajectory is what separates regulation from coincidence.

Caution is warranted on the therapeutic side. A drug that suppresses clotting in a hibernating bear would suppress it in a trauma patient, and the bleeding risk is exactly what an anticoagulant clinic manages. Hibernation is a programmed state with a defined entry and exit, so the mechanism switches with the season; and because it is conserved from bear to human, evolution has kept it for a reason, though the sample is thin, with few bears available and a single injury cohort. Infection complicates the picture, since hibernation suppresses inflammation as well as clotting and the two systems interact, so part of the protection may belong to a broader immune quiescence. What the work establishes is an existence proof: a mammal can be immobile for months without thrombosing, and a mechanism that achieves it is one a prophylaxis could imitate.

Translation is the harder half. A bear's plasma taken in midwinter carries the activity, and whether a component of it can be given to a patient without disturbing the clotting that surgery depends on is a question about dose and timing. A prophylaxis that suppresses clotting too far is as dangerous as one that suppresses it too little, so the window is narrow.
