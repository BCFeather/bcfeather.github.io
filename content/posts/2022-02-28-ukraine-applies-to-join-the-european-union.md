President Zelensky signed an application for Ukraine to join the European Union on 28 February, four days into the invasion, and he recorded the announcement in a video that was posted from a government building in Kyiv. He said the decision had been taken under the constitution and that the country was fighting for its survival, and he asked the union to admit it under a special procedure that would not take years. The document was registered in Brussels the same afternoon.

The response from Brussels was friendly and cautious at once. Ursula von der Leyen, the president of the commission, said that Ukraine was one of us and that the union wanted it in, and she spoke of a people who had shown what European values meant. She did not give a timetable, and she noted that the process of accession involves 35 chapters of law, from competition policy to the independence of the judiciary, and that no applicant had ever completed it in less than several years.

Division among the member states followed a familiar line. The countries on the eastern edge, Poland, the Baltic states and the Czech Republic, argued for a fast track and for a signal that would be heard in Moscow. France and the Netherlands said that an application driven by a war could not shortcut the conditions, and the German chancellor said the union should deepen the association agreement rather than open a membership process. The European Council met in Versailles in March and described Ukraine's future as being inside the union, without a date.

Membership had been a Ukrainian ambition since the 1990s and a policy since 2014, when the association agreement was signed and the trade part was provisionally applied. The agreement had lowered tariffs and aligned regulations, and it had annoyed the Russian government enough to contribute to the crisis of that year. Public support for membership had risen from about half the electorate to more than 70 per cent after the invasion, according to the polls that could still be taken.

Candidate status came in June. The commission delivered an opinion that recommended it, the council granted it at a summit on 23 June, and the conditions attached were the familiar ones: reform of the courts, a law on media ownership, and a procedure for appointing judges that would satisfy the Venice Commission. The government in Kyiv promised to complete them, and the commission was asked to report on the progress by the end of the year.

The process that began on 28 February runs through the longest machinery the union has. Accession negotiations with a country at war open chapters that require legislation, and the reconstruction that will follow the war is a budget that the union will have to fund. What the application did, in the first week, was give the government in Kyiv a claim on the future that its soldiers could fight for, and give the union a reason to describe its own principles in public. Negotiations that followed were slow, as the critics of the fast track had predicted. Hundreds of pages of legislation were reviewed, the vetting of judges continued, and the chapters did not open in the first year, because building a member state takes longer than a war.
