Geophysics has a habit of producing models that fit and cannot be checked. The interior of the planet is reached only through seismic waves, which constrain density and elasticity at depth but not motion, and through the magnetic field, which constrains flow only indirectly. Everything else is inference from a boundary condition, and a model that reproduces an observation is a candidate rather than a finding.

The September paper on the length of a day is a good example of the genre at its best. Two forces act on the fluid core, gravity from the heterogeneous mantle and the Lorentz force from the field the core generates, and their competition can produce fluctuations in rotation that show up as a millisecond in the day. The authors show that a model with plausible parameters reproduces the observed wobble, and they are careful to call the mechanism a proposal.

Careful language is not a formality. The parameters in question, viscosity, field strength and the amplitude of density anomalies, are constrained by ranges and not by measurements, and a model that succeeds inside those ranges has excluded nothing. The claim is that the mechanism can do the job, which is weaker than the claim that it does the job, and the difference is the difference between a candidate and an explanation.

This is not a criticism of the work, which is exactly what a theory paper should be. It is a criticism of how such work gets read. A press release that says a model explains a phenomenon becomes, in a summary of a summary, a discovery about the core, and the conditional disappears somewhere between the abstract and the headline. The conditionals are the science.

Remedy is structural, and it is cheap. A model becomes an explanation when it makes a prediction that could fail, and the prediction here would be a specific relationship between fluctuations in the magnetic field and fluctuations in the length of a day, at a lag the mechanism sets. That relationship is observable in principle, using records that already exist for the past century, and until it is tested the mechanism remains one of several.

Working scientists understand this and write accordingly. The problem is that the incentives of publication reward the confident sentence, and the hedging that makes a paper honest makes it harder to promote. Journals could require a statement of what would falsify the central claim, which is a small change with a large effect on how the work is described afterwards. A falsifiable prediction is the cheapest form of rigour and the hardest to write into a grant application.

None of this argues for scepticism as a pose. Modelling is how a science of inaccessible objects proceeds, and the alternative is to know nothing about the core. It argues for keeping the verb in the right place, so that a paper that says the mechanism can account for the observation is not remembered as one that says it does.
