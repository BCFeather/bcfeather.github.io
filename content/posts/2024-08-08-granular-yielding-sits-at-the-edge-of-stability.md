Sand, snow and coffee grounds behave in ways that neither liquids nor crystals explain. They flow when pushed hard enough, they hold a slope that a liquid could not, and the transition between the two states leaves almost no structural trace, which is why the physics of yielding has been hard to pin down. Avalanches, silo failures and the flow of powder in a hopper all depend on that transition, which makes its physics an engineering question as well as a curiosity.

A paper in *PNAS* on 8 August 2024 reports an experiment that watches the transition directly. The authors use photoelastic disks, a material that reveals the force carried at each contact when viewed through crossed polarizers, so that the particle positions and the force network can be recorded in the same frame. Photoelasticity is an old trick, and combining it with particle tracking at scale is what makes the data set new.

Two descriptions are then applied to the same data. From statistical physics the transition shows critical behaviour, with correlations that grow as the packing approaches yield. From mechanics it shows marginal stability, the condition in which a system sits at the threshold where a small perturbation can propagate without decaying. Criticality is measured through correlation length, and marginal stability through the distribution of contact forces, so the two tests use different statistics.

The two are not rival accounts. Criticality describes how fluctuations organize across the system, and marginal stability describes what each contact can support, and the experiment finds both in the same packing, which suggests they are two views of one transition. Treating them as one transition is the paper's conceptual claim, and the measurement is what supports it.

Friction is the complication that earlier granular experiments could not control. By measuring forces at every contact the authors can separate the frictional part of the network from the part that carries load through geometry alone, and the critical behaviour survives that separation. Separating the two parts of the network is what allows the result to be compared with simulations of frictionless grains.

The experiment is two-dimensional, and a real sandpile is not. Whether the same criticality appears in three dimensions, where the contact network has more freedom to rearrange, is open, and so is the question of how polydispersity changes the picture. Three dimensions would add rotational degrees of freedom, and these are exactly what friction couples to.

For materials the result is practical. A model that treats yielding as both critical and marginal can predict where a packed bed will fail, which matters for silos, for landslides and for the powder beds used in manufacturing. A criterion that holds at the packing scale can be used in continuum models that are otherwise fitted empirically. A silo that is designed against the critical packing rather than against an empirical angle of repose would fail less often.
