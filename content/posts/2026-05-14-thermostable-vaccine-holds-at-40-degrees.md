Most vaccines require storage between 2 and 8 degrees, and the cold chain that maintains it is the largest cost in delivering a vaccine to a rural clinic. A formulation reported this month kept a measles vaccine potent for 30 days at 40 degrees and 75 per cent humidity, and the stabilizer is a silk protein.

The mechanism is vitrification. Silk fibroin forms an amorphous glass around the virus particles as it dries, immobilizing the proteins and preventing the aggregation that heat causes. The glass dissolves on reconstitution, releasing intact particles.

Potency was measured by a plaque assay, which counts infectious particles, and the formulation retained 92 per cent of its initial titre at 30 days, against 4 per cent for the standard formulation. The threshold for release is 80 per cent.

Immunogenicity was tested in a small animal study and the response was equivalent to the refrigerated product, which is the number a regulator would want before a human trial.

Application is the saving in the cold chain. A vaccine that survives 40 degrees can be transported without refrigeration to the last clinic and stored in a cupboard, which removes the refrigerator, the power supply and the monitoring from the delivery cost. That is 20 per cent of the cost per dose in rural settings.

Manufacturing compatibility is the open question. The stabilizer is added before lyophilization, and a manufacturer's existing lyophilizer cycle must be revalidated for the new formulation, which is 18 months of work per product.

Regulatory pathway is a variation rather than a new product. A formulation change requires stability data and a comparability study, and two manufacturers have filed. A licensed thermostable measles vaccine would be the first.

Time above temperature, rather than temperature itself, is the limit. A vaccine that tolerates 40 degrees for 30 days still degrades at 55, and the formulation's data show a 50 per cent loss at 55 over 72 hours. The specification would state a controlled-temperature excursion rather than an ambient rating.

Reconstitution is the second step. A lyophilized vaccine must be mixed with a diluent, and the diluent's temperature and the mixing time affect potency. The trial measured potency after reconstitution and found no loss within two hours.

The comparison with a freeze-dried product is the cost baseline. A standard lyophilized vaccine already tolerates ambient temperature for a week, and this formulation extends that to a month. The difference is what lets a clinic hold stock through a delivery delay.

The formulation's cost is a small addition per dose. The silk protein is produced by a fermentation process that yields 10 grams per litre, and a dose needs 2 milligrams, so the material cost is under a cent. The barrier is approval rather than price.

For a reader, the result is a reminder that vaccine efficacy and vaccine delivery are separate problems. A product that works and cannot be stored is a product that does not reach the people who need it, and the formulation is where that gap closes.
