NVIDIA reported revenue of 35.1 billion dollars for the quarter to the twenty-seventh of October, an increase of 94 per cent on the same period a year earlier and of 17 per cent on the previous quarter. Data centre revenue reached 30.8 billion dollars, more than double the year before, and net income was 19.3 billion. Company guided to about 37.5 billion dollars for the current quarter, slightly above the average analyst forecast, and said that supply of its newest processors remained short of demand.

Numbers on this scale have become routine and are still remarkable. Revenue has roughly tripled in two years, and the company's gross margin, at about 75 per cent, is the highest of any large manufacturer in the industry. Growth has been concentrated in a single product line: the graphics processors that were designed for games are now the machines that train and run large artificial intelligence models, and the customers buying them in volume are a handful of cloud providers and a growing number of governments.

Concentration is the risk that analysts keep returning to. Four customers accounted for about half of revenue in the quarter, and their identities are known to the market. If any of them slows its spending, the effect on NVIDIA's results would be immediate and large, and the company's own guidance assumes that spending continues. Executives said demand visible in their order book extends into the following year, and they described the constraint as capacity in manufacturing and in memory rather than demand. Company also said that production of its newest system, which packs several processors into one rack, had begun shipping in small volumes.

Competition is developing slowly and from several directions. Advanced micro devices has begun shipping accelerators that customers are testing, and several large technology companies have designed their own chips for inference, which is the task of running models rather than training them. Custom silicon of that kind does not compete with NVIDIA's general purpose processor directly, and it reduces the share of the market that the company can charge a premium for. Software that ties customers to NVIDIA's programming model remains the strongest part of its position.

Export controls shape what the company can sell and where. American rules restrict the sale of the most advanced processors to China, and NVIDIA has designed versions that comply with the limits, which the American government has twice tightened. Chinese revenue has fallen as a share of the total, and domestic suppliers have taken part of the gap. Company has said it expects the restrictions to remain and has built its guidance around them.

Question that hangs over the whole sector is whether the spending is investment or a bubble. Cloud providers are building data centres at rates that would have seemed implausible three years ago, financed largely from their own cash flow, and they have not yet shown that the services they sell on those machines earn enough to justify the capital. NVIDIA's results are the clearest evidence that the spending is happening, and they cannot answer whether it will be repaid.
