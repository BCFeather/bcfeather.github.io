title: "Fast growing trees are slow in the field"
date: "2025-03-10T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A global analysis in *Nature* finds that tree species built for rapid resource capture grow quickly in greenhouse experiments and slowly in real forests, which undermines a trait-based shortcut that vegetation models rely on."
---

Plant ecology sorts species along an economics spectrum. A species with thin, nutrient-rich leaves, high specific leaf area and fast uptake grows quickly and lives briefly, and a species with thick, defended leaves grows slowly and persists. The spectrum predicts growth, and the prediction is the basis of the trait values that vegetation models use to estimate how much carbon a forest will store.

The analysis assembled growth measurements from forests across the world and matched them with the leaf and wood traits of the species measured. In controlled conditions the expected relationship held: acquisitive species grew faster than conservative ones. In the field it did not hold, and in stressful sites the fast species were among the slowest.

Water and nutrients explain the reversal. A species that grows quickly needs a supply of water and nutrients that a dry or infertile site cannot provide, and a plant that invests in thin leaves loses water through them. In a favourable site the acquisitive strategy wins, because the supply is there to be captured; in a marginal one the same strategy is a liability, and the species that persist are the ones with thick leaves, dense wood and a slow return on their investment.

The size of the plant matters as well. Greenhouse experiments use seedlings, and forest inventories measure saplings and adults, and the same species can behave differently at different stages. A seedling in a pot has water and nutrients supplied, while an adult in a forest competes for both with its neighbours, and the trait that predicts growth in one setting does not predict it in the other.

What the paper questions is a shortcut that vegetation models have relied on for a decade. Models that estimate forest productivity from trait maps assume that acquisitive traits mean fast growth everywhere, and a global analysis that shows the assumption failing in stressful sites means the maps overestimate growth where it matters most, which is in the dry tropics and at the boreal margin, where a tree grows slowly for reasons that no trait can capture.

Carbon accounting is the practical consequence. A project that credits sequestration on the basis of a species' traits will credit more than the forest delivers if the site is marginal, and the error is largest in the projects that promise the most. and the error runs in the direction that inflates the credit. Forest-based offsets are a large and growing market, and the analysis is a warning about the data that market runs on.

What the authors propose is a correction and not an abandonment. Traits still predict, provided the model includes the environment and the size of the plant, which means that a trait map is not enough on its own. The dataset that the paper assembled is the one that would let a model do that, and the next step is to fit the interaction between trait and site, which no current model does.
