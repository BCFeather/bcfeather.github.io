description: "The errors that matter are not random, they are systematic, and the systems that produce them are the ones that reward confidence."
---

A forecast is a statement about a distribution, and most forecasts are reported as a single number without the distribution attached. The single number is what gets quoted, what gets remembered and what gets compared with the outcome, and that compression is where a large share of the error is introduced. A range that turns out to be wrong is less embarrassing than a point estimate that is wrong, and the incentives run the other way. A distribution that is never published cannot be checked, and the check is the only mechanism that improves a forecaster.

Systematic bias is more common than variance. Forecasters who work inside an organization learn which answer is acceptable, and the adjustment towards that answer is usually small enough to be invisible in any single forecast. Defence planners, budget offices and energy ministries have all produced official numbers that were consistently optimistic across decades, and the consistency is the evidence that the bias is structural. An organization that rewards the optimistic answer gets optimistic answers, and the mechanism is selection rather than dishonesty.

Base rates are ignored because they are boring. A project that is forecast to finish in eighteen months belongs to a reference class of similar projects, and the reference class usually says thirty. The information in the reference class is statistical and the information in the plan is specific, and the specific story wins in a meeting even when it is demonstrably overoptimistic. A reference class requires a database of comparable projects, and the database is the piece that most organizations never build.

Confidence is rewarded more than accuracy. A forecaster who says that an election is too close to call is less useful to a client than one who says who will win, and the market for forecasts pays for decisiveness. Prediction markets correct that incentive for the participants who trade, and they do not correct it for the commentators who explain. A market that pays for a call rewards the bold one, and the bold one is right often enough to keep the business running.

Superforecasters are the exception that proves the rule. The group identified in a long-running research programme updated frequently, kept score, reasoned in probabilities and avoided narrative. Their advantage was not intelligence, it was method, and the method is available to anyone willing to be scored. A method that requires being scored is a method that requires humility, and humility is not a quality that survives a promotion.

What follows for anyone reading a forecast is a short list of questions. What is the base rate, what would change the answer, who is paying for it and when was it last updated. A forecast without those answers is a number with a story attached, and the story is usually the part that was optimized. A forecast that is updated weekly is a forecast that can be wrong in public, and being wrong in public is the price of being right more often.
