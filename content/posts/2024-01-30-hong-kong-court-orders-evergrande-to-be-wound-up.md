A judge in Hong Kong ordered China Evergrande to be wound up on 29 January, after the company failed to produce a restructuring plan that the creditors would accept. The developer had liabilities of more than 300 billion dollars at the peak of its borrowing. The hearing had been adjourned four times at the company's request, and the judge said the progress of the restructuring had been too slow to justify another delay.

Liquidation is the end of a strategy. The company borrowed against land that it had not yet built on, and it kept borrowing as the sales slowed, which worked while the prices rose and stopped working when they did not. A model that depends on the price rising is a model that ends when it stops. The company had sold apartments before they were built, and the buyers who paid in full are the creditors with the weakest claim. The apartment sales fell by half in the year before the order, and the cash that the company collected was used to finish the projects rather than to repay the bonds.

Creditors are the ones who will divide the assets. The banks, the suppliers and the buyers of the unfinished flats are all claimants, and the order of payment puts the households with deposits behind the secured lenders. A queue that puts the savers last is a queue that the government will be asked to rearrange. The administrator will be appointed by the court and will report to the creditors, and the process is expected to take years rather than months.

Local authorities are the second party. They hold the land that the company bought, and they have an interest in seeing the projects finished rather than sold for parts, which is why the liquidation will be supervised rather than simply executed. A winding-up that has to build is a winding-up that is slow. The local governments have set up funds to complete the stalled projects, and the money comes from the bonds that the central government allowed them to issue.

Market read it as a precedent. The shares of the other developers fell, and the analysts said the case would decide whether the rest of the sector is restructured or liquidated. A first case is a template for the others. The other developers that have defaulted are watching the case, and the terms that the creditors accept will set the price of their own restructuring. The rating agencies had cut the company to default three years earlier, and the bonds have been trading at a few cents in the dollar since.

What the order shows is that the authorities have stopped pretending the largest developer could be saved, and the cost of that admission is now being allocated. A company that borrowed against the future and sold it to households is a company whose failure is paid for by the people who bought the future.
