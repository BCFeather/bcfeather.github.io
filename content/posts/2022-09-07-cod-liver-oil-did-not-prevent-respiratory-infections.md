A supplement that has been recommended for a century is a supplement whose evidence base is mostly tradition, and the pandemic supplied a reason to test it properly. Vitamin D was the hypothesis, and cod liver oil was the vehicle that a Norwegian population would accept. A trial in Norway had to be large, because the event rate in a vaccinated population was low.

The trial randomized 34,601 adults aged 18 to 75 who were not taking daily vitamin D supplements to 5 millilitres of cod liver oil, containing 10 micrograms of vitamin D, or placebo for up to six months, in Norway from November 2020 to June 2021. The paper appeared in *The BMJ*, 7 September 2022. A four-endpoint trial needs a multiplicity correction that keeps the false-positive rate honest, and the fallback method is the one the authors chose.

Four co-primary endpoints were predefined: a positive SARS-CoV-2 test, serious covid-19, a negative test, and self-reported acute respiratory infection, and supplementation was not associated with a reduction in any of them. The fallback method was used to handle the multiple comparisons, which is the statistical honesty that a four-endpoint trial needs. A null result this tight on the primary endpoint is what the trial was designed to give, and the tightness is the reason the null is informative.

A positive test occurred in 1.31 per cent of the cod liver oil group and 1.32 per cent of the placebo group, a relative risk of 1.00, and serious covid-19 in 0.70 and 0.58 per cent, a relative risk of 1.20 with an interval that included one. A six-month trial measures one season and not a life, and a cumulative effect would need a longer exposure.

Participants took the supplement for a median of 164 days, which is long enough for a winter and short enough that a cumulative effect might not appear.
