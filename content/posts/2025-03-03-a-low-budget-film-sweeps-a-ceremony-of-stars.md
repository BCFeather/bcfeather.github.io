Ninety-seventh Academy Awards on 2 March gave best picture to Anora, a film made for about six million dollars by a director who had spent his career on smaller budgets. It also took best director, best editing and best actress for Mikey Madison, in a role that carried the film from beginning to end. The ceremony, hosted for the first time by Conan O'Brien, ran long and was almost entirely free of the technical failures that the previous year had produced.

How the awards were distributed tells a story about the electorate. The Academy has expanded its membership over the past decade, adding voters from outside the United States and from below the line, and the effect has been a shift towards films with a critical consensus rather than films with a campaign budget. A best picture winner that opened in six cinemas in October is a winner that the voters actually watched on a screener. A voter who has seen eight of the ten nominees is a voter whose ranking is about craft rather than about marketing.

Campaign spending is still a factor. A distributor that spends twenty million dollars on screenings and events can buy attention, and attention is what a voter with five screeners and a week has to allocate. The studios that can afford a campaign are the ones with a slate to protect, and the independent distributors have learned to run narrower campaigns aimed at specific branches of the Academy. A small distributor with one film can spend more per film than a studio spreading its budget across six releases.

Acting races were less predictable. Adrien Brody won best actor for a performance in a three-and-a-half-hour film about an architect, and Zoe Saldana won supporting actress for a musical that had divided critics. The supporting categories have become the place where a studio can convert a strong ensemble into a prize, and the campaigns reflect that.

International membership is the structural change. A voter in Nigeria or in Poland is less moved by an American awards narrative and more moved by a film that travelled, and the best animated feature went to a Latvian production made with open-source software by a small team. That result would have been unthinkable in the 2000s. A Polish voter and a Nigerian voter are two votes, and the membership is now large enough that those two votes matter.

Ceremony itself is a broadcast with a declining audience, and the Academy has been trading ceremony for reach. Televised categories have been trimmed, film clips have been shortened, and the host is chosen for a younger demographic. A room full of people celebrating a six-million-dollar film is a room that understands its own audience is no longer watching. An audience that watches the ceremony for the moments rather than for the awards is an audience that has already stopped watching the films.
