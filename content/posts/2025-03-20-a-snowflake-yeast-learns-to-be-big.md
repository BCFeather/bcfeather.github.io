---

Snowflake yeast is a laboratory model for the origin of multicellular life. A single cell divides and the daughters stay attached, and the resulting cluster grows into a branching snowflake that sediments faster than a single cell. The cluster is not yet an organism, because each cell is still capable of living alone and a fragment that breaks off can found a new cluster, and the model lets a laboratory watch the first steps of a transition that in nature took hundreds of millions of years.

What the Georgia Tech group did is to make the cells tetraploid. A chemical treatment doubled the chromosome number, and the resulting cells were larger, which made the clusters larger and gave natural selection something to work with. Within fifty days of evolution the tetraploid clusters had become substantially bigger, and the change persisted for 950 days, which is about 5,000 generations.

Size came with a mechanism. The tetraploid cells were elongated, and the elongation was traced to aneuploidy, the loss and gain of individual chromosomes that follows a whole-genome duplication. A cell that carries an extra chromosome adjusts its gene dosage and grows longer, and a longer cell makes a longer branch, which makes a cluster that settles further and faster and that therefore wins the competition for the food at the bottom of the flask.

The second transition was macroscopic multicellularity. Within 600 days of selection some clusters had crossed the threshold at which they can be seen without a microscope, and they had done it without the genetic changes that textbooks say are required. The standard account has multicellularity arising through the cooperation of cells that give up reproduction, and the snowflake clusters kept reproducing by breaking off pieces.

What the experiment suggests is that the early steps are easier than the account assumes. A genome duplication is a single event that happens in a generation, and it supplies both the size and the variation that selection needs. The transition to a body with differentiated tissues is still hard, and the paper places the difficulty at a later step, where the cells have to divide labour and give up their own reproduction.

Generalization is the limit. A laboratory experiment under constant selection is not a Precambrian ocean, and the 950 days of stability were won with a regime that removed the competition that a natural population would face. The authors are careful about the claim, and they present the result as a demonstration of what is possible under selection.

What the result gives the field is a time scale. A transition that is usually described as requiring tens of millions of years has been reproduced in a few hundred, and the difference matters because it says that the first steps of multicellularity may have been frequent in the Precambrian and that the fossil record is not the right place to look for them.
