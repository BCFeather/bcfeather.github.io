European Union leaders agreed on the first of February to create a fifty billion euro fund for Ukraine inside the union's 2021-27 budget, ending weeks of deadlock in which Viktor Orban, the Hungarian prime minister, had blocked the money at a summit in December. Attached to the facility are seventeen billion euros in grants and thirty-three billion in concessional loans, and the arrangement commits the union to financing the Ukrainian state until 2027. A special meeting in Brussels had been called precisely because the December summit failed. The deal was announced within hours of the leaders arriving.

What unlocked it was a review clause. Orban had demanded an annual veto over the spending, on the argument that Hungarian taxpayers should not be committed to four years of payments without a say. He accepted a yearly report by the European Commission and a debate among leaders, with the option of a summit review in 2026 if the money is being spent badly. That is a weaker instrument than a veto and a stronger one than nothing. Both sides described it as a victory within hours of the meeting.

For Ukraine, the arithmetic matters more than the wording. Salaries, pensions and basic services are paid out of this fund, and its failure would have forced the government either to print money or to cut spending in the middle of a war. The American package was still stuck in Congress, where a border-and-aid deal had collapsed in the Senate the previous month. Ukrainian officials had spent December and January telling European capitals that the budget gap was around forty billion dollars, and the facility covers most of it.

Under pressure, the union also settled a question about how it makes decisions. Hungary had used its veto in December in a way that surprised capitals which had assumed the unanimity rule was mostly formal. This time, other leaders worked around it with a mixture of concessions and brinkmanship, including a threat to proceed with the other twenty-six if Budapest did not move. Orban took the deal. On the record, a single member can slow the union down and cannot stop it.

Two smaller decisions were taken at the same meeting and received less attention. Leaders added two billion euros for migration and border management and 7.6 billion for the neighbourhood and the wider world, both parts of the same budget revision. Approval by the European Parliament was still needed, and it came in late February. On the day of the announcement the money was not yet spendable. It was, however, no longer in doubt, and for a government financing a war out of borrowed money that is the difference that counts.

Getting the money out of Brussels will take the rest of the year. Disbursement is tied to a Ukrainian plan that the commission approved in the spring, and payments come in quarterly tranches conditional on reforms in the judiciary, in public procurement and in the management of state enterprises. Officials in Kyiv have said the conditions are manageable and that the first tranche is expected in the spring. Whether the mechanism can move money at the speed a war demands is the question the facility will be judged on, and the answer will not be known until the end of the year.
