The third day of the lunar year is when the city begins to come back. The shutters that were closed on the eve are raised on a handful of shops in the morning, the convenience store on the corner opens for a half day, and the restaurants that serve the neighbourhood put a sign in the window that says they are open from the third.

The people those shops serve are the ones who did not stop. The nurse who finished a night shift, the delivery rider who kept working through the holiday, the metro driver, the security guard at a building site, the staff of a hospital kitchen. The city has a workforce that runs on the calendar of the hospital and the timetable of the train rather than on the calendar of the office.

The economics of opening early are not obvious. A shop that opens on the third pays its staff a holiday rate and serves a fraction of its usual customers, and the decision is usually made because the alternative is worse. A restaurant that stays closed until the sixth loses the customers who go to the one that reopened, and a supermarket that opens early keeps the habit of the household that shops on the way home.

The rhythm of the reopening is staggered and it follows the supply chain. The vegetable stalls come back first because the wholesale market resumes, the restaurants come back next because the staff return from their villages, and the shops that sell the durable goods come back last because their customers are still travelling. The city reassembles in the order of the things it needs.

The holiday also produces a small economy of its own. The temple fair that runs for five days, the cinema that sells out every evening, the park that fills with families, the ice rink that is set up in a plaza. The entertainment that operates during the holiday is the business that compensates for the shops that are shut, and the two are parts of the same week.

What the third day shows is that a city is a set of services rather than a set of buildings. The buildings are there on the first day and the services are not, and the return of the services is what the city feels like. The morning on which the corner shop opens is the morning on which the neighbourhood becomes a neighbourhood again.

For the household that is still on holiday, the third day is also the day on which the festival starts to feel long. The visits have been made, the food has been eaten, and the days that remain are days of rest rather than days of obligation. The calendar that was so full on the eve is empty by the third, and the emptiness is part of the design. A festival that filled every hour of a week would be work, and the days that the calendar leaves open are the ones on which the holiday feels like rest.
