Stress inside soft tissue is invisible to every imaging modality in routine use, and it matters clinically: an artery carries a load that a wall can fail under, a tumour differs from the tissue around it partly in how it bears stress. Elastography measures stiffness, which is a material property, and stress is a different object that depends on the deformation as well as on the material. A method that reports stress without a constitutive law would close a gap that stiffness maps leave open.

The angled shear wave identity is such a method. Two small-amplitude shear waves, launched at an angle to each other, travel at speeds whose difference is proportional to the component of stress along their bisector, and the relation holds whatever the material law happens to be. A preprint posted on 1 November 2023 extends the identity to solids that dissipate energy.

Viscosity alters the measurement, not the principle. Taking a generalised Newtonian viscous stress and allowing transverse isotropy, the authors derive the dispersion relation for small shear waves superimposed on a large static deformation. The identity survives in the same form when the stress is coaxial with the axis of anisotropy, and the speed difference then reports the stress as it did in the elastic case.

Attenuation is the price. In a viscous material the two waves are damped at different rates, and the stress can be recovered only if the attenuation is measured alongside the speed. That is a real experimental obstacle, since attenuation is harder to estimate than a time of flight and is contaminated by diffraction and by the coupling of the waves to the boundary of the sample.

Coaxiality is the condition that makes the identity usable. Muscle is transversely isotropic, with fibres defining an axis, and when a load acts along that axis the formula collapses to the elastic one. When the load is oblique the identity no longer applies directly, and the paper discusses the corrections that the general dispersion relation supplies, which is where the extension earns its keep.

The work is theoretical, and no tissue measurement is reported. A phantom with a known viscous modulus would be the obvious test, and a sample with a controlled anisotropy would show how far the coaxial assumption can be stretched before the correction terms dominate. Memory effects in viscoelastic solids are treated in discussion rather than in detail, which leaves a second axis for later work, and a measurement of attenuation in a phantom would show whether the viscous term can be treated as a perturbation.

For practice, the result says that a stress map in tissue requires two measurements and a model of the medium's viscosity, not one measurement and an assumption. That is more demanding than elastography and also more informative, and the difference matters wherever a diagnosis rests on how a tissue bears load rather than on how stiff it is.
