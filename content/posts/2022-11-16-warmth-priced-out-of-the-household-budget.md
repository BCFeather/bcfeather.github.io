In 2021, 6.9 per cent of the European Union's population lived in a household that could not keep its home adequately warm. The figure comes from the union's own survey of income and living conditions, and it describes a steady condition and not a single event: a flat heated to 16 degrees, a boiler run for two hours a day, a bill settled by cutting the food budget. By October 2022, energy prices in the euro area stood 41.9 per cent above their level a year earlier, and the question that follows is not whether the number above will rise but which households the rise reaches first, and whether the instruments deployed to soften it reach the same ones.

Measurement is the first thing to settle, because the indicator everyone quotes is a self-report. The survey asks whether a household can afford to keep its home warm; it records neither the indoor temperature nor the arrears nor the notice from the supplier. A household that heats one room and calls the result adequate is counted as comfortable, and so is a household borrowing to pay the bill, right up to the month it stops paying. Analysts who work on the subject therefore keep three measures side by side: the self-reported one, the share of households in arrears on utility bills, and the older convention that counts a household as fuel-poor when it spends more than a tenth of its income on energy and is left with too little to live on. The three disagree, and the disagreement is worth keeping.

Comparison across the union runs into climate and construction before it runs into policy. A household in Attica heats for six weeks; a household in Lapland heats for eight months, and the same income buys a different quantity of warmth in each. District heating, electricity tariffs and the age of the housing stock move the figure as much as wages do, which is why the Nordic member states typically report low single figures for the self-reported measure while southern and eastern ones report figures several times higher. A union-level average hides that, and a union-level instrument designed against the average will misprice both ends of it.

Fuel differs from most budget lines in one respect that decides the incidence. A household can postpone a purchase, trade down on food, cancel a subscription; it cannot postpone January. Energy is consumed at the point of use, in a dwelling fixed in place, through equipment the occupant did not choose, and the bill arrives after the cold. Elasticity is low in the short run, and lowest exactly where the budget share is highest, because a household spending a large share of its income on heat has already stripped out the discretionary items that could absorb the increase.

Capacity to adjust is distributed as unequally as income. Replacing a boiler, insulating a loft or fitting double glazing costs money that a poor household does not have, and the tenant who pays the bill is rarely the landlord who owns the fabric of the building, so the incentive to invest is split and usually lost. Prepayment meters, common among low-income tenants, turn a price rise into an immediate interruption: the credit runs out and the supply stops, without a bill and without a warning period. Rationing by price, in other words, arrives earlier and harder for the household with least room to ration anything else.

Tenure decides who can respond. An owner with a mortgage faces the rate on the loan as well as the tariff, and a rise in either can push a household over the forty per cent line that the housing statistics use; an owner without a mortgage has capital and may still lack the cash for a retrofit. A private tenant has neither the fabric nor the incentive, and a social tenant depends on a landlord that may be a housing association with its own capital budget or a municipality with none. The same tariff therefore produces insolvency in one tenure, discomfort in a second and an investment decision in a third, and a policy that addresses the bill alone will reach only the first.

Against that, governments have four instruments, and each has a different incidence. Cutting value added tax or excise reduces the unit price for every kilowatt hour sold, which is fast and administratively simple but pays most to the household that consumes most. A retail price cap does the same through another route: it holds down the visible bill and leaves the difference with the supplier or the taxpayer. A lump sum per household, means-tested or not, is a transfer and not a price, so it protects the budget while leaving the signal intact. Direct regulation of the tariff, of the kind several member states already run, mutes the signal altogether and moves the risk onto the utility's balance sheet.

The distributive arithmetic is worth stating plainly, because it decides the argument. Support proportional to consumption is regressive in cash: a wealthier household with a larger flat, a second home and a car receives a larger subsidy, though the same sum matters less to it. Support proportional to need, through a means-tested transfer or a social tariff, is progressive in welfare terms and harder to administer. A policy can be regressive in its cash flow and progressive in its outcome only if the two are deliberately separated, which is what a well-designed lump sum does and what a VAT cut cannot do.

Fiscal capacity separates the member states as sharply as the weather does. A government with borrowing room can cushion the tariff for a winter and borrow again; one without it must choose between the bill and the deficit, and the choice is made under a fiscal framework that limits both. The union's own instrument is small relative to the sums involved and is designed to preserve the single market and not to redistribute across it, so the cushion is national in practice. That asymmetry matters for the measurement too: the countries whose households are most exposed are often the ones with the least room to compensate, so the observed rate at the end of the winter will reflect the transfer as much as the price.

A counter-case deserves its space. High prices do change behaviour, and the change is not trivial: industrial gas demand fell across the union through 2022, households lowered thermostats, and orders for heat pumps and insulation rose from a low base. A cap that hides the price removes the incentive to conserve at the moment when conservation is cheapest, and the fiscal cost of a general cap reaches into the tens of billions in the larger member states. Where the aim is to cut the quantity consumed and hand the saving to households that need it, a price that moves plus a targeted transfer is the stronger instrument on both counts.

Targeting, though, has failure modes of its own, and they are practical. Means-tested schemes need registers, and registers miss the households that most need help: informal tenants who are not on the lease, migrant workers without a tax record, people on prepayment meters whose consumption never appears in a billing database, pensioners who never claim the benefit they are entitled to. Universal schemes miss nobody and overpay many. The choice is between an exclusion error that falls on the poorest and an inclusion error that falls on the treasury, and the political system usually prefers the second.

Definitional choices are political choices in miniature. The tenth-of-income indicator counts a middle-income household in a cold climate as fuel-poor if it spends enough on heat and is left with little, which is defensible as a measure of hardship and awkward as a measure of deprivation. The consensual measure asks whether the household goes without other things in order to heat, which is narrower and captures the poorest more reliably. A government that prefers the first measure will report a larger problem and target it broadly; one that prefers the second will report a smaller problem and target it narrowly, and neither is lying. The statistics do not settle the argument about who should be helped; they encode an answer to it.

What remains genuinely unsettled is the size of the health effect. Excess winter mortality is measured with a lag and confounded by influenza, housing quality and age structure, and the studies linking cold homes to hospital admissions are observational. The evidence is strong enough to say that cold dwellings are associated with worse respiratory and cardiovascular outcomes; it is not strong enough to say how many deaths a given rise in the gas tariff will cause, and any figure offered this winter would be an inference dressed as a count.

The design question underneath all of this is not technical. A union can treat warmth as a commodity whose price should tell the truth, and accept that some households will be priced out of it; or it can treat a warm home as a service with a floor, and pay for the floor through general taxation. Most member states have chosen a mixture, and the mixture is unstable because the two halves pull in opposite directions, one towards a bill that reflects cost and one towards a bill that reflects need. What the coming winter will show is not whether the instrument works in the aggregate but who falls outside it, and whether anyone is counting them.
