Darya Dugina was killed on Saturday evening when a device exploded under the car she was driving near the town of Bolshiye Vyazyomy, about forty kilometres west of Moscow. She was twenty-nine and the daughter of Alexander Dugin, a writer and theorist whose ideas about a Eurasian empire have been cited by people close to the Kremlin. The car caught fire and the investigators found the remains of a bomb attached to the underside. Her father had been travelling in another vehicle and escaped unhurt.

The investigation has been conducted by the federal security service, and it announced within two days that the attack had been carried out by a Ukrainian woman who had entered the country with her child and rented an apartment in the same building as Dugina. The service published her name, her photographs and the details of a car that it said she had used, and it said she had left for Estonia. The Ukrainian government denied any involvement, and the American administration said it had no information that would connect the government in Kyiv to the attack.

Dugina was a commentator rather than a politician. She wrote for the media outlets that the state funds, she appeared on the television programmes that discuss the war, and she had been sanctioned by the British and the American governments for her role in the propaganda. Her father's influence is debated in Moscow, and the people who have worked with him say that his access to the leadership has been exaggerated. Her death was reported as a loss by the broadcasters she worked for, and the programmes that she appeared on devoted the next day to it.

The timing drew attention. The attack came three days before Ukraine's independence day and six months after the invasion, and it followed a series of explosions and fires at installations inside Russia and in the occupied territories. The security service has blamed Ukraine for several of those, and the Ukrainian government has generally neither confirmed nor denied them. The analysts said the attack may have been intended to produce a public demand for retaliation, and that the leadership has so far answered it with the language it has used since February.

At the same time, the reaction abroad was muted. The European Union's foreign policy office said it had noted the reports and that it did not comment on the death of a person who had promoted the war. The Russian foreign ministry said the attack showed that the country was at war with a terrorism that the west had encouraged, and it demanded that Estonia hand over the woman it named. The Estonian government said it had received no such request.

What the killing does is put a name and a face on the argument about the war inside Russia. Dugina was the daughter of a man whose ideas the state has adopted and a woman who made her own career in the media that promote them, and she was killed by a method that the state has used abroad. The investigation will publish its conclusion and the conclusion will be believed by the people who already believe it, and the argument about who ordered the attack will not be settled by the evidence that has been released.
