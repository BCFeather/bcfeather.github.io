A museum's visible collection is a fraction of what it holds, and the invisible part is usually undocumented. A national museum reported this month that it has catalogued all 400,000 objects in its storage, and the project took eleven years and produced a finding index that changes what the museum can lend.

The backlog was a paper one. Records existed in 34 separate ledgers and card indexes, and a third of the objects had a location that was a room rather than a shelf. The project's team photographed every object and linked the photograph to a location code.

Digitization's cost was 40 dollars per object, of which 70 per cent was the photography and the data entry rather than the software. The project ran on a fixed annual budget and finished within it by processing the largest series first. The by-product is the finding index. A curator planning an exhibition can now ask what the collection holds on a subject, and the answers include objects that no one has seen in fifty years. Two exhibitions scheduled for next year are built from such answers.

What the collection lacks is the second by-product. The index shows that the museum holds 40 objects from one region and 4,000 from another, and the disparity is documented rather than inferred, which is the evidence an acquisitions committee needs. Access policy is the open question. The photographs are public and the objects' locations are not, because a published location is a security risk, and the museum's system shows a photograph and a request form. A museum with 20,000 objects finished the same job faster. A museum with 20,000 objects finished a similar project in two years with two staff, and its catalog is more detailed per object. Scale changes the method and not the value.

A location code is the piece that makes a catalogue usable. An object with a description and no shelf is a record of an object that a curator cannot retrieve, and the project's decision to photograph every object is what allows a code to be verified by a person who has never handled it.

The photography is also the access policy's foundation. A museum that publishes a photograph satisfies a researcher's first question, and a request for the object follows a form. That two-step is the compromise between access and security that most collections now use. A catalogue's maintenance is the cost that follows the project. An object moves, a loan returns, a label is corrected, and a record that is not updated is a record that decays. A museum that finished a catalogue has begun a database.

A collection becomes knowable only when someone can find the objects in it. A catalogue is not a catalogue of what a museum owns; it is a catalogue of what a museum can do, and the difference is the objects no one could find.
