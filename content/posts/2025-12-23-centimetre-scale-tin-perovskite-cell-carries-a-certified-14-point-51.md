Tin perovskites are the plausible successor to lead ones. Take out the lead and the toxicity argument hanging over the technology goes away, and the bandgap can be tuned across much of the visible spectrum. The material fights back in two ways. Tin oxidizes from the divalent to the tetravalent state during processing and operation, which loads the film with defects and drains performance, and efficiency collapses as soon as the active area grows past a few square millimetres. Small-area records have looked respectable for years while centimetre-scale devices trailed badly behind them.

At Fudan University's State Key Laboratory of Photovoltaic Science and Technology, Tianpeng Li and colleagues, with Jia Liang, Yang Wang and Yan Zhao, replaced the electron-transport layer rather than tuning it. The usual choice is a fullerene derivative, most often ICBA, and the team instead synthesized three fluorinated acceptor polymers from a backbone of two diketopyrrolopyrrole units flanking a difluorobenzothiadiazole acceptor and coated cells with them. *Nature Energy* published the report on 5 December 2025, and its centrepiece is an independent certification.

Certified at 15.90 per cent for the 0.04-square-centimetre cell and 14.51 per cent for the centimetre-scale one, the same two devices measured 16.06 and 14.67 per cent in the laboratory, against 11.74 and 11.32 per cent for the fullerene reference under the same conditions, a gap the authors put at nearly 30 per cent. The certificate came from TÜV NORD. Open-circuit voltage stood at 0.99 volts on the small device and 0.98 on the large one, short-circuit current at 19.79 and 19.72 milliamperes per square centimetre, fill factor at 81.98 per cent and 76.47 per cent.

Transport is what the polymer changes first. Electron mobility in the fluorinated films runs from 1.29 to 3.17 square centimetres per volt second against 0.0069 for ICBA, three orders of magnitude apart, and a layer that conducts well forgives the thicker, rougher coating a large area demands. Fluorine substituents and long alkyl side chains together make the film hydrophobic, which matters because moisture is one of the routes to tin oxidation. Defect density fell by an order of magnitude. Both devices kept more than 85 per cent of their starting efficiency after 550 hours of continuous one-sun illumination, a stability the authors attribute to the water-repelling surface.

One square centimetre is a cell. Module-scale results are absent from the paper, and the authors state that two classes of devices were excluded from the statistics, those with visible defects and those with uneven coating, so the reported spread comes from a screened set. Error bars are absent from the efficiency values too. The ageing ran on encapsulated samples at room temperature and moderate humidity, which leaves the harsher qualification conditions untested, and tin oxidation remains the mechanism the whole field manages but has not solved.

If the transport advantage is to survive the coating methods production actually uses, a module-area certified device has to show it first. A long run at qualification conditions would then show whether a hydrophobic layer is enough to hold tin in the divalent state. The transferable part is the substitution itself: the layer that carries electrons is where large-area tin cells had been failing, and it can be changed without touching the perovskite chemistry.
