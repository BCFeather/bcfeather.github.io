Tin perovskites are the plausible successor to lead ones. Take out the lead and the toxicity argument hanging over the technology goes away, and the bandgap can be tuned across much of the visible spectrum. The material fights back in two ways. Tin oxidizes from the divalent to the tetravalent state during processing and operation, which loads the film with defects and drains performance, and efficiency collapses as soon as the active area grows past a few square millimetres. Small-area records have looked respectable for years while centimetre-scale devices trailed badly behind them.

Tianpeng Li and colleagues at the State Key Laboratory of Photovoltaic Science and Technology at Fudan University, with Jia Liang, Yang Wang and Yan Zhao, replaced the electron-transport layer rather than tuning it. The usual choice is a fullerene derivative, most often ICBA, and the team instead synthesized three fluorinated acceptor polymers from a diketopyrrolopyrrole and a difluorobenzothiadiazole backbone and coated cells with them. Their report appeared in *Nature Energy* on 5 December 2025, in a paper whose centrepiece is certification and not a laboratory reading.

Measured numbers carry the argument. A cell with an active area of 0.04 square centimetres reached 16.06 per cent power conversion efficiency with an independently certified value of 15.90 per cent, and the one-square-centimetre device reached 14.67 per cent with a certified 14.51 per cent from TÜV NORD. The fullerene reference under the same conditions gave 11.74 per cent and 11.32 per cent, a gap the authors put at nearly 30 per cent. Open-circuit voltage stood at 0.99 volts on the small device and 0.98 on the large one, short-circuit current at 19.79 and 19.72 milliamperes per square centimetre, fill factor at 81.98 per cent and 76.47 per cent.

What the polymer changes first is transport. Electron mobility in the fluorinated films runs from 1.29 to 3.17 square centimetres per volt second against 0.0069 for ICBA, three orders of magnitude apart, and a layer that conducts well forgives the thicker, rougher coating a large area demands. The fluorine also makes the film hydrophobic, lifting the contact angle from 59.9 degrees to 103.2, which matters because moisture is one of the routes to tin oxidation. Defect density fell by an order of magnitude. The best device kept 86 per cent of its starting efficiency after 550 hours of continuous one-sun illumination at room temperature near 50 per cent relative humidity, against 49 per cent for the fullerene reference, and a stored cell retained 97 per cent over 1,200 hours.

One square centimetre is a cell, not a module, and no module result appears here. The authors state that two classes of devices were excluded from the statistics, those with visible defects and those with uneven coating, so the reported spread comes from a screened set. No error bars accompany the efficiency values. The ageing ran on encapsulated samples at room temperature and moderate humidity, which leaves the harsher qualification conditions untested, and tin oxidation remains the mechanism the whole field manages but has not solved.

Two results would move this from record to route. A module-area certified device would show whether the transport advantage survives the coating methods production actually uses. A long run at qualification conditions would show whether a hydrophobic layer is enough to hold tin in the divalent state. The transferable part is the substitution itself: the layer that carries electrons is where large-area tin cells had been failing, and it can be changed without touching the perovskite chemistry.
