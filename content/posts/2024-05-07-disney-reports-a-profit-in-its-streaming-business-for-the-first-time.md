Disney reported an operating profit of 47 million dollars in its streaming division for the quarter to the end of March, the first quarterly profit since the service was launched, on revenue of 22.08 billion dollars. The shares fell anyway, because the profit came with a warning about the next quarter. The warning concerned the quarter to June, when the company expects the streaming division to lose a small amount again because of the cost of launching in new markets.

Arithmetic of the turn is simple. The company cut spending on new titles, raised prices and added advertising to the cheaper tier, and the combination moved a business that had been losing more than a billion dollars a year into a small surplus. A surplus that small is a signal about direction rather than about scale. The subscriber count fell slightly in the quarter, which is the number the market watched during the years of growth, and the company has shifted the emphasis to revenue per subscriber.

Advertising is the new revenue line. The tier that carries commercials is priced lower and earns more per user than the subscription it replaces, because the advertiser pays for the inventory and the user pays for the discount. A cheaper plan that earns more is a plan that the company wants to sell. The inventory that the advertising tier sells is limited by the number of hours people watch, and the company has begun to sell advertisements inside the sports broadcasts that draw the largest live audiences.

Parks remain the engine. The division that operates the resorts earned several times what the studios earned, and the company said the cruise ships it has ordered will add capacity from next year. A capital-intensive division with pricing power is the balance that the streaming losses were drawing on. The resorts have been raising prices faster than inflation for a decade, and the attendance has held, which is a demonstration of pricing power that few other consumer businesses can match.

Studio business is the laggard. Fewer films were released because of the strikes, and the ones that arrived did not perform as the company hoped, which means the content pipeline that feeds the streaming service is thinner than the schedule assumed. A pipeline that is interrupted takes two years to repair. The films that were delayed by the strikes will arrive over the next two years, and the company has said the slate will be smaller because it is buying fewer scripts and making fewer bets.

What the quarter shows is that a legacy media company can make a streaming service profitable by charging more and spending less, and the question is whether that combination keeps the subscribers the service needs. A media company's streaming profit is now a function of what it spends on content, and the question is how far the spending can fall before the subscribers begin to notice.
