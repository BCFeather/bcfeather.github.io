title: "Carbon offsets turn out to be failing in the forest"
date: "2023-08-24T09:30:00+08:00"

description: "A matched-control evaluation of forest offset projects finds that the emission reductions most of them claim are not additional to what would have happened anyway."
---

A carbon offset claims that an emission avoided somewhere compensates for an emission made here, and that claim rests on additionality. A project that would have happened anyway reduces nothing. Evaluation reported in *Science* on 24 August 2023 tests additionality for forest projects. The authors compare deforestation rates inside project areas with rates in matched control areas, and the difference is the reduction the projects can legitimately claim.

Shortfall is the result. The reduction the projects claim is much larger than the reduction the comparison supports, and the gap is the credits buyers paid for and did not receive.

Matching supplies the method. A control area is chosen to resemble a project area in forest type, in access and in pressure, and an unmatched comparison would confound the two; the counterfactual is what the control estimates, and it is the only number an offset market can be judged on. Leakage is the systemic problem, because a market that protects one forest and logs another has moved the emission rather than removed it.

Permanence adds a third failure. A forest protected for a decade and then cleared has stored carbon for a decade, and a credit assuming a century is a credit that may not exist; a tonne stored and later released is a tonne the atmosphere sees, and the time horizon is a policy choice rather than a physical fact.

Dispute turns on baselines. A project's baseline is a projection of what would have happened, and a projection is an assumption rather than a measurement, which is why a registry's method decides the outcome; a generous baseline produces credits the atmosphere does not honour.

No remedy is proposed, since a better baseline method is a suggestion whose adoption depends on the registries that issue credits. The sample is a set of projects in one country, so the market's other project types stay unevaluated. A comparison with a cookstove project would show that the failure is common across project types, because both rest on a baseline the seller controls and both claim reductions the atmosphere cannot verify.

Climate policy gains evidence about an instrument used at scale, since an instrument that does not deliver is one a government should fix rather than defend. A method using observed control areas rather than projected trends is the reform the approach points to, and its adoption is where the next evaluation would begin. A registry that published its project boundaries and its baseline assumptions would let an independent group repeat the comparison, and reproducibility is where the offset market's credibility would most cheaply be rebuilt. A set of projects matched on the same driver of deforestation would also separate a bad baseline from a bad project, since a project placed where pressure was already falling cannot be additional however carefully it is drawn. A pilot project whose baseline was fixed before the project began would show whether the method can be made honest when the seller no longer chooses the counterfactual.
