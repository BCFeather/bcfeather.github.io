Acute myeloid leukaemia in an older or frail patient is treated with a hypomethylating agent plus venetoclax, a combination that improved survival over the hypomethylating agent alone and became the standard for people who cannot tolerate intensive induction. Both components have usually been given intravenously or subcutaneously in the trials that established them. Those patients are typically over seventy-five or have cardiac, pulmonary or renal disease that makes anthracycline and cytarabine induction dangerous. Transfusion support and prophylactic antimicrobials still have to be arranged, and those visits are the practical limit on how far home treatment can go.

ASCERTAIN-V, reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 3 June, tested an all-oral version: decitabine with cedazuridine, a cytidine deaminase inhibitor that allows the drug to survive first-pass metabolism in the gut and liver, combined with oral venetoclax. The regimen pairs a fixed-dose combination of decitabine and cedazuridine with venetoclax, both taken by mouth, and the trial enrolled newly diagnosed patients ineligible for intensive treatment. The trial was a phase 1/2 with expansion cohorts, which means the response rate carries the uncertainty of a single-arm design.

Appeal is logistical and clinical at once. A regimen that a patient can take at home removes the infusion visits that consume a family's months, and it removes the line infections that come with repeated intravenous access in a population that is immunosuppressed by the disease itself. Home treatment also reduces the time a patient spends neutropenic in hospital, which is where most of the fatal infections in this population are acquired.

Response rates and remission duration in the trial were consistent with what the intravenous combination produces, and the safety profile matched the myelosuppression that hypomethylating agents and BCL2 inhibitors are known to cause. Matching an established regimen is a meaningful result in this setting, because the trial was not powered to show superiority. That comparison is the one that a haematologist will make when deciding whether to switch a stable patient.

Cedazuridine pharmacology is the interesting part. Oral decitabine alone is largely destroyed before it reaches the circulation, and the inhibitor exists specifically to make an oral dose equivalent to an intravenous one. The exposure data in the trial are what allow that equivalence to be claimed. Cedazuridine has no anticancer activity of its own, and its only purpose is to raise the systemic exposure of the partner drug. A fixed-dose combination also removes the dosing arithmetic that an oral regimen would otherwise demand of a patient taking several drugs.

Adoption will turn on price and on compounding, since an oral regimen shifts cost from infusion suites to pharmacy budgets, and on adherence, which is harder to verify at home than in a chair. The trial establishes that the combination works; the health service will decide whether it is worth reorganizing around. Equivalence also depends on patients taking the tablets as prescribed, and adherence in an elderly population with multiple medications is not guaranteed.
