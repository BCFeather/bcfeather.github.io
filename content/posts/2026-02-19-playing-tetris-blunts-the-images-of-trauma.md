Intrusive memories are visual. A flashback is a picture that arrives uninvited, and the treatments that work for post-traumatic stress disorder work on the meaning and the memory rather than on the image itself. That has led to a hypothesis with a specific prediction: if intrusive memories are maintained by visual imagery, then a task that occupies the visual working memory at the right moment should interfere with them.

Tetris is such a task. Rotating and fitting shapes uses the same cognitive machinery that holds a mental image, and the intervention tested in the trial asks people to play the game shortly after a traumatic event while recalling the memory, so that the visual resources that would consolidate the image are occupied elsewhere. The trial was conducted with health-care staff exposed to traumatic events during the COVID-19 pandemic in the United Kingdom and is a Bayesian adaptive randomized clinical trial. It appeared in *The Lancet Psychiatry*, and the University of Oxford reported the results on 19 February.

The effect was large. At one month, participants who received the intervention reported about ten times fewer intrusive memories than those in either control group, one of which listened to music and podcasts about Mozart and the other of which received standard care. At six months, 70 per cent of those who received the intervention reported no intrusive memories at all, and broader post-traumatic stress symptoms were also reduced.

Tenfold is a number that invites scepticism and a number that randomized trials occasionally produce when the mechanism is specific. The comparison against both an active and a passive control is what makes the result credible, because it rules out the possibility that any engaging activity would have done the same. Music was chosen as the active control precisely because it engages attention without engaging visuospatial resources.

The intervention is digital, brief and scalable, which is the part that matters for public health. Trauma exposure is common, therapists are scarce, and a guided task that can be delivered by a smartphone is the kind of treatment that reaches people who would never enter a clinic. The trial delivered it with guidance, and an unguided version is the next thing to test.

Generalizability is the open question. The participants were health-care workers during a pandemic, a population with a particular kind of exposure and a particular kind of baseline resilience, and the intervention was delivered soon after the events. Whether it works for childhood abuse, for repeated trauma or for chronic post-traumatic stress disorder is unknown and is not implied by this result.

Deeper still is a claim about how memory consolidation can be interfered with deliberately. A brief visuospatial task delivered at the right time alters what a person remembers six months later, which is a statement about the mechanics of memory that has therapeutic consequences and that will be tested in other imagery-based conditions.
