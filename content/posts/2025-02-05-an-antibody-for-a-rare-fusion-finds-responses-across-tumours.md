NRG1 fusions are rare. The gene encodes a growth factor that binds the HER3 receptor, and a fusion joins its coding region to a partner that drives expression, which turns the growth factor into a signal that the cell cannot switch off and that no upstream inhibitor can reach. The fusion appears in about one in a hundred lung adenocarcinomas, in a small share of pancreatic cancers and in a scattering of others, and a patient with one has had no targeted option, only chemotherapy and the hope that the tumour is slow.

Zenocutuzumab is a bispecific antibody that binds HER2 and HER3 and blocks the interaction between NRG1 and HER3. The design is unusual, because the antibody does not need to inhibit a kinase; it has to occupy the receptor surface that the ligand uses, which is a job that a large molecule can do and a small molecule cannot.

A paper in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 5 February reports the results from a phase 2 trial that enrolled patients with NRG1 fusion-positive cancers across tumour types. Among 158 patients with measurable disease who had been enrolled at least 24 weeks before the data cut-off, a response occurred in 30 per cent, and the median duration of response was 11.1 months.

Responses appeared in several tumour types, including 29 per cent of the 93 patients with lung adenocarcinoma and in patients with pancreatic and other cancers. That breadth is the argument for a tissue-agnostic approval, which regulators have granted for other fusion-driven drugs and which a rare target needs, because no single tumour type will supply enough patients for a conventional trial with an internal comparator.

Side effects were those of the HER2 and HER3 blockade and of the infusion. Diarrhoea, infusion-related reactions and fatigue were the common events, and the rates were moderate, which matters because the drug is given until progression and a patient may receive it for a year or more. The trial had no control arm, which is standard for a rare molecular subset, and the response rate is the endpoint that a single-arm study can defend.

What the result changes for a patient is the availability of a test. A lung cancer diagnosed without molecular profiling is a cancer that may carry a fusion nobody looked for, and the drugs that make profiling worthwhile have accumulated to the point where a panel is the standard. NRG1 is one of the entries on that panel that has now acquired a treatment.

Limits are the ones a small trial carries. Thirty per cent is a response rate, not a cure, and half of the responders relapse within a year. The mechanism of resistance is unknown, and the next question is whether a second-generation molecule or a combination can extend the duration, which is the question that every targeted drug in a rare subset eventually faces.
