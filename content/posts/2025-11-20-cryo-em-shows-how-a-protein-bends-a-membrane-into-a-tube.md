A cell's membrane is a fluid sheet, and the proteins that shape it into tubes and spheres do so by inserting a wedge into one side. A structural study reported this month resolved how one such protein bends a membrane, and the shape's detail is what links a mutation to a nerve disease.

The protein's shape is a crescent. Its transmembrane domain is an arc that contacts the lipid headgroups on the outer side and leaves a gap on the inner side, and the asymmetry produces a curvature of 1 per 40 nanometres.

Solving the structure at 3.2 angstroms required a lipid environment, using a nanodisc that preserves the bilayer's thickness. Earlier structures in detergent lost the lipid contacts that the mechanism depends on.

A single substitution in the arc's inner face causes the disease. The mutant protein binds the membrane but does not bend it, and a patient's fibroblasts show elongated rather than tubular membrane structures.

The mechanism's generality is the paper's claim. Three other proteins with the same fold produce different curvatures, and the arc's radius correlates with the tube's diameter across the family. A membrane that a protein cannot bend stays flat, and a cell's traffic of vesicles depends on the bending happening on schedule.

Chemical models describe a different scale of the same effect. A lipid's own curvature is a property of its headgroup's size, and a protein's wedge is a local perturbation. The paper's argument is that the perturbation dominates at the scales a cell uses. Reconstitution is the test that follows: a purified protein and a lipid mixture in a test tube should form the same tubes, and the paper reports that they do.

The therapeutic angle is speculative and stated as such. A molecule that fills the mutant's gap would restore the bend in principle, and the paper reports no such molecule. A drug that stabilizes the lattice rather than the monomer is the therapeutic idea the structure suggests, and the paper is explicit that it is an idea.

A crescent's two ends are the membrane-binding faces, and a lattice of crescents closes into a ring whose inner diameter sets the tube's width. The structure explains the width, which a chemical model could not.

The mutation's position explains the disease's inheritance. A substitution on the inner face distorts the ring's curvature, and a cell with one functional copy produces a mixture of rings that is less stable than either. That is the mechanism a dominant negative allele uses.

A membrane's curvature is a physical cost and a protein pays it. Bending a bilayer into a 20-nanometre tube costs energy that a crescent's lattice supplies by binding, and the structure's arrangement is the mechanism that makes the cost affordable at body temperature.

Structure explains a disease here, with a residue's shape changing a membrane's shape. A mutation that changes a residue's shape changes the membrane's shape, and the membrane's shape is what a nerve cell's function requires.
