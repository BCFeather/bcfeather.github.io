A lake is a reservoir with no gauge on most of it. The world has millions of them, and fewer than a thousand are monitored with a level record, which leaves the rest to inference from space.

In *Science* on 19 May 2023, that inference becomes global. The authors combine satellite measurements of surface area and of water level to estimate the volume of large lakes between 1992 and 2020, and find that more than half of them lost water over the period.

Magnitude is the headline. The net loss amounts to a few per cent of the global total, which is a large volume in absolute terms and a small fraction in relative terms. Attribution is the harder half of the analysis, since the authors separate climate drivers such as warming, evaporation and shifting precipitation from human use through irrigation and reservoir management, and the decomposition assigns most of the loss to a combination of the two.

Geography is uneven. Some regions' lakes have grown with increased rainfall while others have shrunk with abstraction, and a global average hides a pattern that matters where the loss is concentrated. Reservoirs need separating from lakes, because a lake managed for hydropower has its level set by an operator and a natural one has it set by climate, and that distinction is what makes the attribution defensible.

Combination is the methodological innovation. A satellite measuring area and one measuring height give different quantities, and a model fusing them estimates volume, which turns many local observations into a global inventory. Extrapolation carries the uncertainty, since a satellite measures area and a model converts it to volume, and that conversion's error is the largest term, which validation against gauges bounds.

Consequences are practical. A shrinking lake loses its fishery, its shoreline and its dilution capacity, and the communities around it lose the water they depend on. Conflict is political, because a lake spanning borders holds shared water, and a decline measured from space is evidence in a negotiation that previously relied on national claims. Large lakes set the bias, as the survey covers lakes above a size threshold and small ones, the majority and the most vulnerable, are not resolved. Future stays unestablished, since a three-decade trend is a trend and a projection needs a model of the drivers.

Water management gains an independent measurement, because a country's own statistics can be incomplete and a satellite record is a check a dispute can use. A shrinking lake is also one whose water rights were allocated in a wetter decade, and a basin plan assuming the old level is a plan that will fail. A basin that closes its water balance can be modelled with the drivers the paper names, which is how the survey becomes an instrument for planning rather than a record of loss. Groundwater complicates the accounting, because a lake fed by an aquifer can decline while its rainfall is unchanged, and the satellites cannot see the aquifer.
