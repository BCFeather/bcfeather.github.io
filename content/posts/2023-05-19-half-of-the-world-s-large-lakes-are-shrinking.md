---

A lake is a reservoir with no gauge on most of it. The world has millions of them, and fewer than a thousand are monitored with a level record, which leaves the rest to inference from space.

The survey in *Science*, 19 May 2023 makes the inference global. The authors combine satellite measurements of surface area and of water level to estimate the volume of large lakes between 1992 and 2020, and find that more than half of them lost water over the period.

The magnitude is the headline. The net loss amounts to a few per cent of the global total, which is a large volume in absolute terms and a small fraction in relative terms.

The attribution is the analysis's harder half. The authors separate the drivers into climate, which includes warming, evaporation and changes in precipitation, and human use, which includes irrigation and reservoir management. The two are not independent, and the paper's decomposition assigns most of the loss to a combination.

The geography is uneven. Some regions' lakes have grown with increased rainfall, and others have shrunk with abstraction. A global average hides a pattern that matters for the basins where the loss is concentrated.

The method's innovation is the combination. A satellite that measures area and one that measures height give two different quantities, and a model that fuses them estimates volume. The fusion is what turns many local observations into a global inventory.

The consequences are practical. A shrinking lake loses its fishery, its shoreline and its dilution capacity, and the communities around it lose the water they depend on.

The conflict is political. A lake that spans borders is a lake whose water is shared, and a decline measured from space is evidence in a negotiation that previously relied on national claims.

The caveat is the large lakes' bias. The survey covers lakes above a size threshold, and small lakes, which are the majority and the most vulnerable, are not resolved.

What the paper cannot establish is the future. A trend measured over three decades is a trend, and a projection requires a model of the drivers.

For water management, the value is an independent measurement. A country's own statistics can be incomplete, and a satellite record is a check that a dispute can use.

The policy use is a warning. A lake that is shrinking is a lake whose water rights were allocated in a wetter decade, and a basin plan that assumes the old level is a plan that will fail.

The uncertainty is in the extrapolation. A satellite measures area and a model converts area to volume, and the conversion's error is the largest term in the estimate. The authors' validation against gauges is what bounds it.
The reservoir distinction matters. A lake that is managed for hydropower has a level set by an operator, and a lake that is natural has a level set by climate. The paper's separation of the two is what makes the attribution of the decline to climate and abstraction defensible.
