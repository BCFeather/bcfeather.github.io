Forecasts are revised for two kinds of reason: the world changes, or the forecaster learns something it already had the data to see. The International Monetary Fund's April outlook contained both. Global growth was marked down to 2.8 per cent for 2025 from 3.3 per cent in January, and to 3.0 per cent for 2026. That is a half point of world output removed in three months, which is a large revision by the standards of a quarterly exercise.

The composition explains the total. Advanced economies were projected to grow 1.4 per cent in 2025 and 1.5 per cent in 2026, while emerging market and developing economies were projected at 3.7 and 3.9 per cent. The gap between the two groups is roughly 2.3 percentage points, which is narrower than it was a decade ago and wider than it was before the pandemic. A world average of 2.8 per cent is therefore a rich-world number with a poor-world offset.

Tariffs were the stated reason. The fund described trade policy as the principal source of uncertainty, and it revised down the economies most exposed to the new duties and to the retaliation that followed. Its own commentary noted that the effective tariff rate facing American imports had risen to a level not seen in decades, and that the effect on growth arrives through three channels: higher prices, lower investment and disrupted supply chains.

Separating the channels matters, because they act on different clocks. Higher prices show up in inflation within months and reduce real incomes; lower investment shows up in orders for machinery within a quarter or two; disrupted supply chains show up when a firm cannot get a component, which is the slowest and the most persistent. A forecast made in April can capture the first and only guess at the other two.

The fund also flagged the financial channel. A tariff shock that raises inflation complicates the job of a central bank, and a central bank that cannot cut leaves the growth shock unoffset. In the fund's framing, the risk was not a recession but a period in which inflation stays above target while growth slows, which is the combination that policy has the least room to answer.

There is a politics inside the numbers. The outlook is published days after the spring meetings, where finance ministers and central bank governors argue about the same document, and the fund's projections become the baseline against which national budgets are defended. A downward revision is therefore an argument as well as a forecast, and the argument is usually about who should adjust first.

What would settle the forecast is the second half of the year. If trade policy settles into a stable set of rates, investment resumes and the revision is undone in the next round. If it keeps changing, the fund's 2.8 per cent will look optimistic, because a forecast assumes a policy that stops moving, and nothing in April suggested that this one would.
