The steppe is the homeland that the standard model gives to the Indo-European languages, and the model has been supported by the genetics of the Yamnaya pastoralists who spread across Europe. A dataset from the southern arc of the same migration puts the direction of travel in question. A model that has been supported by the genomes of one population is a model that a sample from another direction can overturn, and the southern arc is that sample.

Ancient DNA was assembled from the Southern Arc, the region from the Balkans through Anatolia to the Caucasus and the Levant, and compared it with the genomes of the steppe populations. The paper appeared in *Science*, 26 August 2022. A dataset assembled from the Balkans to the Caucasus is a dataset that crosses the boundary that the standard model assumes, and the boundary is what the study tests.

Two streams of migration transmitted Caucasus and Anatolian or Levantine ancestry northward, and the Yamnaya pastoralists who formed on the steppe then spread southward into the Balkans and across the Caucasus into Armenia. The traffic was in both directions, and the first direction had been invisible. A traffic in both directions is the finding that the earlier literature could not see, because it sampled the steppe and the destinations and not the corridor between them.

Anatolia was transformed by gene flow within West Asia, with negligible impact from the later Yamnaya migrations, which is the observation that the rest of the continent contradicts. Everywhere else in the Indo-European range, the steppe ancestry is detectable, and Anatolia is the exception. A continent-wide pattern with one exception is a pattern that a single anomalous region can explain, and Anatolia is the region that the paper explains.

The homeland of the Indo-Anatolian family therefore lay in West Asia, with secondary dispersals of the non-Anatolian Indo-Europeans from the steppe. A homeland is a hypothesis about a language and not about a gene, and the two are being argued from the same data. A homeland argued from genes and a homeland argued from words are two arguments that can agree without being the same, and the agreement is the interesting part.

A corridor that carries people in both directions is a corridor that carries languages in both directions, and the question is which direction came first. The argument is that the Anatolian branch split before the steppe expansion, which is what the linguistics had suggested and what the genetics now supports. A branch that splits before the expansion is a branch that the family tree places first, and the placement is what the genetics now supports.

What the result does not do is remove the steppe from the story, because the non-Anatolian languages still arrive from it. The result rearranges the order of events, and the order is the part that a family tree encodes. An order of events is what a family tree encodes, and the reordering is the contribution that the paper makes to a century-old argument.
