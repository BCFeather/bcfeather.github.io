description: "Japan started discharging treated and diluted water from the wrecked Fukushima Daiichi plant into the Pacific on 24 August 2023, a step the operator had planned for two years and which China answered by banning Japanese seafood."
---

Tokyo Electric Power began releasing the water at about one o'clock on 24 August, through a pipe that runs a kilometre out to sea. The first batch, some seven hundred and eighty thousand litres held in a tank, had been filtered to remove radioactive isotopes other than tritium, then diluted with seawater until the tritium concentration fell below fifteen hundred becquerels per litre, a quarter of the level the World Health Organization allows in drinking water. The plant holds about 1.3 million tonnes in more than a thousand tanks, and the release, at the planned rate, is expected to take thirty years.

This water is the residue of an accident. In March 2011 three reactors melted down after a tsunami flooded the plant, and since then the utility has been pumping seawater through the wrecked cores to keep them cool. The water that comes out is contaminated, and it has been stored in tanks that are now nearly full. The alternative to releasing it, the government has said, is to build more tanks on land that is needed for the next stage of decommissioning, which is the argument that decided the question.

Monitoring has been dense and international. The International Atomic Energy Agency has a team at the plant and published its conclusions in July, finding that the discharge would have a negligible radiological effect on people and the environment. Japanese officials say samples are tested before release and after it, and fish caught near the outlet are checked for caesium and tritium. Independent scientists have said the plan is credible and have added that tritium cannot be filtered out of water and is therefore the one isotope whose concentration matters, and that the Pacific's own background radiation is many times the level the discharge will add.

The region's reaction was political and immediate. China banned imports of Japanese seafood, which amounted to about a tenth of Japan's seafood exports, and Hong Kong followed. South Korea's government said its own tests found the discharge acceptable, and its opposition held protests in Seoul that were large and loud. Japanese fishing cooperatives, who had spent two years lobbying against the plan, said the loss of the Chinese market would finish businesses that had already survived a decade of restrictions.

A legal case continues. China has taken the dispute to the World Trade Organization, and Japan has said it will defend the release there. Pacific island states asked for more consultation before the water was released, and Japan's government argued that the monitoring is more transparent than any alternative would be. The scientific question is narrow; the political one asks who decides what happens to a shared ocean, and on what evidence.

A pipe is discharging filtered water into the Pacific, in a quantity that is small compared with the seawater around it, and the argument about it is larger than the science. Japan has chosen to proceed and China has chosen to punish, and the fish caught off both coasts are being tested by everyone.
