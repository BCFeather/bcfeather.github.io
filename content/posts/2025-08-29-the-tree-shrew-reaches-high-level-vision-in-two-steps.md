Textbook accounts of visual cortex come from primates, where simple features are extracted in the first stages and objects assemble over a hierarchy of areas, each one building on the last. Whether that arrangement is a general solution or a primate accident is hard to test, because testing it means mapping a second mammal's visual system in the same detail and with the same stimuli. The question is not academic, because a brain that reaches objects in two stages has different requirements for wiring, for the number of cortical areas and for the developmental schedule that builds them.

A paper in *Nature* on 27 August 2025 does that for the tree shrew, a small mammal taken to be among the closest living relatives of primates. Its authors implanted Neuropixels probes across cortical and thalamic areas of awake animals, recorded responses to a large battery of images and textures, and measured receptive-field size, response latency, selectivity and how well each area's activity could be decoded back into the object that produced it.

The hierarchy is present in the measurements. Receptive fields grew larger, latencies lengthened and selectivity for natural textures over matched noise rose as recording sites moved anteriorly along the pathway, the same progression that primate work describes. A system that did not have a hierarchy would show none of these trends, and a system that had one would show them in this order. The trend describes a population of neurons, not any single one, which is what makes it comparable across species at all.

The compression is where the tree shrew departs from the model. Area V2 already held a high-level representation: it encoded a complete object space, its activity supported the most accurate decoding and reconstruction of any area measured, and that decoding came close to what macaque posterior inferotemporal cortex achieves and well above what macaque V2 achieves. Face-selective cells, of the kind reported in macaque inferotemporal cortex, appeared from V2 onward. What V2 does not lack is speed, since the representation is available early enough in a response to be read out.

Two readings follow from that. One is that the computations are conserved and the stages are not, so what primates spread across several areas a smaller brain performs in fewer, which would explain how a mammal with a modest cortex manages complex visual behaviour. The other is that the primate hierarchy has been overread, and that part of what looks like staged computation is a description of where electrodes happened to sit.

Limits matter for both readings. Neuropixels give dense coverage in a few penetrations, not a complete map, the stimulus battery defines what counts as high-level, and decoding accuracy measures information present in an area, not information the animal uses. A cell that carries face information may be doing something else with it. What would settle the question is the same experiment in a third species, plus a comparison of developmental timing: whether high-level selectivity appears early in the tree shrew and late in the macaque, or at the same relative point in both.
