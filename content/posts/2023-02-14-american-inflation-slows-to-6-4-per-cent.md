Consumer prices rose 6.4 per cent in the year to January, the Labour Department said on 14 February. Economists had expected 6.2, and the monthly increase was 0.5 per cent after a revised 0.1 per cent in December. The index has fallen for seven months from the 9.1 per cent peak of June, but the pace of the decline slowed. The index is calculated from a survey of 23,000 businesses and 80,000 rental units each month.

Shelter was the largest contributor. Rent and owners' equivalent rent rose 0.7 per cent on the month and 7.9 per cent over the year, and those categories lag actual rents by about a year. Energy fell 1.9 per cent over the month and 5 per cent over the year, and gasoline was 2.4 per cent cheaper than in December. Rent rose 8.6 per cent over the year in the largest cities, and new leases were falling by the spring.

Core inflation, which excludes food and energy, was 5.6 per cent over the year and 0.4 per cent on the month. Services excluding shelter rose 0.4 per cent, and the annual rate for that category stayed above 6 per cent for the fifth month. Goods prices fell 0.5 per cent, and used cars fell 1.7 per cent, which is where the disinflation has been clearest. Apparel rose 0.2 per cent and new vehicles rose 0.2, while airline fares rose 1.4 after falling for three months. Medical care services rose 0.3 per cent, and that category had been falling through most of the previous year.

This was the first inflation report since the January employment figures, which had shown hiring far stronger than expected. Together the two releases made the case for another half-point increase at the March meeting, and futures moved to price it. Two officials had said before the release that they wanted to see a sustained decline in services inflation. The figure was published a day before the retail sales report, which also came in stronger than expected.

Food prices rose 10.1 per cent over the year, the smallest increase since February 2022. Eggs were 70 per cent more expensive than a year earlier, after an outbreak of avian influenza killed 58 million birds. Beef rose 1.9 per cent, and dairy rose 6.4 per cent, both slower than in the autumn. Egg prices had risen because of the outbreak, and they fell 40 per cent between January and May.

Forecasts for the March release pointed to a reading near 6 per cent. The terminal rate question had moved from how high to how long, and the report gave no reason to shorten the wait. Bonds sold off after the release and the two-year yield reached 4.6 per cent, its highest since November. The central bank's own survey of expectations showed households expecting 4.2 per cent inflation in a year.
