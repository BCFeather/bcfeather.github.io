A kelp forest that has been grazed down is supposed to grow back when the urchins are removed, and in many places it does not, which is the observation that a paper in Science published on 22 May set out to explain. The authors report that turf algae, the low mat of weed that colonises a bare reef, release compounds that suppress the recruitment and growth of kelp, so the new state defends itself against the old one.

Experiment is a chemistry problem with an ecological ending. The group grew turf algae in tanks, collected the water, and exposed kelp spores and young sporophytes to it, and the results showed reduced settlement and slower growth relative to controls. A waterborne effect is the kind that a diver cannot see and a model cannot ignore.

Finding matters because restoration has been sold as a matter of removing urchins. Divers cull them, the kelp returns within a season in some places, and in others the reef stays a purple carpet for a decade, and the difference had been attributed to temperature and to spores that never arrived. A chemical inhibition from the resident community is a mechanism that fits the pattern.

Identity of the compounds is the part that will take longer. The paper reports activity in fractions and not in a single molecule, and the concentrations that produce an effect in a tank are not necessarily the ones that matter in a bay with tides. A result that is robust in a mesocosm is a hypothesis in the field and an argument in a grant application.

Management implication is awkward. A restoration project that clears urchins and leaves the turf has created the conditions for the turf to keep the kelp out, and a project that scrapes the rock has to do so at a scale that a dive team cannot reach. A mechanism that operates through the water suggests that timing, flushing and the size of the treated patch all matter, which is a harder set of variables to test than a single culling rate.

Broader claim is about alternative states. An ecosystem that flips from kelp to turf and stays there is an ecosystem with a feedback loop, and loops are what make restoration expensive and what make prevention cheap. A coast that loses its kelp in a warm year may not get it back in a cool one.

What would settle the argument is a field test that removes the turf and measures recruitment with and without the chemical effect. A result that shows recovery when the compounds are absent would turn a tank experiment into a management rule. Kelp is a foundation species, which means that its loss removes habitat rather than a single crop, and a reef that loses its structure loses the fish that a fishery counts on. The paper is a reminder that a grazed reef is not an empty one, and that the community which replaces a forest has an interest in keeping it gone.
