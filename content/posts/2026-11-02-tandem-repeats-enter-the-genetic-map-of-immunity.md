Some parts of the genome are hard to read because they are repetitive. Tandem repeats, the stretches of DNA in which a short sequence is copied again and again, occupy more of the genome than all the protein-coding genes put together, and they are known to matter for gene regulation. They have also been, for most of the genomic era, too awkward to analyse at scale: short reads map badly onto long repetitive stretches, and the standard pipelines were built for unique sequence. Three papers in *Nature* on 29 October, the first major output of the TenK10K project, take them on directly, and the repeats turn out to be doing more than the textbooks allowed.

Scale is the project's precondition. The team sequenced the genomes of nearly 2,000 Australians and used single-cell methods to measure gene activity in more than 5 million of their immune cells, then traced tens of millions of genetic variants back to the cell types in which they act. Against that background, three papers each take a different layer of regulation. The first maps how common and rare variants influence gene activity in individual immune cell types, across 21,000 genes, and finds over 180,000 effects; about a quarter of the affected genes had not been linked to a genetic effect on their activity in the largest previous whole-blood study. The third maps chromatin accessibility, identifying more than 240,000 variants and linking over 28,000 DNA regions to the genes they regulate; on its own it explained the likely role of up to 23 per cent more disease-linked regions than gene activity alone.

A second paper turns to the repeats. Using new methods for analysing whole-genome sequencing data, the team identified more than 69,000 tandem repeat variants associated with changes in gene expression. They also found over 200 genes where the length of a repeated sequence may help explain a genetic link to an immune or blood trait. The count is less interesting than what it makes possible. A class of variation that had been effectively invisible in association studies now has quantified effects on expression, in specific cell types, and can be connected to disease.

The disease link is where the project earns its claim to be useful. The team compared their regulatory maps with genetic studies of 16 conditions, including cancers, immune disorders and neurological diseases, and identified 893 genes tied to diseases or blood traits whose activity had not previously been connected to genetic variation. Many of those effects appeared only in particular cell types, which is a plausible explanation for why earlier whole-blood studies missed them: pooling every cell type averages away the signal. One example the team gives is a variant that raises the expression of a gene called *OSM* in natural killer cells and increases the risk of inflammatory bowel disease, which points at a specific immune pathway as a potential target.

Usual atlas caveats apply. The cohort is Australian, nearly 2,000 people, and the immune cell types measured are those the technology can isolate well; the next phase aims to expand both the number of participants and their genetic diversity. What the papers establish is a resource and a method, not a therapy. But resources of this kind compound: the repeats have been sitting in the genome, unread, for as long as anyone has been sequencing it, and the cost of leaving them unread has just gone down.
