Two years of fieldwork will put numbers on the snow. The China Meteorological Administration set the programme out at a meeting in Hulunbuir on 17 November and described its scope this week: Inner Mongolia, Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang and Xinjiang, the five regions where ice and snow resources and the industry built on them are most concentrated. The survey is meant to close a gap between what a winter looks like and what a resort can plan for, and it is national in method while provincial in execution.

What gets measured is specific: snowfall, snow depth, the number of days with cover, and the thickness of ice, recorded across space and season to build a shareable dataset, zoning maps, a technical guideline and analytical reports. Tourism, forestry, development, sports and industry data are to be brought into the same frame, so that a snow measurement sits beside the visitor numbers and the investment plan it affects. One dataset serving several users is the point: a resort operator, a grid company and a county planner should not need three surveys.

Industry explains the timing. The snow and ice economy has passed a trillion yuan in scale, and snow tourism has moved from a specialist pursuit to mass consumption, which puts pressure on a forecasting service that once served resorts, road crews and sports events. Zoning maps are meant to guide where pistes are cut, how long a season is planned, and where cold-weather testing grounds can sit. Winter testing has grown into a business of its own, with vehicle makers running cold trials in the northeast and in Inner Mongolia.

Part of the answer already exists as a climatology of the snow season. Theoretical snow season, the period in which temperature allows snow to fall or lie, runs from 0 to 5 days across most of the south, to 5 to 25 days on the Huang-Huai plain, about 50 days in northern north China, and more than 100 days in the northeast, northern Xinjiang and the central Tibetan plateau, reaching 150 to 165 days at the best sites. Those bands are averages of long station records, not a forecast for a coming season.

Days that permit skiing are fewer than that. Surface snow near minus 5 degrees Celsius with wind below force 2 to 3 is the working range, and falling snow reduces visibility enough to close runs, so the usable calendar is shorter than the climatology suggests and varies within a single mountain. A resort deciding when to open, to make snow or to staff a lift works from that calendar, not the annual average. Snowmaking widens the range further: a resort can open before natural cover arrives, at a cost in water and power that turns on wet-bulb temperature and wind.

Jilin ran a provincial version first and reports long-series datasets with indices for skiing, snowmaking conditions and rime, which is the template the national programme is scaling. Zoning maps describe a climate and not a hillside, and the density of stations across the five regions sets how fine the first sheets can be. A guideline is due inside the two years, and the maps follow it. They will be published, and read against the seasons they were meant to predict.
