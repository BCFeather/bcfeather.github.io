Permafrost research has a measurement problem that mirrors its subject: the ground moves seasonally by tens of centimetres and permanently by centimetres, and separating the two requires a time series rather than a snapshot. A study published on 12 May in *The Cryosphere* uses ten years of interferometric synthetic aperture radar to do exactly that across a 40,000 square kilometre basin in central Siberia.

The technique exploits phase differences between radar passes. Ground that subsides between acquisitions changes the path length, and the interferogram records it as a fringe pattern. Stacking hundreds of passes and solving for a linear trend, after removing the seasonal term with a thermal model, yields a rate of permanent displacement per pixel.

Results place subsidence between 0.4 and 1.8 centimetres a year, with a median of 0.9. The distribution is not uniform: peat plateaus lost height fastest, mineral soils with thin active layers slowest, and areas underlain by ice-rich yedoma fastest of all where the protective peat had burned.

Attribution to thaw is careful. The authors compare the radar trend with borehole temperature records at eleven sites, and find that subsidence correlates with the deepening of the active layer rather than with the warming of the air. Ground that loses ice-rich material collapses regardless of the summer's air temperature, which is why a single hot year does not produce a step change in the trend.

Carbon consequences follow from the hydrology. A collapsing peat plateau converts from a dry, aerobic surface to a wet, anaerobic one, and the direction of the carbon flux reverses. The paper estimates that the basin's net carbon uptake fell by roughly a fifth over the decade, though it flags the estimate as model-dependent and the uncertainty as wide.

Radar limits deserve mention. Vegetation decorrelation reduces coherence in shrubby areas, which is exactly where the ground is changing fastest, and the method cannot see subsidence under a stable snowpack, so the winter half of the record is reconstructed rather than measured. Pixel resolution of 12 metres also mixes peat plateau and hollow within a single cell.

For infrastructure, the number matters more than the mechanism. Pipelines, roads and foundation piles in the basin are designed against a stated thaw depth, and a rate of one centimetre a year is within design tolerance while a rate of two is not. The value of a decadal trend is that it converts an assumption into a measurement, and design codes can be revised against it.

Comparison with Alaskan and Canadian records is the useful check. Similar InSAR studies over the North Slope report rates of 0.3 to 1.5 centimetres a year, with the same correlation to ice content and the same concentration in ice-rich peat. That two independent analyses, using different satellites and different thermal correction models, land in the same range is the strongest evidence in the paper, and it is stated in a single sentence rather than emphasized.

Model uncertainty runs the other way. Converting subsidence into carbon flux requires a model that couples ground ice loss to hydrology and microbial activity, and the paper's estimate of a fifth of net uptake depends on parameters that have not been measured in this basin. A reader should take the trend as measured and the carbon number as modelled, which is the distinction the authors draw but the abstract blurs.

Wider reading is the caution this literature keeps repeating. Thaw is not uniform, it is not linear, and it is not a single number for a region. The paper's own spread of 0.4 to 1.8 centimetres within one basin is the most useful figure in it.
