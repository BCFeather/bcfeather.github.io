title: "Ketamine relieves suicidal ideation within three days, and then the effect fades"
date: "2022-02-03T10:10:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "In a French trial of 156 patients admitted with suicidal ideation, 63.0 per cent given ketamine were in full remission at day 3 against 31.6 per cent given saline, but the advantage was gone by day 6."
---

Suicidal ideation in a hospitalized patient is treated with observation, medication and time, and the medication takes weeks to work. A drug that reduces the urgency within days would change what a ward can offer, and ketamine's effect on depression raised the possibility.

A French trial tested that possibility directly, randomizing 156 adults admitted voluntarily with current suicidal ideation to two 40-minute infusions of ketamine at 0.5 milligrams per kilogram or saline, at baseline and 24 hours, on top of usual treatment. Patients with schizophrenia, psychotic disorders or substance dependence were excluded. The trial ran across seven teaching hospitals and appeared in *The BMJ*, 2 February 2022. The trial was double blind and stratified by centre and diagnosis, which matters because bipolar and depressive suicidality respond differently to the same drug.

At day 3, full remission of suicidal ideation, defined as a total score of 3 or less on the ideation scale, was reached by 46 of 83 patients given ketamine, or 63.0 per cent, and by 25 of 73 given placebo, or 31.6 per cent, an odds ratio of 3.7 with an interval from 1.9 to 7.3. The separation was present within 24 hours of the first infusion. Ideation is a self-reported outcome, and an infusion that produces a noticeable sensation may unblind a patient even when the syringe is identical.

The advantage did not last. At day 6 the difference had narrowed to a level the authors describe as no longer significant, with remission around 56.3 per cent in the ketamine group and an odds ratio of 0.8 against placebo. By six weeks the two groups were indistinguishable. The authors report the day 6 comparison as non-significant rather than as absent, which is the honest description of a small trial at a single time point.

A single pair of infusions in the acute phase was the design, and that design answers the question the ward asks rather than the question the clinic asks. Maintenance treatment with repeated doses is a separate intervention, and the trial was not built to test whether it prevents relapse. A maintenance strategy would need to solve the problem of repeated dosing in outpatients, including the abuse potential and the blood pressure rise that accompanies each infusion.

Ketamine's psychotomimetic effects were not a major problem in this population because patients with psychosis were excluded, but the exclusion also means the result cannot be generalized to the patients who are hardest to manage. Stratification by diagnosis, into bipolar, depressive and other groups, was prespecified and the benefit appeared across them. The population was admitted voluntarily, which excludes the patients detained under compulsion and the ones who leave before assessment.

The result supports ketamine as a bridge and not as a treatment. It buys time for the antidepressant to work and for the social circumstances to be addressed, and a service that offers it without those would be using a fast drug to postpone a slow problem. A ward that adopts the protocol needs a monitored infusion space and staff trained to sit with a patient through the dissociative phase, and those costs are real even though the drug is cheap.
