Two members wanted to keep it unchanged, and one of them had voted for a hold at the previous meeting as well. The committee met on 23 March and voted seven to two to raise the rate to 4.25 per cent. The increase was the eleventh since December 2021 and took the rate to its highest since 2008. The committee publishes the vote and the minutes together, unlike some other central banks.

Inflation was still high and the labour market remained tight, the statement said. It also said the full effect of the previous increases had not yet reached households, because many mortgages were fixed at lower rates. The bank estimated that a third of mortgage holders would see their payments rise by the end of the year. The estimate was based on a survey of 5,000 households, and it had been revised down from 4 million.

Forecasts in the statement were raised for the near term and lowered for the following year. The bank said it expected inflation to fall below 4 per cent by the end of 2023, which is faster than it had projected in February. The forecast assumed energy prices stayed near the level of the previous month, which they did. The forecast for growth was raised to 0.1 per cent for the year, from a contraction of 0.1.

Two members voted for a half point, and two for no change, which left the majority in the middle. The vote was read as a sign that the committee was close to the end of the cycle. The governor said the bank would respond if the data required it, and that it was not pre-committing to a pause. The governor had voted with the majority in every meeting since he took the role in 2020.

Market rates fell after the decision, and the two-year gilt yield dropped 0.15 points. The pound fell 0.3 per cent against the dollar, and it had risen after the inflation figures the day before. Traders priced the peak at 4.6 per cent by the summer, down from 5 per cent earlier in the month. The market's peak expectation had been 5.25 per cent in the autumn, before the banking stress.

Mortgage rates had fallen from the peak of 6.5 per cent in the autumn to about 5 per cent. The bank's balance sheet was shrinking, because it had stopped reinvesting the proceeds of maturing bonds in the autumn. The programme was running at about 6 billion pounds a month, and the bank said the pace would continue. The bank had bought 875 billion pounds of bonds between 2009 and 2021. May brought another quarter-point increase. The pause came in September, at 5.25 per cent, and the first cut in August 2024. The bank's own review of its forecasting, published in the autumn, said it had been too slow to see the persistence of inflation in 2022.
