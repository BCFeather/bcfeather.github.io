Brightening a cloud is an old idea with a new urgency. Salt particles sprayed from the sea surface would give a low cloud more condensation nuclei, and a cloud with more, smaller droplets reflects more sunlight for the same amount of water. Over a reef, reflected sunlight is cooling, and cooling during a marine heatwave is the difference between bleached coral and coral that keeps its colour. The idea has a physics literature, a modelling literature and, until this April, no outdoor test.

A team working from a research vessel in the Great Barrier Reef reported one in *Science* on 4 April 2025. The apparatus was a seawater atomizer mounted on the ship, which turned filtered sea water into a plume of salt particles at the surface. Aircraft flying above the vessel sampled the air beneath low trade-wind cumulus, and the paper's question was narrow and testable: does the plume reach the height where clouds form, and at what concentration.

It did reach. Measurements from two different sampling strategies detected the plume just below cloud bases at 700 to 900 metres, carried there by the same convective mixing that builds the clouds. For an estimated surface production rate of \(4\times10^{14}\) particles per second, peak concentrations under the cloud base ran to about \(1\times10^{3}\) per cubic centimetre. Those are the first field numbers of their kind, and they come from a system small enough to fit on a ship's deck.

The design is what makes the numbers readable. Salt particles made from sea water are the same material the ocean already supplies, so the experiment did not add a chemical to the air; it concentrated one. Aircraft sampled downwind at two distances, which separates a plume that survives transport from a plume that decays in the first hundred metres. Instrumentation counted particles by size, so the size distribution, which governs whether a droplet forms at all, could be compared with the modelled one.

The limits are arithmetic. The production rate is orders of magnitude below what any regional deployment would need, and the paper reports aerosol concentration, not cloud droplet number, cloud albedo or surface temperature. No cooling was measured, because the experiment was not built to measure it. Salt particles also carry iodine and organic matter from the surface microlayer, and what those do to cloud chemistry at scale is a question the trial did not address, and neither did it test the plume in the strong wind shear that a real operation would face.

Modelling work for the same reef gives the scale of the gap. A regional simulation of the 2022 bleaching event found that emitting 1,570 tonnes of sea spray per day, with particles on the small end of the plausible range, raised summer low-cloud albedo by 11 per cent and cut surface shortwave radiation by 3.5 per cent. That is a number from a model, not from a cloud, but it is the number any future trial has to be judged against.

A next experiment would therefore be a measurement of effect and not of transport. A campaign that counts droplets inside the plume, tracks albedo from a satellite pass and holds the plume over one reef patch for a season would answer the question the physics actually asks. Governance arrives before that answer does: spraying salt over a marine park is a regulated activity, and the permits, the monitoring and the public argument will decide whether the second trial happens at all.
