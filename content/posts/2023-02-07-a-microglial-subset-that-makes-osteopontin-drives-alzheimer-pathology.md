Microglia are the immune cells of the brain, and in Alzheimer's disease they accumulate around plaques and change their state. Activation is too coarse a word for a population that contains several distinct phenotypes.

In *PNAS* on 7 February 2023, one of those phenotypes is resolved. The authors identify a microglial subset marked by CD11c that produces osteopontin, a secreted protein with roles in inflammation and tissue remodelling, and they show that the subset's abundance tracks with pathology.

Osteopontin is the interesting molecule. An intrinsically disordered protein has no fixed structure and binds through short motifs, and it engages integrins on other cells, so a protein that signals through adhesion changes what a cell does rather than what it makes.

Because CD11c marks a microglial state that appears in ageing and in disease, and a state that produces a secreted mediator is one that acts on its neighbours, the population matters as much as the molecule. The marker being an integrin subunit also makes the cells sortable by flow cytometry and by imaging, and a sortable population can be profiled apart from the bulk tissue, which is how the transcriptomic signature was defined.

Around plaques the subset accumulates, since amyloid deposits are a surface for microglial clustering and a cluster is a place where secreted mediators reach high local concentration, which may make the pathology a property of the cluster rather than of any single cell. An autocrine loop completes the picture, because osteopontin's receptors are integrins and the subset's own integrin expression suggests a cell that secretes a ligand for a receptor it displays, a state that sustains itself.

Mechanism is where the tests point. If osteopontin drives the recruitment or activation of other cells, removing it should reduce the pathology, and the loss-of-function experiments support the claim in the model systems used. Therapy and diagnosis follow, with an antibody against the protein or a blocker of its integrin receptor as a way to interrupt the circuit, though osteopontin also acts in bone and in immunity, and a subset that appears in disease and secretes a measurable protein is a candidate biomarker for cerebrospinal fluid. What the study cannot establish is causality in people, because mouse models reproduce plaques rather than the full human syndrome, and the human data stay correlational. The commentary's broader point stands: a disordered protein at the interface of immunity and neurodegeneration is the kind of lever the field has been looking for.
