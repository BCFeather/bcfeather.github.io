Santorini and Kolumbo are seven kilometres apart in the Aegean, and both are capable of explosive eruptions. Volcanologists have treated them as neighbouring systems with separate plumbing, partly because their historic eruptions differ in style and partly because the deep structure between them is offshore and poorly imaged. The two centres have separate catalogues and separate monitoring networks, and the water between them is 500 metres deep. Their historic eruptions differ as well: the Minoan eruption of Santorini was caldera-forming, and the 1650 eruption of Kolumbo was smaller.

The 2025 volcano-tectonic crisis changed the picture. Gradual inflation in the Santorini caldera began in the middle of 2024, and in January 2025 a magma-filled dike intruded, with seismicity concentrated between the two centres. The authors integrate onshore and marine seismology with ground and satellite geodesy to reconstruct the migration before and during the crisis. The seismicity migrated from the caldera towards Kolumbo over weeks, which is the signature of a dike instead of that of fluid movement.

Joint inversion of the deformation data gives the dimensions. Approximately 0.31 cubic kilometres of magma entered the dike, the dike is around 13 kilometres long, it reactivated principal regional faults, and it stopped 3 to 5 kilometres below the seafloor. The source sits in a mid-crustal reservoir beneath Kolumbo, which is what connects the two volcanoes. Volume follows from the deformation, and 0.31 cubic kilometres is small for an intrusion of that length, which implies a thin sheet. A thin sheet is consistent with the seismic signature, which shows a planar swarm instead of a volume.

The arrest depth is diagnostic. A dike stops where buoyancy no longer overcomes the surrounding stress, and the depth the inversion returns is consistent with a reservoir that had been filling before the intrusion. The inflation that started in 2024 is the same reservoir expressing itself at the surface. A dike that stalls at that depth releases its magma slowly, and the surface records the release as continuing inflation instead of as an eruption.

Coupling has consequences for hazard assessment. An eruption at one centre can now be read as a signal about the other, and monitoring networks that treat the two as independent would miss the connection. The crisis did not end in an eruption, which is itself informative about how much magma a dike of this size can carry without reaching the surface. Emergency plans written for one centre would not have covered the other, which is the practical gap the paper identifies.

Deformation inversions are non-unique, and the offshore seismometer coverage is thinner than the onshore network. The volume and the dike length carry uncertainties that the paper reports but that a reader should keep in view. The coupling, however, does not depend on the exact numbers: two volcanoes drawing on one reservoir is a structural statement that a single crisis can support. A second epoch of marine seismology would reduce the depth uncertainty, and continuous geodesy already narrows the volume.
