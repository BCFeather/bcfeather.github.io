A weekly injection of survodutide lowered glycated haemoglobin and reduced weight in adults who have both obesity and type 2 diabetes, according to a trial published in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 1 October. The study, called SYNCHRONIZE-2 and funded by Boehringer Ingelheim, tested the drug at 3.6 and 6.0 milligrams against placebo, and both doses cleared the trial's bar for weight loss.

Obesity drugs have had a strange year. The incretin drugs that began as diabetes treatments turned into weight-loss treatments, and the field then discovered that the two conditions are not one problem with two symptoms. A drug that moves weight hard can still leave glucose control mediocre, and a drug that fixes glucose can leave weight untouched. Trials in people with both conditions are the ones that decide how the two effects trade off.

Glycated haemoglobin fell by 0.9 percentage points at 3.6 milligrams and 0.8 at 6.0, against 0.2 on placebo, from a baseline of 7.4 per cent. The doubling of the dose did not double the effect, which is a pattern that has repeated across this drug class and suggests that the receptor is close to saturated at the lower dose. Weight loss was significantly greater than placebo at both doses, the paper reports, without separating the two in its headline numbers.

Side effects were gastrointestinal and, in the trial's account, mostly mild to moderate and transient. They were also common: 72.8 per cent of participants at 3.6 milligrams and 77.7 per cent at 6.0 reported them, against 38.6 per cent on placebo. Nausea and vomiting at that frequency are the reason these drugs are started at a low dose and escalated slowly, and the reason a trial that reports efficacy without reporting the discontinuation rate is only half a result.

Survodutide is a dual agonist, acting on the glucagon receptor as well as the GLP-1 receptor, which is the mechanistic difference from the drugs already on the market. The glucagon arm is meant to spend energy in the liver as well as to slow the stomach, and the appeal is that weight loss might be achieved at a lower GLP-1 exposure, with less nausea. The trial supports the idea that the combination works; it does not yet show that the side-effect profile is better than a pure GLP-1 drug at matched weight loss, because there was no such comparator.

What comes next is the comparing. Two years of treatment, cardiovascular outcomes, the fraction of participants who stop because of vomiting, and the weight regained after withdrawal are all outside this 48-week report. The glycated haemoglobin result is real and the weight result is real, but the drug will be judged on whether people can stay on it, and on whether the glucagon arm earns its place in a market that already has effective options.
