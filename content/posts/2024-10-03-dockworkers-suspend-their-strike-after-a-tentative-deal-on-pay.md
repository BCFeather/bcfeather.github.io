Dockworkers at ports along the east coast and the Gulf of Mexico ended their strike on the evening of the third of October, three days after it began, after the International Longshoremen's Association and the United States Maritime Alliance reached a tentative agreement on wages. Deal provides a rise of about 62 per cent over six years, taking average pay from roughly thirty-nine dollars an hour to sixty-three, and the two sides extended the contract that expired at the end of September to the middle of January so that talks on other issues can continue.

Strike had started at midnight on the first of October at thirty-six ports, from Maine to Texas, and it was the first walkout of its kind on the east coast since 1977. About forty-five thousand workers were involved, and the immediate effect was visible in container queues outside the terminals and in the diversion of ships that had been due to arrive during the week. Retailers had front-loaded imports in anticipation, which limited the disruption, and the administration of President Joe Biden leaned on both sides publicly to reach a settlement.

Terms of the tentative agreement were reported by people familiar with the talks rather than announced in full. Employers had earlier offered an increase of about fifty per cent over the life of the contract, and the final figure was higher than the one that had been on the table when the strike began. Union said the agreement on wages was a first step and that the remaining questions, including the use of automated equipment at the terminals, would be settled in the extended negotiation period.

Automation is the issue that makes the settlement provisional in a way that a wage deal alone would not. Operators have been investing in cranes and vehicles that move containers without drivers, and the union has argued that the equipment removes jobs from communities that have depended on the ports for generations. Language in the old contract limited the introduction of such machinery, and both sides have said they will return to it in January, which means the dispute can restart in the middle of the winter.

Economic effect of the three-day stoppage was smaller than the warnings that had preceded it. Analysts estimated the disruption in terms of delayed shipments rather than lost output, and the backlog was projected to clear within a couple of weeks, since the ports were operating again before congestion became self-sustaining. Cost of the new contract to the operators is larger and permanent: the wage bill at the terminals will rise by about a fifth over the life of the deal, and that will be passed on in handling charges that shippers pay.

Wider significance lies in what the episode says about the state of American labour. Two years of contracts in ports, railways, airlines and car plants have produced large increases and, in several cases, strikes, and the pattern has been driven by a labour market in which workers have more leverage than at any point since the 1990s. Settlement of the third of October adds the ports to that list. Question left open is whether the automation clause, deferred to January, will produce a second strike, or a contract that buys peace by slowing machines down.
