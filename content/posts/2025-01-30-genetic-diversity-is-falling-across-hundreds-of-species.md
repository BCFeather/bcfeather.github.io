description: "A synthesis of three decades of population genetics in *Nature* reports declining genetic diversity within more than 600 species and populations, a loss that threatens the capacity of those species to adapt as habitats change."
---

Genetic diversity is the raw material of adaptation. A population with many variants can respond to a new disease, a warmer season or a changed food supply; a population with few variants responds by going extinct. Conservation has measured species and habitats for half a century, and it has measured genes for three decades through markers that a laboratory can score, and the two measures have not been combined at scale until now, which leaves conservation with a species count and a genetic signal that rarely appear in the same document.

A paper in *Nature* on 30 January assembled published and archived datasets that tracked genetic variation within populations over time, using markers that range from microsatellites to whole genomes. The authors screened more than 600 species and populations across taxa and regions, and they compared the diversity at the start of each record with the diversity at its end.

Result is a decline. In a large share of the records, heterozygosity and allelic richness fell, which means that the populations lost variants rather than rearranging them. The declines were associated with the pressures that conservationists already name: habitat loss, fragmentation, overexploitation and the introduction of species that compete or hybridize.

Two mechanisms produce the pattern, and they work at different speeds. A population that shrinks loses variants through the randomness of reproduction, which is a slow process that shows up over generations and that a decade of monitoring can just barely detect. A population that is split into fragments loses variants faster, because each fragment drifts on its own and the whole loses the variation that migration used to maintain.

Time series is the strong part of the design. A comparison between a species in a healthy landscape and a species in a degraded one can be confounded by everything else that differs between them; a comparison of the same population before and after a period of pressure removes the confounders that ecology cannot control. The authors use that structure to argue that the declines are directional.

The limitation is the archive. Published records are biased towards species that somebody chose to study, which means mammals and birds with monitoring programmes and not the invertebrates and plants that carry most of the diversity. The 600 species are a sample of the literature, and a sample of the literature is not a sample of the biosphere, which is the gap that a global monitoring programme would have to close.

Policy use is in the indicators. The Convention on Biological Diversity adopted a set of genetic diversity indicators in 2024, and the paper supplies the evidence that the indicators are measuring something that is actually moving. A convention that asks countries to report on genetic diversity needs a baseline and a trend, and the trend is now documented across enough taxa that a country can compare its own record with it.
