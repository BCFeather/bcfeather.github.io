description: "A phase 3 trial in *The Lancet Oncology* finds that thermal ablation of small colorectal liver metastases is non-inferior to surgical resection on survival, which offers a less invasive option to patients who cannot take a major operation."
---

Colorectal cancer spreads to the liver in about half of the patients who develop metastatic disease, and the liver is the organ where a metastasis can sometimes be removed for cure. Surgery is the standard, and the criteria are strict: a limited number of deposits, a size that a surgeon can take with a clean margin, and a patient who can survive the operation and the recovery.

Thermal ablation is the alternative. A needle is placed into the deposit under imaging guidance, and radiofrequency or microwave energy heats the tissue until the tumour is destroyed. The technique has been used for two decades in patients who were not surgical candidates, and the question has been whether it can be offered to patients who are.

The COLLISION trial, reported in *The Lancet Oncology* on 20 January, randomized patients with small colorectal liver metastases to thermal ablation or to surgical resection and followed them for survival. The design was a phase 3 non-inferiority trial, which means the investigators set a margin in advance and tested whether ablation was worse than surgery by more than that margin.

The result was non-inferiority. Survival in the ablation group was not statistically worse than in the resection group, and the secondary outcomes, which include complication rates and the length of the hospital stay, favoured the less invasive arm. The trial enrolled patients whose metastases met the size criteria that both treatments can address, which is the population in which the question is live.

Selection is the part that a reader has to hold on to. The patients were chosen because their disease was amenable to either approach, and the deposits that ablation handles well are the ones that are small, few and accessible. A metastasis next to a major bile duct or at the dome of the liver is a different problem, and the trial does not speak for it.

Local control is the outcome that distinguishes the two techniques. Surgery removes the deposit with a margin that a pathologist can measure, and ablation destroys it in place with a zone that imaging can estimate. The rates of local recurrence at the ablation site were higher in some series, and the trial's follow-up is the evidence that a higher local recurrence rate does not translate into worse survival when the recurrence can be treated again.

What the trial changes is the default. A patient with a small, accessible metastasis who faces a liver resection with a week in hospital and a month of recovery now has an option that costs a day and a needle, and the conversation in a multidisciplinary meeting shifts from whether ablation is acceptable to which patients should still have the operation, which is a question of anatomy, of how much liver has to remain after the treatment and of what the patient can tolerate at the time the metastasis is found.
