description: "259 days of KATRIN data on the tritium beta-decay spectrum challenge the Neutrino-4 claim of a sterile neutrino and constrain the mass range in which such a particle could still exist, as reported in *Nature*."
---

Neutrinos have three known flavours, and a fourth that interacts only through gravity would be invisible to every detector built so far except through one signature: it would mix with the known states and shift their masses. Several anomalies in neutrino experiments have been read as evidence for such a sterile state, and the claims have been difficult to confirm or exclude. The KATRIN experiment attacks the question from the other end. It measures the energy spectrum of electrons emitted when tritium decays, and the shape of that spectrum near its endpoint encodes the mass of the lightest neutrino. A sterile neutrino with a mass of a few electronvolts would add a small distortion, a kink in the spectrum at a specific energy.

The data reported in *Nature*, 3 December 2025 come from 259 days of measurement and 36 million electrons, analysed in the last 40 electronvolts below the endpoint. That window is where a distortion from a sterile state of the kind claimed would appear, and the analysis finds no signal consistent with it.

Exclusion is the result. A claim by the Neutrino-4 experiment of a sterile neutrino at a specific mass is challenged by data that cover the same range with greater sensitivity, and the range of masses and mixing angles in which the particle could exist narrows accordingly.

The methodology is as important as the number. KATRIN's systematic uncertainties are dominated by the understanding of the tritium source and the response of the spectrometer, and the paper's treatment of those effects is what makes a null result credible. A kink search that is limited by systematics can miss a signal, and the reported limits state how much could be missed.

What the experiment cannot reach is the higher-mass region. A sterile state of a few hundred electronvolts, the kind invoked by some cosmological models, lies above KATRIN's sensitivity, and the anomalies that motivate it are not addressed by this analysis. The sterile neutrino is a family of hypotheses, and each experiment closes a window rather than the question.

Cosmology supplies the other constraints. A sterile neutrino that was present in the early universe would affect the expansion rate and the formation of structure, and the cosmic microwave background already excludes some of the parameter space. Combining the laboratory and cosmological limits is where the field's confidence comes from.

KATRIN's next step is statistics and systematics rather than a new principle. More days of data and a better model of the source would tighten the limit, and a definitive exclusion of the few-electronvolt range would remove one of the standing anomalies in particle physics. The comparison with the anomalous experiments is the part the field will argue over. Each of them reported a signal at a significance that was marginal, and marginal signals are what a null result from a more sensitive experiment is supposed to test. KATRIN's answer is that in its window, the distortion is not there.
