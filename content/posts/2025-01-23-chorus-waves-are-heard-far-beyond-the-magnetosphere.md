Chorus waves are bursts of electromagnetic radiation that last a fraction of a second, rise in frequency and sound like birds when converted to audio, which is how they got the name when they were first heard on radio receivers in the 1960s. They form along the magnetic field lines that thread the region around Earth, and they accelerate electrons in the radiation belts, which is why the space weather community cares about them.

Standard accounts have the waves generated by electrons that are injected into the inner magnetosphere during a substorm and that develop an unstable distribution as they bounce between the magnetic mirror points. The instability grows, the waves amplify and the electrons are scattered, and the whole cycle takes place within a few Earth radii of the equator, where the magnetic field is strong and the plasma is dense enough to support the interaction.

A paper in *Nature* on 23 January reports chorus waves at a distance of more than 100,000 kilometres, which is well outside that region and in a part of the magnetotail where the field is stretched and the plasma is thin. The detection came from a spacecraft that was passing through the region during a period of geomagnetic activity, and the waveform shows the rising tone and the discrete elements that identify the chorus mode.

That location matters because the instability that produces chorus depends on the ratio of the electron density to the magnetic field strength, and the ratio in the distant tail is different by orders of magnitude from the ratio near the equator. A mechanism that works in both places is a mechanism that does not depend on the specific parameters of the inner magnetosphere, which is what the existing models assume.

Authors of the paper propose a nonlinear wave-particle interaction in which the electrons are trapped in the wave potential and pushed in energy as they move along the field line. The nonlinear treatment has been applied to chorus before, but mostly to explain the fine structure of the elements, and the paper extends it to the generation itself at a distance where the linear growth rate would be too small to produce the observed amplitude.

What follows for space weather is a wider source region. The radiation belts are populated by electrons that are accelerated somewhere, and if chorus waves are generated in the tail as well as near the equator, the acceleration happens along a longer stretch of the field line and the resulting electron distribution is different from the one the current models inject.

Measurement also bears on other planets. Chorus waves have been detected at Jupiter and Saturn, where the magnetic fields and plasma densities are unlike those at Earth, and a generation mechanism that survives a wide range of ratios is a mechanism that can be applied to those systems. The Earth result is the calibration, and the outer planets are where the model will be tested next.
