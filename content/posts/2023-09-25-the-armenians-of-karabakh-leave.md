The cars began forming at the road out of Stepanakert within hours of the surrender on 20 September, and by the following weekend the line stretched for kilometres. Families loaded what fitted into the boot, burned what did not, and drove north towards the border, where Armenian officials registered them at reception points in Goris and in villages along the road. Some arrived with livestock and with the contents of a single room, and some arrived with nothing but documents. Within a week about a hundred thousand people had crossed, which is most of the population that had lived there since the war in the 1990s, and the flow continued through the end of the month.

What they left behind is emptying. Stepanakert's shops are closed, the university has suspended classes, and the city's remaining residents include the old and the sick. The region's president signed a decree dissolving its institutions by 1 January 2024, and the armed units that had fought in September have handed over weapons under the terms of the ceasefire. Azerbaijan's president has said the Armenians who stay will be treated as citizens and that his country has no plans to expel them.

Aid arrived late, in small quantities and under close supervision. The International Committee of the Red Cross brought medical supplies and fuel, and the United Nations sent its first convoy on the fourth day, after the road had been closed for nine months. Hospitals in Armenia reported that many of the arrivals were malnourished, and that the children had missed years of vaccination. The Armenian government said it could house the displaced in hotels and in public buildings for a few months, and that it would need international help after that, with the winter approaching and housing already scarce.

The politics of the exodus is now the politics of Armenia. Nikol Pashinyan has said his government did not send troops because it had no way to win, and his opponents have called the surrender a betrayal; protest marches have been held in Yerevan, and the country's parliament has debated a commission of inquiry. Russia's peacekeeping force, which was supposed to protect the corridor, has been criticized for standing aside, and its commander has been recalled.

What comes next is a question about recognition. Armenia has not formally recognized Nagorno-Karabakh as Azerbaijani territory, and the border between the two countries remains closed and undemarcated. Talks on a peace treaty have been under way for a year, with the European Union and the United States each offering a format, and the exodus has made the question of the enclave's status a historical matter rather than a negotiating one.

A population that had lived in the mountains for generations has left in a week, and the institutions it built have announced their own end. The people who remain are few, and the argument about their future is being conducted between two governments that did not fight, in a city where most of the neighbours have gone.
