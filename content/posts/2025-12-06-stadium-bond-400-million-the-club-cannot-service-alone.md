A stadium is a long-lived asset with a short-lived tenant, and the financing has to reconcile the two. A club's proposal for a 45,000-seat stadium has a 400-million-dollar cost, and the question before the city is whether to guarantee the bond that the club cannot.

The first constraint is the club's own cash flow. Its annual revenue is 180 million, of which matchday income at the new stadium would be 40, and its operating margin is 12 per cent. A bond at 5 per cent over 30 years needs 26 million a year, which is more than the club's entire operating profit.

Second comes the city's stake. A stadium generates business rates, transport revenue and visitor spending, and the city's consultants estimate 18 million a year in additional local revenue. That revenue accrues to the city and not to the club, which is why a guarantee is a plausible instrument.

Public ownership supplies the precedent. Two stadiums in the same league are owned by their cities and leased to their clubs, and both have required public subsidy for renovation. A reserve fund equal to a year's debt service is what a lender requires, and the club's own cash flow cannot supply it.

Risk allocation is the negotiating content. A guarantee transfers the construction cost overrun to the city and leaves the demand risk with the club, and a revenue-sharing clause can split the upside. A smaller project is the alternative. A 30,000-seat stadium costs 240 million and needs a 15-million annual service, which the club can cover. The club argues that the smaller project cannot host the events that make the larger one viable.

Tournament stadiums show where the public share usually lands. A tournament-funded stadium has a public payer and a defined legacy, and its operating cost often falls on the city afterwards. The league's stadiums built for a World Cup have averaged a 60 per cent public share. A stadium's revenue is event-day and the bond's service is annual. A club's matchday income arrives in 20 instalments a year and a bond's coupon is paid twice, and the mismatch is manageable only with a reserve or a sponsor's guarantee. Public ownership changes the arithmetic.

A city that owns the stadium can issue a general obligation bond at a lower rate and lease the venue, and it takes the event risk onto its balance sheet. The club's 400 million becomes the city's 400 million with a lower coupon and a political constraint. A bond's covenant is the lender's protection and the club's constraint. A covenant that limits a club's spending on players is a covenant that a supporter will blame for a season, and a club that accepts it has traded sporting discretion for a lower coupon.

Two parties hold a risk that neither can carry alone, and the deal is the arrangement. A club cannot service a stadium bond and a city cannot fill a stadium, and the deal is the arrangement that lets each do the part it can.
