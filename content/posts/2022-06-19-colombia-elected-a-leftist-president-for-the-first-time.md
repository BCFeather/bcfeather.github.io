Colombians elected Gustavo Petro as president on 19 June, giving him fifty point four per cent of the vote against forty-seven point three for Rodolfo Hernández, a construction magnate who had campaigned as an outsider. The margin of about seven hundred thousand votes was the narrowest in a runoff since 1994, and turnout of nearly fifty-eight per cent was the highest ever recorded in a second round. Petro becomes the first leftist to hold the office.

Biography explains why the result carries weight beyond the region. Petro was a member of the M-19 guerrilla movement, which demobilized in 1990 after a peace agreement, and he later served as a senator, as mayor of Bogotá and as a presidential candidate twice before. His running mate, Francia Márquez, is an environmental campaigner from the Pacific coast and becomes the first Afro-Colombian vice-president. Polling stations in rural districts stayed open late to accommodate the queues.

His programme is ambitious and expensive. Petro promised to raise taxes on large estates and on land held unproductively, to guarantee a basic income for the poorest households, to move the country's electricity system away from coal and gas, and to stop granting new licences for oil exploration. He also proposed reopening negotiations with the armed groups that still control parts of the countryside, and reforming the police force that was the subject of a bruising international report. The turnout record was helped by a registration campaign and by the sense that the result would be close.

Structural obstacles stand in the way. His coalition holds a minority in the Senate, the constitutional court reviews much of what a president decrees, and Colombia's fiscal rules limit the room for new spending. Oil and coal together supply about half of export earnings, and a president who stops issuing licences while promising transfers has to explain where the money comes from in the interval.

Markets reacted with a mixture of relief and caution. The peso had weakened through the campaign, and the yield on government bonds rose, because investors were pricing the cost of the programme against a fiscal deficit that had widened during the pandemic. Business groups in Bogotá said they would work with the new government, and the country's largest conglomerates began talks about the tax proposals within days.

Regional context gives the result a wider meaning. Chile had elected a leftist constitutional assembly the previous year, Brazil would hold its own election in October, and the pattern suggested that the pandemic had shifted Latin American politics towards parties that promise redistribution. Colombia's own history makes the shift more surprising, because the country spent decades fighting an insurgency of the left. What the election establishes is a mandate and a limit at the same time. Petro won with a majority that was narrow, in a country where the outgoing president's party remains strong, and the promises he made will be tested by a congress that he does not control; the first leftist president in two centuries of the republic will govern with less room than the result implies.
