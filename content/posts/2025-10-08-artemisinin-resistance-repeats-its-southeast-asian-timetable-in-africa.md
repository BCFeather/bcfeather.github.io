Artemisinin combination therapy clears malaria parasites quickly, and the partner drug finishes the job. Partial resistance to the artemisinin half shows up as slower clearance rather than as outright failure, which is why it can spread before it is noticed. Southeast Asia lost a decade to that slow drift, and the markers involved concentrate in one gene, *kelch13*, whose mutations are the best-established predictor. Surveillance data existed, but scattered across papers and databases, and no single view followed the markers across regions.

Reviewed work in *eLife* on 2 October 2025 assembles 112,933 *Plasmodium falciparum* samples collected between 1980 and 2023 into one spatiotemporal resource. The compilation covers *kelch13* point mutations and their frequencies by place and year, and it is presented as a review backed by a dataset, not as a new field study. Nothing in it depends on unpublished access. The value of the compilation is that a country can place its own sequences against a regional curve instead of against a single published survey.

The pattern it makes visible is a repeat. Partial resistance in East Africa, evident by 2024, mirrors what Southeast Asia showed 10 to 15 years earlier, with the same markers rising in the same order and against a comparable therapeutic background. The African curve has not caught up, and the lag is the useful part of the comparison: it gives a window in which surveillance can act before the marker frequencies reach the levels that preceded failure elsewhere.

Why the parallel holds is partly the drug's own pharmacology. Artemisinin kills the young ring stages, whose susceptibility depends on the parasite's protein turnover, and *kelch13* variants change that turnover and not the drug target itself. Because the mechanism is cellular and not target-based, the same variants can gain a foothold wherever the drug is used heavily, and the selection pressure does not require a matching health system. Because the protein sits in the parasite's response to damaged proteins, the variants buy tolerance rather than resistance in the pharmacological sense, and tolerance is what the clearance time measures.

Limits are substantial. The dataset is a compilation, so sampling density varies by country and by decade, and an apparent rise can reflect a new survey instead of a new mutation. Frequencies are reported per site, not per infection, and clinical outcomes are mostly absent, which means the link from marker to treatment failure is assumed from earlier studies. The 1980 start predates the drug's wide use, so early points describe a baseline, not a trend.

The comparison argues for surveillance tied to treatment records, not to sequences alone. A country that sees marker frequencies climb without a matching rise in clearance times is seeing something different from a country where both move together, and only linked data can tell them apart. Sequencing capacity is the binding constraint, and the review's contribution is to show where a small amount of it would answer the most. Where sequences are collected without treatment records the lag cannot be dated, and the comparison then loses its most useful property.
