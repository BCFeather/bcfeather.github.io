April at Chizhou No. 10 Middle School arrived with rain that lasted four days and a notice on the board that somebody had changed. The countdown had been in white chalk since the winter, and on the first Monday after the Qingming holiday it was written in red, with a number in front of it that looked smaller than the calendar said. Nobody asked who had done it. The teachers walked past it and did not erase it.

Mornings started earlier than they had in March. The first bell was at ten past seven and the corridor was full by seven, with boys standing in the stairwell copying a page of notes they had not finished the night before. The classroom smelled of wet coats and of the ink that comes off a photocopier, because the exercises arrived every morning in a stack that was still warm when it reached the back row, and the smell of it is what I remember of that spring.

Lessons had changed shape without anyone announcing it. The teachers had stopped introducing new material in most subjects and had begun to hand back papers with the marks circled and a sentence underneath. Revisions were done in class, the questions were from previous years, and the answers were read aloud by whoever had got them right. The pace was faster and the room was quieter, and the quiet was the thing that felt new.

Physical training was the part that took place outdoors. Running in the mornings had started in the autumn and continued through the spring, on a track that was gravel in places and mud after rain. The exercises were counted and recorded, the results were written on a sheet that would be sent somewhere, and the boys who could not finish were told to walk the last two laps and to come back the next day. A teacher wrote the numbers into a column and said nothing about them.

Evenings were the longest part. I went home at six, ate what my mother had left on the stove, and sat at the desk in the room with the window that faces the courtyard until half past ten. The papers were stacked in the order they would be needed and the stack got shorter and then longer again, because a new set arrived every day and the old ones were never quite finished. My father came in on Saturday nights and asked the same question, and I gave the same answer, and it was true both times.

The date was on the board and it was the fourteenth of June. Everything in April was arranged around a morning three months away that would decide which school I went to in September, and nobody in the room pretended otherwise. The rain stopped at the end of the month, the red number got smaller, and the last spring of middle school ended the way the other springs had, except that this one had been counted.
