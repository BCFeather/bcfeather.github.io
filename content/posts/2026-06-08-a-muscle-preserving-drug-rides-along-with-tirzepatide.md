Incretin-driven weight loss takes fat and muscle together, and the muscle component worries clinicians who treat older patients, because lean mass is what keeps people standing, walking and out of hospital. The fraction lost is typically a quarter to a third of total weight, which in a person with sarcopenia is a meaningful reduction in reserve. The concern is sharpest in older adults, where the loss of a kilogram of muscle can mean the difference between walking independently and needing help. Resistance training preserves some lean mass during weight loss, and a drug that does the same without exercise would be attractive to patients who do not train.

Apitegromab inhibits myostatin, a signalling molecule that restrains muscle growth. Blocking it has been developed for spinal muscular atrophy, where the aim is to add muscle, and the logic of using it during weight loss is to preserve what the incretin removes. Myostatin restrains muscle growth, so blocking it releases that brake, and the effect is an increase in lean mass rather than a change in fat.

A phase 2 trial reported in *Nature Medicine* on 8 June randomized participants losing weight on tirzepatide to apitegromab or placebo and measured lean mass alongside total weight. The design is an add-on, which is the right structure for a drug that has no effect on weight by itself. Body composition was measured by imaging rather than by bioimpedance, which matters because impedance estimates shift with hydration and with the fat loss itself.

Preserving lean mass during weight loss has a plausible functional payoff, since strength and physical function are the outcomes that determine whether an older person can live independently. The trial's functional measures are therefore as important as the body composition numbers. The trial is small, and a phase 2 in this area is designed to show a signal in composition rather than a difference in function. Handgrip strength is a convenient proxy, and the trial measured it alongside imaging.

Combination raises a regulatory question. A drug that improves the quality of weight loss rather than its quantity has no obvious endpoint that regulators accept, and body composition measured by imaging is a surrogate that has not been validated for functional benefit. A surrogate that regulators accept would let the field move faster, and none exists yet.

Cost and complexity are the practical obstacles. An antibody given by injection alongside an injected incretin is a regimen of two injectables for a condition where one already works, and the case for the second has to rest on outcomes that the first does not deliver. That case is not yet made. Regulators have no accepted endpoint for the quality of weight loss, which is why the programme will probably have to run a functional outcome trial. A functional endpoint such as gait speed or chair-rise time is the one that would carry weight with regulators and with geriatricians.
