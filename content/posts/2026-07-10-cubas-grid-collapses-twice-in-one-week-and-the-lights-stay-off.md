Cuba's national electricity system collapsed in the early afternoon of 10 July, the second nationwide blackout within a week, and the state utility said the failure had left the whole island without power. Havana's neighbourhoods went dark in stages through the afternoon, and hospitals switched to generators that had been installed after earlier outages. The government said it would restore service district by district and that nobody should expect a quick return to normal. The utility said the failure began with a trip at a large plant and cascaded as protection systems took units offline one after another.

Fuel supply is the reason the system keeps failing. Cuban plants burn imported crude and fuel oil, and deliveries have fallen as the island's suppliers have cut shipments and as banks have refused to handle payments for cargoes. Officials describe the shortfall as the result of an American oil blockade, and Washington says its measures target state companies while exempting humanitarian trade. Whatever the legal argument, the physical result is that plants run below capacity and that maintenance is deferred. Generators installed after 2022 cover only a fraction of peak demand, and they burn diesel that also has to be imported.

Restarting a collapsed grid is a different problem from keeping one running. Plants need external power to start their own auxiliaries, so the utility has to bring up a few units in sequence, match load to generation, and keep voltage inside narrow limits while doing it. Engineers call the process a black start, and on a system with Soviet-era units and missing spare parts it takes days rather than hours. Engineers also have to hold the frequency steady while factories that restart first draw their motors, which is why big users are asked to wait. That arithmetic leaves no reserve for the next trip.

Services that depend on pumps fail first. Water supply in several provinces stopped when the pumping stations lost power, and households that store water in tanks on roofs found themselves without pressure. Schools closed, food spoiled in refrigerators, and clinics rescheduled dialysis because the machines need a stable supply rather than a generator that surges.

Politics of the outage has been running for three years. The government blames the embargo and the American measures that have tightened since 2019, and the opposition blames mismanagement and the failure to maintain plants that were built in the 1980s. Both accounts contain facts, and the argument between them has become a proxy for a larger question about how the island's economy can function at all. The government has cut the hours of state offices and moved some classes online to reduce daytime demand.

Emigration has become the release valve again. Record numbers of Cubans have left in the past two years, and the remittances they send buy the fuel and the parts that the state cannot. A grid that fails twice in a week is a symptom of a system that has been running on borrowed time, and borrowed time is the one resource the island still has.
