Fighters of the Wagner group crossed from occupied Ukraine into Russia on the night of 23 June, took control of the army headquarters in Rostov-on-Don without resistance, and by the evening of 24 June had driven north to within about two hundred kilometres of Moscow. Their leader, Yevgeny Prigozhin, said he was acting against the defence ministry, whose contract he had refused to sign, and he accused the army's leadership of incompetence and of lying about the war. It was the most serious challenge to the Russian state in the twenty-three years of Vladimir Putin's rule, and it lasted less than thirty-six hours.

The Russian response was improvised and revealing. Checkpoints were thrown up on the roads south of Moscow, and the authorities dug up sections of highway. Videos showed Wagner columns driving past civilian traffic without being stopped, and the air force was ordered to attack them, reportedly with limited effect. Putin appeared on television to describe the mutiny as treason and to compare it to the civil war of a century earlier, the FSB opened a criminal case against Prigozhin, and by the evening it was clear that the regular army had neither the forces nor the appetite to fight the men it had been fighting alongside.

Alexander Lukashenko, the president of Belarus, produced the settlement, saying he had negotiated with Prigozhin and that the advance would stop in exchange for guarantees of safety. The Kremlin agreed to drop the criminal case, to allow Prigozhin to leave for Belarus, and to let Wagner fighters either join the army or return home. The terms were announced by the Kremlin's spokesman as though they were a normal arrangement, and within hours the Wagner columns turned around and drove back to their bases.

Consequences are larger than the terms. A private army that had been built with state money, fed by state rations and used for state purposes turned on the state and was stopped by a phone call rather than by force. That fact is visible to every regional governor, every officer and every country that has watched Russia's army in Ukraine, and it is the clearest demonstration yet that the authority of the centre depends on the willingness of armed men to obey it. The war in Ukraine did not change on 24 June; the Russian system did.

Prigozhin's own position is precarious and the guarantees he received are worth what the Kremlin's word is worth. He built his influence on his relationship with Putin and on the licence to operate that came with it, and that relationship is the thing his march destroyed. Whether he ends up in Belarus, in Africa where his network has operations, or in a courtroom, the episode has already established that the state can be challenged by the instrument it created. The next months will show whether the system repairs itself by absorbing the challenge or by removing the challenger.

A criminal case has been closed, and the aircrew who tried to stop the columns have not been named. The war in Ukraine continues, and the army that Prigozhin accused of incompetence is still in command of it.
