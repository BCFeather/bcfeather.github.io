A Falcon Heavy lifted off from the Kennedy Space Center on 13 October carrying a spacecraft bound for an object that most planetary scientists would like to visit and none has seen up close. The target is 16 Psyche, an asteroid about 280 kilometres across that orbits in the main belt between Mars and Jupiter, and the way it reflects light suggests a surface made largely of iron and nickel rather than rock. The launch had been delayed by a year after the software that runs the spacecraft failed its review.

The hypothesis is that Psyche is the surviving core of a protoplanet whose outer layers were stripped away by collisions early in the history of the solar system. If that reading holds, the mission would give researchers their first direct look at a planetary core, which is otherwise impossible: the Earth's core sits under 2,900 kilometres of rock and the cores of Mars and Mercury are inaccessible. Impacts also delivered metal to the early Earth, and studying a body that may have been one of those donors links the laboratory to the geological record.

Its instruments include a camera, a gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer, a magnetometer and a radio experiment that measures gravity by tracking the signal. The instruments are designed to establish what the surface is made of, whether it has a remnant magnetic field and how porous the body is, and the answers will decide whether the core hypothesis survives. A metal-rich body that formed by slow accumulation of grains would look different from one that cooled from a molten mass. The magnetometer matters because a field would show that the body once had a liquid core.

The journey is long. Psyche will fly past Mars in May 2026 for a gravity assist and arrive at the asteroid in July 2029, then spend twenty-six months in orbit at four decreasing altitudes. The mission has been in development since 2013, was cancelled and restored during the pandemic, and its total cost is close to a billion dollars, which makes it one of the more expensive of NASA's small-body missions.

For the Falcon Heavy, the launch marks a milestone, and the rocket has flown sparingly since its first flight in 2018. Psyche needed the heavy lift because the trajectory requires a large departure velocity, and the rocket's side boosters returned to land at Cape Canaveral, a manoeuvre that has become routine for the company and remains unusual for the industry.

What the mission finds will take years to interpret, and no one will be mining it in any near-term sense. Its distance, its rotation and the cost of returning material make that a thought experiment. The value of Psyche is in the comparison it allows between a metal world and the models that have been built from meteorites, and that comparison begins with a signal travelling back from the belt. The team has said it will publish the first composition and gravity results within a year of arrival.
