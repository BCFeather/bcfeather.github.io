---

A rocky planet's atmosphere is what makes it interesting, and an atmosphere is hard to see at a distance. The James Webb Space Telescope's spectroscopy has made the measurement possible for a few nearby systems.

The observation in *Nature*, 24 August 2023 targets TRAPPIST-1 c. The authors measure the planet's thermal emission during its secondary eclipse, and the spectrum constrains what the planet's atmosphere can contain.

The result is a limit. A thick carbon dioxide atmosphere would produce a spectral signature and a temperature that the observation does not show, and the authors conclude that the planet's atmosphere is thin or absent.

The measurement is the achievement. The planet's signal is a small dip in the star's light, and extracting a spectrum from that dip requires a stability that only a space telescope provides.

The spectrum's interpretation is model-dependent. A planet's emission depends on its surface and on its atmosphere, and a bare rock and a thin atmosphere can produce similar spectra.

The comparison with its neighbours is the frame. TRAPPIST-1's planets are similar in size and different in irradiation, and a comparison across them tests whether an atmosphere survives at a given distance.

The implication for habitability is negative. A planet without a thick atmosphere has no greenhouse and no protection from the star's flares, and the system's inner planets are therefore poor candidates.

The limitation is the wavelength range. A single band constrains carbon dioxide and not water, and a conclusion about the atmosphere is a conclusion about one molecule.

What the paper does not establish is the surface. A rock's albedo and its thermal inertia are unmeasured, and the two would change the interpretation of the emission.

For exoplanet science, the value is a null result with a number. A limit on an atmosphere is a measurement, and the field's models are constrained by limits as much as by detections.

The secondary eclipse is the technique. A planet passes behind its star and its light disappears, and the depth of the disappearance gives the planet's emission.

The star's activity is the noise source. A flare changes the star's brightness and mimics a signal, and the authors' monitoring of the star's variability is what keeps the measurement honest.

The irradiation is the physical driver. A planet close to a cool star receives enough energy to lose an atmosphere to escape, and the paper's estimate of the loss is part of the argument.

The system's resonance is the dynamical context. The planets are in a chain of resonances, and their formation history determines what volatile inventory each began with.

The next measurement is a longer baseline. A fuller spectrum across the infrared would distinguish a bare rock from a thin atmosphere, and the observing time is the currency.

The habitability discussion is a caution. An M dwarf's flares erode an atmosphere, and a planet that has held one is a planet that formed with a large inventory.
