---

A rocky planet's atmosphere is what makes it interesting, and an atmosphere is hard to see at a distance. Spectroscopy from the James Webb Space Telescope has made the measurement possible for a few nearby systems.

In *Nature* on 24 August 2023, TRAPPIST-1 c is the target. The authors measure the planet's thermal emission during its secondary eclipse, and the spectrum constrains what its atmosphere can contain. A limit is the result. A thick carbon dioxide atmosphere would produce a spectral signature and a temperature the observation does not show, so the planet's atmosphere is thin or absent.

Measurement is the achievement. The planet's signal is a small dip in the star's light, and extracting a spectrum from that dip needs a stability only a space telescope provides; the secondary eclipse is the technique, since the planet passes behind its star and the depth of the disappearance gives its emission.

Interpretation is model-dependent. Emission depends on surface and atmosphere, and a bare rock and a thin atmosphere can produce similar spectra. The star's activity is the noise source, because a flare changes brightness and mimics a signal, and monitoring variability is what keeps the measurement honest.

Neighbours frame the result. TRAPPIST-1's planets are similar in size and different in irradiation, and comparing them tests whether an atmosphere survives at a given distance. Irradiation is the physical driver, since a planet close to a cool star receives enough energy to lose an atmosphere to escape. Habitability takes a negative implication. A planet without a thick atmosphere has no greenhouse and no protection from flares, so the inner planets are poor candidates. Flares erode an atmosphere, and a planet that has held one must have formed with a large volatile inventory.

Wavelength range sets the limitation. One band constrains carbon dioxide and not water, so a conclusion about the atmosphere is a conclusion about one molecule. The surface itself stays unmeasured, because a rock's albedo and thermal inertia are unmeasured, and both would change the interpretation.

Exoplanet science gains a null result with a number, since a limit constrains models as much as a detection does. Resonance supplies the dynamical context, because the planets form a chain and their formation history sets each one's starting inventory, while a fuller infrared spectrum would separate a bare rock from a thin atmosphere. A second eclipse at a different phase would show whether the emission varies with the planet's rotation, and a variation would mean the surface is heterogeneous, which is information no single spectrum can carry. Whether a thinner atmosphere of another composition could hide in the same data is the loose end, because the observation constrains one molecule and an atmosphere of nitrogen would barely register in the band that was measured. A longer integration on the same target would narrow the limit rather than change it, and the next decisive step is a spectrum covering the bands where water and methane absorb.
