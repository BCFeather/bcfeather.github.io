A new American tariff schedule covering sixty trading partners took effect on 24 July, replacing the flat 10 per cent global tariff that had been in place since February. The rates run from 10 to 12.5 per cent and rest on Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, which allows retaliatory duties after an investigation. The stated reason was that the partners had failed to implement and enforce their own prohibitions on imports made with forced labour. The trade representative's office published the list of covered economies with the rate attached to each.

Reaction from the affected governments was uniform in tone and varied in remedy. The European Union's foreign policy chief, Kaja Kallas, said the claim that Europe's controls were deficient had no basis, and added that nobody could track tariffs that were imposed and withdrawn on a monthly cycle. New Zealand's prime minister called the duties deeply disappointing, Japan's chief cabinet secretary said Tokyo regretted them, and Brazil's government said it would use its law on economic reciprocity to prepare countermeasures. American business groups were quieter, and several said they would pass the cost on to customers.

Legal foundation is thinner than the rate suggests. A Section 301 action normally follows an investigation with findings about specific practices by a specific country, and this action applied one finding to sixty partners at once. Trade lawyers have noted that the World Trade Organization's dispute settlement would be the natural forum, and that the appellate body has not functioned for years, which is why the remedies announced so far are national rather than multilateral. The investigation had been opened in the spring and concluded in less than ninety days.

Economic effect will show up unevenly. The average tariff rate on American imports rises by a few points, and the burden falls on machinery, chemicals and consumer goods that American firms buy from allies rather than from China. Importers have already told suppliers that they will absorb part of the cost, and retailers have warned that prices will rise in the autumn. Customs brokers have been asking how to classify goods that contain components from several of the listed countries.

Timing explains the politics. The duties arrived four days after the new British prime minister took office, three weeks after a NATO summit at which allies were asked to spend more on defence, and in the same month as a new round of semiconductor export rules. A tariff is a bargaining chip in each of those conversations, and the administration has been explicit that it expects partners to negotiate. The administration had described the flat rate in February as temporary, and this schedule is the replacement it promised.

What happens next depends on whether the partners retaliate together. The European Union has a list prepared and has been slow to use it, Brazil has a statute that lets it respond within days, and Japan has preferred to negotiate. A coordinated response would test whether the administration's leverage survives when it is applied to sixty countries at once, which is a different question from whether it works on one. A case at the trade organization would take years, which is why the responses so far have been national.
