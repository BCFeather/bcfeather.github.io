description: "Cases in the city pass ten thousand a day for the first time, the wards run short of beds, and the government holds to a policy of zero infections."
---

Hong Kong recorded more than ten thousand new coronavirus infections on 25 February, the first time the daily figure had passed that mark, and the hospitals in the city were short of beds. The wave had begun in late December with a cluster among staff at an airport hotel, and it had spread through the densely built districts of Kowloon and the New Territories while the vaccination rate among the oldest residents remained low. The government reported 47 deaths that day, and the count rose through the following week.

The city had kept infections near zero for two years, with quarantine for every arrival, mass testing of buildings and the closure of schools and gyms. That strategy worked while the virus that arrived was the one that had circulated in 2020, and it lost its advantage when Omicron reached a population in which most people over eighty had received no vaccine or only one dose. The government's own advisers had warned in January that a wave would overwhelm the wards, and their warning was published in a document that the government did not act on for two weeks.

What followed was a scramble for capacity. The hospital authority opened beds in car parks and in a sports centre, and patients lay in corridors on trolleys that were parked in rows. The government asked Beijing for help, and teams of health workers arrived from Guangdong along with supplies of antivirals and oxygen, and a temporary hospital was built at the AsiaWorld-Expo near the airport. Queues at the vaccination centres lengthened, and the government made the first dose compulsory for staff in care homes.

Officials had spent two years telling the public that the mainland model of zero infections was the only acceptable policy, and they could not abandon it without saying so. The politics of the wave were as sharp as the medicine. The chief executive announced a mass testing programme for the whole city, then postponed it, then ran it in stages, and the messaging changed from week to week. Businesses that had survived two years of restrictions closed in the spring, and the airport that had been among the busiest in the world handled a fraction of its former traffic.

The wave peaked in early March at more than 70,000 cases a day and then fell quickly, and by the end of the month the daily count was in the low thousands. The deaths, most of them among people over seventy who had not been vaccinated, reached several thousand by the middle of March and continued to accumulate. The government's experts said the mortality rate was several times that of a typical influenza season.

Hong Kong reopened in stages during 2022. Quarantine for arrivals was cut from 21 days to 14, then to 7, then to 3, and the mask mandate lasted until March 2023. The wave of February taught the city that a policy of zero infections in a place that lives on travel and trade can hold only while the virus is containable, and that the cost of holding it after that point is paid in wards and in closed shops.
