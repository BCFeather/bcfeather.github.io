A gene drive is a piece of genetic engineering that breaks a rule of inheritance. An ordinary gene has a fifty per cent chance of passing to offspring; a drive is constructed so that the copy on one chromosome is edited onto the other, which makes the trait spread through a population faster than natural selection would. In a mosquito that carries malaria, a drive that disrupts a fertility gene could crash the population of an entire species. A drive that spreads through a population of a few thousand mosquitoes in a cage is a drive that has demonstrated the mechanism and not the ecology.

The molecular machinery is the same CRISPR system that has been in laboratories since 2012, assembled differently. A guide RNA targets a sequence that is unique to the species, the Cas protein cuts it, and the cell's own repair machinery copies the drive into the break. Resistance arises when the repair goes wrong, and the design of the drive has to account for the mutations that would make the target unrecognizable. The guide sequence is chosen to match a region that is conserved across the species, and a single mismatch can drop the efficiency by half.

Containment is a design requirement rather than a precaution. A drive that escapes a laboratory is a drive that cannot be recalled, so the strains under development are built with molecular brakes: a drive that requires a specific temperature, a drive that is split across two loci so neither alone is viable, and a drive that is programmed to decay after a number of generations. A split drive that requires two genetic elements to come together is the design most regulators prefer, because a release of one element alone is harmless.

Field trials have not happened, and the intermediate step is the large cage. Enclosures of a few cubic metres, in countries with the regulatory capacity to supervise them, allow a drive to be tested against a realistic age structure and mating behaviour without any route to the wild. Results from those cages have generally shown suppression working and resistance emerging slower than models predicted. Cages were built in Italy, in Burkina Faso and in the United States, and each of them required a permitting process that took longer than the experiment.

Governance is where the argument now sits. The Convention on Biological Diversity has a de facto moratorium on environmental release, and the countries that most need malaria control are not the countries that wrote it. A technology that could save hundreds of thousands of lives a year is being held at the laboratory stage by a process designed for risks that are easier to imagine than the disease. A country that carries most of the malaria burden has a regulator with a small staff and no experience of a genetically modified insect.

What would change the calculation is a working suppression drive with a proven containment mechanism and a monitoring plan that a national regulator can enforce. That is a package rather than a result, and the package is harder to assemble than the mosquito.
