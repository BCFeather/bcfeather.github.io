Stomach contents are rare in dinosaurs because soft tissue decays before a carcass is buried, and a specimen reported this month preserves the contents well enough to identify what the animal ate in its last hours. The assemblage is unusual because it mixes plant material with a small vertebrate's bone.

The specimen is a small ornithopod from a lake deposit, preserved in a fine-grained mudstone that excluded oxygen within hours. The body cavity's contents occupy the same volume as the rib cage and are undistorted.

Fern fronds, conifer needles and pollen grains make up the plant material. Pollen's presence is the informative part: it is released over weeks, and its presence in a stomach suggests the animal browsed flowering plants as well as ferns.

A limb bone of a small amphibian or reptile supplies the vertebrate fragment. Its presence does not establish predation, and the paper's authors argue for scavenging because the bone is broken and lacks soft tissue. A bone fragment in a herbivore's gut is evidence of accidental ingestion, and its size suggests a carcass rather than a live prey item.

The teeth tell a compatible story. The animal's dental wear is consistent with shearing fibrous plants, and the stomach's plant fragments are cut at angles that match the wear facets.

Caution comes from what a modern herbivore's stomach holds. A modern herbivore's stomach holds a diverse mix that varies by season, and one meal is one sample. The paper reports the contents as evidence of what was available in the animal's habitat rather than as a diet.

Taphonomy carries the claim. The mudstone's chemistry shows a rapid drop in oxygen and a rise in phosphate, which is the signature of a carcass that sealed before decay, and the team's experiment reproduced that chemistry in a tank.

Stomach contents survive when an animal is buried before its gut's chemistry dissolves them. A lake's fine mud excludes oxygen within centimetres of the surface, and a carcass that sinks into it is sealed in a reducing environment that preserves plant cuticles and pollen grains.

Pollen is the most informative fragment and the least direct. A grain's species identifies a plant that grew where the animal was, and a stomach's mix of fern spores and conifer pollen describes a diet that included the ground cover and the canopy's litter.

A stomach's contents describe a meal and not a diet. A herbivore's annual range covers plants that a single gut does not contain, and a specimen's last meal is a sample of one. The paper's claim is about what an animal ate on its last day, which is a narrower and more defensible statement.

Soft parts surviving is what lets a fossil speak about an afternoon rather than an anatomy. A skeleton says what an animal was; a stomach says what it did on a particular afternoon, which is a different and rarer kind of evidence.
