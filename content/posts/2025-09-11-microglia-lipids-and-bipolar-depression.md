Work published in *PNAS* on 8 September follows a gene to a cell type to a metabolic pathway, and arrives at a possible contribution to bipolar disorder. The gene is *Serinc2*, one of the many loci that genome-wide studies have linked to the condition without explaining what the link means. The cell type is the microglia, the resident immune cells of the brain, which prune connections, respond to injury and consume a surprising share of the organ's metabolic budget. The pathway is the synthesis of phospholipids, the fats that make up membranes.

The chain of evidence runs from patient tissue to cultured cells to mice. In brains from people with bipolar disorder, the authors report changes in microglial state alongside altered expression of the gene. In cell experiments, reducing *Serinc2* shifted the lipid profile of microglia and changed how they behaved towards their neighbours. In animals, manipulations that reproduced the shift produced behavioural changes resembling parts of the disorder, and blocking the lipid pathway reduced them.

None of this establishes cause in people, and the paper does not claim it does. Psychiatric genetics has spent fifteen years producing lists of loci with small individual effects and no clear route from them to symptoms, and the field's harder problem is the step after the list: which cell, which process, which direction. A result of this kind is valuable precisely because it names all three, even if the naming will be contested and replicated or not over the next several years.

The choice of microglia is itself worth noting. Immunity and psychiatry were separate literatures for most of the twentieth century, joined mainly by the observation that infections and inflammation can shift mood and cognition. That join has become a working hypothesis, with microglial state proposed as a contributor to depression, schizophrenia and neurodegenerative disease alike. The hypothesis has produced both real findings and a good deal of overreach, and the useful papers are the ones that specify a mechanism narrow enough to be wrong.

Lipids are an unusually awkward substance to study in this context. They are numerous, they turn over quickly, and standard imaging cannot resolve them in living tissue without a label; much of the evidence therefore comes from post-mortem samples and from cultures that are only approximations of a brain. The authors' contribution is to connect a genetic risk factor to a measurable metabolic flux, which is a step that can be tested independently by other groups, and that is the only kind of claim in this field that ages well, however the replication turns out.

For patients the distance is still long. A pathway identified in a mouse is several steps from a trial, and bipolar disorder is a condition with effective treatments that are old, blunt and poorly matched to individuals. What the study offers is not a new drug target tomorrow but a better map of where to look, which is what basic work in this area has always offered.
