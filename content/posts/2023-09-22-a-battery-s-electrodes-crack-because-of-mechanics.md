A battery's electrode stores ions by changing its volume, and that change cracks a particle and ends a cell's life. The mechanics of the change is a materials problem rather than a chemical one. Treated in *Science* in September 2023, it is measured on individual particles and modelled as a continuum, and the model predicts where a crack starts. Prediction is the finding. A crack begins where stress concentrates, and the concentration depends on the particle's shape and on how fast it is charged.

Rate plays the counterintuitive part. A slow charge lets a particle swell uniformly, a fast one builds a gradient, and the gradient raises the stress; the gradient's source is diffusion, because an ion moving slowly through a particle builds a concentration profile whose steepness sets the strain. Material design points to use. A particle with smaller expansion and a tougher surface resists cracking, and comparing chemistries is the guide; the swelling's origin is chemical, since an ion entering a crystal pushes its neighbours apart.

Architecture is the second lever. A composite accommodating expansion with a binder lasts longer than a dense one, and the model includes the surrounding matrix. Measurement supplies the advance. A particle's swelling is small and fast, and an in situ technique is what resolves it during a cycle.

Assumptions set the limitation, because a continuum treats a particle as homogeneous while a real one has grain boundaries the model smooths. Lifetime is what the paper does not establish, since crack initiation is only the first failure and capacity fade follows a sequence the paper does not simulate.

Battery science gains a mechanical criterion, and the field's designs have been empirical while a stress criterion lets a material be chosen before it is tested. Fracture toughness is the yardstick, because a crack propagates when the material releases more energy than its new surface can absorb.

A particle cycled until it cracked would give the number of cycles at each rate, and that number is what a cell designer would use rather than a stress value. A comparison of two chemistries with the same particle size would isolate the expansion from the toughness, since a material can fail either because it swells more or because its surface absorbs less energy.

An operando measurement of the strain field inside a particle would test the continuum model where it matters most, at the point where the gradient is steepest. A fracture criterion expressed as a maximum particle size for a given charging rate would turn the model into a design rule, and that rule is the practical product the analysis offers. A cell built with the predicted particle size and cycled at the predicted rate would be the end-to-end test, and its capacity retention is what would decide whether the criterion is right. A post-mortem image of the cracked particle would confirm where the model put the crack, and a mismatch there would be the most informative result the comparison could give.
