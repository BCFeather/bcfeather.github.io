A battery's electrode stores ions by changing its volume, and the change is what cracks a particle and what ends a cell's life. The mechanics of that change is a materials problem.

The analysis in *Science*, September 2023 treats it. The authors measure the swelling of individual particles and model the stress that the swelling generates, and the model predicts where a crack starts.

The prediction is the finding. A particle's crack begins at a point where the stress concentrates, and the concentration depends on the particle's shape and on the rate of charging.

The rate's role is the counterintuitive part. A slow charge lets the particle swell uniformly and a fast one builds a gradient, and the gradient is what raises the stress.

The material's design is the application. A particle with a smaller expansion and a tougher surface resists cracking, and the authors' comparison of chemistries is the guide.

The electrode's architecture is the second lever. A composite that accommodates expansion with a binder is more durable than a dense one, and the authors' model includes the surrounding matrix.

The measurement is the advance. A particle's swelling is small and fast, and the authors' in situ technique is what resolves it during a cycle.

The limitation is the model's assumptions. A continuum treats a particle as homogeneous, and a real particle has grain boundaries that the model smooths.

What the paper does not establish is a lifetime. A crack's initiation is the first failure, and a cell's capacity fade follows a sequence that the paper does not simulate.

For battery science, the value is a mechanical criterion. The field's designs have been empirical, and a stress criterion is a way to choose a material before testing it.

The swelling's origin is the chemistry. An ion that enters a crystal occupies a site and pushes its neighbours apart, and the lattice's expansion is the strain that the mechanics describes.

The gradient's source is the diffusion. An ion that moves slowly through a particle builds a concentration profile, and the profile's steepness is the strain's amplitude.

The crack's criterion is the fracture toughness. A material that releases more energy than its surface can absorb propagates a crack, and the authors' criterion is the comparison of the two.

The particle's shape is a design variable. A sphere concentrates stress differently from a platelet, and the authors' comparison of shapes is the paper's parametric study.

The binder's role is the composite's compliance. A binder that deforms with the particle reduces the constraint, and a rigid one transfers the strain to the particle's surface.

The fast-charging trade-off is the practical tension. A driver wants a fast charge and a durable cell, and the paper's rate dependence is the physics behind the trade-off.
The particle's size is the second variable. A large particle develops a steeper gradient at a given rate, and the authors' scaling relation is what tells a designer which size a fast charge requires.
The surface's coating is a mitigation. A coating that conducts and that deforms distributes the strain, and a coated particle survives more cycles than a bare one in the authors' comparison.
