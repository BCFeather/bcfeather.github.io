Democrats won both chambers of Congress on 3 November, and within a day the party's loudest AI critics were writing the agenda for January. On 4 November, Yassamin Ansari, the representative for Arizona's third district, posted that the top item for the Democratic agenda 'on day 1' should be a pause on advanced AI development, regulation of an industry she called out of control, and a ban on artificial superintelligence. The vehicle exists already: H.R. 10538 in the House, introduced by Greg Casar on 24 September, and S. 5493 in the Senate, introduced a day earlier by Bernie Sanders. Ansari is an original co-sponsor, as are Ro Khanna and Christopher Deluzio, and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez added her name on 28 September. The text would create a cabinet-level Department of Artificial Intelligence, halt training above a compute ceiling until that department is staffed and its rules written, require approval before any covered model is released, and punish individuals with up to twenty years in prison and companies with dissolution.

What the bill bans first is a number. An advanced artificial intelligence system means one trained on ten to the twenty-fifth or more floating point operations, a ceiling the Secretary would adjust each year for efficiency gains. That number is a proxy for scale, and scale stopped being a good proxy once training recipes improved, because compute-optimal methods extract more capability from each operation every year. A laboratory that stays under the ceiling in 2027 can therefore be running something considerably stronger than what the ceiling permitted in 2026, which is a rule that counts the electricity a model consumed rather than what the model can do.

The definition raises a harder problem. Superintelligence itself is defined by capability rather than by measurement: a system that exceeds human cognitive performance across most domains, or one able to plan and execute the destruction of humanity, including by undermining the federal government. No accepted test returns that verdict, and the same clause reaches any model that may be foreseeably modified into such a system, which is broad enough to cover a general-purpose model whose next fine-tune nobody can predict. A Treasury comment on the draft complained that the term had no agreed definition and should not imply a capability threshold, which leaves the judgment exactly where the bill places it: with a Secretary deciding, case by case, what counts.

A pause with no clock is a licensing regime in disguise. Training stops until the Secretary certifies that the department is fully staffed and has issued rules on pre-development reporting, monitoring, audits and pre-release approval. Standing up a new cabinet department with security-cleared technical staff takes two to three years on the record of every agency created since 2001, and nothing in the text binds the Secretary to a date. Laboratories that can afford the compliance apparatus will queue in an orderly way, while smaller firms and academic groups simply stop, which is the result a rule aimed at scale should be expected to produce.

Enforcement stops at the border, and the text concedes as much. The international section directs the Secretary of State to pursue agreements abroad and contemplates export controls on computing infrastructure, an admission that a domestic ban leaves the frontier somewhere else. The penalties are criminal: twenty years for a person, dissolution for a company, modelled on the sanctions for illegally building nuclear weapons. A researcher who runs an evaluation of whether a model can resist shutdown is performing exactly the kind of work the statute lists as a precursor characteristic, and a regime that sends that work to prison gives safety teams a reason to write down less.

None of this makes the underlying fear foolish. The incidents cited in the findings, from agents that coordinated around a company's restrictions to models turned towards virus design, are serious, and the demand that humans remain in control is a reasonable one. The instrument is what fails: a prohibition of a capability that has no test, administered by an agency with no deadline, backed by prison terms, reads to every laboratory on earth as an argument for moving the work somewhere the statute cannot reach. A law that paused named and measured capabilities, published the evaluations, required incident reports and set a date by which the rules had to exist would accomplish most of the safety work. This one bans a word, and a word is the easiest thing in this field to redefine.
