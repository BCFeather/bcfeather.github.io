Russia's campaign against Ukraine's electricity system entered its fifth month, and the pattern had become familiar: waves of missiles and drones aimed at substations and generating plants, followed by emergency blackouts and by crews working through the night. The attacks began in October 2022 and were timed for the cold, and the intent was to make the winter uninhabitable rather than to hit military targets. Ukrainian officials described the campaign as an attempt to produce a second wave of refugees.

Capacity, rather than casualties, measured the damage. By the middle of the winter, Ukraine had lost a large share of its generating capacity in strikes that damaged plants that had been built in the Soviet period, and the system was running with a margin that engineers described as a few hundred megawatts. Imports from Poland, Slovakia and Romania were arranged on an emergency basis, and the European network operators synchronized the Ukrainian grid with the continental one in the first weeks of the war.

Repair was the other half of the campaign. Utility crews became targets, and the workers who restored lines did so in shifts and in the dark, using equipment that had been donated by Western utilities. Ukraine's energy minister said that the system had been repaired faster than the strikes could damage it, and independent engineers said that the claim was true in the sense that the lights came back, and that it did not hold in the sense that the repairs were permanent. Transformers take months to build and cannot be stored in advance, which is why the strikes were aimed at them rather than at the boilers.

The humanitarian calculation was made in cities. Municipalities set up heating points in schools and metro stations, distributed generators to hospitals, and asked residents to leave if they could. The United Nations estimated that millions of people had been left without heating for days at a time, and the winter's death toll from cold and from carbon monoxide was recorded as an increase in excess mortality rather than as a number of its own.

Lawyers asked whether the attacks constituted a war crime. Human rights organizations argued that strikes on civilian infrastructure are prohibited when their purpose is to terrorize the population, and the International Criminal Court's warrant for the arrest of the president in March referred to the deportation of children rather than to the grid. What the winter demonstrated was the interdependence of a modern society. A country can lose a bridge and route traffic around it, and a country that loses its substations loses water pumping, hospital heating and the ability of a factory to run. The grid had become the front line in a war that was being fought with missiles, and the crews repairing it had become the units that the other side was trying to kill.
