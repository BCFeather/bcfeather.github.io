A thermogalvanic cell converts heat into electricity by driving a redox couple at two electrodes held at different temperatures. The voltage comes from the entropy of the reaction, and the current is limited by the concentration of the redox ions.

The cell in *Science*, 21 July 2023 removes that limit. The authors run a photocatalytic reaction in situ that regenerates the redox couple and produces hydrogen, so that the concentration gradient is sustained and the cell's output rises.

The coupling is the design. A photoexcited semiconductor drives the oxidation and reduction that the cell's chemistry needs, and the hydrogen evolved is a second product rather than a loss.

The efficiency is the metric. A thermogalvanic cell's conversion of a temperature difference into work is small, and the paper's enhancement is a multiple rather than a percentage.

The waste heat is the resource. A temperature difference of tens of degrees is available from industrial processes and from solar ponds, and a device that uses it is one that recovers energy that would otherwise be rejected.

The chemistry is the constraint. A redox couple's entropy coefficient sets the voltage, and a couple with a large coefficient produces a large voltage per degree.

The stability is the caveat. A photocatalytic surface in a redox electrolyte corrodes, and the authors' protection is what keeps the cell running.

The scale is far from application. A laboratory cell is small, and a device that a plant could use needs an electrode area measured in square metres.

What the paper does not show is a full system. A cell that produces hydrogen and electricity together needs a separation and a storage, and the system's efficiency is lower than the cell's.

For energy conversion, the value is a hybrid that uses both products. A device that makes power and a fuel from the same heat is a device with two revenue streams, and the coupling is the paper's contribution.

The entropy coefficient is the cell's engine. A reaction that changes the number of ions by an order of magnitude produces a large entropy change, and the voltage that follows is proportional to it.

The photocatalyst's role is regeneration. A redox couple depletes at the electrodes, and a photochemical cycle that restores it is what turns a battery-like device into a continuous one.

The hydrogen is the second product. A proton reduction at the counter electrode is a fuel, and the cell's chemistry produces it without an external voltage.

The temperature's magnitude is the practical constraint. A small difference gives a small voltage, and a device must be matched to the heat source it serves.

The comparison with thermoelectrics is the useful frame. A solid-state device has no electrolyte and no corrosion, and a thermogalvanic cell has a larger voltage per degree and a liquid's complications.

The flow design is the engineering. A cell that circulates the electrolyte maintains the gradient, and a stagnant cell depletes. The authors' flow is part of the result.
