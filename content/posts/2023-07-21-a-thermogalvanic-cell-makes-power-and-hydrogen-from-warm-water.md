A thermogalvanic cell converts heat into electricity by driving a redox couple at two electrodes held at different temperatures. The voltage comes from the entropy of the reaction, and the current is limited by the concentration of the redox ions. In *Science* on 21 July 2023, that limit is removed. The authors run a photocatalytic reaction in situ that regenerates the redox couple and produces hydrogen, so the concentration gradient sustains and the cell's output rises.

Coupling is the design. A photoexcited semiconductor drives the oxidation and reduction the cell's chemistry needs, and the hydrogen evolved is a second product rather than a loss. Efficiency is the metric. Conversion of a temperature difference into work is small in such cells, and the enhancement reported is a multiple rather than a percentage point.

Waste heat supplies the resource. A difference of tens of degrees is available from industrial processes and from solar ponds, and a device using it recovers energy otherwise rejected. Chemistry sets the constraint. Entropy coefficient of the couple sets the voltage, and a couple with a large coefficient produces a large voltage per degree; that coefficient is the cell's engine, since a reaction changing the number of ions by an order of magnitude carries a large entropy change.

Photocatalyst supplies regeneration. A couple depletes at the electrodes, and a photochemical cycle restoring it turns a battery-like device into a continuous one. Hydrogen is the second product, because proton reduction at the counter electrode yields a fuel without an external voltage. Stability is the caveat. A photocatalytic surface in a redox electrolyte corrodes, and the authors' protection is what keeps the cell running. Flow is the engineering, since a circulating electrolyte maintains the gradient while a stagnant one depletes.

Scale stays far from application. A laboratory cell is small, and a plant-scale device needs electrode area measured in square metres. A full system is what the paper does not show, because a cell producing hydrogen and electricity together needs separation and storage, and the system's efficiency is lower than the cell's.

Energy conversion gains a hybrid using both products, since a device making power and a fuel from one heat source has two revenue streams. Thermoelectrics offer the comparison, because a solid-state device has no electrolyte and no corrosion while a thermogalvanic cell has a larger voltage per degree and a liquid's complications.

A cell run against a real heat source, with the temperature difference a factory actually offers, would show whether the regeneration keeps pace with the depletion, since a photochemical cycle that is slower than the electrodes' consumption limits the current regardless of its quantum yield. A cost estimate for the pair of products would decide whether the scheme competes, because hydrogen from a thermogalvanic cell is competing with hydrogen from electrolysis, and the heat it consumes is the only input it does not pay for. A separator that keeps the hydrogen apart from the electrolyte would be the next component, since a cell whose two products mix loses the fuel it just made.
