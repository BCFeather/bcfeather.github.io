Pulsars are neutron stars that sweep beams of radio emission past the Earth as they spin, and the first one was found in 1967 because the pulses were so regular that the signal was briefly taken for a message. The population has grown to thousands, and almost all of them rotate between once a second and hundreds of times a second, which is what a star the size of a city does when it conserves the angular momentum of a star the size of the Sun.

A small group of objects does not fit that description. Over the past three years, surveys have found radio sources that pulse on periods of minutes to hours, too slow for a neutron star that is still powered by rotation and too steady for a flare from an interacting binary. The candidates have included a white dwarf with a magnetic field, a neutron star in a wide binary and a star that is somehow still spinning down slowly enough to emit.

An object reported in *Nature Astronomy* on 16 January belongs to that group and extends it. A team at the University of Sydney found it in archival and follow-up observations with the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder, and the period is 6.5 hours, which is the slowest of the slow transients found so far and slower than any rotation-powered pulsar known.

Polarization data carry the second part of the result. The pulses show emission from two magnetic poles rather than one, which happens when the magnetic axis is nearly perpendicular to the rotation axis and the geometry lets both beams sweep past the Earth. For a slow rotator, that geometry is rare, and the authors argue that the object's magnetic field must be arranged in a way that the standard picture of a pulsar does not produce.

What the object is remains open. A neutron star spinning once every 6.5 hours would have to be extraordinarily old and weakly magnetized, and the emission would have to come from a mechanism that survives when the rotation energy is nearly exhausted. A white dwarf with a strong field is the alternative, and the radio emission from such a star has been predicted and rarely seen.

Either answer would be useful. If the object is a neutron star, the population of slow transients is larger than models allow and the rotation-powered pulsar channel has a tail that surveys have not sampled. If it is a white dwarf, the radio emission mechanism is one that theorists have described on paper and observers have now seen, which is the kind of confirmation that a field waits decades for.

Practical consequence is for surveys. Slow transients are easy to miss because a search that requires several pulses within an hour will not detect an object that pulses four times a day, and the discovery came from a re-analysis of data that had been searched with faster assumptions. The next generation of telescopes will produce archives large enough that the re-analysis is the discovery method, which changes how a survey has to be designed.
