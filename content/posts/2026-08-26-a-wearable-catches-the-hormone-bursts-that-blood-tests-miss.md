Primary aldosteronism is the most common cause of secondary hypertension, affecting perhaps one in five people with high blood pressure, and it is routinely missed. The diagnostic test measures aldosterone and renin, and it is usually taken in a clinic at a convenient hour, which assumes the hormone is stable across the day. A study in *Science Translational Medicine* on 26 August shows that the assumption is wrong in a specific and consequential way.

Researchers at Bristol, Manchester, Bergen and partners in Stockholm and Athens used a portable sampling device developed at Bristol to measure steroid hormones around the clock in patients at home, during normal activity and during sleep. The device collects samples through a cannula without disturbing sleep, which is the only way to see the night-time profile at all. Sleep is when aldosterone and its precursor are normally lowest, so a burst at three in the morning is a signal that no clinic schedule samples.

What they found was not persistently raised hormone levels but repeated bursts of secretion, prominent at night while the day-night rhythm stayed intact. The bursts were larger in patients whose disease came from one adrenal gland rather than both, and they disappeared after surgical removal of the affected gland, which is the strongest evidence that the bursts are the disease rather than a correlate. Twenty-four-hour profiles also change the interpretation of the aldosterone-to-renin ratio, which assumes two simultaneous samples rather than a curve.

The diagnostic consequence is direct. A single morning measurement can land between bursts and read as normal, which is why some patients are told they do not have the condition. Sampling across a day, and specifically across the night, would capture the pattern, and the authors argue that the wearable makes that practical outside a research unit. Unilateral disease responding to surgery is the cleanest validation available, because removing the source removes the pattern.

Practicality is the constraint that decides adoption. A cannula, a pump and a night in one's own bed is a better protocol than a hospital admission, and it is still more burdensome than a blood draw. The device has to be worn correctly, the samples have to be handled cold, and the assay has to be standardized across sites, none of which is trivial at scale. Regulatory acceptance of a home-based test is a separate hurdle from the science, and the device will need a validated assay before it is more than a research instrument.

The economic question sits behind the clinical one. Primary aldosteronism is treatable with surgery or with a specific drug, and the cost of missing it is years of uncontrolled hypertension and its complications. A test that costs more per patient and finds a treatable cause in one of five is a bargain in a health system that pays for strokes. Screening every hypertensive patient would be the aggressive option, and the trial's authors prefer a targeted approach for patients whose blood pressure resists treatment.
