A single junction has a theoretical ceiling near thirty-three per cent, and silicon sits close to its practical share of that. A tandem stacks two absorbers so each uses the part of the spectrum it handles best. Two papers in *Science* on 7 July 2023 report tandems above thirty per cent, one addressing the optics of the stack and the other the stability a commercial module would need. Optics begins with current matching, since two junctions in series carry the same current, and a top cell absorbing too little or too much limits the pair.

Light management is the solution there. A textured surface and a reflective back contact trap light in the silicon, while a transparent contact on the top cell lets red light through. A pyramid texture refracts light obliquely, lengthening its path so the red end absorbs more completely.

Perovskite's bandgap is the tuning knob. A gap near 1.7 electronvolts matches the silicon below, and a composition reaching that gap is the material's job. Uniformity is the material's problem, because a mixed-halide film can phase-separate under illumination, and a separated film has two gaps and one current.

Interface carries the stability problem. A perovskite on rough silicon has a contact that degrades, and the second paper's treatment of that interface is the fix. Thermal budget constrains the process, since the silicon cell's metallization is already in place, and a perovskite deposited above it must be processed below the temperature that would damage it.

Certified measurement settles credibility. A result from an independent laboratory is the number the field accepts, and both papers report certified values at a reference spectrum and temperature. Scale sets the limitation, as a cell of one square centimetre is not a module, and a module's efficiency falls with interconnect and edge losses.

Field lifetime is what the papers do not establish, because a twenty-five year warranty is a claim about encapsulation, and tandem encapsulation is not yet standard. Photovoltaics gains a route past a ceiling, since silicon's market share is a manufacturing base and a tandem keeping silicon while adding a layer uses it.

Economics stays open, because an extra layer adds cost and the efficiency gain must exceed it, and these papers establish the physics rather than the business case. An outdoor test at a module scale would settle what the certified cell cannot, since the degradation that matters happens under heat, humidity and cycling rather than under a reference lamp, and a tandem's perovskite layer is the part that answers to weather. Whether a third junction is worth the added optics is the further question, since the spectrum has more bands to divide than two absorbers can use, and each junction adds a current-matching constraint that the stack must satisfy. A module's bill of materials decides the outcome as much as the efficiency does, because the transparent contact and the textured silicon are the two costs that a tandem adds to a line that already exists.
