A single junction has a theoretical ceiling of about thirty-three per cent, and silicon is close to its practical share of that. A tandem stacks two absorbers and lets each use the part of the spectrum it handles best.

The two papers in *Science*, 7 July 2023 report tandems above thirty per cent. One addresses the optics of the stack, and the other the stability that a commercial module would need.

The optics problem is the current matching. Two junctions in series carry the same current, and a top cell that absorbs too little or too much limits the pair.

The light management is the solution. A textured surface and a reflective back contact trap light in the silicon, and a transparent contact on the top cell lets the red light through.

The perovskite's bandgap is the tuning knob. A gap near 1.7 electronvolts matches the silicon below it, and a composition that reaches that gap is the material's role.

The stability problem is the interface. A perovskite on a rough silicon surface has a contact that degrades, and the second paper's treatment of that interface is the fix.

The efficiency's measurement is the credibility issue. A certified result from an independent laboratory is the number the field accepts, and both papers report certified values.

The scale is the limitation. A cell of one square centimetre is not a module, and a module's efficiency is lower because of the interconnect and the edges.

What the papers do not establish is field lifetime. A module's twenty-five year warranty is a claim about encapsulation, and the tandem's encapsulation is not yet standard.

For photovoltaics, the value is a route past a ceiling. Silicon's share of the market is a manufacturing base, and a tandem that keeps silicon and adds a layer is a route that uses that base.

The current matching is the design's central constraint. A series connection forces equality, and a top cell that is slightly too absorptive throws away the excess as heat.

The transparent contact is the optics' hard part. A contact must conduct and transmit, and a metal that does one fails the other. The authors' oxide is the compromise.

The texture is the light trap. A pyramid surface refracts light obliquely, and a long path through silicon absorbs the red end of the spectrum more completely.

The bandgap's uniformity is the material's problem. A mixed-halide perovskite can phase-separate under illumination, and a separated film has two gaps and one current.

The thermal budget is the process constraint. A silicon cell's metallization is already in place, and a perovskite deposited above it must be processed below the temperature that would damage it.

The certification is the standard. A measurement at a reference spectrum and temperature is the only number that is comparable, and the papers' certificates are what makes thirty per cent a claim.

The tandem's economics are the open question. An extra layer adds cost, and the efficiency gain has to exceed it. The papers establish the physics and not the business case.
