Semiconductor spin qubits have been built the cautious way. Electron spins in silicon sit in quiet electrical environments, and the price of that quiet is slow control: moving an electron spin fast needs microwaves delivered through on-chip antennae, and every wire is a heat load and a placement problem a large array cannot carry. Hole spins, the absence of an electron in a valence band, couple more strongly to their surroundings, which gives designers an opening: the same coupling that makes a hole sensitive to noise lets an ordinary voltage swing swing the qubit. The catch is that sensitivity to noise and responsiveness to control were assumed to travel together, which would cancel the advantage.

M. Bassi and colleagues at Pheliqs, a joint laboratory of Université Grenoble Alpes, the CEA and Grenoble INP, with S. De Franceschi and V. Schmitt as corresponding authors, built their devices on a 300-millimetre CMOS production line at CEA-Leti and tested the assumption directly. Two hole spins in metal-oxide-silicon quantum dots defined in natural silicon nanowires were held in a dilution refrigerator near 100 millikelvin while a vector magnet swept the field across every angle. Their report appeared in *Nature Physics* on 12 December 2025.

At the angle where both properties were predicted to coincide, the results are specific. One qubit held coherence for 17.9 microseconds and the other for 25.0, at a precession frequency near 18 gigahertz. Driven purely by oscillating gate voltage at 10.0 megahertz and 24.0 megahertz, the two devices scored quality factors of 360 and 1,200, the second being a figure the authors place above the level reported for semiconductor spin qubits generally. Randomized benchmarking gave single-bit fidelities of 99.53 per cent and 99.71 per cent. The theory attached to those quality factors allows 99.85 per cent and 99.95 per cent, which is to say the machines have not yet reached their own ceiling.

The geometry explains why noise and speed stopped trading. Measuring how the qubit frequency shifts with gate voltage at every magnetic angle, parallel and perpendicular, the team reconstructed the directions in parameter space where electrical noise has no purchase, and found them to be the same directions where a voltage drives the spin hardest. Reciprocal sweetness is their term. A tuning knob then moved the sweet direction by rotating the field's polar angle from 10 to 26 degrees on the first qubit and 15 to 57 on the second, and where the two qubits' sweet directions overlapped, plain voltage tuning raised the overlap from 23 per cent to 60.

Read carefully what was not tested. The two qubits did not interact during this experiment; sweet-spot alignment is a precondition for two-bit gates, not their demonstration. Charge noise in these devices runs about an order of magnitude above the best silicon electron devices, and natural silicon carries nuclear spins that add their own noise. The authors say they have no quantitative measure of disorder in their own devices, and their argument that the recipe survives scaling rests on earlier simulations in which charge defects smear the sweet angles by roughly 30 degrees. That step is inference, and the paper treats it as such.

The checks that follow are named by the same logic. Isotopically purified silicon should lift the coherence times that set the quality factors; a fabricated array with measured sweet-spot overlap would convert the simulation into a number; and two-bit gates at a shared sweet point would show whether reciprocal sweetness pays at the scale where it matters. What the result already changes is the design habit: the operating point is chosen so the qubit deafens noise and accepts control at the same angle, found with a magnet and held with a gate voltage.
