A magnet at low temperature orders: the moments on neighbouring sites line up and the material acquires a direction. A spin liquid is a material in which that does not happen, and the reason is not that the interactions are weak but that they compete. A triangular lattice of moments that each want to point opposite to their neighbours has no way to satisfy everyone, and the frustration keeps the moments moving down to the lowest temperatures measured.

Surviving a magnetic field is the harder test. A field favours order because it picks a direction, and a material that orders in a small field was never a liquid in the sense that matters. A liquid that remains disordered in a field is one where the excitations carrying the entropy are not the simple spin flips that the field can align, and the candidate is a state in which the moments are paired into a singlet and the pairs can move across the lattice.

The evidence is in the neutron spectrum. A liquid produces a broad continuum of scattering, since a spin flip can take any momentum, and an ordered magnet produces sharp peaks at the ordering wavevector. The distinction is visible in the shape of the spectrum and in the temperature dependence, and the experiment that matters cools the sample in a field and looks for the continuum where the peaks would be. A crystal is also what makes the temperature dependence trustworthy, since a powder mixes contributions from grains that are oriented differently and blurs the very feature the experiment is looking for.

The candidate material is a layered compound of the kind that has been studied for a decade, with molecules arranged so that the lattice is triangular and the coupling is close to the threshold where an ordered state is replaced by a liquid. The measurement is dominated by the sample: a powder averages the spectrum over every orientation, and a single crystal is needed to see whether the continuum is really featureless or merely broadened by the averaging.

Field dependence is the new part of the result. A material that loses its continuum at a field of a few tesla has a gap in its excitation spectrum, and the gap closes at a critical field where the singlet pairs break. A material that keeps the continuum up to the highest field the experiment reached is a material in which the pairs are strongly bound, and the bound pairs are what a theory of the state has to explain.

What would settle the question is a measurement of the thermal conductivity in zero field. A liquid of neutral pairs conducts no heat, and a liquid with itinerant moments conducts heat along the lattice like a metal without charge. The experiment has been attempted and the results are not yet clean enough to separate the two, and the state remains a candidate rather than an identity.
