description: "India's parliament passed a constitutional amendment on 21 September 2023 reserving a third of seats in the national legislature and in state assemblies for women, a measure that will take effect only after a census and a redrawing of constituencies."
---

Both houses passed the hundred and twenty-eighth constitutional amendment on 21 September, during a special session held in the new parliament building, and the president signed it the following week. The amendment reserves a third of the seats in the Lok Sabha and in every state assembly for women, and it also applies to the seats reserved for lower castes and tribes. It was supported by the governing party and by most of the opposition, and it passed by the two-thirds majorities that a constitutional change requires in both houses.

Implementation is deferred. The reservation takes effect after a census, which has been delayed since 2021, and after a redrawing of constituency boundaries that has been frozen since 1976 pending a new population count. The amendment says the reservation will last fifteen years, with rotation of the seats at each redrawing, which is a design that critics say will make incumbency difficult and the effect uncertain.

Argument about who benefits has reopened. Opposition parties said the reservation should include a sub-quota for women from lower castes, which the government declined to add, and several said the measure was timed to appeal to women voters before state elections in November. The governing party said the bill was the fulfilment of a demand that its opponents had blocked for a decade, and it noted that a similar bill introduced in 2010 had lapsed after its sponsors could not agree on the sub-quota.

Numbers explain the demand. Women hold about fourteen per cent of the seats in the Lok Sabha and about ten per cent in the state assemblies, and India ranks well below most democracies in the representation of women in national parliaments, below Bangladesh, Pakistan and Rwanda, all of which have reserved seats or quotas. The country has had a woman prime minister, a woman president and a woman chief minister in several states, and its local councils already reserve a third of their seats for women, a change made in 1992 that produced more than a million elected women in village government.

Local precedent is the strongest argument in favour. Studies of the village councils found that women leaders invested more in water and sanitation and in schools than men did, and that the reservation raised the aspirations of girls who saw women in office. The same research found that villages with a woman leader were more likely to report a crime to the police. The same studies found that the effect depended on the seats rotating and on women not being proxies for male relatives, which is the criticism that has followed the new law.

A hundred and twenty-eighth amendment has written a reservation into the constitution, and its effect waits on a census that has not been scheduled. The women who might sit in the seats will arrive after the next count, and whether the rotation makes them permanent depends on how the seats are drawn, which is a task that will be given to a commission that has not been appointed.
