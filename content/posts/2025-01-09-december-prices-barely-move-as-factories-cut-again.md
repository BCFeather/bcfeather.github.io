description: "Consumer inflation stayed near zero and factory gate prices fell for a twenty-sixth month, a combination that raises the real cost of debt."
---

Consumer prices rose 0.1 per cent in December from a year earlier, and the full-year figure came in at 0.2 per cent, the weakest calendar year since the deflation scare of 2009. Factory gate prices fell 2.3 per cent in the month and 2.2 per cent over the year, which means producers have now sold below last year's prices for more than two years running. Food was the volatile part, with pork and vegetables swinging on supply, and services were the sticky part, with rents and repairs still rising. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, stayed below half a per cent for the ninth consecutive month.

Weak pricing power is the story the numbers tell. A factory that cuts prices to keep an order is a factory with spare capacity, and the survey data have shown capacity utilisation below its long-run average in most manufacturing sectors. Vehicles are the clearest case: a price war among electric car makers has pushed sticker prices down by double-digit percentages in two years, and the same pattern has spread to batteries and to household appliances.

Deflation changes the arithmetic of borrowing. A firm with a nominal interest rate of four per cent and prices falling at two per cent pays a real rate of six, and a household with a fixed mortgage feels the same squeeze in its wage bargain. That is why central banks treat persistent negative producer prices as a symptom rather than a curiosity, and why the policy debate in Beijing has shifted from whether to ease to how quickly. The deflator for the whole economy has been negative for several quarters, which means nominal growth is smaller than real growth.

The policy response has been fiscal more than monetary. Subsidies for trade-ins, local government special bonds for infrastructure and a programme to replace urban underground pipes all put orders in front of factories, and each of them shows up in the investment numbers rather than in the price numbers. Rate cuts have been smaller, because the constraint is demand and because the exchange rate limits how far the policy rate can fall.

Prices in the shops are also a distributional question. Falling appliance and car prices help households that buy them, and falling pork prices hurt the farmers who raise pigs, which is why the adjustment is politically sensitive out of proportion to its size in the index. The consumer basket that the statistics bureau uses gives food a weight of about one-fifth, and that weight explains why a single commodity can move the headline.

What happens next depends on two things that are not in the December release: whether the property market stops subtracting from demand, and whether exporters can hold prices in the face of tariffs. Neither is visible in a monthly print, and both will decide whether 2025 repeats the pattern of falling prices or breaks it.
