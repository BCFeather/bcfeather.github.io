description: "Correspondence experiments in six Western countries show that hiring discrimination against minority applicants persists and that its level varies with the local labour market."

Hiring discrimination is measured by sending identical applications that differ only in the applicant's name and counting the callbacks. The method is a correspondence experiment, and it has been run hundreds of times since the 1970s.

In *PNAS* on 7 February 2023, those experiments are gathered into one dataset. Lincoln Quillian and John Lee collect estimates from Canada, France, Germany, Great Britain, the Netherlands and the United States, and standardize them so that countries and decades can be compared.

Level is the first result. Every country in the sample shows discrimination against at least one minority group, and the magnitude varies by country and by the group tested, which is the kind of variation institutional differences would produce. Standardization is the technical core, because different experiments use different occupations, different minority groups and different measures of callback, and a meta-analysis has to convert them to a common scale before any comparison is meaningful.

Because the dataset spans enough years for a change to be visible, the second result is a trend, and the change differs between countries, with a decline where antidiscrimination enforcement strengthened and persistence where it did not; some countries show a flat line, and making the flat lines visible is what turns a trend into a policy failure. Occupational mix confounds the average as well, since discrimination runs higher in customer-facing jobs where an employer imagines a client's reaction and lower in manual work where a skill is easy to verify.

Control is the method's strength. Two applications with the same qualifications and different names isolate the name, and the difference in callback rates is the estimate, which no survey can deliver, because a survey measures what people say rather than what they do. Scope is its limit, since a correspondence experiment sees the first stage of hiring and misses discrimination at interview, at promotion or in pay, so a country can look equal at the callback stage and unequal later.

Publication bias adds a third limit, because studies that find no discrimination are less likely to be published and a meta-analysis of published work overstates the effect, which the paper corrects for. Explanation is what the dataset cannot supply, since statistical and taste-based discrimination produce the same callback pattern and call for different remedies. A baseline is what the field gains, because a country that measures itself against this dataset can see where it stands, and a standing is the precondition for a policy; new experiments can be placed in the series, and a result that contradicts the trend can be examined for a methodological difference rather than assumed to be an error.

Legal exposure is the part the data cannot reach, because a difference in callback rates is not a court finding, and the standards a tribunal applies to a correspondence experiment differ from those a meta-analysis uses. A country that publishes its own rate invites comparison, and comparison is what makes a persistent level politically visible.
