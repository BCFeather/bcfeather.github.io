Hiring discrimination is measured by sending identical applications that differ only in the applicant's name, and counting the callbacks. The method is called a correspondence experiment, and it has been run hundreds of times since the 1970s.

The study in *PNAS*, 7 February 2023 gathers those experiments into one dataset. Lincoln Quillian and John Lee collect estimates from Canada, France, Germany, Great Britain, the Netherlands and the United States, and standardize them so that countries and decades can be compared.

The comparison's first result is a level. Every country in the sample shows discrimination against at least one minority group, and the magnitude varies by country and by the group tested. The variation is the kind that institutional differences would produce.

The second result is a trend. The dataset spans enough years for a change to be visible, and the change differs between countries. A country whose antidiscrimination enforcement strengthened shows a decline, and one whose enforcement did not shows persistence.

The method's strength is its control. Two applications with the same qualifications and different names isolate the name, and the callback rate difference is the estimate. No survey can do that, because a survey measures what people say rather than what they do.

The method's limit is its scope. A correspondence experiment measures the first stage of hiring, and discrimination at interview, at promotion or in pay is invisible to it. A country can look equal at the callback stage and unequal later.

The publication bias is the third limit. Studies that find no discrimination are less likely to be published, and a meta-analysis that includes only published work overstates the effect. The paper's correction for that bias is part of its method.

The policy reading is about enforcement. A decline in a country with active enforcement and persistence elsewhere is a natural experiment, and the comparison is closer to causal than the raw levels are.

What the dataset cannot do is explain. A level of discrimination is an outcome, and the mechanisms, which include statistical discrimination and taste-based discrimination, produce the same callback pattern and require different remedies.

For the field, the value is a baseline. A country that measures its own discrimination against this dataset can see where it stands, and a standing is the precondition for a policy.

The standardization is the technical core. Different experiments use different occupations, different minority groups and different measures of callback, and a meta-analysis has to convert them to a common scale before any comparison is meaningful.
Occupational variation is a confound worth naming. Discrimination is higher in customer-facing jobs where an employer imagines a client's reaction, and lower in manual work where a skill is easy to verify. A country's occupational mix can change its average.
The direction of the trend is not uniform. Some countries show an improvement that the reforms explain, and others show a flat line that the reforms do not. The paper's contribution is to make the flat lines visible, because a flat line is a policy failure.
For researchers, the dataset is a resource. New correspondence experiments can be placed in the series, and a result that contradicts the trend can be examined for a methodological difference rather than assumed to be an error.
