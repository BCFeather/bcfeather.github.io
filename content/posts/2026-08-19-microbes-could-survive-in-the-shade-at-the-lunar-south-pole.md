Planetary protection has an awkward arithmetic: every mission carries microbes, and the question is not whether they arrive but whether they can persist. The Moon was assumed to settle the question in the negative, because the surface is bathed in ultraviolet radiation and swings between extremes of temperature. A model published in *Science Advances* on 19 August suggests the answer depends on where the microbe lands. The organisms chosen for the model are the ones most likely to travel with a spacecraft, which makes the study an assessment of mission risk rather than of astrobiology.

A NASA-led study with authors at the University of Maryland combined maps of ultraviolet fluence over a lunar day with maximum summer temperature, and then asked whether the tolerance limits of specific organisms could be met inside that envelope. Permanently shadowed regions, the floors of polar craters that never see sunlight, came out as survivable for every organism tested, and the surrounding terrain offered scattered niches in the lee of rocks and crater rims.

Ultraviolet fluence is an accumulated quantity, so what kills a spore is the total dose over an exposure rather than the peak intensity at noon. Shade reduces the dose enough that a week becomes possible for organisms with a high tolerance, and the authors emphasise that the survival window varies dramatically between species, with *Aspergillus* among the hardier fungi and some bacterial species failing much sooner. A tolerant spore and a fragile vegetative cell differ by orders of magnitude in dose, so a single survival map would be misleading and the paper reports species separately.

Temperature sets the other bound. The lunar day reaches well above the boiling point of water at the equator, and the polar summer is cooler, but the same terrain that provides shade also provides cold, and an organism needs a window in which neither condition is fatal. The maps therefore show bands rather than zones, and those bands fall close to the candidate landing regions for Artemis III. Subsurface access is the other variable, since a sample from a metre below the regolith is shielded from ultraviolet entirely and the surface model says nothing about it.

The implication for exploration is about contamination and about false discovery. If terrestrial microbes can persist near a site for a week, then a sample taken weeks after landing could contain chemistry that looks like biology, and the distinction between ancient lunar chemistry and astronaut contamination becomes harder to draw. That is the reason planetary protection rules exist, and this work suggests the rules should be site-specific.

What would settle it is an experiment rather than a model. A small payload could expose known organisms at a candidate site and read survival directly, which is cheaper than the alternative of assuming a bound in either direction. Until then, the useful conclusion is that the Moon is not uniformly sterile, and the maps say where to look. Planetary protection policy is written in categories, and a category that assumes rapid inactivation may need revisiting if a week of persistence is enough to contaminate an instrument.
