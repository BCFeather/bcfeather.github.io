Guangzhou's export fair, which opens in the middle of April, is the largest trade event of its kind, and the work that makes it happen begins in the factories of the Pearl River Delta months before the halls are swept. A factory that intends to exhibit decides in January which products to show, builds the samples in February, photographs them in March, and ships them to a booth that is four metres wide. The sample is the argument that the company makes about what the market wants, and it is made a year before the orders arrive.

The companies that exhibit are not the largest. The very large manufacturers have their own sales offices and their own customers, and the fair is the place where a mid-sized factory meets a buyer from a country it has never sold to. The economics of the trip are calculated carefully, a booth costs a sum that a small firm feels, the samples cost more, and the staff who attend lose two weeks of production time. A firm that goes three years without an order from the fair stops going.

What the fair shows, year by year, is the direction of the demand. The booths that are crowded in one year are the ones that make the product that the buyers came for, and the shift from one product to another is visible in the floor plan before it is visible in the customs data. The current shift is towards the small electrical goods, the home storage, the components for the solar installations, and away from the categories that the tariffs have made expensive.

The buyer side has changed too. The large importers still send a team, and the teams are smaller than they were, because a buyer can now request a sample by courier and place an order through a platform. What the fair provides that the platform does not is the ability to see a factory's representative handle a question about a defect, which is the kind of information that a purchase order depends on.

The tariff and the fair sit awkwardly together. A buyer from the American market who attends the fair is a buyer who is calculating the landed cost of the sample in his hand, and the calculation includes a duty that may change before the container arrives. The factories that have adapted have moved their sales towards the markets that the duty does not reach, and the fair is where that shift is negotiated face to face.

What the two weeks in Guangzhou produce is a set of orders that will be manufactured over the following year, and a set of impressions that will shape the next one. The impressions are the reason the fair has survived the platform, because a buyer who has shaken hands with a supplier will call that supplier when a shipment is late, and the call is worth more than the catalogue, which is a fact that every sales director at the fair knows and none of them can measure.
