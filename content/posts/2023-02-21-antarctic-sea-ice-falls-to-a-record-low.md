Satellite data reported on 21 February showed sea ice around the continent at its annual minimum. The figure was 1.91 million square kilometres, about 100,000 below the record of February 2022. The satellite record begins in 1979, and five of the ten lowest minima have occurred since 2017. The centre calculates extent from microwave sensors that have flown since 1978 and are cross-checked against ships.

Minimum extent arrives in February, at the end of the southern summer, and the ice then grows through the winter. This year the growth was slower than usual in the Weddell Sea and faster in the Ross Sea, which is the pattern that has appeared since 2016. Winds and ocean temperatures both contribute, and the relative weight of each is still argued. The Weddell Sea lost ice earlier than usual, and the Ross Sea gained it later, which shifted the total.

Sea ice extent matters for albedo, because white ice reflects sunlight that open water absorbs. It also matters for the creatures that live under it, from algae to penguins. A record low in one year does not by itself prove a trend, and the scientists who published the figure said so. Albedo loss in the southern summer is small in global terms because the sun is low, and the effect is mostly local.

Over the full record the Arctic has lost about 12 per cent per decade, while the Antarctic changed much less until recently. The southern hemisphere had been expected to lose ice more slowly because the ocean around it is deep and cold. The last six years have changed that expectation, and the models are being revised. The models used in the last assessment assumed the southern ice would change little this century.

Research published in the same week found that the ice shelves holding back the glaciers in the Amundsen Sea were thinning faster than in the 1990s. Those shelves float, so their loss does not raise the sea level directly, but it speeds the glaciers behind them. The glaciers in that sector hold enough water to raise the level by 1.2 metres if they all went. The Amundsen Sea sector holds the Thwaites glacier, which has been thinning for two decades. Satellite altimetry showed the shelf thinning at 2 metres a year in places, which is fast for ice.

Seasonal forecasts for the winter were for a recovery towards average, and the September maximum was close to the mean. Whether the minimum returns to the old range depends on the wind patterns over the continent, and those are the least predictable part of the system. The centre publishes the figures monthly, and the next minimum is due in February 2024. The September maximum was 18.1 million square kilometres, within 2 per cent of the average. The centre's monthly report is published on the first Tuesday of each month, and it includes a graph back to 1979.
