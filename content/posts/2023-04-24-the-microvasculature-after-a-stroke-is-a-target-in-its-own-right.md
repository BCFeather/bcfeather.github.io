A stroke is described as an occlusion, and the occlusion is the event. What follows is a response of the tissue, and the small vessels that supply it are where much of the response happens.

The profiling in *PNAS*, 18 April 2023 characterizes that response. The authors measure molecular changes in the cerebral microvasculature after a stroke and identify pathways that change and candidates that might act on them.

The microvasculature's role is the reason for looking. A capillary bed that is damaged after an occlusion fails to reperfuse, and a tissue that is not reperfused dies even after the artery is opened. The no-reflow phenomenon is a vascular problem.

The method is a compartment-specific measurement. A whole-tissue homogenate mixes neurons, glia and vessels, and isolating the vessels is what makes a vascular signal visible.

The findings include inflammation and barrier changes. The vessels after a stroke become leaky and adhesive, and the molecules that mediate the change are the candidates that the paper lists.

The therapeutic logic is a window. A drug given after the artery is opened acts on the tissue's response rather than on the clot, and a target in the microvasculature is accessible from the blood.

The limitation is the model. A rodent's stroke is induced and its vasculature is not a human's, and a target that works in a mouse's capillary bed may not work in a patient's.

What the paper does not show is a treatment effect. A candidate identified by profiling is a hypothesis, and a trial is what would test it.

The biomarker angle is a secondary use. A molecule that the vasculature releases after a stroke could mark the injury's extent, and a blood test for it would be a practical tool.

For stroke medicine, the value is a shift in target. The field's effort has gone into reopening arteries, and the tissue's vascular response is a second front that the paper opens.

The timing is the analysis's axis. A response at one hour differs from a response at one day, and the paper's time course is what separates an acute change from a repair.

The cell types are the resolution's problem. A vessel is lined by endothelium and surrounded by pericytes and astrocytes, and a bulk measurement conflates them. Single-cell methods are the next step.

The comparison with other organs is instructive. The brain's vessels have a barrier that other organs lack, and a response that is specific to the brain's endothelium is a target that a systemic drug can hit without affecting the rest of the body.
The reperfusion injury's mechanism is partly vascular. A vessel that loses its barrier lets fluid and cells into the tissue, and the swelling that follows compresses the capillaries further. The loop is a reason a vascular target could break it.
The drug's delivery is the practical constraint. A molecule that must reach the capillary bed within hours has to be given intravenously, and a target that is expressed on the endothelium is the one a bolus can reach.
