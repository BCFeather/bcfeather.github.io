A stroke is described as an occlusion, and the occlusion is the event. What follows is a response of the tissue, and the small vessels that supply it are where much of that response happens, which is why a therapy aimed at the clot alone can leave the tissue to die.

In *PNAS* on 18 April 2023, that response is characterized. The authors measure molecular changes in the cerebral microvasculature after a stroke and identify pathways that change and candidates that might act on them.

Because a capillary bed damaged after an occlusion fails to reperfuse, and tissue that is not reperfused dies even once the artery is open, no-reflow is a vascular problem, which is the reason for looking at the small vessels at all. Compartment matters for measurement, since a whole-tissue homogenate mixes neurons, glia and vessels, and isolating the vessels is what makes a vascular signal visible.

Profiled by time course, the response separates acute change from repair, because an hour and a day look different in the same tissue. The findings are inflammation and barrier change, with vessels becoming leaky and adhesive, and the molecules that mediate the change are the candidates the paper lists. The loop then tightens: a vessel that loses its barrier lets fluid and cells into the tissue, the swelling that follows compresses capillaries further, and a vascular target could break that circle.

Therapeutic logic follows the timing. A drug given after the artery is opened acts on the tissue's response rather than on the clot, and a target on the endothelium is reachable from the blood and by a bolus. What the brain offers is specificity, since its vessels carry a barrier that other organs lack, so a response confined to cerebral endothelium is one a systemic drug can hit without touching the rest of the body.

Resolution limits what the profiling can say. A vessel is lined by endothelium and wrapped by pericytes and astrocytes, a bulk measurement conflates them, and single-cell methods are the next step. What the model cannot supply is human relevance, because a rodent's stroke is induced and its vasculature is not a patient's, and a target that works in a mouse's capillary bed may fail in a ward. Profiling yields hypotheses rather than effects, since a candidate identified this way still needs a trial. A secondary use is diagnostic, because a molecule the vasculature releases after a stroke could mark the injury's extent and a blood test for it would be a practical tool. Stroke medicine gains a second front: its effort has gone into reopening arteries, and the tissue's vascular response turns out to be a target in its own right.

What the profiling cannot order is the sequence, since a molecule that rises at one hour and falls at six may be a cause of the later change or a consequence of it, and a perturbation experiment is what would put the pathways in order.
