The National Collegiate Athletic Association's basketball tournament is the most valuable property in American sport after the professional leagues, and its financial structure is unusual: the broadcast contract pays roughly 900 million dollars a year, and the association distributes about 200 million of it to conferences as units earned by participating teams.

Distribution is by performance. A conference earns a unit for each game a member plays, and the unit is paid out over six years, which means a deep run in March pays a conference for years. A single unit is worth about 340,000 dollars per year across the payout period, and a conference with four teams in the tournament and two deep runs can earn 10 million.

Players receive none of this directly. Their compensation, since 2021, has been through name, image and likeness deals, which are negotiated with collectives funded by boosters and businesses. Estimates put the total for men's basketball at 500 to 800 million dollars a year, spread unevenly across a few hundred athletes.

The two systems are unconnected by design. The association distributes to institutions; the collectives pay individuals; the broadcast contract pays the association. Each has its own accounting, and the total that reaches a player depends on his market rather than on the tournament's revenue.

Title IX complicates the comparison. A university that funds a women's programme at parity does so from the same athletic budget, and a men's basketball tournament unit funds the department rather than the team that earned it. That cross-subsidy is the arrangement most athletic directors defend.

Collective regulation is the live issue. The association has proposed a cap on collective payments and a clearinghouse to review deals above a threshold, on the argument that unregulated payments are inducing recruitment violations. The proposal faces a legal obstacle: a cap agreed among competitors is a restraint of trade unless it is bargained with a players' association that does not exist.

Conference realignment follows the money. Schools move to conferences with more tournament units and larger broadcast shares, and the movement has made traditional rivalries a scheduling inconvenience. That is a rational response to a distribution formula, and it is the clearest evidence that the formula drives behaviour.

Unit distribution has a quirk that favors stability. A conference's units persist for six years regardless of whether its teams keep winning, so a single deep run keeps paying after the coach who produced it has left. That lag is why realignment decisions are made on multi-year revenue projections rather than on a season's results.

Broadcast inventory is the pressure point. The tournament's contract bundles every game into one package, and the association has resisted selling the early rounds separately even though the individual games would fetch more on their own, because the bundle keeps the smaller conferences inside the deal.

For a reader, the interesting question is whether the tournament's value can keep growing while its labour is paid through a separate channel. Professional leagues resolved this by sharing revenue with players through a collective agreement. College sport has not, and the current arrangement is stable only as long as the collectives' benefactors remain willing.
