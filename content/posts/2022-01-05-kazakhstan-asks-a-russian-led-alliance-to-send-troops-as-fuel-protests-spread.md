description: "A doubling of the price of bottled gas in a western oil town turned into nationwide unrest within four days, and the president blamed foreign-trained terrorists while a Russian-led force deployed."
---

Kazakhstan's president, Kassym-Jomart Tokayev, asked the Collective Security Treaty Organization to send forces on 5 January, after protests that began in the western oil town of Zhanaozen spread to Almaty and to the capital. The Russian-led alliance answered within hours, and Russian paratroopers arrived the next day alongside troops from Belarus, Armenia, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan, a deployment the alliance described as temporary and limited to protecting government buildings. The request was made under article four of the treaty, which obliges members to consult when a signatory faces aggression, and the clause had never been used.

A price was the trigger. Zhanaozen's residents rely on bottled liquefied gas for cars and heating, and the government lifted a price cap on 1 January, which doubled the cost within a day. Drivers blocked the main square, the crowd grew, and within three days the protest had moved across the country and had stopped being about gas.

By then the demands had widened and the language had hardened. Crowds in Almaty seized the airport and set fire to the city hall and to shops that belonged to families connected to the former president, Nursultan Nazarbayev, whose relatives had held seats in the security council and stakes in the oil industry for three decades. Tokayev removed Nazarbayev from the council chairmanship on 5 January and dismissed the government, and he described the crowds as bandits and terrorists trained abroad, a claim he did not support with evidence. He also said that the unrest had been organized from abroad, and he named no country, which left his audience to fill in the blank.

Shooting started on 6 January. Tokayev authorized the use of force without warning against what he called armed groups, and witnesses in Almaty described volleys of gunfire on the main squares. The interior ministry later put the number of dead above two hundred, with most of them civilians, and the authorities said several thousand people had been detained, numbers that took weeks to emerge and that independent monitors could not verify.

It was the first occasion on which the alliance had deployed troops at a member's request, and it has been cited by Moscow ever since as a precedent. It also let Tokayev win a domestic argument: the forces guarded buildings while his own security services disarmed the networks that had grown around Nazarbayev's family, and the political transition that followed in the autumn moved the capital's elite decisively away from the old patron.

A price cap removed on new year's day is a policy no government can defend, and a bargain with a foreign alliance is one it cannot easily leave. Russian units stayed until mid-January, and their departure was as carefully staged as their arrival. The government has since described the episode as an attempted coup, and it has used that description to justify laws that restrict online speech. Kazakhstan's January showed how fast a subsidy can turn into a legitimacy question, and how quickly a government under pressure will hand its streets to someone else's soldiers.
