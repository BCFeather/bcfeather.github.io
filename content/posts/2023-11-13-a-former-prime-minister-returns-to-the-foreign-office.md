Rishi Sunak appointed David Cameron as foreign secretary on 13 November, bringing back a former prime minister who had left politics after the Brexit referendum in 2016 and had spent the years since writing memoirs and advising a collapsed finance firm. The reshuffle also removed Suella Braverman as home secretary, a week after she published an article accusing the police of favouritism and was widely reported to have ignored the prime minister's instructions on its content.

Cameron's appointment required a peerage, because he holds no seat in the House of Commons, and he was made a lord and a minister of state in the same announcement. It also required an explanation, because a man who campaigned to remain in the European Union was now running a department whose central task is the relationship with Europe. Sunak framed the move as a decision about experience, saying the country needed a foreign secretary who had dealt with leaders and crises, and that the war in Ukraine and the conflict in Gaza demanded a steady hand.

The Braverman dismissal was the other half of the same calculation. She had become the most visible voice of the party's right wing, and her article in the Times, in which she wrote that the police treated protests differently depending on who was marching, prompted criticism from senior officers and from within the cabinet. James Cleverly, who had been foreign secretary, moved to the Home Office, and the trade secretary, Kemi Badenoch, stayed in place.

Contrast told the story. Cameron represents a government that wants to look competent and international, and Braverman represented a government that wants to fight a culture war. Sunak has spent his first year trying to hold both wings together, and the reshuffle was an attempt to put the more respectable face forward without losing the argumentative base. Within hours, supporters of Braverman were describing the move as a betrayal, and supporters of Cameron were describing it as a rescue.

Cameron's return also carries a record. His government imposed austerity after 2010, called the referendum that ended his premiership, and left behind a foreign policy that had been built around membership in the European Union. He has defended his decisions in interviews and in his book, and he arrives at a department that is smaller in ambition than the one he left. Colleagues have said he is a good negotiator and a poor campaigner, and the job he has taken is mostly the former.

The timing matters for both conflicts. British policy towards Ukraine has been among the most forward-leaning in Europe, and policy towards Israel and Gaza has been constrained by domestic politics and by the need to keep a coalition of allies together. A foreign secretary who knows the American administration and the European capitals personally is an asset in that work, and one who has been out of office for seven years is a risk in a department that runs on relationships. The answer will arrive in the next few months, in a series of summits and votes.
