Rocks older than 3.9 billion years survive in a handful of places, and the Acasta Gneiss Complex in Canada has supplied almost all of the evidence about Earth's first 500 million years. When plate tectonics began is a separate question, and one that the Acasta rocks have been read to answer in both directions, as evidence for a stagnant lid and for early subduction. A new locality therefore matters more than its size.

Xie, Dong and Wan describe a trondhjemitic gneiss from eastern Hebei, in the North China Craton, in *Nature*, 11 November 2026. Three samples were dated by sensitive high-resolution ion microprobe uranium-lead analysis on zircon, with hafnium and oxygen isotopes measured on the same grains; the least altered crystals, those with less than 20 per cent discordance, define the age. Conditions of melting were reconstructed by thermodynamic modelling of an amphibolite source.

The weighted mean age of the clearest population is 3,951 ± 3 million years. Magmatic zircon carries oxygen-18 averaging 6.77 ± 0.23 per mille above the standard, and the hafnium isotope term has a weighted mean of 1.45 ± 0.27. Modelling places the parent melt at 8 to 16 per cent partial melting of basaltic crust, above 1.6 GPa and between 740 and 820 degrees Celsius. A second zircon group near 3.45 billion years records later overgrowth.

Heavy oxygen marks a source rock that had exchanged with low-temperature water, so the basalt had been altered at or near the surface before it melted; zircon above 5.5 per mille is conventionally read as carrying a supracrustal component. Positive hafnium values mark a mantle already depleted in the Hadean, which dates the extraction of the protolith. Pressure separates this rock from Acasta, where the roughly 4.0-billion-year-old Idiwhaa unit formed at low pressure; the Hebei gneiss points to burial to more than 1.6 GPa along a cool thermal gradient.

Transient subduction is an inference from a pressure-temperature path, and no structural or petrological witness of a descending slab accompanies it. Three samples from separate outcrops of the same gneiss body carry the claim, and part of the zircon population lost lead or grew rims, so the age rests on the least altered crystals. One outcrop cannot show how widespread the burial was, and the Hadean depletion is read through a single isotope system in a single mineral.

Further localities older than 3.9 billion years would test whether burial at that age was regional or isolated. Independent thermobarometry on other minerals would check the pressure estimate, which at present comes from modelling rather than from a coexisting assemblage; the starting composition for that model is an Acasta amphibolite, chosen because it is the only mafic rock of comparable age available. If other terrains yield the same oxygen and hafnium signature, the case becomes one about a process rather than about a single outcrop, and a shared convention for reporting zircon discordance would let ages be compared between laboratories.
