Vaccination is a procedure with a time and a date, and the field has treated both as administrative details. A trial reported this month found that a morning dose of an influenza vaccine produced a 1.6-fold higher antibody titre at one month than an afternoon dose, and the mechanism is the body's own daily rhythm.

The trial enrolled 280 adults over 65 and randomized them to a 9 a.m. or a 3 p.m. appointment. Antibody titres against the three strains were measured before and at one and three months. Morning appointments produced a higher geometric mean titre for two of the three strains, and the difference was largest for the strain with the weakest baseline immunity. At three months the gap narrowed but did not close.

Glucocorticoid rhythm is the mechanism. Cortisol peaks in the morning, and a rise in glucocorticoids at the time of antigen exposure shifts the response towards a humoral one. The trial measured salivary cortisol at each appointment and found the expected curve. A clinic's appointment book is the intervention, and a change of two hours in a booking template is a change that costs nothing to test at scale.

Practicality is the obvious objection. A clinic's appointment schedule is set by staffing, and a morning-only policy would halve its throughput. The trial's authors propose a morning preference rather than a requirement, which is what a health system can absorb. Other timing effects do not generalize the same way. A measles vaccine's response does not show the same time dependence in a smaller study, and the difference may be the adjuvant or the age of the participants. One effect does not generalize to a schedule.

Limits are the sample and the season. A single season's influenza vaccine varies in its own immunogenicity, and 280 participants is small for a subgroup analysis. The trial has been extended for a second season with 600 participants. The effect size is modest and the mechanism is plausible. A glucocorticoid's diurnal peak suppresses inflammatory signalling in the morning, and a vaccine's adjuvant works partly through that signalling. A timing effect of 1.6-fold is in the range that a scheduling change could capture at no cost.

Who benefits is the practical question. An older adult's immune response is slower and a 1.6-fold difference may matter; a younger adult's may not. A clinic that moves its appointments to the morning spends nothing and gains an effect that a trial measured in one age group. A timing effect is cheap to adopt and hard to prove. A clinic that offers morning appointments changes a rota and not a vaccine, and a health system that wants the effect confirmed needs a trial at a scale that no manufacturer will fund for a product already licensed.

Vaccine performance belongs to an encounter rather than to a product alone. The time of day is a variable a clinic controls and a trial can measure, and it has been unexamined for decades.
