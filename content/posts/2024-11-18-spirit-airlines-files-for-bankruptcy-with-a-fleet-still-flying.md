Spirit Airlines filed for chapter 11 protection on 18 November, a step that follows a failed attempt to merge with a larger rival and a decade in which its costs rose while its fares did not. The airline continues to operate, its schedules are unchanged for now, and the filing begins a process in which creditors, lessors and bondholders negotiate over an airline that leases most of its aircraft. A filing that keeps the aircraft flying is a filing that buys time from the people who own them.

Model that made it successful stopped working. A carrier that offers a low fare and charges for everything else depends on a cost base that is lower than its competitors, and that base was built on wages and on aircraft that were bought cheaply. When the industry's pay scales rose during the recovery, the gap closed. A cost base that depends on a wage scale is a cost base that rises when the industry raises its own. A carrier that charges for a cabin bag is a carrier whose fare looks lower than it is.

Merger was the exit that did not happen. A combination with a larger carrier was blocked by a judge who accepted the argument that it would remove a competitor that kept fares down. A carrier that is the price discipline for an entire market is a carrier that the regulator will not let disappear quietly, which is the paradox at the centre of this case. A judge who blocked a merger to protect competition is a judge who has now left the market to restructure alone.

Leasing is what keeps the airline alive. Most of the fleet is leased, and a lessor that takes aircraft back loses the rent, so the lessors have an interest in a restructuring that keeps the airline flying. The negotiations are therefore about rent reductions and about which aircraft stay in the fleet, rather than about liquidation. A lessor that accepts a lower rent keeps an aircraft in service and a customer in place.

Passengers are protected in the short term and exposed in the long one. A ticket on a carrier in bankruptcy is usually honoured, because the airline needs the revenue, and a route that is cut is a route that a passenger discovers at short notice. The cities that depend on the low fare have no substitute. A city that loses a route loses a connection that its airport had used to attract business.

What the filing shows is that the ultra-low-cost model has a narrow margin for error. A carrier that is a few dollars per seat more expensive than it planned is a carrier that cannot cover its leases, and the discipline that made it profitable is also what leaves it without a cushion. A cushion of a few dollars a seat is a cushion that one fuel spike removes.
