Sheikh Hasina resigned as prime minister of Bangladesh on 5 August and left the country by helicopter after a morning of protests that had reached the gates of her residence. The army chief announced that an interim government would be formed, and a Nobel laureate economist was sworn in as its head within three days. The protests had begun with a demand about jobs and had become a demand about the government, which is the trajectory that a quota dispute takes when it is met with force.

Trigger was the quota dispute that had begun on the campuses in July. The government had answered the protests with curfews, a communications shutdown and arrests, and the death toll over the month reached several hundred. Repression that produces more funerals is repression that builds a larger crowd. The chief who announced the transition had been appointed by the prime minister, and the fact that he acted against her is the signal that the institutions had changed sides.

Army's decision was the decisive event. A force that had been deployed against protesters refused to fire on them in the final days, and a chief who announces a transition rather than a crackdown has chosen the side that he believes will survive. Armies rarely decide the outcome and they decide the timing. The reserves had fallen through the year as the central bank defended a managed rate, and the negotiations with the lender of last resort had already begun before the government fell.

Economy was the reason the crowd was so large. Growth had been strong and the benefits had gone to a small group, and the reserves that the central bank held had been spent defending a currency peg. A country that has used its reserves to support an exchange rate is a country that has less room when the politics turns. The police had been the instrument of the crackdown and had then disappeared from the streets, and the new government had to rebuild a force that the public did not trust.

Interim government inherited a police force that had dissolved, a bureaucracy that had been purged of the party's opponents and an economy that needed an immediate infusion of foreign exchange. A caretaker administration is an administration without a mandate, and it has to negotiate with the institutions that served the old government. The economy needs dollars for fuel and for food, and the interim administration's first calls were to the institutions that can provide them.

What the transition shows is that a long-serving leader can be removed in an afternoon when the security forces decline to protect her. The institutions that remain are the ones the next government has to work with, and the reckoning with the past will be decided by how those institutions behave. The party that had governed for fifteen years remains organized, and whether it is allowed to compete in the next election will decide whether the transition is a beginning or an interval.
