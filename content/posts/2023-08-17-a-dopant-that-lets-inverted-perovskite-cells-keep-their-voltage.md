An inverted perovskite cell puts the hole-selective contact at the bottom, and the architecture is the one that tandems need. Its weakness is the interface between the perovskite and the transport layer, where defects cost voltage.

The dopant in *Nature*, 17 August 2023 addresses the interface. The authors use a dimethylacridine derivative to modify the perovskite's surface and the adjacent transport layer, and the cell's open-circuit voltage rises.

The chemistry is the mechanism. A molecule with a Lewis-basic nitrogen can coordinate an under-coordinated lead ion, and a molecule with an aromatic core can align with the perovskite's surface.

The doping is not a bulk addition. The molecule sits at the interface, where it passivates a defect without changing the absorber's own electronic structure.

The voltage is the metric. A cell's efficiency is the product of its current, its voltage and its fill factor, and a defect at the interface costs the voltage term most.

The stability is the second claim. A passivated interface resists ion migration, and the authors' test at elevated temperature shows a slower decline.

The comparison with the conventional architecture is the frame. The inverted cell's contacts are different, and a dopant that works for one may not work for the other.

The scale is the limitation. A laboratory cell is small, and a module's interconnects and its lamination add failure modes that a cell test does not see.

What the paper does not establish is a field lifetime. A stability test in a chamber is an accelerated approximation, and a roof's twenty-five years is the target.

For photovoltaics, the value is a molecule that does a specific job. The field's passivants have often been discovered by screening, and a dopant designed around a defect is a rational step.

The Lewis base is the functional group. A lone pair on nitrogen binds a metal ion that would otherwise trap a charge, and the binding is the passivation.

The aromatic core is the second function. A conjugated molecule can stack against the perovskite's surface, and the stacking organizes the interface rather than leaving it disordered.

The transport layer's doping is a separate effect. A dopant that also improves the hole-selective layer's conductivity contributes to the fill factor, and the authors separate the two contributions.

The energy alignment is the constraint. A passivating molecule shifts the surface's energy levels, and a shift in the wrong direction blocks extraction.

The tandem's requirement is the motivation. A top cell that must be processed on silicon needs a low-temperature route, and an interfacial treatment is easier to integrate than a bulk change.

The reproducibility is the practical issue. A molecule that must be deposited as a monolayer is sensitive to concentration, and the authors' window is part of the result.
The interface's defect density is the measured quantity. A trap that sits in the perovskite's surface gap captures a carrier and releases it later, and the release is a loss that a voltage measurement perceives as a deficit.
