An inverted perovskite cell puts the hole-selective contact at the bottom, and that architecture is the one tandems need. Its weakness sits at the interface between perovskite and transport layer, where defects cost voltage. In *Nature* on 17 August 2023, the interface is treated. The authors use a dimethylacridine derivative to modify the perovskite's surface and the adjacent transport layer, and the cell's open-circuit voltage rises.

Chemistry supplies the mechanism. A molecule with a Lewis-basic nitrogen coordinates an under-coordinated lead ion, and a molecule with an aromatic core aligns with the perovskite's surface. The lone pair on nitrogen binds a metal ion that would otherwise trap a charge, and that binding is the passivation. Doping stays interfacial. The molecule sits at the surface, where it passivates a defect without changing the absorber's own electronic structure, and a conjugated core stacks against the surface so the interface is organised rather than disordered.

Voltage is the metric. Efficiency is the product of current, voltage and fill factor, and a defect at the interface costs the voltage term most; defect density is the measured quantity, because a trap in the surface gap captures a carrier and releases it later, and the release reads as a voltage deficit.

Stability is the second claim. A passivated interface resists ion migration, and the test at elevated temperature shows a slower decline. Transport-layer doping is a separate effect, because a molecule also improving the hole-selective layer's conductivity contributes to the fill factor, and the authors separate the two contributions.

Comparison with the conventional architecture frames it. The inverted cell's contacts differ, and a dopant working for one may not work for the other. Energy alignment constrains the design, since a passivating molecule shifts surface levels, and a shift in the wrong direction blocks extraction.

Scale sets the limitation. A laboratory cell is small, and a module's interconnects and lamination add failure modes a cell test does not see. Reproducibility is the practical issue, because a molecule deposited as a monolayer is sensitive to concentration, and the reported window is part of the result.

Field lifetime remains unestablished, because a chamber test is an accelerated approximation and twenty-five years on a roof is the target. Photovoltaics gains a molecule doing a specific job, since passivants have mostly been found by screening and a dopant designed around a defect is a rational step. Tandems supply the motivation, because a top cell processed on silicon needs a low-temperature route, and an interfacial treatment integrates more easily than a bulk change. A shelf test at module scale would show whether the passivation survives lamination, since the treatment is a monolayer and a laminated stack is where a monolayer is most easily disturbed, and that test is what separates a cell result from a product claim. A comparison with the conventional architecture under the same protocol would show whether the gain is specific to the inverted stack, and a negative result there would still be informative about where the loss was coming from.
