An optical nanofiber confines light to a waist thinner than a wavelength, and an atom held near the waist sees a field that varies sharply across its own size. The strong gradient is what makes such a fibre useful for coupling atoms to guided modes, and it is also what makes the coupling depend on details that a plane wave would not resolve. An atom a hundred nanometres from the waist sits in a field whose amplitude varies by a large factor across the atomic wavefunction.

Electric dipole transitions dominate the usual treatment. A quadrupole transition couples through the gradient of the field instead of its value, so the geometry of the mode matters twice over, and the paper that follows takes that geometry seriously. The gradient of a guided mode is not simply a scaled copy of its amplitude, and its phase varies along the fibre, so the two directions of propagation are not related by a simple sign.

Work posted to arXiv on 11 November 2022 computes the coupling for an atom on the transverse axis of the fibre, with the quantization axis along the other transverse direction and the field in a quasilinearly polarized fundamental mode. The atomic levels are magnetic sublevels of hyperfine states, so the transition is labelled by the difference of the sublevel quantum numbers.

A directional dependence is the result. For two of the combinations, the absolute value of the quadrupole Rabi frequency depends on whether the light propagates along the fibre or against it, and the dependence is absent for the others. The paper lists which sublevel differences produce which pattern, so a reader can look up the case of interest. The pattern is set by how the mode's polarization and its gradient transform under a reversal of the propagation direction.

Spontaneous emission inherits the effect. An excited atom emits into the guided modes as well as into free space, and since the coupling to a guided mode is directional, the emission into that mode is directional too. An atom prepared in a given sublevel therefore acts as a one-way source, which is the kind of element a chiral network needs. Directionality of that kind is what allows a photon to be routed without a circulator, and a circulator is the component that fails first in an integrated photonic circuit because it needs a magneto-optic material that does not integrate well.

The paper does not treat a real fibre with a finite waist length or a dielectric surface close enough to shift the levels, and the shift is the complication that experiments meet first. Nor does it consider motion of the atom, which in a trap is a further source of averaging, since the atom samples the gradient over a range of positions and the asymmetry is diluted.

A measurement of the emission asymmetry for a single trapped atom is the test the result invites, and the parameters in the paper are the ones such an experiment would use.
