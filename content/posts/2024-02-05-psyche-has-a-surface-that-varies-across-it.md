One object in the main belt is treated as a fossil. that planetary scientists treat as a fossil: 16 Psyche, the largest of the M-type asteroids, a body long read as the stripped metallic core of a differentiated planetesimal and the parent of the iron meteorites. A NASA mission of the same name is on its way to look at it. Until the spacecraft arrives, the surface has to be inferred from remote sensing, and a preprint posted to arXiv on 5 February 2024 argues that the inference has been too uniform.

Work began from Atacama Large Millimeter Array data analysed by de Kleer, Cambioni and Shepard in 2021, which yielded a single global thermal inertia and dielectric constant. Those two numbers are proxies: thermal inertia speaks to regolith grain size, porosity and consolidation, while the dielectric constant speaks to metal content against silicate. The 2021 work also noted thermal anomalies that surface albedo alone could not explain. The new analysis refits the same data set with both parameters free to vary across the surface.

A fit with two free parameters across a resolved disc can always absorb structure, and the authors acknowledge the trade-off. What they report is the pattern that survives the fitting, not a map with sharp edges.

The fit returns a surface that is heterogeneous in both properties, and the two do not track each other. Patches with a high dielectric constant, which the authors read as a higher ratio of metal to silicate, are not the patches with high thermal inertia. The lack of correlation is the interesting result, because a simple picture in which exposed metal is bare and silicate regions are dusty would have produced one.

Topography adds a second pattern. The lowlands inside a large depression carry distinctly lower thermal inertia than the surrounding highlands, which points to a thin cover of fine regolith, fractured bedrock or implanted silicate-rich material lying over a metal-rich substrate. Each of those explanations implies a surface worked over by impacts, and all of them describe a body that has been collisionally processed rather than a core that was merely exposed.

The spacecraft is due to arrive in 2029, and its instruments will test the patchiness directly rather than through a fit. For the mission, the practical consequence is that a lander or an orbiter cannot treat Psyche as one material. Radar and thermal measurements will need to be read patch by patch, and a sample from one site says less about the whole body than it would on a homogeneous target. Radar and gravity data from the spacecraft will test whether the lowland cover is regolith or bedrock, and that is the measurement the fit cannot supply. Origin is the question this paper cannot settle either. A surface that mixes metal and silicate at small scales, with a cover that varies from patch to patch, could be a core with debris on it, or an object that never fully separated, and the distinction waits for the spacecraft.
