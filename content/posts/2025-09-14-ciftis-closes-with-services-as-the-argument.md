Trading at Shougang Park in western Beijing finished on 14 September, when the 2025 China International Fair for Trade in Services closed after five days. This was the first edition held at a single site, the old steelworks having been converted over several years into a campus of exhibition halls, and the arrangement suits a fair that has always been part trade, part conference and part demonstration of intent.

Figures released at the close were: nearly 2,000 firms exhibiting on site and about 5,600 online, more than 250,000 visits, 85 countries and international organizations taking part, and over 900 outcomes recorded across construction, information technology and finance. Thirteen thematic forums and 81 specialist ones ran alongside, with some 260 speakers drawn from international organizations, ministries, companies and universities, and 113 institutions used the week to release 198 products or reports.

What the fair is designed to show is a shift in the composition of Chinese trade. Goods trade has plateaued as a share of the total; services have not. In the first seven months of the year, services imports and exports came to 4.58 trillion yuan, up 8.2 per cent, with travel services the fastest-growing category at 62.9 per cent and knowledge-intensive services up 6.8 per cent. Those are the numbers the exhibitors were arranged to illustrate, and the ministry that publishes them repeats them at every such gathering.

Exhibits on the floor were heavy with the things that now count as services: software, logistics platforms, medical devices with a service contract attached, education products, financial tools, and a great deal of robotics doing the work of demonstration. A humanoid that pours tea is a service product in the sense the fair cares about, because the sale is the deployment and the maintenance rather than the machine alone.

Whether any of this survives contact with the market is the question a trade fair cannot answer. Deals signed at exhibitions have a way of being announcements rather than contracts, and the 900 outcomes include memoranda and intentions as well as orders. Presence of 480-odd Fortune 500 firms and industry leaders makes the floor look serious, and does not by itself prove that the services on display have buyers.

Direction of policy, by contrast, is not in doubt. The fair is the annual place where the direction is stated: China intends to sell services, and to sell them to the countries whose delegations were given the largest stands. A domestic audience of 116,000 trade visitors, up twelve per cent on last year, is the more meaningful number for exhibitors, because those are the people who sign, and because a leisure crowd does not return on the second day.

The next edition will be judged on smaller things. Which products returned, which exhibitors came back with contracts, and whether the foreign share of the floor held. A fair's second act is always less impressive than its first, and rather more informative than the opening one.
