Nervous systems are built from neurons, and neurons are cells that talk across gaps. The synapse is the textbook's unit, and the textbook's first chapter is a single cell that became specialised for signalling.

In *Science* on 21 April 2023, that chapter is complicated. The authors describe the nerve net of a ctenophore, a marine invertebrate, and find that its neurons fuse into a syncytial network in which cytoplasm is continuous, so signals travel through connected cells rather than across synapses.

Because ctenophores are among the earliest branching animals with neurons, the animal's position gives the finding its weight, and a syncytial net at that point is a different starting condition for the ancestral state. The comparison with cnidarians sharpens the argument: they have conventional neurons with synapses, and a ctenophore with a syncytium suggests the two designs diverged early, with neither obviously primitive.

Function is the open question, since a continuous cytoplasm can carry a signal faster and with less precision than a chain of synapses, and that trade-off is one an evolutionary account has to explain. Development supplies the route, because a syncytium forms when cells fuse or when divisions leave the cytoplasm connected, and developmental observations identify which route this animal takes. Anatomy carries the proof, because electron microscopy resolves membranes and a region without membranes between nuclei is a syncytium rather than a cluster of cells, while serial sections make the claim anatomical instead of inferred.

Fixing the tissue risks fixing an artefact, since a syncytium can appear when a preparation collapses cells together, and live imaging plus several techniques are what rule that out. How far the fusion extends is the remaining uncertainty: a net with some fused regions and some separate cells would be a hybrid, and the quantification of the proportion is the number that matters. Conduction measured with electrodes is the next step, because the speed and the spread of a signal distinguish continuous cytoplasm from a chain of cells.

A neuron is defined by function rather than by boundary, and an animal whose neurons have no boundary argues that cell theory's neatness is a convenience, which is the paper's most careful claim. Behaviour stays speculative, since a net carrying signals through cytoplasm may support a simpler repertoire and the animal's movements are where a functional account would start. The genome deepens the puzzle, because it carries genes for synaptic proteins, and an animal with a syncytial net and synaptic genes has machinery that exceeds its anatomy. A field that assumes a single architecture misreads an animal built on another, and this case widens the space of possible nervous systems.

How a syncytium regenerates is another open question, because a net without cell boundaries may repair a wound differently from one built of separate cells, and the capacity to recover from injury is where the anatomy becomes a physiology rather than a description.
