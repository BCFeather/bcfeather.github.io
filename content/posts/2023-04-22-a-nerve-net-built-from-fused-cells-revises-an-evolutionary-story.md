Nervous systems are built from neurons, and neurons are cells that talk across gaps. The synapse is the unit of the textbook account, and the account's first chapter is a single cell that became specialized for signalling.

The anatomy in *Science*, 21 April 2023 complicates that chapter. The authors describe the nerve net of a ctenophore, a marine invertebrate, and find that its neurons fuse into a syncytial network in which cytoplasm is continuous and signals travel through connected cells rather than across synapses.

The finding's weight is the animal's position. Ctenophores are among the earliest branching animals with neurons, and their nervous system's organization bears on what the ancestral condition was. A syncytial net at that position is a different starting point.

The comparison with cnidarians is the argument. Cnidarians have conventional neurons with synapses, and a ctenophore with a syncytium suggests that the two designs diverged early and that neither is obviously primitive.

The functional question is how a syncytium computes. A continuous cytoplasm can carry a signal faster and with less precision than a chain of synapses, and the trade-off is the kind that an evolutionary account has to explain.

The development is the mechanism's route. A syncytium forms when cells fuse or when divisions leave the cytoplasm connected, and the paper's developmental observations identify the route in this animal.

The implication for the neuron's origin is the paper's most careful claim. A neuron is defined by its function and not by its boundary, and an animal whose neurons have no boundary is an argument that the cell theory's neatness is a convenience.

The caveat is the preparation. A syncytium can be an artefact of fixation, and the authors' use of live imaging and of multiple techniques is what rules that out.

What the paper does not establish is the extent of the fusion. A net with some fused regions and some separate cells would be a hybrid, and the paper's quantification of the proportion is the number that matters.

For evolutionary neuroscience, the value is a case that widens the space of possible nervous systems. A field that assumes a single architecture is a field that misreads an animal built on another.

The imaging is the evidence. Electron microscopy resolves membranes, and a region without membranes between nuclei is a syncytium rather than a cluster of cells. The authors' serial sections are what make the claim anatomical rather than inferred.

The behavioural correlate is speculative. A net that carries signals through cytoplasm may support a simpler repertoire, and the animal's movements are the observation that a functional account would start from.

The molecular signature is suggestive. The animal's genome carries genes for synaptic proteins, and an animal with a syncytial net and synaptic genes is an animal whose machinery exceeds its anatomy, which is the puzzle the paper leaves open.
The functional assay is the next step. A syncytium's conduction can be measured with electrodes, and the speed and the spread of a signal along the net are what distinguish a continuous cytoplasm from a chain of cells.
