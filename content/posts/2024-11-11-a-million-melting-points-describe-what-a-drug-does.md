A drug's mechanism is usually established one protein at a time, and a paper in *eLife* on 11 November 2024 approaches it a million measurements at a time. The method is a proteome-wide thermal shift assay, which detects the proteins a compound stabilizes or destabilizes. Thermal stability is an indirect readout, and indirectness is what makes a proteome-scale assay possible, since binding itself cannot be measured at that scale. Proteome-wide assays trade depth for breadth, and breadth is what a discovery programme needs when nothing is known.

The principle is that a protein bound by a ligand melts at a different temperature from one that is not. A shift is therefore evidence of engagement, and the assay reports it for thousands of proteins in parallel. A shift can mean binding, a conformational change or a downstream effect, so the assay reports candidate interactions rather than established ones.

More than a million measurements were collected across compound treatments, a scale that earlier versions of the assay could not reach. Efficiency is what converts a useful method into a screening tool. Abundance bias means the map is densest where detection is easiest, and a reader who wants a target list should know where the map is thin.

The output is a map rather than a hit. A compound that engages one protein directly and three others indirectly produces a pattern, and the pattern is what a mechanistic hypothesis has to explain. Similarity to a known compound is an inference a medicinal chemist can test with one experiment, which is why the profile is useful before any structure exists. A pattern is a hypothesis about a network, and a network hypothesis is what the assay is best at generating.

For the same reason, the assay does not report binding affinities, and a shift can arise from an indirect effect. Specificity is inferred rather than measured, so any flagged protein needs a follow-up. A million measurements match a decade of structural biology, and the comparison explains why the approach attracted attention so quickly.

In the clinic, the value for discovery is early triage. A compound whose thermal profile resembles that of a compound with a known mechanism is a candidate for the same mechanism, and that inference is cheaper than a structure. Negative results end a line early, and ending a line early is a saving that no follow-up study can recover once a programme has committed. Triage is the stage where most compounds fail, so a cheap assay that fails them earlier is worth more than a precise one that fails them later.

On the modelling side, the limits are those of any proteome-scale method, since abundant proteins are detected and rare ones are missed, and a negative result is informative because it says the compound engaged nothing detectable. The method's natural companion is a genetic screen, since a thermal shift tells which protein and a knockout tells whether that protein matters.
