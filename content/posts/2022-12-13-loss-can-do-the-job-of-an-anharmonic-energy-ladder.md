description: "A cavity whose losses depend on the photon number emits single photons without relying on an anharmonic spectrum, which offers high purity at high repetition rates."
---

A single-photon source has to deliver one photon and then stop, and the standard way to make it stop is to make the second photon expensive. An anharmonic ladder does that: after one photon is absorbed the next transition is off resonance, so a second photon cannot follow, and the emitter has time to reset. The ladder has to be anharmonic by more than the linewidth, otherwise the two-photon transition stays in resonance and the source emits pairs.

Placement of the mechanism is usually in the energy levels, where an anharmonic ladder keeps the two-photon transition out of reach. Work posted to arXiv on 13 December 2022 places it in the losses instead, and the shift is the idea the paper is built on. If the decay rate of the cavity depends on how many photons it holds, then a state with two photons can be made to decay faster than a state with one, and the excess population is removed rather than prevented. A decay rate that depends on photon number is a property of the cavity rather than of the emitter, and that is why the mechanism can be transplanted.

The authors call this non-Hermitian anharmonicity, and they demonstrate it in two kinds of system. One is a hybrid metallodielectric cavity weakly coupled to a two-level emitter, a design chosen because the metal supports losses that can be engineered while the dielectric keeps the mode confined. Weak coupling keeps the emitter from being absorbed by the metal, and the design balances the two requirements against each other.

The other demonstration is theoretical, and the pair is what makes the claim general. A mechanism that works in two settings is a mechanism rather than a coincidence of one platform, and the paper argues that the principle survives changes of the cavity. A principle that survives a change of platform is what makes a proposal worth testing, and the second example is there to show that it does.

Purity and rate are the two figures the paper reports. Purity means that the emitted field has one photon at a time rather than a mixture with two, and rate means how often the source can fire. The two usually trade against each other, and the claim is that a loss-based mechanism can deliver both at once. The paper reports both numbers for its two systems, and the comparison between them is the part that a device engineer would read first.

What the paper does not do is treat the emitter's own decoherence, which in a solid-state setting competes with the engineered loss and can degrade purity. A reader should also note that the weak-coupling regime is the one analysed, so a strongly coupled cavity is outside the treatment. A strongly coupled cavity would shift the levels as well, and the analysis does not include that shift, so the two mechanisms would compete.

A device that runs the scheme at a repetition rate set by the loss, with the emitter driven continuously, is the experiment the result invites, and the parameters the authors give are those such a device would start from. Whether the scheme survives a real emitter is the question that separates a proposal from a source, and the paper's parameters are where a test would begin.
