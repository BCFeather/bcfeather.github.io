title: "Disney sues the governor who took its district"
date: "2023-04-26T23:40:00+08:00"

description: "Disney filed a federal lawsuit against Florida's governor and a newly appointed board on 26 April 2023, arguing that the state had punished the company for its speech by dissolving the special district that had governed its resort for fifty-five years."
---

Disney sued Governor Ron DeSantis in federal court in Tallahassee on 26 April, alleging that the state had retaliated against the company for criticizing a law that restricted classroom discussion of gender and sexuality. The suit names the governor, the newly created district board and its members, and it asks a judge to set aside arrangements that had been in place for half a century. Disney argued that the retaliation took the form of dissolving the Reedy Creek Improvement District and replacing it with a board the governor appoints.

The sequence began in March 2022, when the company said publicly that it would pause political donations in Florida and that it opposed the law its critics call Don't Say Gay. The legislature responded within a month by passing a bill to dissolve the district, which had allowed Disney to run its own fire service, building code and bond issues across 25,000 acres. The dissolution was signed in April 2022 and took effect in June 2023, after the company and the state had spent a year arguing about the terms.

The legal claim rests on the First Amendment. The complaint argues that the state's actions were motivated by the company's speech, that they singled out one business for punishment, and that the governor's public statements make the motive easy to establish. It cites the governor's remark that the company had got itself into a fight it could not win, and it notes that no other business in the state faces the same treatment. It also argues that the dissolution was passed without a study of its effects, and that the state's own lawyers had warned that the measure would be vulnerable.

For the state, the district was a privilege rather than a right. The governor's office said that a corporation should not have a government of its own and that the legislature was entitled to reorganize a special district it had created. The new board, appointed in early 2023, discovered that the outgoing supervisors had signed agreements that stripped it of most of its powers, and it voted to void them, which produced a second lawsuit in state court.

Business consequences are already visible. The district's bonds trade at a spread that reflects the dispute, the company's development plans in Florida have been put on hold, and the governor's campaign has used the fight to raise money nationally. Disney employs about 75,000 people in the state, and its resort is the largest single taxpayer in the counties around it.

Now the case is a test of how far a state may go in punishing a company for what it says. The company has not asked for damages, and it has asked for the arrangements to be restored and for the state to be barred from further retaliation. A judge will decide whether the motive is as plain as the complaint says, and the answer will matter to every business that has ever taken a public position in a state that disagreed.
