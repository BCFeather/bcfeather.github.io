Guidelines recommend a target, and guidelines are written from trials that tested a drug rather than a number. The distinction matters because a trial of a statin against placebo shows that lowering helps without showing where to stop, and the trials that have pushed LDL very low have mostly been done in populations whose baseline risk was high. A trial that randomises two targets is the only design that answers the question a clinician asks at the moment of prescribing.

This one was run in South Korea as an open-label superiority trial, randomising 3,048 patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease to a target below 55 milligrams per decilitre or a target below 70. Treatment was intensified with whatever agents were needed to reach the assigned target, and the primary endpoint was a composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, any revascularisation or hospitalisation for unstable angina at three years. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 28 March.

Achieving the targets proved feasible. The median LDL cholesterol during the trial was 56 milligrams per decilitre in the intensive group and 66 in the conventional group, which means the separation was real but narrower than the targets imply. A primary endpoint event occurred in 100 patients in the intensive group, a Kaplan-Meier estimate of 6.6 per cent, against 147 patients in the conventional group.

The gap between assigned and achieved targets is instructive. A target below 55 produced a median of 56, and a target below 70 produced a median of 66, so the achieved difference was about ten milligrams and the trial still found a difference in events. That suggests the benefit is continuous and not threshold-shaped, which is consistent with the epidemiology and inconsistent with the way targets are argued about.

Open-label design is the limitation that matters most. Patients and investigators knew the assignment, which affects the decision to revascularise and the decision to admit for unstable angina, and two of the five components of the composite are decisions and not events. The hard components, death and infarction, are less susceptible to that bias and the trial reports them separately, which allows a reader to weigh the composite against them.

Generalising to other populations is the second limitation. The trial was conducted in Korea in a single health system, with a population whose baseline LDL and drug tolerability differ from those of European or North American cohorts, and the result is best read as support for intensive treatment in patients who can reach the target, and not as a mandate for a specific number everywhere.

The practical consequence is that the argument about targets is now supported by a randomised comparison and not by extrapolation. That is a small addition to the evidence base and a large one to the confidence of a clinician who has been asked, for twenty years, whether lower is better and has had to answer with an extrapolation.
