Plastic debris reaches habitats that have no people in them, carried by wind, water and clothing, and what that means for terrestrial animals has been hard to establish. Most monitoring counts particles in soil, water or scat, which measures exposure rather than what an animal has taken up. Blood is the compartment where systemic exposure becomes visible, and until now it had been sampled in people rather than in wildlife.

Ferraz, Desbiez and Noonan sampled lowland tapirs and giant anteaters at three Brazilian sites between February 2021 and November 2022, as reported in *Nature*, 11 November 2026. The sites run from the Arc of Deforestation in the Amazon through an agricultural mosaic in the Cerrado to a cattle ranch in the Pantanal. Blood was analysed by confocal Raman spectroscopy for particles above one micrometre, with identification requiring a hit quality index above 75, and each animal's movements were tracked by GPS so that exposure could be matched to habitat use. Forty-two of the 47 animals yielded enough tracking data for the habitat models; the rest stayed in the descriptive counts.

Across 26 tapirs and 21 anteaters, the team imaged 17,165 particles above the quality threshold and identified 2,815 as plastic, 16.4 per cent of the total. Concentrations averaged 73.8 particles per millilitre and ranged from none to 1,211.3. Tapirs carried the heavier burden at 98.9 particles per millilitre, with a 95 per cent confidence interval of 52.0 to 188.0; anteaters averaged 42.8, with an interval of 26.7 to 68.6; the difference between species did not reach significance at *P* = 0.067. Four animals had no detectable particles at all.

Human footprint index and distance to waste facilities both tracked the concentrations, which is what a landscape-level exposure story predicts. The Pantanal ranch, the least disturbed site, still produced detections: the model's prediction at a footprint index of zero was 10.5 particles per millilitre, with an interval of 4.6 to 24.2. Particles averaged 10.2 micrometres in length, small enough to cross lung and gut barriers, and the two species differed in polymer mix even where they shared a site.

Observational work of this kind carries limits the authors set out. Forty-seven animals at three sites in one country cannot describe terrestrial wildlife generally, and a concentration in blood is neither a tissue burden nor a harm. Blank controls contained particles, larger on average than those in the samples, so some contamination is possible even after correction. Species differ in size, diet and behaviour, and no comparable measurements exist for other land mammals.

Repeated sampling of the same individuals would show whether burdens rise and fall with movement, which a single sample per animal cannot. Tissue pathology would connect a concentration to an effect. Standardized Raman workflows would let wildlife values be read against the 1.9 to 108 particles per millilitre reported in human blood, which is where the comparison starts to carry weight.
