description: "American consumer prices rose 2.4 per cent in the year to May and core inflation stayed at 2.8 per cent, a fourth month in which the expected tariff effect failed to appear."
---

Four months of warnings about tariff-driven inflation have now produced four months in which the number did not move, and the May report kept the pattern. Headline consumer prices rose 2.4 per cent on the year and 0.1 per cent on the month, core prices rose 2.8 per cent on the year, the slowest since the spring of 2021, and core rose 0.1 per cent on the month.

Components show where the pressure is and is not. Shelter rose 0.3 per cent for a second month and remains the largest single contributor, while gasoline fell 2.6 per cent and did the work of holding the headline down. Core goods were 0.3 per cent higher on the year and core services 3.6 per cent, which is a services economy still cooling at the edges.

Tariff effect is late and not absent. Importers front-loaded inventories before the April escalation, and several of them absorbed the duty to keep a price point, so the pass-through that economists expect has been deferred into the summer. Used vehicles and apparel are the categories where a rebound is most likely and least welcome.

Travel is the offset that cannot last. Airfares and hotel rates have fallen at an annualised pace of about 21 per cent over three months, which is a discount that reflects softer demand and cheaper fuel, and it will stop contributing once the comparison base catches up. A core rate that is flat because travel is falling is a core rate that is not yet telling the truth, and the truth will arrive with the June and July prints.

The Federal Reserve's own measure is lower. The personal consumption expenditures index was estimated at 2.3 per cent for May with a core rate of 2.6 per cent, both below the consumer price index, which is why the committee can describe inflation as somewhat elevated and still expect it to fall. Two measures of the same economy differ by half a point for reasons of weights, scope and the treatment of medical care.

The politics of the number are simple. A president who wants lower rates can point to a fourth month of muted inflation and a softening labour market, and a committee that wants to wait can point to 2.8 per cent core and to a tariff that has not yet been paid for. Both readings come from the same release.

What would settle the summer is whether goods prices keep climbing through the third quarter. A core rate that stays at 2.8 into September would show that the pass-through was delayed; one that rises towards 3.2 would show that the delay was the point. A flat core print in a month when tariffs were in force is the single most useful fact the committee has received this year. What the release cannot settle is whether the muted number reflects a tariff too small to matter or a tariff that has been postponed by inventory.
