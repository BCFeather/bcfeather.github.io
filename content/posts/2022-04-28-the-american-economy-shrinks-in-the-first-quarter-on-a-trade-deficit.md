---

The Commerce Department estimated on 28 April that American output fell at an annual rate of 1.4 per cent in the first quarter, the first contraction since the second quarter of 2020 and a sharp reversal from growth of 6.9 per cent in the previous quarter. Economists had expected an increase of about 1.1 per cent, and the gap was almost entirely explained by two components that say little about domestic demand. The estimate was the first of three, and revisions in the following months would move it by several tenths of a point.

Imports rose faster than exports, and the trade deficit subtracted about 3.2 percentage points from the growth rate. Firms had rebuilt inventories aggressively in the second half of 2021 and slowed in the first quarter, which subtracted another point. Both effects are the arithmetic of a reopening economy: when demand shifts from goods to services, imports of goods and stock building stop adding to output. None of it suggests that households stopped spending, which is the part of the report that matters for the next quarter.

Detail underneath was more reassuring than the headline. Consumer spending rose, and the increase came from services such as travel, restaurants and health care rather than from goods, which is the rotation that the pandemic had delayed. Business investment in equipment rose at a double-digit rate, and spending on intellectual property held up, which is what firms do when they expect demand to continue. Residential construction weakened as mortgage rates climbed.

Inflation did the rest of the damage. The deflator for domestic purchases rose at an annual rate of about 7 per cent, so nominal spending grew while real output fell, which is the definition of a squeeze rather than a downturn. Households spent more and bought less, and the saving rate fell to its lowest level since 2008. Real disposable income fell for a fourth consecutive quarter, and households paid more for the same basket of goods than they had a year earlier.

The figure arrived in a labour market that was still adding jobs at a pace of hundreds of thousands a month, which is why most analysts read the quarter as a statistical artefact rather than the start of a recession. Two consecutive quarters of falling output is a common rule of thumb for a recession, and this quarter put the economy one step towards it, a fact that political campaigns would use repeatedly through the summer. First-quarter growth had also been flattered by a small import bill at the end of 2021, which made the starting point high.

Investors treated the report as confirmation that the Federal Reserve would move faster. The dollar rose to its highest level against a basket of currencies in five years, and futures priced a half-point increase at the May meeting with near certainty. Whether the tightening that follows can slow inflation without turning a statistical contraction into a real one became the central question of the year. Several Fed officials said publicly that the quarter would not change their plans, which was the cue markets took.
