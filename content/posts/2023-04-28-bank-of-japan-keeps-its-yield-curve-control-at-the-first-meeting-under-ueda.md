Japan's central bank left its policy unchanged at the first meeting under Kazuo Ueda on 28 April. The central bank kept the target for the ten-year yield at about zero and the cap at 0.5 per cent, and it kept the short-term rate at minus 0.1 per cent. The decision was unanimous, and the governor said the board had not discussed the timing of the exit. The bank also kept the forward guidance that it would continue with the purchases until the inflation settled at the target.

Its statement dropped the reference to the pandemic. The board removed the phrase about the effects of the virus on the activity and the prices, which is the first step towards the normal discussion of the policy. The change was small and the market read it as the signal of the eventual exit. The removal of the phrase was read as a technical change, and the statement kept the reference to the risks from the overseas economies. The previous governor had written the phrase in 2020, and the board has been trimming the emergency language since the autumn.

Forecasts were revised. The board raised the forecast for the core inflation to 1.8 per cent for the year to March 2024 from 1.6, and it lowered the one for the growth to 1.4 from 1.7. The core measure has been above the target for a year, and the wages are the variable that the governor said he would watch. The forecast for the core-core inflation was raised to 2.5 per cent for the year, and the one for the wages was left out of the report. The governor said the wage talks in the spring had been stronger than expected, and the effect would show in the services prices later in the year.

Currencies moved around the decision. The currency fell by about 1 per cent against the dollar after the statement as the traders had positioned for a tweak. The ten-year yield stayed below the cap, and the board said it would buy the bonds as needed. The currency has fallen by about 4 per cent against the dollar this year, and the imported inflation is the part the bank says will fade. The ten-year yield has been trading at about 0.45 per cent, and the bank bought about 3 trillion yen of the bonds in the quarter to hold it there.

Its governor said the policy would be reviewed at every meeting. He declined to say what would trigger the change, and he said the bank would look at the trend of the wages and the services prices rather than at the level of the headline. The board has kept the policy for seven years, and the governor is the first in that time to have been appointed from outside the bank's own staff. The deputy governors were chosen from the ministry and the bank, and the market reads the mix as a cautious one.

This meeting closes the chapter of the previous governor. A bank that keeps the cap at 0.5 per cent while the inflation runs at 3 per cent is a bank that is betting the price pressure will fade, and the bet is the one that the next meetings will test. The market expects a change in the summer, and the meeting in June is the next one that the traders will watch. A cap that is held while the inflation runs at 3 per cent is a cap that has become a subsidy to the borrowers, and the subsidy is expensive.
