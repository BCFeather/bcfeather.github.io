title: "Wrecks become reefs: what the war left on the seabed"
date: "2025-09-28T09:05:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A survey published this week finds that the metal hulls sunk in two world wars now carry biological communities comparable to natural rocky reefs, and that the older structures are the richer ones."
---

A great deal of metal was left on the seabed by the twentieth century, and a paper published this week in *Communications Earth and Environment* reports on what has grown on it. Researchers mapped and surveyed wrecks from the two world wars in the North Sea and the English Channel, comparing the biological communities on their hulls with those on natural rocky ground nearby, and found that the difference is smaller than expected and often runs in the wreck's favour.

Stated plainly, the mechanism is straightforward. A hull is hard substrate in a sea that is mostly mud and sand, and hard substrate is scarce in the southern North Sea. Anything that needs to attach, from barnacles to sponges to the juveniles of fish that prefer structure, will colonize whatever offers a surface. A wreck offers a large one, standing clear of the bottom, with cavities, decks and edges that produce a range of light and current conditions in a small area, and with vertical faces that a flat reef does not provide, which is why divers and fish find the same corners.

Age matters. The survey found that wrecks from the First World War, now a century old, carried more species and more mature communities than those sunk in 1939 to 1945, and that the difference was not simply a matter of size, since several of the older hulls are smaller than the newer ones, and one of the richest sites is a trawler rather than a warship. Corrosion opens the structure, and a hull that is collapsing provides more surfaces and more shelter than one that is still intact. The process takes decades, and the ships are now far enough into it to be interesting, with plating thin enough to admit light and divers both.

Fishing is the complication, and a heavy one. Wrecks attract fish, and fish attract trawlers, and the gear that snags on a wreck does damage that a hundred years of corrosion does not. The authors note that several of the sites surveyed are protected and that protection is unevenly enforced, which is a familiar problem in marine conservation and not a new one.

Policy questions follow from the biology. A wreck is a war grave for some countries and a dive site for others, a navigation hazard, a source of oil that may still be in the tanks, and now a habitat. Managing one object under four legal regimes is what marine spatial planning is supposed to do and mostly does not, and the survey is the kind of evidence that makes the argument concrete enough for a planning decision.

Nobody is proposing to sink more ships, and the authors say so plainly. The finding is more modest, and more useful: the sea remembers what was done to it, in forms that are measurable, and some of the unintended consequences have turned out to be habitats, which is a small consolation and a real one.
