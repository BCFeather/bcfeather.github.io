description: "Across forty-three growth trajectories from a field mouse to a grey whale, marine mammals show lower catabolism and a flatter scaling exponent, which a model ties to the metabolic cost of adipose tissue."
---

Body size in mammals is usually read through a scaling law: metabolism rises with mass to a power below one, and the constant in front of the power varies with lifestyle. The largest animals alive are marine, and their size has been explained by buoyancy, by heat conservation and by the cost of locomotion, which are arguments about physics rather than about cells.

A different account is offered in a preprint posted to arXiv on 4 October 2022, and it starts from growth curves. The authors assemble 43 ontogenetic trajectories spanning species from a 27-gram Taiwan field mouse to a grey whale of about twenty million grams, fit each with a growth model that separates catabolism from anabolism, and extract four parameters per species: the catabolic constant, the anabolic constant, a scaling exponent and the asymptotic mass.

Across that set, marine mammals show a minimized catabolism and a minimized scaling exponent relative to terrestrial species of comparable size. Both reductions push the asymptotic mass upward, since a lower running cost leaves more of the assimilated energy for growth, and the effect is largest in the species with the most fat.

The mechanism proposed is cellular rather than ecological. Adipose tissue has different scaling properties from the rest of the body: adipocytes demand less energy per unit mass, so an animal with a large fat fraction carries a lower average metabolic rate than a lean animal of the same mass. Fat therefore does two things at once, storing energy and lowering the rate at which the body spends it.

One consequence of the model deserves emphasis. If fat lowers the average metabolic rate, then the scaling exponent of a species is not a fixed property of its physiology but a function of its body composition, and two animals of the same mass with different fat fractions should sit on different curves. The trajectories in the sample support that reading, since the marine species depart from the terrestrial relation at the sizes where blubber becomes a large share of the body, which for a whale is a size well below adulthood, and the departure is the largest single signal in the sample.

What the analysis does not do is separate fat's thermal role from its metabolic one. In water, a large blubber layer also reduces heat loss, which lowers the energy an animal must burn to stay warm, and the model's catabolic constant cannot distinguish that saving from the cellular one. Nor does the sample include enough small marine species to test whether the relation holds at the low end of the size range.

An experiment that could settle it would compare fat fractions across species at a fixed mass, and ask whether the metabolic rate falls in proportion. Until then the result stands as a coherent account of why the sea grows its animals large, with a mechanism that a physiologist can test and a scaling law that a palaeontologist can apply.
