Spatial ability is usually studied in a laboratory with a few dozen undergraduates and a maze drawn on paper. The environment in which those undergraduates grew up is treated as a constant, and the question of whether the layout of a city leaves a mark on the people who learn to move through it has been difficult to ask at scale. The tool that made the study possible was a game that thousands of people played for reasons of their own, which is a different sampling frame from a laboratory experiment.

A video game answered it. More than 397,000 people in 38 countries played a navigation task embedded in a game, and their performance was compared with the entropy of the street network around the place they grew up. The study appeared in *Nature*, 30 March 2022.

Street network entropy is a measure of how irregular a city's layout is. A grid has low entropy because every intersection looks like every other, and an organic city with curved streets and dead ends has high entropy because the local geometry varies from block to block. The measure is computed from the intersection density and the angle distribution of the streets, and it captures how much information a walker needs in order to predict what comes next.

People who grew up outside cities navigated better overall than people who grew up in them. Within cities, the match between the layout of the game and the layout of childhood predicted performance: those from low-entropy cities such as Chicago did better on regular levels, and those from high-entropy cities such as Prague or from rural areas did better on entropic levels.

That is a transfer result rather than a general ability result, and it suggests that navigation is learned through exposure to a particular kind of environment. A child who walks the same grid every day learns a schema that generalizes to other grids and does not generalize to a medieval centre. A schema theory of navigation predicts exactly this pattern, since a person who has learned one regularity applies it where it fits and struggles where it does not.

The sample is self-selected, since the game was played by people who chose to play it, and the analysis adjusts for age, education and video-game experience. Whether the effect is large enough to matter outside the game is a question the study cannot answer. The effect sizes are modest, and a difference of a few seconds on a game level is not the same as the ability to find one's way in a new city.

Urban design is the application. A city that is legible and navigable may support cognitive development in ways that a sprawl of cul-de-sacs does not, and the result gives planners a reason to consider the shape of the network as well as its capacity. The study also raises a question for schools, since children who grow up in planned suburbs may be practicing a narrower set of navigation skills than children who grow up in old towns.
