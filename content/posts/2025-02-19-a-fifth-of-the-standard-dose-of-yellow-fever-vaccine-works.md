---

Yellow fever vaccine is made from a live attenuated virus and is given as a single dose that protects for decades, which makes it one of the most effective vaccines in use. The problem is supply. The world's production comes from a handful of facilities, and a shortage in 2016 during an outbreak in Angola and Congo exhausted the stockpile and forced a dose-splitting policy that had never been tested in a trial.

The dose in the vial is defined in international units, and the standard is 13,803 units, a figure that reflects the original seed, not any immunological requirement. A tenth of that, or 500 units, was the dose used in the emergency campaigns, and the evidence for it came from immunogenicity studies instead of from a randomised comparison with the standard dose.

NIFTY, reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 19 February, randomised participants to one of four doses and measured seroconversion at 28 days, which is the point at which a protective antibody titre has developed. The lowest dose tested was non-inferior to the standard, and the intermediate doses behaved the same way, so the trial supports a dose that is a small fraction of the current one and that no manufacturer has yet put on a label.

Safety was the other endpoint. Vaccine-associated adverse events were reported in 111 participants, of whom 103 were mild, seven moderate and one serious, and the rates were similar across the dose groups. A live vaccine's risks do not scale with the dose in any simple way, and the absence of a dose-dependent increase in reactogenicity is the finding that makes a reduced dose usable in a campaign.

Immunology explains why. The attenuated virus replicates in the recipient, and the immune response depends on how much replication occurs and not on how many particles were injected, provided the dose is above the threshold at which infection is established. A dose that is a fraction of the standard still initiates the infection, and the replication that follows is the same in kind.

What the result does for an outbreak is to multiply the stockpile. A fifth of the dose means five times the number of people protected from the same quantity of vaccine, and a campaign that would have run out in a month can run for five. The trade is that a fractional dose requires a different presentation and a different label, and the manufacturers and regulators have to agree on both before a country can use it in a routine programme.

Question that remains is durability. The trial measured seroconversion at 28 days, and the protection that matters lasts decades; the follow-up will show whether a fractional dose produces the same persistence. The existing evidence from the emergency campaigns suggests it does, and a trial that confirms it would settle a debate that has been conducted on borrowed evidence for nine years.
