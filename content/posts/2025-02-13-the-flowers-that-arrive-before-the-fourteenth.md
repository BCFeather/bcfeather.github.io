The rose that is sold on the fourteenth of February was cut in a greenhouse weeks earlier, and the chain that carries it from the farm to a shop counter is the reason the price of the flower triples in a fortnight. The trade is organized around a single day, and the organizing is done months in advance.

The supply is concentrated. The roses that reach the northern hemisphere in February come from the highland farms of Ecuador and Colombia, where the light is constant and the altitude produces a long stem, and from the greenhouses of Yunnan, where the wholesale market at Dounan handles a volume that dwarfs any other in Asia. The farms that sell into this window plan the planting in the previous summer, because a rose takes about sixty days from the cut of a stem to the bloom.

The cold chain is the technical core. A cut rose is a living stem that continues to respire, and the vase life that a customer expects is decided by the temperature in the twenty-four hours after the cut. The farm cools the flower to two degrees within an hour, the box travels in a reefer container or in the hold of a freighter, and the shop that breaks the cold chain at the wrong moment loses the sale.

The air freight is the bottleneck in a peak week. A long-haul aircraft carries pallets of flowers and the capacity is fixed, so the price of a kilo of flowers rises as the fourteenth approaches, and the exporters who booked in December pay a fraction of what the spot market asks in February. The freight is the largest single line in the cost of a rose that travels.

The retail side has its own arithmetic. A florist orders on a forecast, the forecast is based on last year and on the weather, and the order is placed with a wholesaler who can deliver on the morning of the thirteenth. The shop that under-orders sells out by noon and the shop that over-orders throws away the stock on the fifteenth, and the wastage is the cost that the price of the day has to cover.

The Chinese market has added a second date to the calendar. The twentieth of May has become a flower day on the strength of a pun in the sound of the numbers, and the farms now plan two peaks instead of one. The second peak is smaller and it has made the business less seasonal, which is what the growers wanted.

What the day shows is that a romantic gesture has a supply chain behind it. The flower in the hand is the end of a process that began with a planting decision, passed through a cooling room and an aircraft hold, and ended in a bucket outside a shop. The price of the day is the price of that process compressed into a week, and the part of it that the buyer never sees is the cold that has to be paid for from the cut to the counter.
