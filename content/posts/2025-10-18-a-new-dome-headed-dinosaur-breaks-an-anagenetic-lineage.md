Pachycephalosaurs carry a dome of fused frontal and parietal bone, and the dome's size and shape are the main evidence for how the group evolved. A hypothesis current since the 1990s holds that western North American dinosaurs evolved in single lineages that changed gradually as the Western Interior Seaway rose and fell, and that the Campanian *Stegoceras validum* gave way to the Maastrichtian *Pachycephalosaurus wyomingensis* through an intermediate form. Domes vary from flat-headed forms to skulls thicker than the braincase, and that range makes growth series hard to read.

*Zoological Journal of the Linnean Society* published the description on 1 October 2025. The specimen comes from the Two Medicine Formation of Montana and is the first pachycephalosaurid reported from that unit. The authors describe the skull, place it in a phylogenetic analysis, and test whether it occupies the intermediate position the anagenetic hypothesis predicts. Because the unit had yielded no pachycephalosaurid before, the skull is the first specimen from it that can carry characters into an analysis. The description records the dome's sutures, which is where the diagnostic characters sit.

The dome is massive enough to make the animal the third largest pachycephalosaurid known from North America. Phylogenetically it falls far from both *S. validum* and *P. wyomingensis*, which refutes the proposed lineage rather than filling it. The intermediate is not an intermediate: it is a side branch that lived alongside one of the endpoints. Size is inferred from the dome's width and from the reconstructed skull length, and the paper ranks the animal against the known North American forms. That ranking describes the sample, not the species' maximum size.

That result shifts the mechanism. If sea-level cycles drove evolution here, they did so by splitting and rejoining populations that then diverged, which produces branching and extinction rather than one graded series. The same pattern has been reported for tyrannosaurids and ceratopsids, and the value of this specimen is that it extends the test to a group where the evidence had been missing. Sea-level rise fragments a coastal plain into islands, and island populations diverge quickly when they are small and isolated.

What one skull cannot show is variation. A species defined from a single dome has no range of shapes, so sexual dimorphism, ontogeny and individual variation all go unconstrained, and dome size in particular changes with age in better sampled taxa. The formation's age is known coarsely, which limits how precisely the animal can be placed against the seaway's cycles. Ontogeny is the largest single uncertainty, since dome shape changes through growth in every member of the group with a growth series. A juvenile can look like a separate species, and several taxa were named that way.

More specimens from the Two Medicine Formation would do the most good, since a refutation that rests on one animal's position in a tree is a claim about that animal. Computed tomography of the dome would show the internal structure that growth models depend on, and a dated horizon would let branching be compared with the sea-level record instead of merely contrasted with it. Until more material arrives the claim stands as a refutation of one hypothesis, not a confirmation of the alternative, which is the honest position for a single skull.
