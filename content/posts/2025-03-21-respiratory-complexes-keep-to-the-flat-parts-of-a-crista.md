title: "Respiratory complexes keep to the flat parts of a crista"
date: "2025-03-21T14:05:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A study in *Science* on 21 March used cryo-electron tomography to resolve the respiratory complexes as they sit inside a living algal cell, and found that the assemblies are confined to flat membrane regions while the ATP synthase rows occupy the curved tips."
---

A mitochondrion generates usable energy by pumping protons across an inner membrane and letting them return through a turbine, and the proteins that do it are organized into supercomplexes. The arrangement had been studied by dissolving the membrane and purifying the proteins, a method that tells a biochemist which proteins associate and destroys the geometry of where they sit.

Cryo-electron tomography avoids the destruction. The technique freezes a cell fast enough that the water becomes glass instead of ice, images it from a series of angles in an electron microscope, and reconstructs the volume. The authors applied it to *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii*, a single-celled alga whose mitochondria are large and regular enough that the reconstruction reaches molecular resolution.

What the reconstruction shows is a partition. Complexes I, III and IV assemble into a respirasome that sits in the flat part of the crista membrane, and the rows of ATP synthase dimers sit at the curved tip, where the membrane bends. The two assemblies occupy different territory, and the boundary between them is sharp enough that the authors could measure it and could count how many particles sat on each side.

Geometry has a functional argument behind it. A respirasome that channels electrons between complexes without releasing the carrier into the bulk membrane is more efficient, and a membrane region that is flat is the region where such a channel can be built. The curved tip is where the ATP synthase needs to sit to dimerize into rows, and the shape of a crista therefore sorts the machinery.

Carrier proteins were visible in the reconstruction. The authors resolved how cytochrome *c* binds to the surfaces it visits, and the binding sites are positioned so that the carrier can move between complexes without leaving the assembly. That is the detail that a purified structure cannot show, because the carrier is washed away with the detergent along with the lipids that hold the assembly together.

The result argues against the picture in which the inner membrane is a well-mixed soup of proteins. A membrane that is partitioned by curvature is a membrane in which the reactions are organized in space, and the organization costs nothing, because the shape of a crista is already there and the proteins only have to sort themselves into it. Bioenergetics textbooks will need a diagram that places each complex on a part of the crista, which is a revision that a decade of purified structures could not have forced.

Limits are the ones that a single organism and a single state impose. The alga is not a mammalian cell, and the reconstruction captures one moment and not the response to a change in demand. A mitochondrion that is making more ATP may rearrange itself, and the technique is now fast enough that the rearrangement can be watched over minutes, which is the experiment that the authors say they will run next.
