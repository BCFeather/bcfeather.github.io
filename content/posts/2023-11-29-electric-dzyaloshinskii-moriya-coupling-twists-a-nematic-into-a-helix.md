Liquid crystals order without freezing, and the polar versions of that order have only recently been realized in the laboratory. A ferroelectric nematic carries a spontaneous electric polarization, which makes it useful for fast electro-optic switching and unusual in its response to fields. Helical structures in such a phase normally require chiral molecules, since a twist has a handedness and an achiral molecule does not supply one.

An electrical analogue of the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction changes that expectation. In magnets the term favours a non-collinear arrangement of neighbouring spins and produces helices and skyrmions, and the preprint posted on 30 November 2023 argues that the same geometry acts on electric dipoles in a soft material. Achiral molecules then form a heliconical phase without any chiral dopant.

The structure is a cone rather than a simple helix: the dipoles precess around an axis while tilting away from it, and the pitch of the precession comes out comparable to the wavelength of visible light. Because the pitch is in that range, the phase can be addressed optically, which is a property that most liquid-crystalline helices do not offer at this scale, and a pitch that responds to composition would turn the phase into a tunable filter.

A transition to the uniform polar phase unwinds the helix critically. Approaching the transition the twist costs less and less energy, the pitch grows, and the structure unwinds continuously rather than in a jump. The critical behaviour is what distinguishes a genuinely helically ordered phase from a transient texture that a field can erase.

The comparison with the twist-bend nematic is instructive. That phase also forms a heliconical structure from achiral molecules, and its mechanism is usually attributed to molecular shape and packing entropy. Here the mechanism is electrostatic, with dipoles arranged non-collinearly by an interaction that favours a fixed angle between neighbours, which is a different route to a similar geometry.

Consequences for materials follow from the combination. A phase that is polar, chiral in structure and achiral in composition offers second-harmonic generation and electro-optic effects without the constraint that the molecules be synthesized as single enantiomers. Whether those effects survive in a device at room temperature depends on the stability of the phase, which the paper does not establish, and stability is the first question a display engineer asks.

Limits are those of a single report. The phase is identified in a particular compound, the temperature window is finite, and the continuum model that explains the pitch has parameters that a measurement must supply. A heliconical phase that unwinds under a modest field is a phase that a device would have to bias, and the bias is a design constraint that the electro-optic applications would inherit. A mechanism is what a materials designer needs, and this paper supplies one. If an electric Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya term can be tuned by composition, the pitch becomes a design variable, and a family of polar liquid crystals with pitches spanning the visible spectrum becomes conceivable.
