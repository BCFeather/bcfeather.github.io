title: "What the month did to the idea of too big to fail"
date: "2023-03-31T23:30:00+08:00"
categories: [economy]
description: "The failures of March 2023 reopened the argument about deposit insurance and about banks that are large enough to be rescued, and the argument was conducted with a set of numbers that had been settled for a decade."
---

Three banks failed in eleven days, and two of them were resolved with a protection that the law had written as an exception. The insurance limit of 250,000 dollars per depositor had been set in 2010, and it had been raised from a hundred thousand in 2008 as a temporary measure and then made permanent. The limit was designed for households, and the deposits that ran in March belonged to companies with payrolls and with balances in the millions. The three institutions held a combined four hundred billion dollars in assets, and none of them was large enough to be covered by the regime that the 2010 law had designed.

The argument that followed had two versions and both were about the same asymmetry. One version said that the limit should be raised or that insurance should cover all deposits, because a bank run is a self-fulfilling event and the cost of stopping it is smaller than the cost of letting it happen. The other version said that insuring everything would remove the discipline that depositors impose on banks, and that the discipline had been the point of the limit.

None of the banks that failed was among the largest, and that was the part that unsettled the supervisors. The rules written after 2008 had concentrated on the institutions whose failure would threaten the system, and they had required those institutions to hold more capital and to write resolution plans. A bank of two hundred billion dollars in assets was below the threshold that the 2018 law had set for the strictest regime, and its failure still required a systemic risk exception, which meant that the threshold had been set in the wrong place.

Markets read the guarantee as extended in fact even where the law was silent. Deposits moved to the largest banks and to the money market funds, and the regional banks that remained paid more for funding and saw their margins compressed. The consolidation that followed was the outcome that the regulators had said they wanted to avoid, and it happened anyway, because the depositors had made their own judgment about which banks would be protected.

The official response was to propose rather than to legislate. The insurance fund published options for raising the limit for business accounts, the Treasury studied the question, and the Congress held hearings in which the supervisors were asked why they had not seen it coming. The proposals were reasonable and the Congress was divided, and the division was the same one that had blocked every change to the regime since 2010. The proposals were published in April and received the arguments that had been made in 2010, and the arguments had aged well.

What the month established was a distinction that the crisis of 2008 had blurred. The banks that were saved in March were saved as depositors rather than as institutions, and the shareholders and the managers lost, and the creditors of the holding companies were exposed. That is a different bargain from the one that had been struck in 2008, and it was struck by three agencies in a weekend without a vote in the chamber that had written the rules.
