Boeing's largest union voted on the evening of 4 November and 59 per cent of those who cast ballots accepted a fourth offer, ending a strike that had stopped production of the 737 MAX and the 777 for seven weeks. The contract raises wages by thirty-eight per cent over four years, adds a twelve thousand dollar ratification payment and keeps the retirement plan as it was. A seven-week stoppage at the narrowbody line is a seven-week delay for every airline that was waiting for an aircraft.

Retirement was the issue that kept the strike going. The union wanted a defined-benefit plan restored after a decade in which new hires had been moved into a defined-contribution arrangement, and the company refused at every round. A workforce that has watched its retirement risk migrate to the stock market is a workforce that prices that loss into its wage demand. A pension that a company removed in 2014 is a pension that a workforce remembers at every negotiation.

Company finances set its position. Boeing had burned through cash for years, had cut production of the 737 MAX after a door panel blew out in January, and had replaced its chief executive in the summer. A planemaker that is already borrowing to fund its own assembly lines cannot easily take on a long-term pension liability, so it offered wages instead. A balance sheet that is already leveraged turns a wage increase into a financing decision.

Wage settlement will be paid for in the price of an aircraft. A thirty-eight per cent increase across thirty-three thousand workers adds a cost that has to be recovered over the life of a programme that is already sold to airlines on fixed contracts. Boeing's customers will meet that cost as delays and as higher prices on the next order. A cost that cannot be passed on in a catalogue price is a cost that comes out of the development budget.

Restarting production is a slower process than stopping it. A line that has been idle for seven weeks has to be requalified, its suppliers have to be brought back to schedule, and the trained workers who left for other jobs have to be replaced. The first aircraft delivered after the strike will be a signal about how much of the system survived. A supplier that has laid off staff during the stoppage does not restart at the same rate. A line that restarts slowly delivers late for a year, and the airlines have already priced the delay into their schedules.

What the strike settled is the price of labour at a company that spent twenty years treating it as a variable cost. The workforce has now established that it can halt the assembly of the most important product in its industry, and the next negotiation will begin from that fact. A union that has shown it can stop the line has changed the terms of every future contract.
