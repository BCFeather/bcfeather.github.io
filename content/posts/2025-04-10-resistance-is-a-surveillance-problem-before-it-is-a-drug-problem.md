Antimicrobial resistance is usually described as a pipeline problem, and the pipeline is genuinely thin. The larger problem is that the map of resistance is drawn from data that most of the world does not collect, and a gene that emerges in a place without a laboratory is a gene that is discovered after it has travelled. A hospital that has no microbiology laboratory treats pneumonia with a guess, and a guess is what selects for resistance.

The global surveillance system collects isolates from participating countries and tests them against a panel of drugs. Participation is voluntary, the methods differ between laboratories, and the countries with the largest human and animal populations are the ones with the fewest submitted isolates. A map with holes is a map that shows where sampling happens rather than where resistance happens. A country that submits isolates from a few urban hospitals is a country whose map describes its cities.

The genetics explain why the holes matter. Resistance genes travel on plasmids that move between species, and a gene that confers resistance to a last-line drug can appear in a hospital in one country and in a pig farm in another within years. The colistin resistance gene that was found in China in 2015 had been selected by agricultural use, and it was detected because a group happened to be looking. A plasmid that carries three resistance genes at once is a plasmid that a single drug switch cannot solve.

Agriculture is the volume. Most antibiotic use worldwide is in animals, and the drugs that are used for growth promotion in some countries are the same classes that treat humans. A country that restricts agricultural use reduces selection pressure, and a country that does not becomes a reservoir that its neighbours cannot isolate from. A farm that uses a antibiotic as a growth promoter is a farm where the dose is low and continuous, which is the ideal condition for selection.

Diagnostics are the practical fix. A hospital that can identify an organism and its resistance profile in hours treats the right drug, and one that waits three days treats broadly. Rapid tests exist and are expensive, and the economics of a test that saves a drug are the economics of an insurance system that pays for the drug instead. A test that costs twenty dollars is a test that a rural clinic cannot run, and a test that is subsidized is a test that changes prescribing.

What a surveillance system buys is time. A gene detected early can be contained with infection control, and a gene detected late is a gene that is already in the environment. The cost of the laboratories is small against the cost of a last-line drug that stops working, and the laboratories are the part of the response that is easiest to defer. A laboratory network that is funded only during an outbreak is a network that is closed when the outbreak ends.
