Anvil clouds form at the top of deep convection and spread into thin sheets that reflect sunlight and trap heat. Their area response to warming is one of the largest uncertainties in climate sensitivity. Cloud-resolving simulations supply the microphysics and global models supply the coverage, and the two scales have to be reconciled before a feedback can be stated. Policy ranges are quoted in whole degrees, and a shift of a few tenths of a degree is invisible at that resolution. A weak feedback is a small number with a large consequence, and the consequence is that the upper end of the sensitivity range loses support. The upper end is where the tail risks live, and a tail that loses support is a tail a policy can discount.

A paper in Nature Geoscience on 1 April 2024 examines that response with physical reasoning and observations together. A feedback that depends on area is a feedback that models can get wrong in either direction. Reconciliation is an approximation, and the paper is explicit about which part of the response its approximation covers. Invisible at the resolution of a headline is still visible at the resolution of an assessment, and assessments are where this result will be used. Support is what an assessment weights, and weighting is how a single paper enters a consensus statement.

If anvils shrink with warming, the reflection they provide declines and the planet warms further. If they expand, the opposite happens, and the sign is what matters. Detrainment sets how much ice leaves the convective core, and detrainment is a parameter that models tune rather than derive. A result used in an assessment becomes part of the next model generation, and that generation is what will test it.

Physical constraints come from the microphysics that set the anvil's edge, and observational constraints come from satellite records. Combining the two is what narrows an estimate that either alone leaves wide. A tuned parameter is a place where a model can drift away from the atmosphere, and the drift is what the observational check is meant to catch.

Neither alone can settle the sign. The result is a weaker area feedback than the model ensemble suggests, and weaker means a smaller uncertainty in sensitivity. Satellite instruments see the top of a cloud and infer the rest, and inference is where a thin anvil becomes ambiguous.

A smaller uncertainty is the practical gain, since sensitivity is what a policy number rests on. Satellite records are short relative to a climate trend, and the paper treats that limitation as a constraint on its confidence. Ambiguity in retrieval is a measurement problem rather than a modelling one, and the paper weights its records accordingly.

A short record can bound a feedback without resolving its trend. A feedback that is weaker than the ensemble median moves the sensitivity distribution, and moving a distribution is how a paper changes a policy range.
