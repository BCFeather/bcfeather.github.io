Freshwater communities are assemblages of species that share a lake or a river, and their composition is a record of what the water has been through. A paper in Nature Ecology & Evolution on 30 January 2024 asks how fast that record is being rewritten. The record in a lake is layered, and a layered record separates the pressures in time. A global loss is the sum of local ones, and summing is what turns a community record into a conservation argument. A pressure that has stopped leaves a legacy in the sediment, and a legacy is not evidence that the pressure still operates. A pattern that holds across continents is a pattern a global policy can cite, and citing is how a community study enters a framework.

Speed is the question, and the answer distinguishes pressures that have ended from pressures that continue. Recent anthropogenic pressure is the variable that the analysis isolates, and isolation is what allows a comparison with the past. Separation in time is what the analysis exploits, and exploiting it is the methodological contribution rather than a by-product. A pattern across many lakes is a pattern no single lake could show, and pooling is the design that makes it visible. Habitat mosaics are what make a regional analysis comparable, and comparability is what a global claim requires.

Past pressures left a legacy in the sediment and in the species list, and a legacy is different from an ongoing disturbance. Ongoing disturbance keeps removing species, and removal is a process with a rate. Rivers connect and lakes isolate, so the two habitats convert a pressure into a change at different rates. A global claim drawn from regional datasets is a claim about consistency, and consistency is what the pooling demonstrates.

The result is that recent pressure explains more of the change than the historical burden does, and more is a statement about rate rather than about magnitude. Magnitude accumulates, and rate determines what a management plan can still influence. A rate is what a manager can compare against a target, and comparison is the only use a rate has.

Freshwater is the habitat where the effect is clearest, because a lake cannot be recolonized from the sea and a river is a corridor with barriers. Barriers make a local extinction permanent, and permanence is the property that turns a change into a loss. Anthropogenic pressure is not one variable, and the analysis decomposes it into classes that act at different scales.

Conservation inference follows from the ordering of causes, since a pressure that is still acting is a pressure that a policy can still stop. Classes acting at different scales are what separate policy instruments can address.

A legacy cannot be stopped, and treating it as the main cause misdirects effort. Freshwater species are more often endemic than marine ones, and endemism turns a local loss into a global one.
