description: "A joint dataset from twelve Chinese cities, published on 26 March, shows that canopy cover explains more of the temperature difference between neighbouring blocks than building density does. The measurement is careful; the policy inference is harder."
---

On 26 March, a consortium of municipal meteorology offices and two university public health departments released a dataset covering 2,140 census blocks in twelve Chinese cities, each block measured on the same afternoon with the same instrument. Between the coolest and the warmest block in a single city the surface temperature differed by as much as 9.4 degrees Celsius. Between cities, the difference was smaller. Anyone who has walked from a tree-lined street into a bare new development knows the effect in their skin; the dataset puts a number on it and, more usefully, ranks the causes.

Canopy cover came first. A regression across all blocks put tree cover ahead of building density, ahead of distance to the nearest park and well ahead of the age of the housing stock. Blocks with more than 30 per cent canopy ran roughly 3.5 degrees cooler at mid-afternoon than blocks with less than 10 per cent, holding built-up area constant. Building density mattered, but it mattered less than most urban heat literature assumes, and it mattered mostly through a single mechanism: the amount of pavement and roof area that stores heat through the day and releases it after sunset.

That mechanism explains the timing of the peak. Surface temperature in a dense block peaks around two in the afternoon; air temperature peaks an hour or two later, and the gap between blocks widens in the evening, when the stored heat in asphalt and concrete begins to radiate and there is no evapotranspiration to offset it. Trees do not remove heat so much as convert it: water drawn from soil and evaporated from leaves consumes energy that would otherwise raise the surface temperature. In a block with adequate soil moisture, that conversion is cheap. In a block where trees stand in a sealed planter over compacted ground, it is not.

Humidity is the part of the result that will travel furthest. In three of the twelve cities the canopy effect was weaker in blocks beside large water bodies, and the paper argues that this is because the air was already close to saturation in the afternoon, which limits further evaporation. The same reasoning applies to the tropical south: tree cover helps, but the help is bounded by the physics of a wet atmosphere, and planting programmes that promise a fixed number of degrees disregard that bound.

Measurement limits deserve a paragraph. The survey is an afternoon snapshot in spring, not an annual mean; it captures surface temperature from a radiometer rather than the air temperature people actually breathe; and it cannot separate mature canopy from a sapling row that will not cast the same shade for a decade. The authors are explicit that their model explains about 60 per cent of the variance, which is impressive for a field survey and useless as a design tool for a single courtyard.

What the dataset changes is the argument. Municipal heat plans in China have tended to treat the problem as one of green space quantity, measured in square metres per person, and to place that green space where land is cheap. The block-level result says that placement and maturity, not area, decide who gets the cooling. A city can hit its per-capita target and still leave the densest, oldest blocks hottest, because those blocks have the least room for soil and the most heat stored in their surfaces.
