description: "The composite purchasing managers index rose to 52.3 in February, a nine-month high, as energy prices fell and supply chains eased."
---

S&P Global published the flash estimate on 21 February, and the composite index came in at 52.3. That was up from 50.3 in January and the highest since May, and anything above 50 signals expansion. The manufacturing index stayed below the mark at 48.5, and services reached 53.0, the strongest in eight months. The index had been below 50 for five months, and the February reading ended the longest stretch since 2012. The survey is compiled from replies gathered in the second half of the month, and the response rate was 85 per cent.

Improvement was driven by the fall in energy prices. Gas on the Dutch benchmark had fallen to 50 euros a megawatt hour from 300 in August, and the forward curve for next winter was lower still. Firms reported that input cost inflation had slowed for the fifth month, and selling price expectations eased. That matters for the central bank, which had been watching the pass-through from costs to prices. Electricity prices for German industry were still twice the level of 2019, and firms said that limited investment.

Supply chains also improved. Delivery times lengthened less than at any point since 2020, and the backlog of orders fell in manufacturing. The port congestion that had defined the previous two years had cleared, and freight rates were back to pre-pandemic levels on the main routes. The number of ships waiting outside Rotterdam fell to 20 from 80 a year earlier. Air freight volumes rose in January for the first time since the autumn, according to the same survey.

Some 5,000 companies reply to the survey, which is watched because it appears three weeks before the official figures. It has overstated weakness twice in the past year, which the compilers acknowledged. Revisions to the flash number are usually small, and the final reading was published on 1 March at 52.0. The survey's employment index rose to 53.6, which means firms were hiring again.

Divergence inside the currency area remained. Germany returned to growth for the first time since June, helped by a mild winter and the reopening of factories that had cut output in the autumn. France grew more slowly, and the surveys in Italy and Spain were the strongest of the four largest members. Germany's index rose above 50 for the first time since June, and France stayed just below. The four largest members account for about 75 per cent of the index.

February brought a half-point increase from the central bank, with another signalled for March. The survey gave its officials an argument for continuing, since demand was holding up better than the winter forecasts had assumed. Economists revised their first-quarter growth estimates up to 0.3 per cent from zero, and several said the recession they had predicted in the autumn would not arrive. The bank's next meeting was on 16 March, and the deposit rate was 3 per cent after the February rise. The bank's own forecast for the quarter was a contraction of 0.2 per cent, which the survey put in doubt.
