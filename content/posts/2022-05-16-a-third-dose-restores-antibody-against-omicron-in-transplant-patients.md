Solid-organ transplant recipients take immunosuppressants for the life of the graft, and the drugs that protect the organ blunt the response to vaccination. Two doses left a substantial fraction with no detectable antibody, and the question was whether a third would convert them. A patient whose immune system is suppressed by design is the extreme case for any vaccine, and the transplant population is where the limits of a schedule show up first.

A prospective cohort of transplant recipients measured anti-spike antibody after each dose, and compared the seroconversion rate after the third with the rate after the second, with the mycophenolate dose and the age of the recipient as the variables of interest. The paper appeared in *JAMA*, 16 May 2022.

About a third of the patients who had no response after two doses produced detectable antibody after the third. The response was weakest in patients taking mycophenolate at a full dose and in the oldest recipients, and strongest in those on a low dose or on a regimen that spared the drug. A seroconversion rate of about a third is disappointing and it is not nothing, because the patients who convert are the ones who were closest to a response after two doses.

A third dose is not a solution for everyone in this population, and the result quantifies how partial the solution is. A patient who seroconverts may still have antibody below the level that protects against Omicron, and the titre matters as much as the presence. A titre that is detectable and a titre that neutralizes are different quantities, and the paper reports the first while the reader needs the second.

Mycophenolate inhibits the proliferation of B cells, which is the step that a vaccine response depends on, and the association is a mechanism rather than a confounder. A clinician who wants to improve the response has the option of reducing the dose, which is a decision about the graft rather than about the vaccine.

A single centre supplied the cohort and modest in size, and the antibody assay is one of several that could have been used. The correlation between antibody and protection in this population is not established, because the patients who seroconvert may differ in ways that also affect their risk. An assay that measures binding antibody does not measure neutralization, and a patient with binding antibody and no neutralizing activity is a patient who is still unprotected.

A practical recommendation follows a third dose for transplant recipients, with counselling that it may not work and with the alternative of passive prophylaxis for those who do not respond. A fourth dose and a prophylactic monoclonal antibody are the options for the patients who do not respond, and the choice between them depends on the variant that is circulating. A monoclonal antibody that is active against the circulating variant is the fallback, and the supply of such products has been the constraint throughout the pandemic.
