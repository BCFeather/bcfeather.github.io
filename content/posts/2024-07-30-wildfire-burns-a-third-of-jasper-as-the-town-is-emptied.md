A wildfire entered the townsite of Jasper in Alberta on 24 July and destroyed about a third of its buildings, in a national park that had evacuated twenty-five thousand residents and visitors in a few hours. The fire arrived with a wind that pushed it faster than the models had predicted and with a plume that grounded the aircraft that would have dropped water on it. The fire had been burning south of the town for days and was reported under control on the morning it entered, which is the pattern that has made these fires hard to fight.

Fuel is the paradox. A park that is protected from logging and from development accumulates dead trees, and a forest that has not burned for a century is a forest that is a reservoir of fuel. A protection policy that treats fire as an enemy is a policy that guarantees a larger fire later. The park had run prescribed burns in some areas and had been prevented from running them in others by a beetle infestation that left standing dead timber.

Town is built into the forest. A settlement in a national park is a settlement with one road out and with a perimeter that cannot be defended once a fire is inside it, and the evacuation worked because it began early. A community with one access road is a community that has to leave before the fire arrives. The evacuation was ordered at night and the highway out was closed behind the last convoy, and the residents who stayed were told that no rescue would come.

Insurance and rebuilding are the next problem. A town that is rebuilt in the same place is a town that will face the same fire, and the building code that is adopted now determines what survives the next one. A code that requires fire-resistant roofs and cleared perimeters is cheaper than a rebuild. The insurance losses will be shared between private insurers and a provincial scheme, and the rebuilding will face a labour shortage that the region already has.

Climate is the multiplier. A fire season that begins in May and ends in September is a season that has extended at both ends, and the number of days with extreme fire weather has risen. A season that is longer is a season that exhausts the crews before the peak. The fire weather index has risen across the boreal forest, and the number of days when a fire can grow at this speed has increased in every decade since the 1980s.

What the fire shows is that the boundary between the wild and the built is a decision that has been made badly. A town inside a forest is a town that has to manage the forest, and the management is a programme of controlled burns that the public has to accept before it is needed. A town that manages the forest around it is a town that has accepted fire as a process rather than an emergency, and acceptance is the hard part of the policy.
