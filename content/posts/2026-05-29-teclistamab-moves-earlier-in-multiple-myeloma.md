Bispecific antibodies entered myeloma at the end of the line, in patients who had exhausted proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulatory drugs and anti-CD38 therapy. The question that follows every effective salvage drug is whether it does more good earlier, when the patient is less heavily pretreated and the immune system is less damaged. That sequencing logic is what the trial tests, and it is the same logic that moved anti-CD38 antibodies and CAR-T products earlier in the disease course.

The trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 29 May answered that question for teclistamab, a bispecific that binds CD3 on T cells and BCMA on plasma cells. Patients with one to three previous lines were randomized to teclistamab or to standard combinations of pomalidomide, bortezomib and dexamethasone, or carfilzomib and dexamethasone.

Complete response or better was 65.9 per cent with teclistamab against 16.8 per cent with the comparator regimens. Estimated 18-month overall survival was 79.2 per cent against 68.6 per cent, a hazard ratio for death of 0.60, with a confidence interval from 0.43 to 0.83. A complete response rate four times higher than the comparator is a large difference for a disease where depth of response predicts duration of remission.

Depth of response is the currency in myeloma, because a patient who reaches MRD-negative complete response has a different trajectory from one who only achieves a partial response. Moving a bispecific earlier buys that depth at a point when the marrow can still tolerate the treatment, which is the difference between a bridge and a remission. The comparator regimens were picked because they are what a patient in this position actually receives, which makes the comparison a practical one rather than a straw man.

Toxicity is the cost of the mechanism. Grade 3 or 4 adverse events occurred in 84.9 per cent of teclistamab recipients against 76.3 per cent, and grade 5 events in 6.5 against 3.5 per cent. Cytokine release syndrome occurred in 66 per cent, mostly grade 1 or 2, and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity in 4.1 per cent. Those are manageable in a centre that does this routinely, and that is the constraint on how widely the drug can move. Infections are the other signal to watch, because BCMA-directed therapy removes the plasma cells that make protective antibody and hypogammaglobulinaemia follows.

The practical consequence is a sequencing decision. A patient who could receive a bispecific in second line may now be offered it instead of a triplet, and the regimens displaced become the next line rather than the current one. Payers will notice that the earlier use costs more per patient-year, and the trial's survival curve is the argument against counting only the acquisition price. Centres that cannot manage cytokine release syndrome will refer rather than treat, and that referral pattern is already shaping how bispecifics spread geographically.
