Semiconductor manufacturing has been organized around wafer diameter for fifty years, because a larger wafer yields more die per process step and spreads the fixed cost of a fab over more output. The industry moved from 150 to 200 millimetres in the 1990s and from 200 to 300 in the 2000s, and each transition wiped out the firms that could not finance it. The assumption that 200 millimetre capacity would disappear has been wrong for fifteen years. A 300 millimetre line costs several billion dollars, and a mature-node fab costs a fraction of that, which is why the older size survives in the segments that cannot fill a large line.

The reason is that most chips do not need the most advanced node. Power transistors, analog circuits, microelectromechanical sensors and embedded controllers are built on process generations from 350 to 90 nanometres, and those processes run perfectly well on 200 millimetre wafers. Moving them to 300 millimetres would require requalifying a recipe that customers have already designed around. Requiring a new mask set and a new yield ramp for a part that sells for a dollar is a cost that no customer will accept.

Silicon carbide is the clearest case. Power devices for electric vehicles are built on 150 and 200 millimetre SiC substrates, and the crystal growth is slow and defect-prone, so a larger wafer is harder to make rather than cheaper. The industry is expanding 200 millimetre SiC capacity rather than chasing 300, and the constraint is the boule rather than the lithography. A silicon carbide wafer also needs a different thermal budget, and the equipment that handles it is not the equipment that handles silicon.

Equipment is the second reason. A 200 millimetre fab can use tooling that is twenty years old and fully depreciated, which makes it the cheapest place to build a mature chip. The used equipment market for 200 millimetre tools is thin and prices have risen, because demand for that capacity has outlived the supply of machines. Depreciation is the accounting fact that decides where a commodity chip is made, and a fab that has written off its tools can undercut a new one.

Shortages in 2021 and 2022 made the point. Automotive customers were short of parts built on mature nodes, and the shortage was in power management and microcontrollers rather than in processors. Governments responded by subsidizing new mature-node capacity, which is now arriving into a market that may be oversupplied. Car makers responded by signing direct contracts with foundries, which changed the bargaining structure of the whole supply chain.

Where that capacity sits is the strategic question. Mature-node fabs are the ones that export controls treat least strictly, which is why Chinese foundries have expanded there, and why the American and European response has focused on the same nodes. A technology that was supposed to be obsolete has become the front line of industrial policy. The policy that subsidizes a mature fab also decides which customers it serves, and that decision is now made with security in mind.
