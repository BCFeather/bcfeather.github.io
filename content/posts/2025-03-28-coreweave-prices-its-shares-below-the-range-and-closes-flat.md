CoreWeave rents out graphics processors by the hour, and its customers are the artificial intelligence laboratories that need compute and do not want to build a data centre. The company began as a crypto miner, converted its fleet when the mining market collapsed, and grew into a business whose contracts with Microsoft and with other model developers made it one of the largest buyers of NVIDIA hardware in the world.

Offering had been marketed at 47 to 55 dollars, and the company cut the range and then priced at 40, which reduced the amount raised from about 2.7 billion to 1.5 billion. NVIDIA took 250 million dollars of the stock as an anchor investor, a commitment that a supplier makes when it wants a customer to have capital, and the company's own borrowing is secured against the chips that it owns.

First day was flat and the context was hostile. The Nasdaq fell nearly 3 per cent on the same day, the index was heading for its worst quarter since 2022, and the tariff measures of the preceding weeks had made investors unwilling to buy a company whose business plan depends on spending that continues. A listing that prices below its range and closes unchanged is a listing that the market accepted and did not celebrate.

Balance sheet is the part a reader should examine. CoreWeave carries debt that it raised against its equipment, and its contracts are long-term commitments from customers whose own revenue is uncertain. A business that borrows to buy depreciating hardware and rents it on multi-year contracts is a business that works while demand grows and that becomes fragile when a customer renegotiates.

Valuation depends on the denominator. The company was valued at about 19 billion dollars on a non-diluted basis and about 23 billion on a fully diluted one, and the difference is the stock that employees and convertible holders will receive. A headline that quotes the smaller number and a comparison that uses the larger one is the kind of discrepancy that appears in every listing of a company with a large equity compensation programme.

Alternative is the comparison that the market made. A buyer who wanted exposure to artificial intelligence could buy the shares of NVIDIA, which sells the chips, or Microsoft, which buys them, and both were cheaper on almost every measure than a company that owns the hardware and owes the debt. The listing therefore tested whether the public market would pay for a pure play, and the answer on the first day was that it would pay the offer price and not more.

What the listing established is a public price for compute. A company that rents processors now has a market valuation, a disclosed contract book and a debt schedule, and those numbers are the reference for every private data centre business that is raising money. Whether the reference holds depends on the next two quarters, and the executives said as much in the interviews that followed the debut.
