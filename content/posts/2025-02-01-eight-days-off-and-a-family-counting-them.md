The holiday in China runs for eight days around the festival, from the twenty-eighth of January to the fourth of February, and by the fourth day a family is counting the days that remain. The count is not a complaint, it is the way the holiday is experienced, and the same count appears in the messages that people send to each other from the third day onwards.

Arithmetic of the eight days is a compromise between two calendars. The festival itself is a day, the household needs a week to prepare and to visit, and the country needs the offices to reopen. The state sets the days off and the employers adjust, and the eight-day block that results contains three weekend days that would have been days off anyway.

Counting also structures the holiday. The first two days belong to the immediate household, the next two to the relatives, the fifth and the sixth to the trips that were planned in December, and the last two to the return journey and the rest before work. The structure is informal and most families follow something like it.

Travel on the last two days is the busiest part of the period. The trains that run on the fourth are full, the highways that lead to the coastal cities carry the returning workers, and the stations that were quiet on the first of the month are crowded on the fourth. The return is a mass movement that the timetable is built to absorb.

Employers have their own arithmetic. A company that restarts on the fifth has a day of low output while the staff arrive and settle, and a company that restarts on the sixth has two. The factories that run a production line plan the restart around the supply of parts, and the parts arrive when the suppliers reopen, which is the week after the holiday rather than the day.

Counting is also a cultural habit that predates the holiday. The lunar calendar divides the month into the first, the middle and the last, and the days of the festival have their own names rather than numbers. The counting that a family does in February is a modern version of that, and the number of days is the only part that the state decides.

For the person who has to work through the holiday, the arithmetic is different and it is invisible to the people who do not. The eight days are eight days of a normal wage plus the holiday rate, and the household that earns the extra money is the household that has the quiet streets. The city needs both kinds of household, and the calendar only describes one of them.

What the fourth day shows is that a holiday is a quantity as well as a date. The quantity is what a family plans around, and the planning is what turns eight days into a shape with a beginning, a middle and a return.
