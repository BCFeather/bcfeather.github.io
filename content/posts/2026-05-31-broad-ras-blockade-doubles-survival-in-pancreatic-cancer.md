Pancreatic cancer has been the graveyard of targeted therapy. *KRAS* is mutated in more than nine in ten cases, and for thirty years the protein was considered undruggable because it has no deep pocket to bind. Covalent inhibitors for the G12C substitution worked in lung cancer and had almost no application here, because pancreatic tumours carry G12D and G12V far more often. That history is why the result reads as a departure rather than an increment, and why the same question is now being asked of every RAS-driven tumour.

The trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 31 May took a different route. Daraxonrasib binds cyclophilin A and blocks the active, GTP-bound state of RAS, which means it does not require a particular mutation and can act on wild-type RAS as well as mutant forms. Five hundred patients with previously treated metastatic ductal adenocarcinoma were randomized to the drug or to chemotherapy, and 91.8 per cent carried a RAS G12 mutation. Pancreatic tumours are also poorly vascularized and densely stromal, which is why so many agents that work in a dish fail in the pancreas, and a systemic inhibitor has to reach cells through that barrier.

Median overall survival was 13.2 months with daraxonrasib against 6.7 months with chemotherapy in the overall population, and 13.2 against 6.6 in the RAS G12 subgroup. The hazard ratio was 0.40 in both, which is a 60 per cent reduction in the risk of death and a result that pancreatic oncology has not seen before.

Progression-free survival was 7.2 months against 3.6, a hazard ratio of 0.49, and the objective response rate was 31.6 per cent. Those numbers describe a drug that shrinks tumours rather than one that stabilizes them, which matters because response opens the possibility of later local therapy in a disease where most patients never get there. The response rate is the number that will be quoted to patients, and a third of patients shrinking is different from a third of patients stable, which is what chemotherapy usually delivers.

The design of a multi-selective inhibitor carries a theoretical cost. Wild-type RAS does useful work in normal tissues, and blocking it everywhere could produce toxicity that a mutation-specific drug avoids. The trial's adverse event profile and the drug's tolerability in a population that has already had chemotherapy will decide how widely it is used.

For patients this changes the second-line conversation. For the field it changes the target: RAS is now a druggable protein without a mutation-specific key, and the next trials will ask whether the same approach works in first line, in combination, and in the lung and colorectal cancers where *KRAS* drives growth with different tissue context. Whether resistance arrives through new RAS mutations or through bypass signalling is the next question, and the trial's progression biopsies are the material that will answer it.
