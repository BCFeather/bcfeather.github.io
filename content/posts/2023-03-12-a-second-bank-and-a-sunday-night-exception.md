Signature Bank was closed by the New York State Department of Financial Services on Sunday, 12 March, and the insurance fund took it over in the same way it had taken the other one. The bank had 110 billion dollars in assets and a client base of law firms, real estate companies and cryptocurrency businesses, and its deposits had been leaving since the previous Thursday. Its closure came two days after the first, and the two failures together changed the question that the authorities were answering.

The decision that mattered was announced on the same evening and covered both banks. The Treasury, the Federal Reserve and the insurance fund said that all depositors of both institutions would be made whole, including those above the insurance limit, and that the cost would be met by the insurance fund rather than by the taxpayers. The legal instrument was the systemic risk exception, and its use required the votes of two-thirds of the boards of the fund and the Fed and the approval of the Treasury, which is a procedure that had been used once before, in 2008.

The exception's defenders described it as a decision about contagion. A regional bank with uninsured deposits in another state was losing deposits by the hour, and the message that uninsured money would be paid in full at the failed banks was intended to stop the run at the ones that were still open. Its critics described the same decision as a guarantee for large depositors that the law had deliberately withheld, and they noted that the shareholders and the bondholders of the two banks lost their claims and that the managers were dismissed.

Signature's failure had its own peculiarity. The bank had been the main banking channel for several cryptocurrency firms, and its deposits had swollen and then shrunk with the sector, and the withdrawal that killed it came after the first failure had made every uninsured depositor in the country read the same news. The regulators said the bank was solvent when it was closed, which is the sentence that made the case for the exception and the case against the system that had needed it.

Bank stocks opened on Monday lower and then recovered through the day, and the regional bank index fell by more than a fifth over the week. Deposits moved from smaller banks to the largest ones, and the largest ones reported inflows that they had not asked for, which is what a flight to safety looks like in a system with too few places to hide. The Fed's discount window lent record amounts, and the facility it created on the same Sunday lent more.

What the weekend established was a precedent that would be argued about for years. Two banks, both with a concentrated and uninsured deposit base, both closed within three days, and both resolved with a protection that the statute allowed and that the politics made unavoidable. The precedent was read in the markets as a guarantee for large deposits up to some unstated size, and the guarantee was read by the banks as a reason to compete for them.
