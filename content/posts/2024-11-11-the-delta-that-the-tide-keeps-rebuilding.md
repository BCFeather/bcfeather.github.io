The Sundarbans is a delta that is being built and taken away at the same time, and the balance is decided twice a day. The Ganges and the Brahmaputra bring silt down from the Himalaya and spread it across a plain of mud and mangrove, and the tide that comes in from the Bay of Bengal carries some of it further inland before it settles. Where the silt settles the land rises, and where the tide scours a channel the land falls, so the coastline is a set of gains and losses and not a line. The forest that grows on this shifting ground is the largest mangrove in the world, and its shape follows the water. A delta is what happens where a river loses its speed, and the speed depends on the tide as much as on the slope of the plain.

Mangroves are built for that. The trees have aerial roots that take oxygen from the air at low tide and stilt roots that hold them in soft mud, and their seeds germinate on the parent tree and fall as seedlings rather than as seeds. Salt is excluded at the root, and the small amount that gets through is deposited in old leaves that drop and carry it out of the plant. The species arrange themselves by how much salt and how much flooding they tolerate, so the forest has a zonation that reads as a map of the tide. The leaves that drop also build soil, since the roots and the litter trap sediment that the tide brings in. The forest also filters the water that passes through it, and the fishery at the edge of the delta depends on that filtration.

The tide range is the engine. A rise of several metres twice a day pushes water through the creeks and over the flats, and the water that leaves carries suspended sediment with it. The sediment settles where the current slackens, which is at the edge of the forest and in the lee of the roots, and the forest therefore builds its own substrate. A coast with mangroves gains height at a rate that can keep pace with a slow rise in sea level, and a coast without them does not, which is why the loss of the forest is a problem for the land behind it as well as for the trees. The rate at which it can keep pace is what the projections of sea level are measured against, and a forest that lags becomes a mudflat. A forest that is cut for timber loses height faster than the tide can rebuild it, and the loss is measured in hectares per year.

People live in this. The delta is densely settled on its Indian side and less so on the Bangladeshi side, and the pattern of settlement is a set of embankments that hold the water out of fields that grow rice in the wet season and lie fallow in the dry. The embankments were built to stop the tide and they also stop the silt, so a field behind a embankment does not rise with the water outside it, and the difference accumulates over decades. The response has been to open the embankments in places and let the tide back in, which restores the sediment and the mangrove and takes the field out of rice. The engineering of the embankments was done by the colonial and then the national water authorities, and the design is still the one being repaired.

Salt is the other pressure. Shrimp ponds are dug into the edge of the forest and filled with brackish water, and the water that is pumped out carries salt into the soil behind the ponds. A field that has been salted grows rice poorly for years, and a farmer who has rented land to a shrimp operator has a cash income and a soil that will not recover on the timescale of a mortgage. The economics are real and so is the damage, and the two are not separable by a policy that treats them as different questions. The ponds also need fresh water for the first weeks, and the wells that supply it draw on the same aquifer the fields use.

Cyclones arrive with the tide. A storm surge driven by a cyclone into a delta with a mangrove fringe loses height in the forest before it reaches the embankment, and a surge that arrives at a cleared coast does not. The cyclones that have hit the delta in the past two decades have killed people behind embankments that failed and have damaged forests that then regrew, and the difference between those outcomes is a measure of what the trees are worth. The forest is not a decoration on the coast; it is the part of the coast that absorbs the water. The forest also absorbs the wave energy of a normal tide, and a coast without it loses soil on every high tide.
