Spinal cord injury severs the pathway between intention and movement, and brain-computer interfaces have spent two decades reconnecting the first half of it. The typical system reads motor cortex activity and drives a cursor or a robotic arm, which restores control but not sensation, and leaves the user dependent on a device that must be worn and calibrated. A system that also returns touch, and that leaves something behind when it is off, would be a different kind of intervention.

A double neural bypass, reported in *Nature Medicine* on 16 July 2026, does both. Chad Bouton and colleagues recorded cortical activity associated with the intention to move, decoded it, and used the signal to drive electrical stimulation of the forearm muscles and of the sensory nerves. The participant, a man with a complete cervical injury, could grasp and manipulate objects and reported feeling contact at the fingertips. The decoding was calibrated to the participant's own cortical activity, which is what makes the system usable without a generic model.

What makes the result more than a demonstration is the persistence. Some gains remained when the stimulator was switched off, which suggests that the repeated pairing of intention, movement and sensation engaged a recovery process rather than merely substituting for one. That claim is the fragile part of the paper, since a single participant and an open-label design cannot separate rehabilitation from device effect. The carry-over was largest in the tasks that were practised most, which is consistent with a training effect rather than a device effect. The device also required daily sessions, and the gains were measured immediately after training rather than at a distance from it.

Physiology is plausible. Movement intention and sensory feedback are normally coupled, and an injury removes both sides of the loop. Restoring the coupling, even artificially, gives the remaining circuits a signal to work with, and activity-dependent plasticity is the mechanism that would explain a carry-over effect. Whether that plasticity generalises beyond one highly motivated participant is unknown.

Limits are those of a first-in-human study. One participant, one injury level, intensive training, and a device that requires a surgical implant and a laboratory team. The sensory report is subjective, the functional gains are measured on a small set of tasks, and the durability of the effect over months rather than weeks has not been shown. A single participant also makes the result impossible to replicate in the sense that matters, since no one else can be tested against the same baseline.

What comes next is a larger, controlled study, with participants randomised to device plus rehabilitation or rehabilitation alone. If the carry-over survives that design, the intervention becomes a rehabilitation technology rather than a permanent prosthetic, and that would change both the cost and the ethics. Until then, the result is a proof of concept with an unusually interesting side effect. The control arm would need the same surgery and the same training, which is a demanding design but the only one that isolates the bypass itself.
