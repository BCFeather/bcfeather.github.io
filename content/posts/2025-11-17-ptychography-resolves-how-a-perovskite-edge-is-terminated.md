Halide perovskites are cheap to make and hard to keep. Their solar cells reach efficiencies that rival silicon, and their films degrade under heat, moisture and illumination in ways that no encapsulation has fully solved. Much of the degradation begins at surfaces and edges, where the crystal stops and the chemistry changes, and where the atomic arrangement has been difficult to see. Biao Yuan, Zeyu Wang, Timothy Pennycook and colleagues report in *Nature*, 13 November 2025 an electron ptychography study of methylammonium lead iodide that resolves those edges atom by atom. Ptychography reconstructs a phase image from a series of overlapping diffraction patterns, and at the resolutions reported here it can place columns of atoms and identify which species terminate a surface.

The finding is a majority termination by methylammonium and iodine at the edges the group examined. That is a chemical statement about how the crystal closes: the organic cation and the halide sit at the boundary, and the lead framework ends behind them. The stability of those edges, and of the defects inside the film, then depends on how many vacancies of which kind are present.

Vacancies are the currency of perovskite chemistry. An iodine vacancy can trap an electron, a lead vacancy can shift the local charge balance, and a methylammonium vacancy can leave a void that moisture fills. The paper's contribution is to link the population of each kind to the structure that survives, so that a recipe can be judged by the defects it leaves rather than by the efficiency it delivers on the first day.

The technique is the other result. Electron microscopy of these materials has been destructive, because the beam knocks atoms out of a soft lattice faster than a conventional image can be recorded. Ptychography uses the dose more efficiently, and the reconstruction tolerates the damage that remains, which makes atomic-resolution imaging of a beam-sensitive crystal a practical measurement rather than a demonstration.

What the study does not provide is a route from structure to lifetime. A film that is well terminated at the edge the beam happened to sample may still fail at a grain boundary elsewhere, and degradation under light and heat involves ion migration that a static image cannot show.

Watching the same edge while it degrades is the next experiment, with the beam dose low enough that the observation does not cause the process. Coupling that to device data, so that a structure can be tied to a measured lifetime, is what would turn an atomic picture into a design rule for stable perovskites.

Sample preparation is part of the result. A film lifted out of a device and thinned for the microscope is not the film that operates in a module, and the surfaces exposed by thinning are new ones. The group's images are of edges that exist in the material, but the question of whether the preparation changed them is one every atomic-resolution study of a soft crystal has to answer. Composition is the other variable the field will want mapped. The paper examines methylammonium lead iodide, while the best cells now use mixed cations and mixed halides, where phase segregation under illumination is the dominant degradation path. Whether the same termination rules apply in those compositions is an open question, and a useful one, because that is where the devices are.
