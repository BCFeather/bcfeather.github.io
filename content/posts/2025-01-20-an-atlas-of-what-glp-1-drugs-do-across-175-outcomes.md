description: "A study in Nature Medicine maps the associations between GLP-1 receptor agonists and 175 health outcomes in a large cohort, finding lower risks for several conditions outside diabetes and higher risks for a defined set of gastrointestinal and renal problems."
---

GLP-1 receptor agonists began as diabetes drugs, became obesity drugs and are now being tested for heart failure, kidney disease, sleep apnoea, addiction and neurodegeneration. Each indication has its own trial, and the trials are expensive, which is why the list of conditions that the class might treat has grown faster than the evidence for most of them.

A study in *Nature Medicine* on 20 January takes a different approach. Instead of testing one hypothesis at a time, the authors built a cohort of patients who were prescribed a GLP-1 receptor agonist and a comparison group who received usual care, and they screened 175 health outcomes for associations with the drug. The design is observational, and the authors present the result as a map of what to investigate rather than a set of indications.

Reductions are broad. Use of the drugs was associated with lower risks of substance use disorders and psychotic disorders, of seizures and of neurocognitive conditions including Alzheimer's disease and dementia, and with lower risks of coagulation disorders, cardiometabolic conditions, several infections and a group of respiratory diseases. Some of these associations have trial support and some do not, and the paper does not treat them as equivalent.

Increases are narrower and more concrete. Gastrointestinal disorders topped the list, which matches the clinical experience of nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea that limits the dose in a share of patients. Hypotension and syncope appeared, as did arthritic disorders, kidney stones, interstitial nephritis and drug-induced pancreatitis, and those are the events that a prescriber already watches for in the first months of treatment, when the dose is being raised and the patient is learning what the drug feels like.

Value of the map lies in the size of the cohort and the range of outcomes. A trial answers one question with precision; an observational screen answers 175 questions with less precision and tells a programme which trials are worth running. The authors are explicit that the associations are not causal, that the comparison group differs in ways the adjustment cannot remove and that a signal in a database is a hypothesis.

Two of the signals are already being tested. The neurocognitive associations connect to the trials of semaglutide in Alzheimer's disease, which reported their first results in 2025, and the substance use associations connect to the trials in alcohol and opioid use disorder. A screen that points at trials already running is a screen that will be judged by their results, and in the meantime it tells a clinician which signals are worth mentioning to a patient and which are worth waiting for.

For a prescriber, the practical content is the risk list. A patient starting a GLP-1 drug should be told about the gastrointestinal side effects, about the rare pancreatic risk and about the dehydration that follows vomiting, and the study's contribution is to put those risks beside the possible benefits in one frame, where a patient can weigh them together.
