Members of Congress marked the first anniversary of the attack on the Capitol on 6 January with a series of events inside the building, remarks from the House speaker and a prayer service, and with a prosecutorial effort that had produced hundreds of cases. The day itself produced no violence and a great deal of commentary about how the country had settled into two accounts of the same afternoon. The House chamber stayed closed to visitors for the day, and the galleries above it were empty.

The numbers are large and unglamorous. Federal prosecutors had charged more than seven hundred people by the anniversary, most of them for misdemeanours such as entering restricted grounds, and a smaller group with seditious conspiracy or assault on officers. Judges had begun to sentence the first defendants, and the sentences ranged from weeks in prison for people who walked in and out to several years for those who attacked police lines.

A select committee of the House, formed in the summer, spent the autumn issuing subpoenas and taking depositions. Its immediate task was to establish who organized the rally, what the former president did while it was under way, and whether anyone in the executive branch tried to interfere with the certification of the vote. Several witnesses had refused to appear, and the committee had begun to test how far it could enforce its demands. Its chairman said the panel would follow the evidence wherever it led, and its members included two Republicans who had voted to impeach the former president.

The political context has not moved much. Most Republican voters, in the polling that followed, said they believed the election had been stolen or that the violence had been the work of the other side, and most Democratic voters said the day showed a threat to the constitutional order. A congressional investigation of an attack on the Congress is a strange thing to do while a large faction inside the Congress questions whether the attack mattered, and the committee's public hearings in the summer would be aimed as much at that faction as at the evidence.

Institutional consequences accumulated quietly. The Capitol police board changed its procedures for requesting National Guard support, the architect of the Capitol began a security review of the building's windows and tunnels, and the Justice Department created a unit dedicated to domestic terrorism. Few of those changes were debated in public, and all of them will outlast the anniversary speeches. Congress also created a new office for the sergeant at arms to coordinate with the Capitol police, and its first budget request included money for a rapid-response team.

Anniversaries invite conclusions, and this one supports a modest one. A building was breached by a crowd that believed a fabricated story, and the year that followed produced prosecutions, a committee and new barricades, and it did not produce a shared account of what happened. Democracies survive contested stories most of the time, and they do so by keeping the machinery of proof running.
