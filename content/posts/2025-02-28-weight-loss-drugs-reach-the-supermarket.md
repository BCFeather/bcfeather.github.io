Drugs that mimic the hormone GLP-1 have moved from a diabetes treatment to a mass-market weight loss product in five years, and the consequences are showing up in the food industry. Weekly injections suppress appetite and slow gastric emptying, and patients report a quieter relationship with food that they describe as the absence of a background noise. The effect on grocery baskets is smaller than the effect on calories and larger than most forecasters expected. The class now includes two products that dominate prescriptions, and both are injectables given once a week.

The mechanism is understood well enough to explain the side effects. GLP-1 slows the rate at which the stomach empties and signals satiety to the brain, and a patient who eats less loses weight and also loses muscle if protein intake falls. Clinical guidance now emphasizes resistance training and protein, and the food industry has noticed that the same patient buys smaller portions and more protein-dense products. Patients who stop the drug regain most of the weight within a year, which makes adherence the central clinical variable.

Supply has been the constraint on growth. Manufacturing a peptide at scale requires a fermentation or a synthetic route that takes years to expand, and the two leading products were in shortage for most of 2023 and 2024. That shortage pushed patients towards compounded versions made by pharmacies, which regulators have moved to restrict. Restricting compounded versions closed a cheaper route to the same molecule, and it moved patients back into a supply that was already short.

Price is the next variable. A monthly course costs several hundred dollars in the United States and far less in markets where the government negotiates. Employers and insurers have begun to cover the drugs for obesity rather than only for diabetes, and the budget impact of covering a condition that affects forty per cent of adults is the reason several of them have added restrictions. Coverage decisions are made by employers and by public insurers, and both are looking at the five-year budget rather than at the first year.

Food companies are responding in two ways. One is reformulation towards smaller pack sizes and higher protein, which is a product decision. The other is a lobbying position, which is that the drugs should be treated as a medical intervention rather than as a substitute for dietary change. Both responses assume the drugs are here to stay. The smaller basket is visible in categories that are consumed at home, and less visible in restaurants, where portions are fixed.

The unresolved question is what happens when patients stop. Weight regain after discontinuation is the norm in the trials, and a drug that has to be taken indefinitely is a drug with a lifetime cost. The food industry is planning for a world where a large minority of adults eats less, and the health system is planning for a world where it pays for that minority indefinitely. A food industry that has spent a century selling more has to learn to sell less, and the learning is not going smoothly.
