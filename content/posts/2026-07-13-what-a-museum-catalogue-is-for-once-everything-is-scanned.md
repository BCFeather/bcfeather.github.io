A museum catalogue used to be a book about things, written by people who had handled them. The entry recorded a provenance, a condition, an attribution, and a set of measurements, and the entry's authority came from the fact that someone had stood in front of the object and decided. Digitisation was sold as access, and it delivered images, but it also changed what a catalogue entry is for. The book was a argument as well as a list, and the argument was carried by the sentences between the entries.

An online record is read by people who will never see the object, and it is also read by software. That second audience is the one that changed the form. A record has to be structured so that a search engine can find it, a linked-data graph can connect it, and a researcher can download it in bulk. The prose that used to carry the curator's judgement has been replaced by fields that carry values, and the values are easier to aggregate and harder to argue with. A query that returns four thousand objects is an answer to a question nobody asked, and the curator's work of selection disappears into the result set.

Gains are real. A scholar in another country can now compare a hundred objects instead of the six in a printed catalogue, and provenance research on looted art has advanced because records that were once locked in drawers are now queryable. Attribution disputes that used to depend on who was allowed into the store room now depend on who can see the high-resolution image, which is a different and better kind of gatekeeping.

Losses are subtler. A field labelled 'date' cannot say that the date is a guess, and a field labelled 'artist' cannot carry the sentence 'attributed to, on the evidence of the underdrawing'. Museums have conventions for uncertainty, but those conventions live in prose, and prose is what the database replaced. The result is a record that looks more certain than the curator is.

Some institutions have responded by keeping a free-text note field, and the note field is where the old catalogue survives. It is also the field that search engines ignore, which means the nuance is present and invisible. A better design would make uncertainty a first-class value, queryable and displayable, rather than a sentence appended to a structured record. Making uncertainty queryable is a technical problem with a technical solution, and the institutions that have solved it are the ones with a research department rather than only a collections department.

Deeper still is what a catalogue is for. If it is a finding aid, then fields and links are the right form, and the prose is decoration. If it is a scholarly argument about objects, then the form has to carry doubt, and the current standard does not. Museums are choosing the first without quite admitting that they have given up the second, and the choice is being made by database designers rather than by curators.
