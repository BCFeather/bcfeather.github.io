Ships have been leaving the ports of Odesa, Chornomorsk and Pivdennyi since August, under an agreement signed in Istanbul in July. The deal allowed Ukrainian grain to be exported through a corridor the Russian navy promised to leave alone, and it allowed inspectors from four parties to check each vessel as it passed through the Bosphorus. Before that agreement the ports had been closed by the threat of mines and by the presence of warships offshore, and the silos behind them were full to the roof with a harvest that had nowhere to go. Traders were paying storage charges on grain they could not sell, and farmers were asking whether it was worth harvesting the next crop at all.

A first term of one hundred and twenty days was due to expire on 19 November. In the week before that date traffic through the corridor slowed to a handful of vessels a day, and the queue of loaded ships waiting for inspection grew longer by the hour. Insurers had already begun to ask what would happen to a vessel that was still inside the corridor when the agreement lapsed, and the answer they received gave them little comfort.

On 17 November the United Nations and Turkey announced an extension for a further one hundred and twenty days. The announcement was short and said nothing about whether the terms had altered. Russia had complained for weeks that the parallel agreement covering its own food and fertiliser exports was being honoured in name only, and that restrictions on its banks and shipping still made sales difficult. Its foreign ministry had also said that the corridor was being used for military purposes, a charge that Kyiv and the United Nations rejected.

Stakes were visible in the price of wheat. In the spring, before the corridor opened, the price had risen by half and the fear in importing countries was of a shortage rather than of a cost. The months since August brought the price down, and news of the extension moved it a little further. Buyers in Egypt, Indonesia and East Africa had been watching the expiry date as closely as any finance ministry watched a bond auction.

A corridor of this kind is a narrow arrangement inside a wide war, and it depends on goodwill that either side can withdraw at short notice. Insurance for the ships remains expensive, crews remain unwilling to sail into a war zone, and the inspections are slow by design, because each side wants assurance that no cargo of weapons passes under a cargo of corn. Only a fraction of what the ports could load is actually moving, and the silos are still full, which is why the extension mattered more to the exporting side than to the ships themselves.

For the farmers of southern Ukraine the extension meant a second season of selling at a price rather than storing at a loss. For importers in the Horn of Africa and in North Africa it meant the ships would keep coming through the winter. Neither group has any influence over the next expiry date, and both will read the news again in March.
