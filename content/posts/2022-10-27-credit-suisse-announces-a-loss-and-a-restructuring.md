Credit Suisse published results on 27 October, and it lost 4.03 billion Swiss francs in the third quarter. Revenue fell 30 per cent, and the loss was larger than any the bank had reported since 2008. The figures included a charge of 1.5 billion for the settlement of a case in America and a write-down on leveraged loans. The bank's shares had fallen 45 per cent since the start of the year, and they fell further on the day.

A restructuring was announced alongside the results. It would cut 9,000 jobs by 2025, which is 17 per cent of its staff, and it would separate its investment bank from the rest of the group. It planned to raise 4 billion francs in a rights issue, and it said it would wind down most of its securitized products business.

In 2022 it was convicted in Switzerland over money laundering tied to a Bulgarian cocaine network, and it was under investigation in America for its dealings with investors in Mozambique. The history behind the loss was long. The bank had been fined for helping clients evade American taxes in 2014, and it had settled a case over Mozambican debt in 2021. The American case concerned the sale of mortgage-backed bonds before the financial crisis.

Capital was thin. Its common equity tier one ratio was 12.6 per cent, which is above the minimum but below its own target. The bank had lost 4 billion francs of market value in the year, and its shares had fallen 60 per cent. The rights issue was priced at a 30 per cent discount, and it was backed by the Saudi National Bank.

Clients had withdrawn money. The bank said outflows were 12 billion francs in the quarter, and the figure was larger in the weeks before the announcement. Its wealth management business, which is the part it intended to keep, lost 8 billion of assets, and the bank said the outflows had stabilized in October. The restructuring was the third attempt in three years. The previous plans had cut costs and had not fixed the investment bank, which is the unit that produced the losses. This plan was different in that it shrank the unit rather than trying to make it profitable, and the bank said it would focus on wealth management and on its Swiss business. The wealth business had 1.5 trillion francs of assets, and the bank said it was the part worth keeping.

Analysts said the franchise had been damaged in a way that a capital raise could not repair. The shares fell 20 per cent on the day and recovered on the news of the Saudi investment. The bank became the subject of merger speculation, and the Swiss government said it was monitoring the situation. The bank was taken over by UBS in March 2023, in a deal arranged by the government. UBS paid 3 billion francs for a bank that had been worth 60 billion five years earlier, and the state guaranteed 9 billion of losses. The combined bank became the largest in Europe, and it cut thousands of jobs.
