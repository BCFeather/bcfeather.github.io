description: "Two high-profile superconductor claims collapsed within a year, and the machinery that caught them is slower and more expensive than the machinery that produced them."
---

A claim of room-temperature superconductivity attracts attention faster than any other result in physics, because a material that conducts without resistance at ambient conditions would rewire the electricity grid. Two such claims have collapsed in the past three years, one from a laboratory in Rochester and one from a group in Korea, and both collapses followed the same sequence: a striking measurement, a wave of replication attempts, and a failure that the original authors explained rather than accepted. The first claim produced three retractions across two journals, and the second produced a wave of attempted replications that never found the effect.

The replication attempts are the part of the system that worked. A superconductivity claim is falsifiable in a specific way, because the Meissner effect, the resistance drop and the heat capacity anomaly have to appear together. A group that measures one of them and not the others has a partial result, and a group that measures resistance alone has a measurement that can be produced by several artefacts. A resistance measurement can be produced by a contact that is not properly attached, and a heat capacity measurement requires a sample of known mass.

Journals are the slow part. A paper that reports a spectacular result can take months to review and weeks to publish, and the review process is not designed to audit raw data. Several publishers have begun to require the deposition of raw data for claims of this kind, which is a change that would have caught at least one of the two cases.

The incentive structure is the deeper cause. A career is built on visible results, a spectacular claim produces visibility, and the cost of being wrong arrives years later and is shared with co-authors. That is a system that rewards the claim and underprices the check, and no amount of editorial policy changes the incentive unless the check is rewarded too. Two of the retracted papers had co-authors who had left the laboratory before the retraction, and the correction process took longer than the review.

Paper mills are the industrial version of the same problem. Firms that sell authorship on fabricated papers operate at a scale that individual retractions cannot address, and the journals that have been most affected are the ones with publication fees and short review times. A publisher that charges for acceptance and pays reviewers nothing has built a market for exactly that product. A fabricated paper costs a few thousand dollars to commission and sells for a few thousand more, and the journals that publish it are not the ones that matter.

What has improved is the infrastructure of correction. Preprint servers let a claim circulate before review, community forums let specialists pick it apart, and several journals now publish replication studies as a category. The correction is still slower than the error, and the distance between them has narrowed only because the informal system has become faster than the formal one. The replication studies that settle a question are often published weeks after the claim, and the news that reports the claim never reports the replication.
