The Atlantic meridional overturning circulation is a conveyor that carries warm surface water north and cold deep water south, and it is responsible for the mild winters of western Europe. It moves about eighteen million tonnes of water per second, a thousand times the flow of the Amazon, and it has been measured continuously only since 2004, when an array of instruments was anchored across the basin at twenty-six degrees north. The array was designed for four years and has been renewed eight times, which is a measure of how much the question matters to the countries that pay for it.

The array measures a quantity that cannot be observed from a satellite. Temperature and salinity are visible from orbit, and the overturning is a transport that has to be inferred from the density differences between the eastern and western sides of the basin. The instruments resolve that difference with moorings, and a cable on the sea floor measures the pressure gradient that drives the flow. A mooring that is recovered and redeployed every eighteen months carries an instrument package that is calibrated against a laboratory standard before it goes back.

Twenty years is short for a circulation that varies on decadal timescales. The record shows a seasonal cycle, a year-to-year wobble, and no unambiguous trend, which is not the same as stability. A system with a large natural variability can hide a trend for decades, and the models that project a weakening in a warming climate are more confident than the observations are. A trend of one Sverdrup per decade would be invisible in a record with a standard deviation three times as large.

Palaeoclimate is the other source of evidence. Ice cores and sediment records show abrupt changes in the circulation at the end of the last ice age, with temperature shifts in the North Atlantic of several degrees within decades. Those events are the reason the circulation is treated as a threshold rather than as a dial, and thresholds are what a twenty-year record cannot rule out. An abrupt change in the past was accompanied by ice-rafted debris, and the sediment record is the only place where the timing is resolved to a decade.

Fresh water is the mechanism that would push the system. A warmer North Atlantic increases rainfall and melts Greenland, and fresh water is less dense, so it stabilizes the surface layer and weakens the sinking. The Greenland ice sheet has been losing mass since the 1990s, and the freshwater flux is the variable that the next generation of models is trying to constrain. A freshwater anomaly that persists for a decade is the kind of forcing that a model can simulate and an observation array can only watch.

Statistical analyses published in 2023 and 2024 argued that a collapse within this century cannot be excluded, and the observational community has been careful about those claims. A circulation that shuts down would cool Europe, raise sea level on the American east coast and shift the monsoon, and the cost of being wrong in either direction is large enough that the monitoring array has been funded again. A collapse that is slow enough to be adaptive is a collapse that a farmer in Brittany will experience as a change in rainfall rather than as a catastrophe.
