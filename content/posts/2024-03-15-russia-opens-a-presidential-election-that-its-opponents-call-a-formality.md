description: "Polls opened across Russia on 15 March for a three-day presidential election in which Vladimir Putin faced three token candidates, with independent monitoring absent and opposition figures barred or in prison."
---Polling stations opened across Russia on the fifteenth of March for a presidential election held over three days, the first since a constitutional amendment in 2020 reset the term counters and allowed President Vladimir Putin to run again. Ballot listed four candidates: Putin, who ran as an independent, and three nominees from parties that support the government, representing the Communist Party, the nationalist Liberal Democratic Party and a new party called New People.

Opposition that might have contested the result was absent by design. Boris Nadezhdin, an anti-war candidate, was barred by the central election commission in February after it found irregularities in the signatures he had collected, and his appeal was rejected. Alexei Navalny had been in prison and died there in the middle of February. Several journalists and activists who had said they would monitor the vote were detained in the weeks before it.

Monitoring that would ordinarily be provided by international organizations was missing. The OSCE declined to send observers after Russia invited a smaller mission than the organization requires, and the organization said the conditions for a genuine assessment were not present. Domestic monitors from independent groups were largely excluded, and the few who attempted to report on turnout faced police visits and administrative charges.

Voting was organized to produce a high turnout as well as a high result. Election was extended to three days, electronic voting was offered in most regions, and authorities offered prizes and concerts at polling stations. In the border regions of Kursk, Belgorod and Bryansk, where Ukrainian drones and shelling have been active, voting was moved indoors and officials said security required it. Residents of occupied parts of Ukraine were bused to polling stations in Russia, a practice that Kyiv and Western governments described as illegal.

Results announced on the eighteenth of March gave Putin 87.28 per cent of the vote on a turnout of 77 per cent, both of which were records for the post-Soviet period. Communist candidate Nikolai Kharitonov took about four per cent. Officials said the result showed that the country was united behind its leadership in a war, and opposition figures abroad said the figures demonstrated that the vote had been an exercise in managed turnout rather than an election.

Previous result is the standard against which this one was measured. In 2018 Putin took 76.7 per cent on a turnout of 67 per cent, and officials presented the figures as evidence of genuine support rather than of control of the ballot. The 2020 amendments to the constitution, which were approved in a referendum that independent observers described as conducted without meaningful debate, reset his term count to zero and added provisions on social policy that were used to mobilize support.

The Consequence of the fifteenth of March is six more years of the same government, which in practice means a war that continues and a system that has been narrowed further. Putin will be seventy-two at the start of his next term and seventy-eight at its end. Succession, which has been the central question of Russian politics for a decade, has been postponed again, and the election offered no mechanism to answer it. What the weekend produced was a number and a mandate. What it did not produce was any indication of who governs after him, or how the system would choose.
