By the autumn of 2021 the English vaccination programme faced a specific arithmetic. Two doses of ChAdOx1-S or BNT162b2 had been given to most adults, protection against symptomatic infection with the Delta variant had fallen, and a booster was being offered from 14 September to everyone over 50 and to younger adults with clinical risk. Whether the third dose restored protection, and how long the restoration lasted, could not be read off the antibody titres alone.

The answer came from the test-negative design that Public Health England had used through the pandemic. People who took a PCR test and tested positive were compared with people who took a test and tested negative, and vaccination status was read from the national register. The analysis covered 13 September to 5 December 2021, when Delta dominated, and measured both relative effectiveness against a two-dose course and absolute effectiveness against no vaccination. It appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 14 January 2022.

Relative effectiveness against symptomatic disease 14 to 34 days after a BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273 booster ran from about 85 to 95 per cent, whatever the primary course, and absolute effectiveness ran from 94 to 97 per cent across age groups. Against hospitalization or death, absolute effectiveness of a BNT162b2 booster ran from about 97 to 99 per cent, again across age groups and again independent of the primary course. Waning at 10 or more weeks was limited, and against severe endpoints there was no evidence of it at all.

Two features of the design make those numbers credible. Comparing testers with testers holds constant the behaviour that leads someone to seek a test, which differs sharply between the vaccinated and the unvaccinated, and the national register ties each swab to a vaccination record rather than to a recollection. The short window between booster and test also limits the confounding that accumulates over months.

Residual weaknesses are those of any observational design. People who accept a booster differ from those who decline in ways that registers do not capture, infection history is imperfectly known, and the endpoint of symptomatic disease depends on who gets tested. The follow-up ends at ten weeks, which is enough to see the absence of rapid waning and not enough to see what happens at twenty.

Linkage to the national immunization register meant that no dose was self-reported, which removes a source of error common in survey-based studies.
