The party was founded in 2019 by a former agricultural journalist, and it had never held a seat in a provincial council before the election of 15 March. It won about a fifth of the vote, became the largest party in all twelve provinces, and took sixteen of the seventy-five seats in the indirectly elected senate. The result was the largest swing in a Dutch election since the second world war, and the establishment that it displaced was the coalition that had governed since 2017. Its founder had been a journalist on a farming magazine, and the party's first campaign was run from a kitchen table in Overijssel.

Nitrogen was the issue that produced it. The government had committed to reducing nitrogen emissions by half by 2030, and the reductions would have required thousands of livestock farms to close or to shrink. The rules came from the European Union's habitat directive, and the Dutch courts had ordered the state to enforce them, and the enforcement fell on a sector that had been told for thirty years that it was the country's most efficient.

Its programme was thin and its message was simple. It promised to protect farmers, to reconsider the nitrogen targets, and to restore what it called the country's common sense, and it did not publish a detailed budget. Its leader, Caroline van der Plas, was a familiar face on television and a former member of a farming organization, and her campaign was conducted in barns and in market squares rather than in the usual debating halls.

Turnout was 58.6 per cent, which was two points higher than in 2019, and the increase was concentrated in rural municipalities where the previous election had been ignored. The counting took two days, and the result changed the composition of the senate that would be elected in May, which meant that the national government's ability to pass legislation would depend on a chamber elected by farmers who had just voted against it. The provinces elect the senate, and the senate can block legislation, which is the mechanism that turned a rural protest into a national constraint.

Within a fortnight the coalition said that the message had been received. The prime minister, Mark Rutte, acknowledged that the nitrogen policy had been designed in The Hague without the consent of the people who would have to live with it, and his agriculture minister resigned within a month. The government then began negotiations with the provinces over a fund that would pay farmers to relocate or to innovate, and the fund was the compromise that the election had bought.

The result was read across Europe as part of a pattern. Farmers' parties, rural protests and the rejection of environmental rules had become a political force in the Netherlands, in Germany and in France, and the parties that carried them had almost nothing else in common. The pattern was about who bore the cost of a transition, and about whether the people who bore it had a way of saying so, and the Dutch election was the clearest case of the year.
