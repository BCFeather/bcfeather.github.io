A container ship began loading at Ningbo-Zhoushan on 15 August for a voyage that its operator says will take about twenty days to reach Felixstowe. The route runs north of Siberia rather than through the Suez Canal, and the sailing marks the start of a weekly scheduled service rather than a one-off trial. Forty days is the typical transit on the southern route, and the difference is large enough to change how inventories are planned. The operator schedules departures once a week while the ice allows, and it has booked space for the next eight sailings.

Cargo explains the timing. Electric vehicles, lithium batteries and solar modules, the three categories Chinese exporters have pushed hardest this year, travel well in cold air, and the Arctic crossing cuts the emissions of the voyage roughly in half. A green product on a green corridor is a marketing argument as much as a logistical one, and European buyers who publish their supply-chain footprints have started to ask which route their goods took. The operator has also promised a fixed sailing day each week, a commitment that container lines usually avoid on routes where weather can close the water for days.

Alternatives have become expensive. Insurance for the Red Sea has risen, the Suez Canal's charges have climbed, and the Strait of Hormuz has been unreliable since the Iran war began in the spring. A route that avoids all three has an appeal that has nothing to do with distance, and the shipping companies that tested it in 2025 found that the savings in time outweighed the cost of icebreaker escort. European importers have noticed as well: a shorter route means less working capital tied up in containers that spend a month at sea.

The idea has a paper trail. Xi Jinping proposed the Polar Silk Road in 2017, the Arctic policy white paper of 2018 put it in writing, and several Chinese carriers ran commercial sailings in 2025. What changed this August is frequency: a weekly service turns an experiment into a timetable, and a timetable allows a European retailer to book space the way it books space on any other lane.

Limits remain. The passage is open for a few months each year, icebreakers escort convoys, insurance is bespoke, and the shallow straits along the way constrain the size of ships. A carrier that misses the window reroutes south and loses the advantage entirely, and a single bad season can wipe out the savings of three good ones. Russian authorities control the permits, charge for icebreaker support and can close the route on short notice, which makes the schedule a political arrangement as much as a nautical one.

Seasonal, escorted, thinly insured and short-lived each year: the Arctic lane will not replace Suez. It does give Chinese exporters a second door, and in a year when the first door has been expensive, a second door is worth building a timetable around. The more interesting question is what happens when the ice closes in November, because a weekly service that stops for seven months must keep its customers through the gap.
