President Biden arrived in Belfast on 11 April for a visit timed to the twenty-fifth anniversary of the Good Friday Agreement, which was signed on 10 April 1998. He met the British prime minister, the Irish prime minister and the five main parties in Northern Ireland, and he gave a speech at a university in the city that mixed genealogy with a warning. The anniversary itself had passed without a sitting assembly to mark it.

Signed on 10 April 1998, the agreement ended thirty years of violence by building a settlement on three legs, and each leg has been failing in a different way. The power-sharing executive at Stormont collapsed in February 2022 when the Democratic Unionist Party withdrew, and it has not returned because the party refuses to enter a government while the trade rules of the Windsor Framework stand. Turnout at the last election was the highest in the history of the devolved institutions, and the institutions have been empty since.

Biden's personal history was the reason for the length of the visit. His ancestors left Ireland in the nineteenth century, and he spent two days in Dublin and in counties Louth and Mayo meeting relatives whose names appear in the parish registers. He told the Dail that Ireland was part of his soul, and the Irish government, which has a substantial interest in American investment and in American support over Brexit, gave him a welcome that no other foreign leader receives. The visit was covered live on both sides of the border, and the Irish government used it to argue that the relationship with Washington is worth more than the quarrel with London.

Political message was delivered more carefully. The President said that the peace dividend required a functioning government and that the United States would support investment in a region whose economy was growing below the British average. He did not criticize the British government's handling of the protocol directly, and the British government did not ask him to, which is the arrangement that has kept the visit from becoming a dispute.

Security was heavy even by the standards of presidential travel. Roads were closed in Belfast, the police came from forces across the United Kingdom, and the visit cost the Northern Ireland budget an amount that the executive that would have paid it does not exist to approve. A small protest on the route held banners about American foreign policy, and a larger one was cancelled because the organizers could not guarantee safety, and they blamed the police for refusing to protect it.

What the anniversary demonstrated is how long a peace can hold without the institutions designed to carry it. The violence that the agreement ended has not returned, the border on the island has stayed open since Brexit was negotiated, and the generation that grew up after 1998 has no memory of the war. The settlement works in the streets and has stopped working in the buildings, and twenty-five years is long enough to see which of those facts matters more, and the next five will be spent finding out.
