Jupiter's irregular moons are relics, most of them captured or scattered early in the solar system's history, and their orbits encode that history. The problem is that they are small, far away and lost in Jupiter's glare. Most are known only as points of light with approximate orbits, and physical measurements such as size, shape and albedo are rare enough that a single well-characterized object is worth a paper. An occultation also fixes the position to a few tens of kilometres, which matters as much as the physical parameters for a mission that has to arrive at a specific point in space.

Kallichore is one of the few that matters practically, because it is the only irregular moon of Jupiter that the European Space Agency's Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer could reach on a close flyby. Its orbit and its size were poorly constrained, which makes any flyby planning speculative. A team combined Hubble photometry and astrometry with a multi-site stellar occultation campaign and observations from the 10.4-metre Gran Telescopio de Canarias, and reported the result in *Nature Astronomy* on 24 July 2026.

Orbital uncertainty fell by up to about 80 per cent, and the campaign produced a shape. Kallichore is elongated, with a minimum semi-axis ratio of 1.53 plus or minus 0.10, an area-equivalent diameter of 3.8 kilometres with an upper error of 2.3 and a lower error of 0.3, and a dark surface. The occultation is what makes the size measurement possible, since a star disappearing behind the moon gives a chord across its silhouette rather than a brightness estimate.

Shape and albedo carry information about origin. An elongated body with a dark surface is consistent with a captured planetesimal that has been battered rather than a fragment of a larger parent, and the axis ratio is the kind of number that models of collisional evolution can be tested against. A flyby would resolve the question directly, which is why the improved orbit matters as much as the physical parameters. A moon this dark is also hard to observe from the ground, and the campaign's success came from combining three independent techniques rather than from any one of them.

The uncertainties are still large. A single occultation chord constrains one projection, the albedo depends on an assumed phase function, and the diameter's asymmetric error bars show how much the geometry limits the answer. A body this small is also difficult to keep in a stable orbit around Jupiter, so the dynamical history is a model rather than a measurement.

The practical payoff is mission planning. A reduced orbit uncertainty turns a speculative flyby into a feasible one, and JUICE's trajectory designers can now ask whether Kallichore is reachable at an acceptable cost in propellant and time. A second occultation, from a different geometry, would tighten the shape further and settle whether the moon is a fragment or a captured body.
