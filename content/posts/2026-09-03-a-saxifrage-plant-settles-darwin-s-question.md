Charles Darwin spent the last decades of his life on plants, and the books are stranger than the reputation suggests. In 1875 he argued that two species of *Saxifraga*, alpine herbs with sticky hairs, might be carnivorous, trapping insects and absorbing nutrients from them. His experiments were inconclusive because he could not demonstrate digestion, and the claim sat in the literature as a curiosity for a century and a half.

A study reported in *Nature Communications* and summarized in *Science* on 3 September tests it with methods Darwin did not have. Zhang and colleagues combined field observation, chemical assays for digestive enzymes and genetic analysis of *Saxifraga candelabrum*, a species whose leaves are covered in glandular hairs and which is regularly found with insects stuck to them. The evidence points in the direction Darwin suspected.

Three findings carry the argument. The plant releases enzymes that break down insect tissue, it traps small insects while avoiding the pollinators that visit its flowers, and it absorbs nitrogen from captured prey, with labelling showing the nutrient appearing in floral tissue. Carnivory in the strict sense requires all three, since a plant that traps insects without digesting them is a plant with sticky leaves and nothing more.

The selective trapping is the elegant part. A plant that eats its pollinators loses more than it gains, and the field data show that the victims are small walkers, while the larger flying insects that visit flowers escape. Selection on hair geometry and on the timing of nectar production could produce that bias, and the study supports the interpretation with the composition of the catch.

Genomic comparison puts the species in company. Its genome shares evolutionary changes with other carnivorous plants, including alterations in genes for nutrient transport and for the secretion of digestive enzymes, which suggests that the capacity has been assembled more than once in flowering plants. Carnivory is expensive, requiring enzymes, traps and the loss of photosynthetic area, and it tends to appear in nutrient-poor habitats such as alpine soils.

Alpine habitats are exactly where a plant might benefit from catching insects, since nitrogen is scarce and the growing season is short. The species lives on cliffs and scree where soils are thin, and the flowers are the sink to which nitrogen is directed, which connects the strategy to reproduction rather than to growth alone. That connection is speculative and follows from the tissue in which the label accumulated, which was the flower and not the leaf.

A broader claim follows, which is that carnivory may be more common than the botanical catalogue suggests. Trapping hairs and digestive secretions are easy to overlook, and species that catch a few insects per season would never be described as carnivorous by a botanist relying on visual inspection. The tools now exist to test the question in other genera, and the paper provides a template that a laboratory could apply without a decade of field work.
