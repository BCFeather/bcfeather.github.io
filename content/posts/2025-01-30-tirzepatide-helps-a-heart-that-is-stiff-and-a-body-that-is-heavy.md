Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction is the form that appears in older patients, in women more often than men, and in people with obesity, diabetes and hypertension. The heart muscle contracts normally and the ventricle does not fill well, so the pressure inside rises with exercise and the patient becomes breathless. Trials of drugs that work in the other form of heart failure failed in this one for twenty years.

Two developments changed the field. The first was the recognition that obesity is part of the mechanism: fat tissue around the heart and in the pericardium stiffens the ventricle, and inflammatory signals from adipose tissue add to the problem. The second was the arrival of incretin drugs that produce weight loss of 15 to 20 per cent, which is more than any lifestyle programme has achieved in a trial.

SUMMIT, reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 30 January, tested tirzepatide, a dual agonist of the GIP and GLP-1 receptors, in patients with heart failure and preserved ejection fraction who had a body mass index of 30 or above, which is the threshold for obesity. The primary endpoint was the change in the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire clinical summary score at 52 weeks, and the treated group improved by 19.5 points.

Secondary endpoints told the same story. Exercise capacity measured on a six-minute walk improved, the inflammatory marker CRP fell with the weight loss, and the composite of cardiovascular death and worsening heart failure was lower in the treated group. The trial was not powered for mortality, and the composite result is the kind that a larger trial confirms rather than a finding that changes a guideline on its own.

The safety profile is the one the class carries. Gastrointestinal adverse events were the most common reason for discontinuation, and the rate of discontinuation in the treated arm was higher than in the placebo arm, which is the cost that every prescription for this class carries. Gallbladder events and the loss of lean mass during rapid weight loss are the concerns that a heart failure population raises more acutely than a diabetes population.

What the trial changes for a patient is the absence of an alternative. A person with this diagnosis has been treated with diuretics, with blood pressure control, with exercise and with weight loss advice, and the advice produces five per cent loss on a good year. A drug that produces fifteen per cent and improves the symptom score is a different offer, and the question becomes who pays for it and for how long.

What it changes for the field is the definition of the disease. A condition that was described as a disorder of diastolic function is now being described, in part, as a disorder of adipose tissue and inflammation, and the drugs that treat the adipose tissue also treat the symptoms. Whether that distinction survives the next decade of trials is the question that the class has opened.
