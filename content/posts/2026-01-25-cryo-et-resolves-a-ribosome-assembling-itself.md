Ribosomes assemble from three RNA strands and more than fifty proteins, and the order of assembly has been inferred from structures caught at the end. A paper published this month in *Nature* used cryo-electron tomography on intact bacterial cells to capture seven intermediate stages, and the sequence it reveals differs from the textbook order.

The method is the advance. Cryo-electron tomography freezes a cell in vitreous ice and images it from many angles, producing a three-dimensional reconstruction without extracting the complex from its environment. Particle picking then classifies the ribosome-like objects by their shape and produces an average for each class.

Resolution reached 4.5 angstroms in the best class, which is enough to place protein densities but not to see side chains. That is sufficient to identify which ribosomal proteins are present at each stage, because each has a distinctive shape and mass.

The sequence starts where the textbook does, with the RNA scaffold, and diverges at the middle. Two small ribosomal proteins that the textbook places late bind early and nucleate the assembly of a domain, and one large protein that the textbook places early binds only after the domain is folded. The paper describes this as a dependency graph rather than a linear order.

Kinetics support the structural claim. The team tagged three proteins with a fluorescent label and measured their incorporation rates in live cells, and the rates match the order derived from the tomography within a factor of two.

Coexisting intermediates are the most interesting observation. About 12 per cent of the particles are partly assembled, and the distribution across stages is not the smooth curve a single-pathway model predicts. The paper argues for parallel assembly pathways that converge.

Limitations are clear. The classification averages are limited by the number of particles in each class, and the least populated stage has 340 particles, which is enough for a 12-angstrom map and not for a reliable atomic model. The paper reports that map at lower resolution and does not overinterpret it.

Sample thickness is the technical limit. A cell thicker than 300 nanometres cannot be imaged without sectioning, and the sections must be cut frozen and transferred without devitrification. The team used a focused ion beam to mill 180-nanometre lamellae, and 40 per cent of the prepared lamellae were usable.

The result's importance is methodological as much as biological. Assembly intermediates are transient and rare, and a method that captures them in a native cell at 4.5 angstroms is what makes the study possible; the specific order it reports is the first of many such questions the technique can now address.

Particle classification is where the interpretation lives. A tomogram contains thousands of ribosome-like objects at different stages, and the sorting is done by an algorithm that groups them by shape and then averages within each group. A grouping error merges two stages and produces an average that belongs to neither, which is why the team validated each class against a chemical footprint of which proteins were present.

For a reader, the result illustrates what cryo-electron tomography has changed. Structures of molecular machines used to be snapshots of purified, crystallized states; now they are frames from a cellular film, and the interesting biology is often in the transitions rather than in the endpoints.
