Gentle stroking produces a distinct sensation that is not pain and not itch, and the spinal circuitry that carries it has been unknown because the response is easy to confuse with other somatosensory signals. A neuropeptide has now been identified as its transmitter. The somatosensory system has been mapped for pain and itch in detail and for pleasant touch only in outline, and the missing element was the transmitter rather than the anatomy. A neuropeptide is easier to manipulate than a circuit, because a genetic deletion of the ligand or the receptor removes the signal without touching the anatomy of the spinal cord.

A study in mice found that sensory neurons expressing prokineticin 2 signal to neurons expressing prokineticin receptor 2 in the spinal cord, and that removing either the ligand or the receptor abolished the response to pleasant touch without affecting pain or itch. The paper appeared in *Science*, 28 April 2022.

The behavioural assay is a conditioned place preference in which an animal learns to return to a place where it was stroked. Mice without the pathway did not develop the preference, and they responded normally to painful and itchy stimuli, which is the specificity that the claim requires. A conditioned place preference measures affect and not sensation, and an animal that has no preference may still feel the stroke, which is a limitation the authors acknowledge.

The receptor neurons respond most vigorously to gentle stroking and encode a reward value, which is a description of a population that is doing something more than reporting a stimulus. A neuron that signals reward is part of the affective and not the discriminative side of touch. A reward value can be assigned only if the brain knows where the stroke is coming from, so the spinal population must carry both a location and a valence, and the study measures the second.

A behavioural consequence is the striking part. Mutant mice showed profound impairments in stress responses and in prosocial behaviours, which suggests that the experience of pleasant touch is a requirement for normal social development and not a luxury. A population that encodes reward is a population that can be suppressed when the social context makes contact unwanted, and that flexibility is what a reward signal provides.

A mouse is not a human, and the human literature on touch deprivation points in the same direction without establishing the same circuit. The implication for developmental psychology is that affectionate contact is a signal to the nervous system and not only a cultural practice. A developmental requirement for affectionate touch is consistent with the literature on institutionalized children, who show altered social behaviour years after the deprivation ends.

A clinical direction is autism and social avoidance, where the response to touch is often altered, and the finding gives a molecular handle on a phenomenon that has been described behaviourally for decades. A drug that targets the receptor would have to be given chronically, and the receptor is expressed in the gut and the reproductive system as well as the spinal cord, which limits the specificity.
