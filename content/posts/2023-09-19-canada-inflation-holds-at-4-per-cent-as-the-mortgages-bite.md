title: "Canada inflation holds at 4 per cent as the mortgages bite"
date: "2023-09-19T09:20:00+08:00"

description: "The headline rate rose from 3.3 per cent on the back of the petrol prices, and the measure of the mortgage interest costs reached its highest since the 1980s."
---

Canadian consumer prices rose 4 per cent in the year to August, Statistics Canada said on 19 September, up from 3.3 per cent. The core measures that the central bank follows averaged about 3.6 per cent. The figure was in line with the forecast, and the measure has stayed between three and four per cent since the spring after peaking at 8.1 last year.

Petrol propped up the headline. The price of the fuel rose by about 10 per cent on the year after a rise of 3 per cent in July, and the carbon levy added to the pump price in the provinces that have one. A headline that follows the barrel is a headline that the policy cannot address. Gasoline contributed about half of the monthly increase, and the analysts expect the effect to reverse in the autumn as the crude prices soften.

Mortgage interest is the unusual line in the report. The cost of the loans rose by about 30 per cent over the year, the fastest since the 1980s, because the borrowers are renewing at rates that are far above the ones they signed. A renewal that arrives is a household budget that breaks. About 4.5 million mortgages will be renewed by the end of next year, and the average rate on the new loans is about two points above the old ones.

Groceries are the line the shoppers see. The food prices rose by about 6 per cent and the fresh vegetables by about 10, and the supermarkets have said the suppliers have not yet passed the lower freight costs through. A grocery bill that stays high is an inflation that the shopper feels weekly. The food purchased from the stores rose by about 6 per cent, and the restaurant prices by about 5, both slower than in the spring. The shelter costs are the largest single component of the basket, and the rent itself rose by about 6 per cent as the population grew faster than the housing stock.

Policy is being guided by the core. The governor has argued that the mortgage costs are a mechanical result of the past increases and that the policy should look through them, which is the reason the bank held the rate at 5 per cent in September. A measure that is excluded is a rate that can be argued down. The bank has raised the rate ten times since the spring of last year, and the governor has said it will hold until the core is clearly falling. The bank's next decision is in October, and the economists expect another hold if the core stays near three and a half per cent.

What the month shows is that the inflation is being pushed by the two prices the policy cannot touch, and the core is the number that decides the next move. A rate that is argued down is a rate that the borrowers will feel anyway, and the renewals are the transmission that the policy cannot avoid.
