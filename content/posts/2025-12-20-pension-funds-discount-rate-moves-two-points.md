A pension fund's liabilities are a promise discounted to today, and the rate used to discount them determines whether the fund looks solvent. A public fund lowered its assumption this month from 7 to 5 per cent, and the funded ratio fell from 78 to 61 per cent with no change in benefits.

The arithmetic is sensitive in both directions. A 100-basis-point fall in the discount rate raises a 20-year liability by 15 per cent, and the fund's liability is 4.2 billion, so the change added 900 million.

Actuaries' practice is the contested part. A public plan discounts at the expected return on assets, which lets a fund improve its funded ratio by investing in riskier assets. An economist's view discounts at a rate that reflects the promise's risk, which is closer to a government bond's yield.

Contribution rises are the consequence. A 61 per cent funded ratio under the fund's policy triggers a 6-point rise in the employer contribution over eight years, which for a city means 40 million a year. A 20-year liability's duration is 12 years, and a 16 per cent rise in its value is the arithmetic that a two-point change produces.

Peer assumptions are the reference a board actually uses. A neighbouring fund retains 7.25 per cent and a 74 per cent ratio, and its actuary's report notes that the assumption is above the fund's own 10-year realized return of 6.1.

Benefit design is the alternative to contributions. A fund can raise the retirement age or change the formula for new hires, and the board's decision this month kept benefits intact and deferred the choice to next year's negotiation.

The market's view is a third input. The fund's bond allocation now yields 4.2 per cent, and a portfolio that returns 7 per cent must earn 9 on its equity sleeve, which is above most long-run forecasts.

A discount rate is an assumption about the future and a liability is a promise about it, and the two are linked by a member's life expectancy. A 20-year liability's duration is 12 years, and a rate change's effect is proportional to that duration.

Contribution policy is where the change lands. A funded ratio below a threshold triggers a contribution schedule, and a schedule spread over eight years halves the annual increase at the cost of paying more later. A board chooses between a larger bill now and a larger one later.

A fund's assumption is compared with its peers and not with a market. A public plan that discounts at 5 per cent and a private plan that discounts at 4.5 are measuring different promises, and a board that changes its rate is making a statement about its own credibility as much as about its arithmetic.

An assumption is a policy, and a discount rate is the clearest case of it. A discount rate is a number that a board chooses, and a plan's funded status is what that choice produces rather than a fact about its promises.
