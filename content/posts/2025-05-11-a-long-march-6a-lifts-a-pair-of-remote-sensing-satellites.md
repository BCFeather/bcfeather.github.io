At 21:27 on the evening of 11 May, a Long March 6A rose from the Taiyuan Satellite Launch Centre in Shanxi and placed the second group of Yaogan-40 satellites into orbit. The launch was routine in the way that Chinese launches have become routine, which is the point: it was the latest in a series that has turned a rare event into a schedule.

The vehicle itself is the interesting part. The Long March 6A is the first Chinese medium-lift rocket to combine solid strap-on boosters with liquid-fuelled core stages, a hybrid configuration that engineers spent years arguing over. Liquid engines give the core throttle control and a higher specific impulse; solid boosters give the first stage a large, cheap, instantly available thrust at liftoff. Bundling the two is a compromise, and the compromise is what makes the vehicle flexible enough to serve a wide band of payload masses without a dedicated design for each. It also makes the vehicle cheaper to fly than a purely liquid design of the same class, which is the arithmetic that matters when a customer is launching satellites by the dozen.

That flexibility matters for a constellation programme. The Yaogan series is usually described as remote-sensing, and the phrase covers optical imaging, radar, electronic intelligence and signals work depending on which group is being launched. The 40th series appears to be an imaging family, and this second group joins a first that went up in 2024. Constellations of this kind are built group by group, and each group launch is a small increment in revisit time, which is the metric that actually matters: how often the same patch of ground can be looked at again.

Revisit time is what turns imagery into a service. A satellite that passes over a flood plain once every four days is useful for mapping. One that passes every six hours is useful for disaster response, for shipping, for agriculture, and for the other things that governments and insurers want to see quickly. The economics of a constellation are driven by the number of satellites and the cost per kilogram to orbit, and both of those are moving in the same direction.

There is a second order of interest in the cadence itself. China launched this vehicle twice in the space of a few weeks in this configuration, and the pace at Taiyuan has been rising across the board. Launch cadence is a physical expression of industrial capacity: it requires propellant production, range scheduling, payload integration and a supply chain that can keep up. A country that can launch on a two-week rhythm in one vehicle family can also launch on that rhythm in a crisis, and militaries notice this.

The satellites themselves are not the story. The story is that a medium-lift rocket with an unusual architecture has moved from a test article into a workhorse, and that the workhorse is being used to build a constellation whose value lies in how often it comes back. Both of those are long-term projects, and both of them just took another step on an ordinary Tuesday night in May.
