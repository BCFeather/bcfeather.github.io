Narcolepsy type 1 is a disease of missing neurons. The cells that make orexin, the peptide that keeps a person awake and holds muscle tone in place, are destroyed by an immune process, and patients are left with daytime sleepiness that no stimulant fully treats and with cataplexy, the sudden loss of tone that follows strong emotion. Every approved drug works downstream of the missing signal.

Replacing the signal directly was the obvious idea and a difficult one, because orexin receptors are widespread and a drug that hits them systemically can disturb sleep, blood pressure and bladder function. Oveporexton is an oral agonist of the orexin 2 receptor, and two phase 3 trials, First Light and Radiant Light, tested it in 168 and 105 participants with narcolepsy type 1 over twelve weeks. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 9 September. The receptor subtypes matter, since the 2 receptor carries most of the arousal signal and agonizing it selectively avoids the gut effects of a broader agonist.

Wakefulness moved in the right direction and by a lot. Mean sleep latency on the maintenance of wakefulness test improved by 14.3 to 19.8 minutes depending on dose, against a change of minus 0.4 to minus 0.8 minutes on placebo, with adjusted *P*<0.001 for every comparison. The Epworth Sleepiness Scale fell by 9.7 to 11.8 points, against 1.5 to 1.7 on placebo, which is the difference between falling asleep at a desk and not.

Cataplexy responded most strongly. Median reductions in weekly cataplexy rates ranged from 79.0 to 88.8 per cent, against 27.7 to 39.1 per cent on placebo, again with adjusted *P*<0.001 throughout. A placebo response of that size is worth noting, since it reflects how much attention and expectation shape a symptom that patients report themselves. A drug that raises alertness does not restore lost neurons, so the effect lasts as long as the drug does and stops when it is withdrawn.

Tolerability is the price of replacing a signal that the body normally delivers in pulses. Adverse events occurred in 86 to 89 per cent of participants on the drug, against 43 to 54 per cent on placebo, and the common ones were increased urinary frequency and transient insomnia, affecting most participants on active treatment. Neither is dangerous, and both are the kind of effect that decides whether a patient keeps taking a drug for decades.

Twelve weeks is a short window for a lifelong disease, and the trials cannot say whether tolerance develops or whether the bladder effect persists. The mechanistic argument is strong, since this is the first class to act on the missing pathway itself, and the practical argument will be settled by extension studies and by how patients weigh eight hours of alertness against a night of broken sleep.
