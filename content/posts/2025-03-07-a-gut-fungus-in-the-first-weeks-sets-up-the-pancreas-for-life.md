title: "Gut fungus in the first weeks sets up the pancreas for life"
date: "2025-03-07T20:40:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A study in *Science* on 7 March reports that mice need a microbial signal in a narrow neonatal window to build a full complement of insulin-producing cells, and that a fungus common in the infant gut supplies it."
---

A fetal pancreas is built before birth, but the population of beta cells that produce insulin continues to expand and to mature in the weeks afterwards. Epidemiologists have noticed for years that disruptions in that period, including antibiotic use in a newborn, correlate with a higher risk of type 1 diabetes later in childhood, and the mechanism behind the correlation had not been identified, so the clinical advice had to rest on an association that nobody could explain.

Work began with germ-free mice, which are raised without any microbes at all. Those animals developed fewer beta cells, smaller islets and a glucose response that stayed impaired into adulthood, even after they were given a normal microbiota later. The deficit was set during a developmental window and could not be reversed afterwards, which is the finding that turns a correlation into a mechanism.

Filling the window required a specific organism, and the team identified *Candida dubliniensis*, a fungus that colonizes the infant gut, as sufficient to restore beta cell development, and it identified several bacteria that did the same. The effect ran through macrophages that reside in the islets, which the microbiota recruits and which in turn support the growth of the beta cells.

Cell wall is the part that carries the signal. *C. dubliniensis* has a wall composition that the immune system reads differently from the walls of related species, and the fungal component that produced the effect was a polysaccharide rather than a live organism. A purified molecule that works without colonization would be the version that a clinic could use.

Human evidence accompanied the mouse work. The authors examined tissue from infants and found a population of islet macrophages with the same markers, and they found that the fungi they had identified appear in the gut of human infants in the same period, which places the window and the organism in the same place in a human as in a mouse. The human data are correlative, and the program that they describe is the one that the mouse experiment tested.

Implications for newborn care are direct. Antibiotics given to a premature infant save lives and remove the microbes that the study says are needed, and the question is whether a course can be shortened, whether a probiotic or a purified wall component should be given alongside, and whether either would do any good in a infant whose gut has already been cleared. Those are clinical trials, and the paper argues that they should be run before the practice changes on its own.

What the result does for the field is to give the hygiene hypothesis a mechanism for one disease. The idea that early microbial exposure shapes the immune system has been supported by epidemiology and by association, and this paper names organisms, a cell type and a developmental window at which the exposure has to happen, which is the specificity that the hypothesis has lacked.
