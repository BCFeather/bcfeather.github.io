title: "Dapagliflozin helps after a valve is inserted"

description: "A trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 29 March found that dapagliflozin reduced heart failure events and death in patients who had just undergone a transcatheter aortic valve replacement, a setting in which the drug had not previously been tested."
---

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement is a procedure in which a new valve is delivered through a catheter and expanded inside the diseased one, and it has replaced open surgery for most patients over 75. The heart that has been carrying a stenotic valve is often damaged by years of pressure overload, and a fraction of the patients who survive the procedure develop heart failure in the months afterwards, which is the problem that the trial addressed.

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors were developed as diabetes drugs and then shown to reduce heart failure hospitalizations in patients with and without diabetes. The benefit appears within weeks of starting, does not depend on glucose lowering, and is attributed to effects on sodium handling, on filling pressures and on the kidney. A drug that works that quickly is a drug that might work in the peri-procedural period, which is where the trial placed it.

Trial randomized 620 patients to dapagliflozin and 637 to placebo, starting within a few days of the procedure, and followed them for a median of about a year. The primary endpoint was a composite of death, stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure hospitalization and urgent visits for heart failure, and it occurred in 15.0 per cent of the treated patients against 20.1 per cent of the placebo group, a hazard ratio of 0.72 with a confidence interval of 0.55 to 0.95 and a *P* value of 0.02.

Components behaved in the direction that the mechanism predicts. Heart failure hospitalization fell by about a third and the urgent visit component fell by more, while all-cause death was 7.8 per cent against 8.9 per cent, a hazard ratio of 0.87 that the trial was not powered to resolve. A drug that reduces the events that fill hospital beds without clearly reducing mortality is a drug that a health system will still buy, because the events are what the budget pays for.

Safety was consistent with the class. There was no excess of ketoacidosis, no excess of genital infection that reached statistical significance, and no excess of hypotension in a population that had just had a valve dilated. Volume depletion is the risk that matters in this population, and the trial reported that it did not materialize.

Result arrives at a moment when the class is being extended into every cardiac setting. Trials have shown benefit in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, with preserved ejection fraction, after myocardial infarction and in chronic kidney disease, and each new setting has widened a market that was already large. A trial of a drug in a procedural population is a trial that a guideline committee will read carefully, because the evidence base for periprocedural management is thin.

What the paper does not answer is the duration. The trial treated for a year, and the population is one that usually takes a diuretic and a beta blocker for life, so the question of whether to continue the drug is a question that the data cannot settle. The cost is also a question, and the answer depends on the price that a health system negotiates for a drug whose patent has years to run.
