description: "The Federal Reserve left its benchmark rate at 5.25 to 5.5 per cent on 20 September 2023 and signalled in its projections that borrowing costs would stay high through next year, a message that markets read as higher for longer."
---

The Federal Open Market Committee kept its target range unchanged at 5.25 to 5.5 per cent on 20 September, the second consecutive meeting without a change after eleven increases in eighteen months. The projections published with the decision showed most officials expecting one more increase this year and fewer cuts next year than they had forecast in June, which moved the median estimate for the end of 2024 up by half a percentage point. Jerome Powell, the chairman, said the committee was prepared to raise rates again if the data warranted it and would keep policy restrictive until inflation was clearly falling.

Data has been surprisingly strong. Consumer prices rose 3.7 per cent in the year to August, up from 3.2 per cent in June because of energy prices, and core inflation has been falling slowly. Unemployment is 3.8 per cent, the economy added more than a hundred thousand jobs a month through the summer, and retail sales have been growing. The committee raised its forecast for growth this year to 2.1 per cent, which is more than double what it expected in June, and it still expects inflation to fall without a recession.

A soft landing has become the base case, and nobody on the committee is sure it will hold. Powell described the economy as expanding at a solid pace and said the labour market was coming into better balance, with wage growth slowing and hiring returning to pre-pandemic levels. The risk he named is that consumers keep spending because the labour market is tight, and that the last mile of disinflation proves slower than the first, which would force the committee to raise rates and produce the recession it has been trying to avoid.

Markets adjusted without panic. Yields on two-year Treasuries rose to their highest level since 2006, the dollar strengthened, and equity indexes fell modestly and recovered within days. Traders moved their expectations for the first cut into the second half of next year, and mortgage rates followed the ten-year yield to a level not seen in more than twenty years. The financial conditions that the committee watches have tightened without a single rate change.

International consequences are what the chairman has been asked about at every meeting. Higher American rates pull capital towards the dollar and raise borrowing costs for countries that borrow in it, and several emerging markets have been intervening to support their currencies. The European Central Bank and the Bank of England have both stopped raising rates in the same month, and the Bank of Japan has been loosening its grip on bond yields, which leaves the Federal Reserve setting the price of money for the world.

A committee that has raised rates eleven times has now decided to wait, and its projections say it will wait for most of a year. The economy it is watching has been stronger than it expected, and the question the chairman cannot answer is whether that strength is the sign of a landing or the reason there will not be one.
