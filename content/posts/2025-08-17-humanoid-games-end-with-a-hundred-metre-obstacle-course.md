The first World Humanoid Robot Games opened at the Ice Ribbon arena in Beijing on the evening of 14 August and closed on the 16th, with 280 teams from 16 countries entered. The events were chosen so that a machine could fail in public: a 100-metre sprint, a 100-metre obstacle course, five-a-side football, boxing, and a set of household tasks. Entry was open to university teams, companies and hobbyist groups, which is why the field ranged from machines costing more than a car to ones assembled in a dormitory.

The obstacle course turned out to be the useful one. It put gravel, a narrow bridge, a knee-high barrier and a spiral staircase in sequence, and a good run took a little over two minutes. The machines that finished did so by walking slowly and re-planning often. The ones that fell did so at the bridge, where the surface gives way under a foot and the balance controller has to decide, in a few milliseconds, whether to trust its own estimate or the contact it can feel.

Football was the other test with a clear purpose. Five machines on each side, a ball, and no referee able to keep up meant that most of the play was about recovery: getting up after a collision, finding the ball again, and not walking into a teammate. A team from Beijing won the final, and the match was decided less by shooting than by how quickly the losing side could reorganize after a fall.

The sprint produced the cleanest numbers. The fastest machines now run at speeds that would have been a research result two years ago, and the limiting factor has moved from the motors to the feet: traction, the compliance of the sole, and the ability to absorb an impact without losing the next stride. Several teams replaced their feet between heats, which is the sort of tuning that only a competition schedule forces. The winning time in the sprint was under twenty seconds, and the difference between first and eighth place was smaller than the difference between the first and second attempts of the same machine.

Engineering teams arrived a month early to practise, which is an expensive way to prepare and an honest one. The value of the event is not the medals. It is that a machine designed in a laboratory meets a floor it did not model, and the failure appears in a video that everyone can watch.

Whether that translates into factory work is the open question. A warehouse floor is flatter than the obstacle course and far less forgiving of a dropped parcel. What the three days showed is that the walking problem is mostly solved and that the reliability problem, which is a different problem, has only started. A machine that can walk across gravel is not yet a machine that can be left alone with a pallet, and the gap between those two things is measured in maintenance hours rather than in intelligence.
