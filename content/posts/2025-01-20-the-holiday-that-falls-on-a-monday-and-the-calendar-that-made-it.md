America's holiday for Martin Luther King falls on the third Monday of January, which is the twentieth this year. The date is not the anniversary of his birth, which was the fifteenth, and the rule that sets it is a compromise that the Congress made in 1983 and the states ratified over the following decade.

Monday was the compromise. A holiday that falls on a fixed date produces a midweek day off, which the employers dislike and the retailers accept, and a holiday that is moved to a Monday produces a long weekend, which the travel industry prefers. The law that created the King holiday was signed with the Monday rule attached, and the same rule governs the birthdays of Washington and Columbus.

It is also the one holiday that a state can rename. Several states observe it under a name that combines the civil rights leader with a local figure, and a few observe it as a day of service rather than as a commemoration. The variation is a reminder that a federal holiday is a recommendation to the states rather than a command, and the states have used the discretion.

A day of service is the form that the holiday has taken in the last two decades. The volunteering that the organizations promote on the day is a way of making the commemoration active, and the argument behind it is that a holiday about a man who organized a movement should be a day on which people organize something. The argument is persuasive and the participation is uneven.

Its place in the American calendar is particular. It falls between the new year and the February holidays, in the coldest stretch of the winter, and it is the first long weekend of the year. The retail promotions that surround it are about mattresses and cars, which is the fate of every American holiday that does not have a religious content.

What the calendar does is decide which events a country will remember every year. A holiday is a repetition, and a repetition is the form of memory that a state can guarantee. The dates that are chosen and the rules that move them are a statement about what the country thinks a holiday is for, and the statement is made in the language of a Monday.

Other countries have faced the same arithmetic and answered it differently. A day that marks a birth produces a parade and a speech, and a day that marks a death produces a silence and a wreath, and the two rituals ask different things of the people who attend. The American version of this holiday has drifted towards the first, which is why the schools teach it with a worksheet and the volunteers staff a food bank.

For the historian, the interesting part is the gap between the event and the holiday. The man was killed in April, the bill was signed in November, and the first observance was in January. The calendar that remembers him is a construction of three decades, and the construction is what the country agreed to.
