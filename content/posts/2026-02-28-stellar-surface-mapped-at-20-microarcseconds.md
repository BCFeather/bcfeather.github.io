A star other than the Sun is a point of light to almost every telescope, because its angular diameter is milliarcseconds and atmospheric turbulence blurs anything smaller. An interferometric array combining four 8-metre telescopes resolved the surface of a red giant at 20 microarcseconds, and the image shows convection cells rather than a smooth disc.

The technique combines light rather than collecting it. Interferometry measures the phase difference between apertures separated by a baseline, and the resolution is set by the baseline, 200 metres here, rather than by the mirror. The array observes in the infrared, where the star is bright and the atmosphere is steadier.

Reconstruction is the computational work. The array records visibilities and closure phases on 30 baselines, and an image is reconstructed by fitting a model to those measurements. The reconstruction used a maximum-entropy prior and produced a map with 12 resolution elements across the disc.

The features are the result. The image shows 5 to 7 bright cells, each about 0.8 milliarcseconds across, which corresponds to a convective cell 30 times the size of a solar granule. A dark patch near the limb is a magnetic spot, cooler by 400 kelvin.

Comparing with the Sun is the frame. Solar convection cells are 1,000 kilometres across and last 10 minutes; a red giant's cells are 100 times larger and last months, because the surface gravity is a hundredth and the density a millionth. The image is the first direct check of that scaling.

Time series are being collected. The team has 14 epochs across two years, and the cells appear and disappear on a timescale of 200 days, which matches the theoretical overturn time. That is a measurement of convection in a regime no laboratory can reproduce.

Limits are resolution and interpretation. Twelve elements across a disc is enough to count cells and locate a spot, not to measure their velocities. The team is adding a fifth telescope to improve the sampling, and a spectroscopic channel to get velocities.

Calibration of the interferometer is the hidden work. The array measures phase differences with a laser metrology system that tracks the path length to a nanometre, and the closure phases are corrected for the detector's own gain drift. The paper's error budget lists 14 terms, and the largest is the atmosphere.

The target's choice was deliberate. A red giant is large enough to resolve and bright enough to observe in a short exposure, and its convection is theoretically predicted. A dwarf star would be a harder observation and a less informative one.

The convection model is what the image tests. A red giant's surface is a pattern of granules whose size theory predicts from the star's scale height, and the image's granules are 30 per cent larger than predicted, which points to a mixing length calibration that needs revision.

For a reader, the significance is that a star's surface is now an image rather than an inference. Models of convection have been calibrated on one star, the Sun, and this observation tests them at a surface gravity a hundred times lower, which is where the models were least constrained.
