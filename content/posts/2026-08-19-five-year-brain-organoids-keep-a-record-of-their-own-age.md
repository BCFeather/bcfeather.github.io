Brain organoids were supposed to be a shortcut to early development, and for a decade they behaved like one: a few months of growth, a resemblance to a fetal cortex, and then a slow decline as fragile cells died. The human brain takes about twenty years to mature, so an organoid that lasts five years is not a better model of infancy. It is the first model with enough time on its clock to study what happens after birth. A model that stops at a few months is a model of a trimester, and the questions that clinicians ask about children begin after that point.

Paola Arlotta and colleagues at Harvard cultured human brain organoids for more than five years, the longest reported, and published the result in *Nature* on 19 August. Their design included chimeric organoids assembled from cells of different ages, which is the experiment that separates developmental age from time spent in culture, since a cell that is chronologically old and a cell that is merely long-cultured are different objects. Five years is long enough that the culture medium, the incubator and the handling protocol all had to be stable for a decade, which is an achievement in itself.

Chimeras answered the question sharply. Older progenitor cells bypassed early developmental stages and produced mature neuron types in two weeks that normally take two months to form, which implies the cells carry a marker of their age and read it out regardless of the culture clock. Whole-genome profiling of long-cultured organoids showed patterns closer to late-prenatal and postnatal human brain than to the fetal state that earlier organoids resembled. Chimeras are a technical tour de force because two populations of cells have to be assembled without one outcompeting the other.

Structure followed the same direction. As the organoids aged they acquired more mature gene expression, a wider variety of neuronal types and dendritic spine morphologies that resemble postnatal tissue, which is the part earlier work could not reach because the cells did not survive long enough to build those structures.

What the model still cannot do is the part that matters clinically. An organoid has no blood supply, no immune cells and no vasculature to deliver a drug or remove a metabolite, and a five-year culture in a dish is not five years of experience. The claim is about developmental timekeeping, not about modelling a brain, and the paper is careful to keep the two apart. Spines are the structure that synapses use, so their appearance is a morphological sign that the tissue has begun to organize rather than only to grow.

The practical payoff is a platform for questions that need maturity: how neurons age, how glia mature, and whether the cell types that appear late in development behave differently from those that appear early. A culture that survives five years makes those experiments possible, which is a different contribution from making a brain in a dish. Whether mature organoids reproduce the vulnerabilities of adult tissue, including the ones that make neurons die in disease, is the next test.
