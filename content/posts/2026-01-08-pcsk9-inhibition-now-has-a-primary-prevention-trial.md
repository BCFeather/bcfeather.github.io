Statins earned their place through trials in people who had already had a heart attack, and the same logic was later extended to primary prevention in patients whose risk was high enough to justify a daily tablet. Antibody inhibitors of PCSK9 lowered LDL cholesterol even further and were tested in secondary prevention, where they reduced events. Whether that reduction extends to people who have never had an event, and whether it is worth an injection every two weeks for years, was an open question until this winter.

VESALIUS-CV randomized 12,257 patients with atherosclerosis or diabetes and no previous myocardial infarction or stroke, all of whom had an LDL cholesterol level of at least 90 milligrams per decilitre. Participants received evolocumab 140 milligrams every two weeks or placebo, and the median follow-up was 4.6 years. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 8 January, and the trial was funded by Amgen, which makes the drug.

Both primary endpoints moved. A three-point composite of coronary death, myocardial infarction or ischaemic stroke occurred in 336 patients on evolocumab, a five-year Kaplan-Meier estimate of 6.2 per cent, against 443 patients and 8.0 per cent on placebo, a hazard ratio of 0.75. The four-point composite that adds ischaemia-driven revascularization occurred in 747 against 907 patients, 13.4 against 16.2 per cent, with a hazard ratio of 0.81.

The population was not a general primary prevention cohort. Everyone enrolled had either established atherosclerosis or diabetes, which places them at substantially higher risk than an unselected adult, and 93 per cent were White. The median age was 66 and 43 per cent were women, so the trial describes an older, high-risk group and does not answer the question of what to do for a healthy fifty-year-old with a mildly raised LDL.

Safety was unremarkable, with no between-group difference in the incidence of safety events, which is reassuring for a drug that has been in use for a decade. The trial does not report the injection-site reactions and the practical burden that patients describe, and those are the reasons people stop taking an injectable therapy that produces no symptomatic benefit.

Cost is the part that will decide adoption. A PCSK9 inhibitor costs thousands per year in most health systems, and a 25 per cent relative reduction in a population whose absolute risk is six per cent over five years means roughly 55 patients treated for five years to prevent one event. That number is defensible and it is not cheap, and payers will compare it with the price of a statin that achieves a smaller reduction for a few dollars a month.

The scientific contribution is the confirmation that lowering LDL below the levels statins achieve reduces events in people without prior events, which settles an argument about whether there is a floor beneath which further reduction does nothing. The clinical contribution is a new option for a group that had one, and the policy contribution is a price negotiation that has already begun.
