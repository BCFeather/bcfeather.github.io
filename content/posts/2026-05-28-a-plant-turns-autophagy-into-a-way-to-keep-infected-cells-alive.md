Autophagy is usually filed under housekeeping: a cell digests its own components when nutrients run short or when damaged organelles need clearing. A paper in *Science* on 28 May describes a use that runs against that reading, in plants infected by a positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus. The setting is a model plant, not a crop, which is the usual first step and the usual reason a result takes a decade to reach a field.

Positive-sense RNA viruses take over the host's endomembranes to build replication factories, and the plant's defence response can destroy the tissue that harbours them. Clavel and colleagues worked in *Arabidopsis thaliana* and found that selective autophagy promoted the survival of infected host cells rather than their death.

The mechanism depends on two metabolic enzymes, nitrilase 1 and inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase 1, acting as selective autophagy receptors. Receptors of this kind give autophagy its specificity: they bind a cargo and attach it to the autophagosome, so the cell chooses what to remove rather than degrading at random. The choice is what makes the system a regulator rather than a disposal service.

The cargo in this case is EDS1, a central immune regulator. Removing it damps the defence response enough that the infected cell survives the infection. The plant pays for that by tolerating more virus, which looks like a bad trade until the alternative is necrosis, and a necrotic leaf is a dead leaf.

That trade-off is the interesting part. Immunity is expensive, and a strong response can kill the tissue it was meant to protect. The paper describes a circuit that keeps the response below the threshold where it becomes self-destructive, which is a form of tolerance rather than resistance, and tolerance has been a neglected half of plant immunity. Resistance keeps the pathogen out; tolerance keeps the plant alive with it, and the two are bred for in different ways. Breeders have spent decades on resistance genes because presence is easy to score, and tolerance needs a different assay altogether. A tolerant variety looks healthy and carries virus, which is good for yield and awkward for certification schemes that test for presence rather than for damage. Seed lots from such a plant can pass a visual inspection and still spread the pathogen to a neighbour's field.

Whether the same circuit can be tuned is the translational question. Crops that tolerate a virus rather than resisting it can still yield, and a tolerance strategy puts less selection pressure on the pathogen than a resistance gene that a virus can overcome. The two enzymes are the handle, and whether their activity can be adjusted without breaking the metabolic routes they also serve is the experiment that would make this useful in a field. Both enzymes sit on primary metabolism, so a chemical that pushes autophagy harder may also push the plant's nitrogen and nucleotide balance somewhere it cannot afford.
