No-poaching agreements are promises between employers not to hire each other's staff, and they are invisible to the worker whose salary they set. Adam Smith described the practice in 1775, and the technology sector practised it openly enough that the United States Department of Justice investigated. The timing of that inquiry gives an economist a natural experiment, because firms outside it did not change their behaviour at the same moment. The agreements covered engineers rather than executives, and they were enforced through recruiting practice that never appeared in a contract.

*The Economic Journal* published the estimate on 15 October 2025. The author uses a difference-in-differences design around the investigation and draws on Glassdoor salary reports, which allow employer and job-level controls that a bare wage survey cannot supply. The comparison is between firms named in the inquiry and comparable firms that were not. Glassdoor reports give salaries by employer and by job title, so the design compares the same title before and after instead of firms as wholes. Firm fixed effects absorb the level differences that make technology pay look uniform from outside.

Salaries at the colluding firms were 5.6 per cent lower on average than they would otherwise have been. Stock bonuses and reported job satisfaction fell as well, which matters because equity is where technology pay is most discretionary. The 5.6 per cent is the number the paper turns on, and it is an average across job levels, not a figure for any one role. Equity is where technology pay is most discretionary, so a fall there suggests the agreement reached beyond base salary.

The mechanism is monopsony rather than collusion over prices. A group of employers that agree not to bid for each other's workers behaves as one buyer in a local labour market, and the wage it pays sits below what competition among them would produce. Because the agreement binds mobility and not output, the loss falls on the workers who would have moved, who are often early in a career. Monopsony also explains why the effect appears at the firms named, not across the industry, since a buyer has power only over the workers it would otherwise have hired.

What the design cannot do is separate the agreement from the firms. The named companies were also the largest and fastest growing in their region, and a control firm of similar size is hard to find. Glassdoor reports are self-selected and skew towards white-collar roles, so the estimate describes the segment that posts salaries, not the workforce as a whole. Public announcements supply the treatment date, and firms named later are excluded from the pre-period comparison. Wage growth at the control firms is the counterfactual, and it is assumed, not observed.

The result bears on enforcement elsewhere. Franchise no-poaching clauses, which bind low-wage workers across chains, have been challenged state by state, and the same design could be applied to them where wage data exists at the right granularity. Whether 5.6 per cent travels to those settings is a question about local labour markets, not about the method. Enforcement has since reached franchise chains, which changes the expected cost of an agreement without changing the mechanism.
