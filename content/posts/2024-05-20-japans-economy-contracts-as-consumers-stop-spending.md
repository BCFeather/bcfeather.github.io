Japan's economy contracted at an annual rate of two per cent in the first quarter, according to the figures published on 16 May, the first decline in a year, and household consumption fell for the fourth quarter in a row. Business investment rose, which is the part of the report that the government emphasized. The contraction was larger than the forecasters expected, and the revision to the previous quarter removed the growth that the government had cited in the spring. The figure was revised from an initial reading that had already shown a decline, and the government described the quarter as a pause rather than as a turn.

Consumption is the weak link. Real wages have been falling for two years as prices rose faster than pay, and the households that had savings from the pandemic have spent most of them. A consumer who is poorer in real terms is a consumer who postpones the purchase that the economy needs. The households that own homes have seen the value of their asset fall, and the ones that rent have seen the rent rise, which is a squeeze that the wage round has only partly relieved.

Currency is part of the mechanism. A weak yen raises the price of imported food and fuel, and the Bank of Japan has been unwilling to raise rates quickly because the government is still supporting the economy through the budget. A policy that protects exporters is a policy that taxes households. The exporters have been compensated by the exchange rate, and their profits in yen have risen even as the volume of what they sell has not.

External demand did not help. Exports were flat as the demand from Europe and from China slowed, and the surplus that the country has relied on for decades has narrowed. An economy that depends on selling abroad is an economy exposed to the slowest parts of the world. The demand from abroad has been weak in the markets that buy the country's machinery, and the orders that the manufacturers report are a leading indicator that has been falling for a year.

Rebound is expected rather than observed. The forecasters expect growth to return in the current quarter as wages begin to rise in real terms, and the spring wage round produced the largest increases in three decades. A wage settlement is the transmission that the central bank has been waiting for. The government has said it will spend more in the autumn, and the stimulus it can deliver is limited by a debt ratio that is the highest among the large advanced economies.

What the quarter shows is that a country can have full employment and a shrinking economy at the same time, and the difference between the two is what the households can buy with the wages they are paid. A country with a shrinking population and a shrinking output is a country that has to decide whether the second is a consequence of the first.
