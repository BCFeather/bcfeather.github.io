description: "Six major marathons now admit around thirty elite runners each, with pacers outside that count. The cap is not about fairness on the road; it is about television minutes, prize dilution and the arithmetic of a mass start."
---

Boston, Berlin, London, Chicago, New York and Tokyo each admitted between twenty-eight and thirty-four elite entrants this spring, with pacing squads counted separately. Registration opened and closed within a day in three of the six. The cap is the least discussed and most consequential number in elite road racing, and it is worth taking apart, because it decides who can earn a living from the sport's most visible events.

Prize money explains the first layer. A major with a 1.2 million dollar purse and a field of eighty would pay the winner less, per head, than the same purse spread over thirty. Appearance fees complicate the calculation: the top eight runners in any field are paid to be there, and the fee for a world record holder is a large fraction of the total budget. Shrinking the field lets a race buy depth among the next twenty rather than paying a long tail that television will not show.

Television minutes are the second layer, and the binding constraint. A two-hour broadcast has room for perhaps six named athletes in the lead pack plus a chase group, and cameras cannot follow forty people strung over three kilometres. Every additional elite entrant costs airtime that the race would rather spend on the leaders, the course and the city. This is also why the cap is applied unevenly: races with a strong national field admit more domestic runners, because the domestic broadcast values them.

The third layer is safety and the mass start. Elite runners start ahead of the field but converge on the same roads within twenty minutes at the point where the courses rejoin. A larger elite field extends that convergence window, increases the number of runners passing through water stations at the same moment, and complicates the lead vehicle's timing. Race organisers describe this as an operational constraint. It is one, though a modest one, and it is the argument most often used in public.

What the cap actually rations is opportunity. A runner ranked thirtieth in the world can get into one major a year; the same runner at forty-fifth cannot, and the difference between those rankings is often a single fast course on a day with good weather. The consequence shows up downstream in national federations, where selection criteria for championships increasingly reference major-race results rather than times set in smaller races.

Alternatives exist, and each has a cost. Expanding the field dilutes the broadcast; adding a second start wave would fracture the race but allow more entrants; paying a development tier of appearance fees would widen access without widening the field. None of these is being tried at scale, because the present arrangement is stable and the people it excludes have no vote in the room where the number is set.

Worth saying plainly: the cap is defensible on television grounds and indefensible as a ranking system. It is a private decision about a public championship, taken by six organisations that coordinate through a series agreement, and the sport has never pretended otherwise. A reader following the spring season is watching the consequences of a number agreed in an office, not a field assembled by merit alone.
