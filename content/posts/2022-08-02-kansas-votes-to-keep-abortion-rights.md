Voters in Kansas rejected a constitutional amendment on Tuesday that would have allowed the legislature to restrict abortion, and the margin was fifty-nine to forty-one. The vote was the first statewide test of public opinion since the Supreme Court overturned the national right in June, and it took place in a state that voted for the president by fifteen points two years ago. The amendment had been placed on the primary ballot by the legislature, which expected a smaller and more conservative electorate. Turnout was about fifty per cent, which is high for a primary.

The text of the amendment did not ban anything directly. It would have removed the protection that the state's Supreme Court found in the constitution in 2019 and returned the question to the legislature, which had already passed a ban that the court blocked. Supporters described the measure as a way to settle the question democratically, and opponents described it as a way to write a ban without saying so. The campaign that followed was fought over that difference, and the advertisements were mostly about who would decide.

The result was a surprise to both sides. The secretary of state's office reported that the no vote led in rural counties as well as in the suburbs of Kansas City and Wichita, and that the margin in some of them was larger than the state's partisan lean would suggest. Analysts said the amendment had mobilized women voters and independent voters who do not usually turn out for a primary, and that the wording of the question had confused some of the people who voted for it. The governor, who is a Democrat, had opposed the amendment and said the vote showed that the state wanted to keep the right.

Money came from outside the state. The campaigns raised more than twenty million dollars, which is a large sum for a state referendum, and most of it was spent on television in the last three weeks. The national groups that support abortion rights treated the vote as a template, and the groups that oppose them said the turnout showed that their own voters had stayed at home. Both sides said the result would be used in the other states that have similar ballots in the autumn.

At the same time, the legal effect is narrow and immediate. The 2019 ruling of the state's Supreme Court stands, and the clinic in Wichita that had been at the centre of the case will stay open. The legislature can still pass restrictions that the court would have to review, and it can try again with a differently worded amendment. The political effect is wider, because a red state has now shown that its usual majority no longer settles the question.

What the vote means for the autumn is the part that strategists are arguing about. The issues that were supposed to dominate the campaign were inflation, the price of petrol and the border, and a referendum in August has put a different question in front of the voters. The candidates who are running in states with similar ballots will have to answer it, and the answer that worked in Kansas is the one that will be tested next. The polls had predicted a close race, and the result was wide.
