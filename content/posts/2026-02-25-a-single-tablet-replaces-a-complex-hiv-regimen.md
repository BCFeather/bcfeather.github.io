---

HIV treatment has been simplified repeatedly over thirty years, from a handful of pills taken several times a day to one tablet once a day, and the simplification has always traded potency for convenience until trials showed that no potency was lost. The patients who remain on complex regimens are those with resistance, with drug interactions or with a history of failure, and they are the patients for whom a simpler option is most valuable and least likely to preserve control.

Bictegravir and lenacapavir are two drugs with different mechanisms and long half-lives, and combining them in a single tablet was the goal of the ARTISTRY programme. ARTISTRY-1 randomised participants with virological suppression on a complex multi-tablet regimen to switch to the single tablet or to remain on their regimen, and the primary outcome was the proportion with HIV-1 RNA of at least 50 copies per millilitre at week 48 using the snapshot algorithm. The results appeared in *The Lancet* on 25 February.

The switch was non-inferior. Virological failure occurred in 0.8 per cent of participants on the single tablet and in 1.1 per cent of those who stayed on their complex regimen, which is a difference that does not favour the old regimen and a rate that is low in both arms. In a population that has already failed therapy, keeping the failure rate below one per cent is the result that matters.

Safety and tolerability were consistent with the individual components. Lenacapavir is a capsid inhibitor with a long half-life and a formulation history that includes twice-yearly injection, and bictegravir is an integrase inhibitor that has been used in single-tablet regimens for years. Combining them orally is a pharmacological achievement and not a new mechanism, and the safety database that comes with the components carries over.

The population is small and selected. Participants had to be suppressed on a complex regimen to enter the trial, which excludes the patients whose regimens are failing and who would benefit most from a switch. A trial that shows a switch is safe in stable patients is a necessary first step and is not evidence that the same switch rescues someone with resistance.

Practical benefit is adherence and quality of life. A patient on four tablets takes four chances to forget, and a patient on one takes one, and the difference is measurable in a disease where a missed dose is a risk of resistance. The trial's secondary endpoints address that, and the field has learned to take patient-reported outcomes seriously in a condition that requires lifelong therapy.

Price and access will decide adoption, as they do for every antiretroviral. A single-tablet regimen combining two branded components is expensive, and the countries with the most people living with HIV are the countries least able to pay for it. Lenacapavir's own pricing has been the subject of a global debate, and a combination tablet will re-enter that debate.
