title: "Bird flu spreads across European farms"
date: "2022-10-20T09:20:00+08:00"

description: "A record number of outbreaks were reported in October, and the virus was found in mammals for the first time."
---

France, Germany, the Netherlands and Italy were the countries with most cases, and the virus was the H5N1 strain. The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control reported 1,100 outbreaks in poultry farms in the autumn, the highest number since it began counting in 2016. The season had begun in August, three months earlier than usual. The figure was compiled from notifications that member states are required to file within 24 hours.

Seabird colonies in Scotland lost 40 per cent of their breeding pairs in the summer, and the deaths were attributed to the same strain. The virus had also been found in wild birds along the coasts of the North Sea and the Baltic. The virus was carried by migrating birds, and the autumn migration spread it southwards. The migration route runs from the Arctic to the Mediterranean, and it passes through 20 countries.

Mammals were infected for the first time in Europe. The virus was detected in foxes and in mink in Spain and in Finland, and in seals in the Baltic. The genetic analysis showed a mutation that improves the virus's ability to infect mammalian cells, and the virologists said it was not yet a change that would spread between people. The mutation had been seen once before, in a mink farm in Spain in 2021.

Poultry farms responded with culling. France culled 1.5 million birds in the autumn, and the Netherlands culled 200,000 in a single week. The culls raised the price of eggs and of poultry meat, and the French government said it would compensate farmers at 85 per cent of the value of the flock. The virus had been circulating in Asia and in Africa for two decades, and it had killed 870 people since 2003, according to the World Health Organization. The human cases were almost all traced to direct contact with infected birds, and there had been no sustained transmission between people. The risk to the public was assessed as low. Egg prices in France rose 20 per cent in the autumn, and the government capped them in January.

Vaccination of poultry was the policy question. Several countries had stockpiles of a vaccine that could be deployed in an emergency, and the European Union had rules that restricted its use because vaccinated birds cannot be distinguished from infected ones in trade. The rules were revised in 2023 to allow vaccination in specific regions. The outbreak changed the way farms are run. Several countries required that poultry be kept indoors in high-risk areas, and the Dutch and French governments ordered that in October. The measures were unpopular with free-range producers, whose certification depends on outdoor access, and the label rules were suspended for the season.

A vaccine for mammals was tested in the autumn, and the first human case in Europe in the current wave was reported in Britain in January, in a person who kept infected birds. The virus persisted into 2023 and reached South America for the first time, where it killed seabirds in Chile and in Peru. The vaccine reduces mortality and does not prevent infection, which is why trade rules are strict.
