Britain's central bank raised its policy rate to 5 per cent on 22 June, a half-point increase that surprised the market, which had expected a quarter. The vote was 7 to 2, with the dissenters on both sides. The rate is now the highest since 2008. The increase was the thirteenth in a row, and the bank has raised the rate by about 5 percentage points since the end of 2021. The two dissenters wanted to hold, and they argued that the effects of the earlier rises had not yet reached the households.

Inflation data did the arguing. The consumer prices rose by 8.7 per cent in May, which was above the forecast, and the core stayed at 7.1. The bank said the food prices and the services had been stickier than the models had assumed, and the risk was that the expectation would settle above the target. The food inflation was about 19 per cent, and the services about 7.4, which are the two lines the bank watches. The bank said the wage growth was running at about 7.5 per cent in the private sector, which is above the level consistent with the target.

Markets had priced the quarter. The traders had put about a 60 per cent chance on the smaller move, and the pound jumped by about 0.8 per cent against the dollar when the decision landed. The two-year gilt yield rose by about 20 basis points. The mortgage rates rose above 6 per cent for the two-year fixes, and the lenders pulled about 10 per cent of the products from the market.

Growth is weak. The economy grew by about 0.1 per cent in the first quarter, and the bank expects the second to be flat. The mortgage market has been repricing: about 1.5 million households will see their fixed deals expire this year, and the average new fix is about 6 per cent. The unemployment rate was about 3.8 per cent, and the vacancies had fallen to about 1 million.

Its governor defended the size. He said the committee had decided to act faster rather than to promise more later, and he rejected the charge that the bank was overreacting to one month of the data. He also said the bank would not hesitate to raise again. He said the bank would watch the wage settlements of the autumn, and the next meeting is in early August.

A decision of this size puts Britain at the front of the tightening among the large economies. A rate of 5 per cent with the inflation at 8.7 is a real rate that is still negative, and the gap is the arithmetic the committee has to close. The market now expects the rate to peak at about 6 per cent in the winter, which is higher than the forecast of the bank.
