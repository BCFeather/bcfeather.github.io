Ice is a good sacrificial material. Printed into a shape, frozen solid, coated with a precursor and then melted, it leaves a cavity with smooth walls, and the technique has been used to make microchannels and vascular networks in materials that cannot be machined. The technique belongs to a family of methods that build a shape from a material meant to disappear, and ice is the cheapest of them.

Freeform printing of ice has been demonstrated, yet its physics has been treated as a matter of trial and error. Droplets land, freeze, and either pile up as a stack of beads or fuse into a continuous filament, and which of those happens depends on the rate at which the droplets arrive. Which mode occurs is not a matter of preference, since the two produce different surface finishes and different mechanical strength.

A paper in *PNAS* on 23 July 2024 works the problem out with two models. A two-dimensional thermo-fluidic model captures the transition from layered deposition to continuous printing, and a three-dimensional model handles oblique deposition, where the nozzle is not perpendicular to the growing structure and where curved and overhanging geometry becomes possible. Coupling heat transfer, fluid flow and the phase change in one set of equations is what makes the transition predictable rather than empirical.

The continuous mode has a clear physical signature. At high deposition rates a liquid cap persists on top of the printed ice, and each new droplet merges into that cap before it freezes. The resulting surface is smooth, and the structure grows as a single body rather than as a stack. A cap that persists is a reservoir, so the droplet rate sets how much liquid is available to smooth the wall before freezing begins.

Diameter is controlled by frequency rather than by nozzle size alone. Faster deposition gives a wider filament because the cap spreads before it solidifies, and slower deposition gives a narrower one, so the printed resolution is a dynamic quantity rather than a fixed one. That dependence gives a printer a second control axis beyond the nozzle, which is unusual in extrusion methods.

Oblique deposition is what enables the interesting shapes. Writing at an angle, with the stage or the nozzle tilted, produces overhangs that would collapse in a layered print, and the three-dimensional model predicts the range of angles that remain stable. Angles beyond the stable range produce a filament that sags before it freezes, and the model marks where that boundary lies.

Where the paper stops is at the material. The models assume pure water and a controlled cold stage, and additives such as salts or polymers change the freezing behaviour and the mechanical strength of the ice. Scaling to smaller nozzles, where surface tension dominates, is also unexplored. Even so, the result turns a craft into a design problem. A printed ice mould can now be specified by its deposition rate and its writing angle, which is what a manufacturer needs before the technique can produce a channel with a chosen diameter. Additives shift the freezing point and change the crystal structure, and neither is in the model as it stands.
