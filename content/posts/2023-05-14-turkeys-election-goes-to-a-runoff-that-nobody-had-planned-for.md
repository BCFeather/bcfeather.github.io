---

Turkey held presidential and parliamentary elections on 14 May, and the result was the one that the polls had hinted at and the government had spent weeks denying: President Recep Tayyip Erdogan finished below fifty per cent, at 49.5, and will face Kemal Kilicdaroglu, the candidate of a six-party opposition alliance, in a runoff on 28 May. Kilicdaroglu took 44.9 per cent. Sinan Ogan, a nationalist who ran on a platform of rejecting refugees, took five per cent and has become the most courted man in the country.

Parliament went the other way. The alliance led by the president's Justice and Development Party held a majority, which means that even a victory for the opposition in the second round would leave a president governing against a parliament that his opponents do not control. That split is the structural fact of Turkish politics, and it explains why the opposition campaigned on a return to a parliamentary system while the president campaigned on the strength of the executive he built after 2017.

Campaigning turned on the economy and on the earthquake. Inflation ran above forty per cent through the winter, and the government responded with a combination of subsidies, a minimum wage increase and an unorthodox monetary policy that kept the lira propped up by increasingly expensive interventions. The earthquake of 6 February, which killed more than fifty thousand people in the south-east, exposed the failures of a construction boom that had been the government's showcase, and the opposition expected that anger to carry the cities. It carried some of them and not enough.

The turnout was extraordinary even by Turkish standards, at nearly 89 per cent, and the count was conducted in the open, with party observers at every ballot box, which is the reason that the result was accepted by all sides within hours. That acceptance is itself news in a country where the last two decades have been punctuated by allegations of manipulation, and where the opposition had prepared to contest. The election authority's numbers were published as they arrived, and the parties read them out to their own crowds.

The runoff now turns on two things. The first is the five per cent that went to Ogan, whose supporters are young, secular and hostile to the presence of more than three million Syrian refugees, and who will extract a price for an endorsement. The second is the Kurdish vote, which went overwhelmingly to Kilicdaroglu through the party that dominates the south-east, and which the president will try to portray as a deal with an organization he calls a terrorist front.

Whoever wins on 28 May inherits an economy that has been kept afloat by measures that cannot be repeated, a currency that has lost most of its value in five years, and a reconstruction bill in the earthquake zone that will run into tens of billions of dollars. The election has been described as the most important in Turkey's modern history, which is a phrase used in every election. This time the description is defensible, because for the first time in twenty years the answer is genuinely unknown.
