Rate-setters cut by a quarter point on 7 November, the second reduction of the cycle, and the language that accompanied it was more careful than the move. Officials described inflation as closer to target while noting that the labour market had cooled enough to justify a slower pace of easing, and the phrase that had implied a steady sequence of cuts was dropped. A second cut in a cycle is a cut that a committee makes when it is less afraid of the downside than it was.

Data supported a pause in the sequence. Inflation had fallen from its peak but had stopped improving for several months, and the last stretch towards two per cent was being held up by services prices that reflect wages rather than goods. A committee that has cut twice can afford to wait for the next reading before it commits to a third. A services price that reflects a wage is a price that a central bank cannot fix with a rate.

Growth was the other half of the argument. Consumer spending had held up, unemployment remained low by historical standards, and the economy was expanding at a rate that did not require emergency support. A central bank cutting into a strong economy is a central bank that risks re-accelerating demand, which is why the guidance became conditional. A consumer who is still spending is a consumer whose real income has held up, which is the part of the story that surprises forecasters.

Bond markets had already priced the move and were more interested in the path. Yields on shorter maturities fell less than the cut implied, which is a market saying that it expects fewer cuts than the statement suggests. A bond market that disagrees with a central bank is a bond market that will be tested by the next employment report. A market that prices fewer cuts than the statement implies is a market that expects the data to push back.

Politics made the context unusual. A newly elected government had promised tariffs and tax cuts, both of which could push prices up, and the central bank was being asked to forecast policies that had not been written. Officials said they would respond to the measures when they existed, which is the only honest answer and not a satisfying one. A tariff that raises the price of an import is a tariff that a central bank has to look through or respond to.

What the meeting established is that the easy part of the cycle is over. Inflation that falls because supply chains have normalized is different from inflation that has to be squeezed out of a labour market, and the second kind requires a central bank to hold rates higher for longer than a government would like. A cycle that ends early leaves rates above the level that a weak economy would need, and that is the risk the committee is accepting.
