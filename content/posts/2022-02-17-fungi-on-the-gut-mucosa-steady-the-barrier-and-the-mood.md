title: "Fungi that live on the gut lining steady the barrier and the behaviour"
date: "2022-02-17T09:55:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A consortium of mucosa-associated fungi strengthened the intestinal barrier through interleukin-22 and changed social behaviour in mice through interleukin-17 signalling in neurons."
---

Bacterial members of the gut microbiota have been studied for two decades, while the fungal component has been treated as a bystander that matters only when it overgrows. Candida after antibiotics and fungal sepsis in immunocompromised patients are the events that made fungi visible, and both are departures from a healthy state. A community that is present in health and that does something useful has been harder to demonstrate.

A study mapped where fungi sit along the gastrointestinal tract and found a community associated with the mucosa and not with the lumen, in mice and in human samples. Colonizing germ-free mice with a defined consortium of those mucosa-associated fungi protected them against intestinal injury and bacterial infection. The paper appeared in *Cell*, 16 February 2022. These organisms adhered to the epithelium, which is a different ecology from the luminal community that grows in food residue.

Barrier protection ran through interleukin-22 produced by CD4 helper T cells, which is the cytokine that drives epithelial repair and antimicrobial peptide secretion. Interleukin-22 acts on the epithelium and not on immune cells, so the effect is a local repair programme and not a systemic anti-inflammatory state. The behavioural effect ran through interleukin-17 receptor signalling in neurons, a separate arm of the same type 17 response.

Social behaviour in mice is measured by how readily an animal approaches and interacts with a familiar cagemate, and colonized animals interacted more. The effect was absent in animals lacking the interleukin-17 receptor in neurons, which localizes the behavioural change to a specific signalling route and not to general wellbeing. That arrangement also means that the two effects can be dissociated experimentally, and the study does so with selective blocking.

Two arms of one immune response therefore produce two different benefits, and the fungal community is the trigger for both. That arrangement is economical from the host's point of view, since a single sensing system reports the fungal load and adjusts both the barrier and the brain. Fungi are a small fraction of the community by mass, and their effects are disproportionate because the immune system recognizes their cell wall components directly.

The translation to human health is speculative. Mice raised without microbes have an immune system that is unlike any person's, the consortium was defined in the laboratory and not isolated from a natural community, and the behavioural assay is a proxy for sociability rather than a measure of it. A human trial would need to define a dose, a delivery route and an endpoint, and none of those are obvious for a live consortium.

The result nevertheless puts fungi into the same frame as bacteria. A mucosal community that is spatially organized and that signals through type 17 immunity is part of the host's regulatory apparatus, and depletion of it, whether by antibiotics or by diet, is a change with consequences beyond the intestine. The broader implication is that antibiotic treatment, which removes bacteria and leaves fungi, changes the mucosal community in a direction that the study suggests is unfavourable.
