The People's Bank of China announced on 7 May that it would cut the rate on seven-day reverse repurchase agreements by ten basis points, taking it to 1.4 per cent, and would lower the reserve requirement ratio by half a percentage point from 15 May. The two moves together are the standard package: one sets the price of money, the other sets its quantity. Both were announced in advance of their effective dates, which is the convention the central bank has used since 2019 to avoid surprising the interbank market.

A reserve cut releases about one trillion yuan of long-term liquidity into the banking system. The rate cut is smaller and mostly symbolic, because the seven-day reverse repo is the anchor for the loan prime rate, and a ten-basis-point move there translates into a similar move in the rates that mortgages and corporate loans are priced from. The transmission is imperfect: deposit rates fall more slowly than lending rates, which is why bank margins narrow after a cut of this kind.

The package was announced at a press conference alongside the financial regulator and the securities regulator, which is the format Beijing uses when it wants the market to read a set of measures as a single policy stance rather than as separate technical adjustments. The governor described ten measures, of which the reserve and rate cuts were the first two. The others included relending quotas for technology firms, a facility for service consumption, and support for private bond issuance, all of which are smaller in size but targeted at sectors the reserve cut does not reach.

Housing was addressed directly. The housing provident fund loan rate was cut by 25 basis points, which affects existing mortgages as well as new ones, and relending facilities for subsidized housing were expanded. Auto finance companies and financial leasing companies had their reserve ratios cut to zero, a targeted move aimed at the consumer credit that sits behind car sales and equipment purchases. Banks that fund mortgages gain most, and the announcement said so plainly.

Timing tells you what the policy is for. The announcement came a day after a holiday in which retail sales grew but big-ticket categories grew faster, and a week before the April data was due. Growth in the second quarter was expected to slow as the tariff increases of the spring worked through export orders, and the central bank moved before the data rather than after it.

Whether it works is the open question. Chinese households have been saving rather than borrowing for three years, and a cheaper loan does not by itself change that. The measures that matter for consumption are the ones that change income expectations, which is why the fiscal side of the package, including the consumer trade-in subsidies, is the part that economists watch. A cheaper mortgage helps a household that was already going to buy; it does not create one that was not.
