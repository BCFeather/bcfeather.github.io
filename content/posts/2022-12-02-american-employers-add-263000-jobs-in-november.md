Payrolls rose by 263,000 in November, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on 2 December. The forecast had been 200,000, and the figure was the seventh month in the year above 250,000. The unemployment rate was 3.7 per cent, unchanged, and the participation rate fell to 62.1. The figure was 100,000 above the monthly average needed to keep unemployment steady.

Wages rose 0.6 per cent on the month and 5.1 per cent over the year, both above the forecast. The monthly figure was the largest since January, and it surprised a market that had expected a slowdown. Average weekly hours fell to 34.4, which offsets some of the wage effect because fewer hours are worked. Wage growth for production workers was 6 per cent, and for managers 4.5.

Sectors were concentrated in services again. Leisure and hospitality added 88,000 jobs, and health care added 45,000. Government added 25,000, and construction 15,000. Retail lost 30,000, and the sector had shed jobs for four months. Transportation and warehousing lost 12,000. The household survey showed employment rising by 200,000, and it was the stronger of the two measures for the first time in a year. The two surveys had diverged through 2022, and the gap narrowed in the autumn. The statisticians said the annual revision in February would reconcile them. Health care had added 45,000, and it had added 500,000 over the year.

Futures had priced a half-point increase at 75 per cent before the release, and they kept that expectation afterwards. The report was the last before the December meeting of the Federal Reserve. The market's peak rate moved up to 5 per cent, and the two-year yield rose 0.1 points. The unemployment rate for black workers was 5.7 per cent, and for Hispanic workers 4.1.

Rates went up by 50 basis points on 14 December, and the projections showed a peak of 5.1 per cent in 2023. The chairman said the labour market was still too tight and that the committee had not seen the slowdown it needed. The report supported that view, and it was cited in the statement.

September and October were revised down by 40,000 in total, and the three-month average was 271,000. The revisions were small. The figure for the year was revised in February to 4.05 million jobs, which was the second largest annual increase on record after 2021. Hiring slowed in the first half of 2023 as the rate increases took effect. Payroll growth averaged 250,000 in the first quarter and 180,000 in the second, and the unemployment rate stayed below 4 per cent. The labour market did not break, and that was the basis of the soft landing. The figure was revised down to 4.05 million in February, and the monthly average was 340,000.
