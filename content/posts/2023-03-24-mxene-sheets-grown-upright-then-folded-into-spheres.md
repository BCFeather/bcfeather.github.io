MXenes are two-dimensional carbides and nitrides, and their promise in energy storage comes from the space between layers. An ion that can slip between sheets is an ion that charges a battery quickly, and the spacing is the property synthesis has to control.

In *Science* on 24 March 2023, the spacing is produced from the vapour. The authors grow Ti2CCl2 by chemical vapour deposition, and the sheets emerge perpendicular to the substrate rather than flat on it, so the layers stand like a forest and the gaps between them face the electrolyte.

Folding is the second step. The upright sheets bend and close into microspheres, and the interior is a cavity bounded by layered walls, which gives a powder whose particles are porous at two scales. Substrate belongs to the recipe, since a surface with a particular lattice match favours the perpendicular orientation, and the choice of substrate is what selects this growth mode rather than another.

Because a MXene's surface groups determine its electronic properties and its wettability, the chlorine termination is the chemical choice, and a vapour-phase process produces that surface cleanly and reproducibly. Etching supplies the comparison, as the standard route makes a MXene by etching a MAX phase in acid, leaving a clay-like powder with a fluoride surface and a stack that has to be delaminated, whereas a vapour route deposits the material where it is wanted.

Access is the electrochemical consequence. An electrode of upright sheets has channels running from the surface to the interior while an electrode of stacked sheets has channels parallel to the current collector, and the first fills faster. Intercalation is the core of the application, since ions entering between layers change conductivity and volume, and a structure accommodating that change without cracking is the one that lasts.

Evidence comes from imaging. Scanning electron microscopy shows the upright sheets and the spheres, and cross-sections show the internal cavity, without which the morphology claim would be an inference from diffraction. Stability is the mechanical question, because a hollow sphere can collapse under cycling, and microscopy after cycling is what indicates survival.

Scale sets the limitation. Chemical vapour deposition is a high-temperature process with a slow deposition rate, and a battery material is made by the tonne, so the paper demonstrates a morphology rather than a manufacturing route. Full-cell performance stays unmeasured, since a half-cell in a coin format gives a capacity and a full cell with a cathode gives an energy density, and that difference is where promising materials fail. What the field gains is a morphology synthesis can target, because a sheet standing up is a sheet an ion can reach from both sides. A second gap is the electrolyte, since the chloride surface wets differently from a fluoride one, and the rate an ion enters depends on that wetting as much as on the channel that carries it. Cycling stability across a thousand cycles, rather than a micrograph taken after a few, is the measurement that would turn a morphology into a candidate.
