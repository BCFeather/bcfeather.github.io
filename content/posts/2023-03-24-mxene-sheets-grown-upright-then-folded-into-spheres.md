---

MXenes are two-dimensional carbides and nitrides, and their promise in energy storage comes from the space between layers. An ion that can slip between sheets is an ion that charges a battery quickly, and the spacing is the property that synthesis has to control.

The growth reported in *Science*, 24 March 2023 produces the spacing from the vapour. The authors grow Ti2CCl2 by chemical vapour deposition, and the sheets emerge perpendicular to the substrate rather than flat on it, so that the layers stand like a forest and the gaps between them face the electrolyte.

The folding is the second step. The upright sheets bend and close into microspheres, and the sphere's interior is a cavity bounded by layered walls. The geometry gives a powder whose particles are porous at two scales.

The chlorine termination is the chemical choice. A MXene's surface groups determine its electronic properties and its wettability, and a chlorine-terminated surface is one that the vapour-phase process can produce cleanly and reproducibly.

The comparison with etching is the advance. The standard route makes a MXene by etching a MAX phase in acid, which leaves a clay-like powder with a fluoride surface and a stack that has to be delaminated. A vapour route deposits the material where it is wanted.

The electrochemical consequence is access. An electrode made of upright sheets has channels that run from the surface to the interior, and an electrode made of stacked sheets has channels that run parallel to the current collector. The first fills faster.

The mechanical question is stability. A sphere with a hollow core can collapse under cycling, and the paper's microscopy after cycling is the evidence that the structure survives.

The scale is the limitation. Chemical vapour deposition is a high-temperature process with a slow deposition rate, and a battery material is made by the tonne. The paper is a demonstration of a morphology rather than a manufacturing route.

What the paper does not measure is the full cell. A half-cell in a coin format gives a capacity, and a full cell with a cathode gives an energy density, and the difference is where promising materials fail.

For the field, the value is a morphology that synthesis can target. A sheet that stands up is a sheet that an ion can reach from both sides, and the paper shows that a vapour process can make one.

The substrate is part of the recipe. A surface with a particular lattice match favours the perpendicular orientation, and the authors' choice of substrate is what selects the growth mode rather than another.

The intercalation chemistry is the application's core. Ions entering between layers change the material's conductivity and its volume, and a structure that accommodates the change without cracking is the one that lasts.

The imaging is the evidence. Scanning electron microscopy shows the upright sheets and the spheres, and cross-sections show the internal cavity. Without those images the morphology claim would be an inference from diffraction.
