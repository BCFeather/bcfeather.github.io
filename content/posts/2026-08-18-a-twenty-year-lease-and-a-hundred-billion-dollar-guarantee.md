Financing has become the part of artificial intelligence that decides what gets built, and the arrangements now being disclosed are large enough to be industrial policy by another name. OpenAI signed a 20-year lease for a campus in Ohio on the site of a former Cold War uranium enrichment plant, and NVIDIA committed a 105 billion dollar financing guarantee, according to a regulatory filing published on 17 August. A 20-year lease is longer than most of the hardware it will house, which means the contract outlives two or three generations of chip.

Scale is the argument for the site. Capacity could reach eight gigawatts, which would make it the largest single artificial intelligence facility, and the filing says it will run exclusively on NVIDIA chips. SoftBank and SB Energy, the site's owner, committed more than four billion dollars to local energy infrastructure and plan at least ten gigawatts of new generation, with OpenAI putting 40 million dollars into local priorities. An eight gigawatt campus, for comparison, is roughly the load of several large cities, and the filing treats the generation as part of the project rather than as an assumption.

Vendor financing is the part that draws scrutiny. NVIDIA's guarantee is its largest to date, and the company also put 1.5 billion dollars into SB Energy. A supplier underwriting its customer's lease is a structure that concentrates risk: if the tenant cannot pay, the chipmaker's balance sheet absorbs the shortfall, and the revenue that justified the guarantee depends on the same demand that the guarantee is financing.

NVIDIA has answered the circularity criticism by pointing out that OpenAI pays the lease and that the deal was struck with the discipline applied to supply chain management. That is true and does not settle the question, which is whether a 20-year obligation backed by a supplier's guarantee behaves like a customer contract or like a captive financing arrangement when demand growth slows. Depreciation is the accounting question underneath: a guarantee that matures in twenty years against assets that lose value in five is a mismatch that accounting standards do not resolve well.

Energy is the second constraint and the one the filing spends most of its language on. Ten gigawatts of new generation in a decade is a programme rather than a purchase, and the interconnection queue, the transformer backlog and the local permitting process each have their own timetable. A campus that reaches eight gigawatts only when the wires arrive is a lease whose value depends on someone else's construction schedule.

The wider read is about who carries the risk of overbuilding. Data centre capacity is being financed on the expectation that inference demand keeps compounding, and structures like this one spread that expectation across a chipmaker, a bank, a landlord and a tenant. If the expectation holds, the arrangement looks like foresight; if it does not, the losses will be concentrated exactly where the guarantee sits.
