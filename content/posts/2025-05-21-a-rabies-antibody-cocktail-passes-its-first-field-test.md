Rabies post-exposure treatment is a vaccine plus immunoglobulin, and the immunoglobulin is the scarce part. It is made from pooled human plasma, supply is limited, and in much of the world patients receive the vaccine without it, which is a treatment that works less well than the one in the guidelines. A manufactured antibody would fix the supply problem.

A study published in *npj Vaccines* on 21 May 2025 reported the safety of a cocktail of two monoclonal antibodies, docaravimab and miromavimab, in patients with severe animal bites. The design was prospective and observational, conducted at a single anti-rabies clinic in Bangalore between October 2021 and December 2022, and it enrolled 159 participants.

Administration was mostly local. The cocktail was injected around the wound in 94.3 per cent of patients, and both locally and systemically in 5.7 per cent, which is the pattern a clinic would follow with the plasma product, and which also means the systemic exposure that drives most reactions was limited in most patients. Adverse events were reported in 10.7 per cent of cases, all local and mild to moderate, and all resolved.

No systemic reaction and no rabies appeared. That is the sentence the study exists to support: six months of follow-up produced no cases of the disease, and no patient had a serious adverse event, which in a disease that kills once symptoms appear is the finding that permits the next study, since the alternative treatment is a plasma product whose supply a clinic cannot control. A monoclonal product does not carry the batch-to-batch variability of pooled plasma, and it can be manufactured at a scale that plasma cannot, which is why the World Health Organization has listed antibody cocktails as a priority for countries where immunoglobulin is scarce.

Design is the limitation. There was no control arm, no comparison with human immunoglobulin, and the sample is one clinic in one country, so the study establishes tolerability and not efficacy. Rabies is also too rare and too fast to be studied in a randomised trial of outcomes, which is why every product in this field is licensed on immunogenicity and on safety, and why a safety study in a single clinic is publishable at all.

Immunogenicity data are the ones a regulator will read. A monoclonal antibody must reach the wound before the virus reaches the nerves, and its dose has to be enough for a patient whose exposure is unknown. The paper reports safety and administration, and the neutralising titre data belong to the next paper, which is why the authors describe this as a first step and not a replacement.

What would settle the product is a comparison of neutralising antibody levels against the plasma standard, in enough patients to show non-inferiority, plus a price that a ministry of health in an endemic country can pay. A cocktail that costs more than the plasma it replaces solves the supply problem and not the access one.
