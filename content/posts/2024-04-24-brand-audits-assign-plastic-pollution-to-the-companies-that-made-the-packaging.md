Plastic pollution is counted in items, and an item carries a brand that identifies who made it. A paper in Science Advances on 24 April 2024 uses brand audits to allocate responsibility globally. Audits sample waste in a place and a season, and sampling is what makes a global claim dependent on where the auditors went. Policy design follows from concentration, since a deposit scheme can target the firms whose packaging dominates the sample. A deposit scheme needs a brand to charge, and a brand is the entity that the audits can identify. A representative sample of waste is hard to draw, and drawing it is the methodological contribution. A sample drawn from beaches understates what sinks, and sinking material is the part that reaches the food chain.

Allocation is the policy question that counting alone leaves open. Producer responsibility is a principle that assigns the cost of waste to the company that placed the material on the market. Dependence on sampling is not a flaw when the sample is designed, and the paper describes a design rather than an accumulation. Targeting is easier when the target is a legal entity, and a multinational is a legal entity with subsidiaries that differ by country. Identification is where an audit becomes an instrument of policy, and the instrument is the point of the paper.

Assigning cost requires attribution, and attribution requires a sample of waste that is audited rather than assumed. The audits record brand names in a standard way, and standardization is what allows pooling across countries. Packaging carries a brand on a label, and a label fades, which is why the audit protocols photograph the item before cleaning. Country differences are where enforcement happens, and enforcement is the step that a global analysis cannot take. Instrument design is contested by industry, and the contest is over whether the sample is representative.

Pooling across countries is what makes a global claim possible. A small number of companies accounts for a large share of identifiable items, and concentration is what makes a targeted policy feasible. Photography is the chain of evidence, and a chain is what a company would contest if the finding were used in litigation. Representativeness is the vulnerability, and the paper's methods section is the answer to it.

A policy aimed at five firms is easier to enforce than one aimed at thousands. Litigation is a plausible use of the data, and plausibility is why the method is documented in the paper rather than in a supplement.

Attribution is limited to packaging that carries a legible brand, and unlabelled waste is excluded. Voluntary commitments are the industry's current response, and a commitment without a baseline cannot be audited.

Exclusion biases the sample towards branded goods, and the bias is stated. A baseline is what the brand audits supply, and supplying it is the contribution that outlasts the specific shares.
