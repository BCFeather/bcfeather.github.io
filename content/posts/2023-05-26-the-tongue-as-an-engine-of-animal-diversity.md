description: "A survey argues that the tongue, an organ usually treated as an accessory, has shaped how animals feed, breathe and sense, and that its variation tracks major evolutionary transitions."
---

The tongue is a muscle covered in mucosa, and its textbook function is to move food and to shape speech. A survey in *Science* on 26 May 2023 makes the case that the organ drives diversity rather than merely accompanying it.

Comparison carries the argument. Tongues vary in length, stiffness, in the papillae covering them and in the glands supplying them, and that variation correlates with diet, habitat and the mechanics of feeding rather than with body size alone.

Extremes supply the evidence. A tongue extending beyond the body, one that harpoons prey, one carrying a sticky coating and one used to smell are all answers to the same problem of acquiring something at a distance. Biomechanics frames it, because a tongue is a hydrostat that moves by changing internal pressure, and a hydrostat bends, extends and stiffens in ways a jointed limb cannot, which explains the range of shapes on offer.

Senses are the underappreciated part. A tongue carries taste buds and, in some animals, chemical sensors that detect prey, so the organ is a sensory surface as well as a manipulator. Respiration is the surprise, since in some reptiles the tongue's movement is coupled to the airway, and the organ takes part in breathing rather than only in eating.

Evolution explains what the shapes bought. A tongue reaching into a flower makes a pollinator, and the coevolution of tongues and flowers is among the clearest cases of a lineage entering a niche through a change in one organ. Development asks how such different forms arise from a similar primordium, and the review of genetic control suggests a small number of patterning genes can produce a large range of shapes.

Fossil record sets the limitation, because a tongue is soft tissue that does not fossilise, which leaves the history to be inferred from living animals and from the bones supporting the organ. Quantitative test is what the survey does not provide, since a correlation between form and diet is a pattern, and comparative analysis with phylogeny is the method that would test it.

Zoology gains a change of emphasis: feeding has been studied through jaws and teeth, and the tongue is the other half of the apparatus and the half evolution has varied most. Soft robotics has already borrowed the design, because a hydrostatic organ that extends, stiffens and bends is a source of ideas for machines that must reach into confined spaces, and the biology is ahead of the engineering here. A phylogenetic comparison would also test whether the extreme tongues arose once or many times, and repeated origins would be stronger evidence for the organ as an engine of diversification than a single spectacular case could be. Field observations of feeding rates would add the missing performance measure, because a tongue that reaches further is not automatically a tongue that feeds faster, and the two need separating. Comparative anatomy across the vertebrates would tell whether the hydrostatic design constrains the tongue's reach or merely permits it, and the constraint is what a biomechanical argument ultimately needs.
