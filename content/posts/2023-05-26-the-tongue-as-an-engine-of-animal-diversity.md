description: "A survey in *Science* on 26 May 2023 argues that the tongue, an organ usually treated as an accessory, has shaped how animals feed, breathe and sense, and that its variation tracks major evolutionary transitions."
---

The tongue is a muscle covered in mucosa, and its textbook function is to move food and to shape speech. A survey in *Science*, 26 May 2023 makes the case that the organ is a driver of diversity rather than an accessory.

The argument is comparative. Tongues vary in length, in stiffness, in the papillae that cover them and in the glands that supply them, and the variation correlates with diet, with habitat and with the mechanics of feeding.

The extremes are the evidence. A tongue that extends beyond the body, a tongue that harpoons prey, a tongue that carries a sticky coating, and a tongue that is used to smell are all solutions to the same problem: how to acquire something at a distance.

The biomechanics is the framework. A tongue is a hydrostat, a structure that moves by changing its internal pressure, and a hydrostat can bend, extend and stiffen in ways that a jointed limb cannot. The physics explains the range of shapes.

The sensory role is the underappreciated part. A tongue carries taste buds and, in some animals, chemical sensors that detect prey, and the organ is therefore a sensory surface as well as a manipulator.

The respiratory role is the surprise. In some reptiles the tongue's movement is coupled to the airway, and the organ participates in breathing rather than only in eating.

The evolutionary claim is that the tongue's diversification enabled lineages to occupy new niches. A tongue that reaches into a flower is a tongue that makes a pollinator, and the coevolution of tongues and flowers is one of the clearest cases.

The developmental question is how such different shapes arise from a similar primordium. The paper's review of the genetic control of tongue development suggests that a small number of patterning genes can produce a large range of forms.

The limitation is the record. A tongue is soft tissue and does not fossilize, so the evolutionary history is inferred from living animals and from the bones that support the organ.

What the survey does not provide is a quantitative test. A correlation between tongue form and diet is a pattern, and a comparative analysis with phylogeny is the method that would test it.

For zoology, the value is a change of emphasis. Feeding has been studied through jaws and teeth, and the tongue is the other half of the apparatus, and the half that evolution has varied most.

The policy use is a warning. A lake that is shrinking is a lake whose water rights were allocated in a wetter decade, and a basin plan that assumes the old level is a plan that will fail.

The uncertainty is in the extrapolation. A satellite measures area and a model converts area to volume, and the conversion's error is the largest term in the estimate. The authors' validation against gauges is what bounds it.
The engineering interest is real. A hydrostatic organ that extends, stiffens and bends is a design that soft robotics has borrowed, and a tongue's mechanics are a source of ideas for machines that must reach into confined spaces.
