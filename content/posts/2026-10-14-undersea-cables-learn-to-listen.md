There are roughly one and a half million kilometres of submarine telecommunications cable on the seabed, and almost all of it carries laser light through optical fibre, amplified every fifty to a hundred kilometres by a repeater. The repeaters exist to keep the signal alive. A group of operators and research institutes has been testing whether they can also be used to measure the ocean, and the early results suggest that the answer is yes, with a caveat about who is allowed to look.

A property telecom engineers treat as a nuisance supplies the measurement. Light travelling through a fibre emerges with its polarisation slightly rotated, and the rotation depends on the strain and temperature along the path. A passing seismic wave flexes the cable, changing the rotation in a way that can be read out at the receiving end. The technique, known as state-of-polarisation sensing, requires no new hardware on the seabed: the repeaters already contain the polarimeters, and the interrogator sits on shore.

Results reported this year are mostly seismological. Several cables in the Pacific and the Mediterranean recorded earthquakes that were also detected by land stations, and the cable records filled in the offshore path that the land network cannot see. The ocean is opaque to seismic waves only in the sense that nobody has instruments in it; a cable is a long, dense array that happens to be there already.

Tides and ships are the second application. The same data, filtered differently, shows the tidal loading of the seabed and the pressure signature of large vessels passing above. Ports and navies have obvious interest in the second signal, and that is where the regulatory problem begins. A cable is private infrastructure carrying commercial traffic, and continuous sensing turns it into an instrument that can observe things its owners did not set out to observe. The research consortium has been careful to publish only aggregated data, and the operators have been careful to say that the sensing channel is isolated from the traffic channel.

Technical limits are real. Repeater spacing determines the spatial resolution, which is coarse, and ageing fibre has polarisation properties that drift for reasons unrelated to strain. Calibration requires a reference event, which for earthquakes means waiting for one. Coverage is excellent on the busy routes between continents and poor in the southern ocean, which is precisely where the seismic gap is largest.

Strategy is what makes this more than a curiosity. Ocean-bottom sensing has been a gap in every Earth observation system, and the alternatives, dedicated cables and moored instruments, are expensive and sparse. Using existing infrastructure is cheap, and cheap instruments get deployed widely. If the technique is standardized, the ocean could acquire a sensor network of a kind it has never had, built out of the one piece of infrastructure that already spans it.
