description: "NVIDIA disclosed that a new export licence for its H20 accelerator would cost up to 5.5 billion dollars in the quarter, and that the requirement has no end date."
---

H20 was built to be legal. When export controls tightened in 2022 and again in 2023, NVIDIA designed a part for the Chinese market that sat below the performance thresholds the rules defined, and the H20 became the workhorse of that business. A part designed to a rule is a part whose existence depends on the rule, and on 9 April the rule changed: the government told the company that a licence would be required for H20 exports to China, Hong Kong and Macau, and to any firm headquartered in a country in the D5 group.

Four days later the terms hardened. On 14 April the company was told the licence requirement would stay in effect for the indefinite future, which removes the assumption that a delay is a negotiation. NVIDIA disclosed the change in a filing on 15 April, and the filing carried a number: up to about 5.5 billion dollars of charges in the quarter that ends on 27 April, covering H20 inventory, purchase commitments and related reserves.

Accounting for a stopped shipment produces a charge. Wafers already at the foundry, packages already in test, and components already ordered cannot be sold into the market they were designed for, and the value of those commitments has to be written down in the quarter the rule arrived. A company that builds to forecast, as NVIDIA does, holds inventory ahead of demand; a rule that closes a market turns that inventory into a loss in one step.

The stated risk was supercomputing. The government said the licence requirement addresses the possibility that the covered products could be used in, or diverted to, a supercomputer in China. The H20's memory bandwidth and interconnect bandwidth are the quantities the rule names, which is a technical way of saying that the part's value in a cluster is not its arithmetic but its ability to feed it. Exports below that envelope, or parts with lower bandwidth, are not covered.

Investors marked the shares down by several per cent, and the reaction was about more than one quarter. China had been a large share of the data-centre business, and the H20 was the only high-end part that could be sold there. A charge is a one-time cost; the loss of a market is a permanent change in the shape of the revenue, and the filing told the market that the second was now the case.

The charge is also an estimate. The company said up to 5.5 billion dollars, which means the final figure depends on how much inventory can be redirected to other customers and how much of the purchase commitment can be cancelled. Firms in this position renegotiate with suppliers, sell parts into secondary markets and write off the remainder, and the number that appears in the quarterly report will be the outcome of those negotiations rather than the first estimate.

What comes next is a question about the boundary. Export controls have moved from a list of parts to a list of performance thresholds, and each move has produced a compliant design below the line. A licence requirement with no term and no published criteria makes that design exercise much harder, because a company cannot know which specification will be judged to be a supercomputer component next year.
