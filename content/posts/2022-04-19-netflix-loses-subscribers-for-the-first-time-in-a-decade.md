---

Netflix reported on 19 April that it had lost 200,000 paid subscribers in the first quarter, the first quarterly decline since October 2011, against guidance it had given three months earlier of a gain of 2.5 million. Revenue rose 9.8 per cent to 7.87 billion dollars and net profit fell to 1.6 billion from 1.71 billion a year earlier, and the company told investors to expect another two million net losses in the current quarter.

Management attributed part of the loss to its withdrawal from Russia, which accounted for about 700,000 subscribers, but not all of it. Growth had already stalled in the United States and Canada, where the market is saturated and where price increases had begun to bite, and the quarter's subscriber add came almost entirely from the Asia-Pacific region, at lower average revenue. Latin America and Europe contributed little, and average revenue per member fell as growth shifted to cheaper plans. The company had raised prices in the two largest markets in the previous year.

A bigger number in the report was an estimate rather than a result. Netflix said that about 100 million households, close to half of its total base, were using accounts shared with people outside the household, and that in many of those homes the service was watched without being paid for. That disclosure reframed the problem from market saturation to monetization, and it pointed at the two fixes the company would soon announce: a cheaper advertising-supported tier and a charge for sharing accounts outside the home. Analysts had asked for years whether password sharing could be turned into paying accounts, and this was the first disclosure that put a number on the opportunity.

Market reaction was severe and quick. Shares fell 35 per cent on 20 April, closing at 226.19 dollars, down 122.42, the largest one-day drop in the company's history and a loss of roughly 54 billion dollars in market value. The stock had already fallen through the winter as subscribers plateaued after the pandemic surge, and this report removed the last argument that the slowdown was a pause.

Damage spread beyond one company. Streaming rivals that had been valued on subscriber growth, including Disney and Warner Bros. Discovery, fell in sympathy, and the sector's assumption that the world would keep adding paid accounts at the pace of 2020 looked wrong. Hollywood had reorganized itself around the idea that streaming subscribers rise forever, and that idea lost its evidence in one afternoon. Advertising-supported tiers moved from experiment to plan at every studio within weeks.

The strategic problem is harder than the accounting. Growth in the rich markets came from raising prices and converting shared accounts, both of which risk driving customers away, while growth in poor markets comes at prices that barely cover content. Netflix had spent a decade borrowing to buy programming on the promise of scale, and the promise now required a different arithmetic. The company had committed to a slate of programming that assumed the subscribers it had just lost, which is the part of the report nobody wanted to discuss.
