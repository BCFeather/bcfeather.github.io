China Central Television broadcasts a gala on the evening of the lunar new year's eve that runs past midnight, and almost every household that celebrates the holiday has it on in the background. The programme mixes songs, comic sketches, dance and a countdown, and its running time is fixed by the clock rather than by the material. What makes it unusual as a broadcast is that the advertising inside it is sold as a separate market with its own calendar and its own price discovery. An advertiser buying the first break is buying attention that has already been earned by the holiday rather than by the programme.

The economics of the gala rest on a scarcity that no other programme can match. A four-hour slot on the most-watched night of the year gives an advertiser a reach that cannot be bought elsewhere, and the inventory is limited to the breaks and to the placements inside the programme. Prices for the seconds around the countdown have historically been the highest in Chinese television, and the auction for them takes place months before the broadcast. A single thirty-second slot near midnight has been reported at prices that would buy a prime-time week on an ordinary channel.

Programming is shaped by the same constraint. A sketch that runs long pushes the schedule, and the schedule is a contract with the advertisers who bought the position. That is why the gala tends to favour familiar forms and to cut material that needs time to develop, and why critics describe it as a variety show with a fixed architecture. Producers work to a running order that is locked weeks in advance, and the dress rehearsal is timed to the second.

Audiences have fragmented. Younger viewers watch clips on short-video platforms during the evening and skip the live broadcast, and the ratings that the broadcaster reports now include those secondary views. The result is a programme that is simultaneously less watched as broadcast and more consumed as excerpt, which changes what an advertiser is buying.

Competition has appeared on the same night. Provincial broadcasters and online platforms schedule their own galas, and several of them target younger audiences with faster formats. Those shows cost a fraction of the national production and reach a fraction of the audience, and they have taken the part of the market that wants something other than a family programme. Sponsorship has moved from the break to the set, with brand names appearing in the staging and in the credits.

The ritual is the part that does not translate into an advertising metric. Families watch together because the evening is a holiday, and a programme that is on in the background is doing a different job from one that is watched attentively. A broadcaster that optimizes for the second job and loses the first has traded a national habit for a rating, and the trade is not obviously good. Broadcasters have responded by putting the strongest material early, when the family is still at the table.
