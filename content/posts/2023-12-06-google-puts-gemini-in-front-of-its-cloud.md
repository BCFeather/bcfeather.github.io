description: "Google released Gemini on 6 December 2023 as a family of three models rather than a single product, claimed state-of-the-art results on reasoning benchmarks and put the largest version behind its cloud for enterprises and researchers."
---

Google announced Gemini on 6 December, a family of three models rather than one, and described it as the centre of its artificial intelligence work for the next several years. The largest, Gemini Ultra, was released to researchers and cloud customers for testing; the middle one, Pro, went into the company's own products; the smallest, Nano, was designed to run on phones. The announcement came three weeks after Microsoft had put its own models at the centre of an annual developer conference.

Benchmarks were the substance of the pitch. Google said Ultra had scored above ninety per cent on a set of 57 subjects in a widely used test of general knowledge, the first model to do so, and had beaten the best previous systems on reasoning tasks that involve diagrams, tables and multi-step mathematics. Independent researchers could not check all of it on the day, because the largest model remained closed to the public, and the company promised a wider release early in 2024.

Architecturally, the model is a single network trained on several kinds of data at once, which is the technical claim worth noting for people who do not follow the field. Earlier systems stitched a language model to an image encoder; Gemini handles text, images, audio and code in one stack, which makes multimodal tasks cheaper to serve and easier to train together. The company published figures for training scale that implied tens of thousands of chips working for weeks, a number that says less about the model than about the electricity bill behind it.

Distribution is the advantage Google bought with the launch. Gemini Pro went into the company's AI features in Workspace and into its cloud console, and the company cut the price of its generative API by more than half for large customers, a move that reads as a bid to make the model the default for businesses that had started with OpenAI. Android's default assistant was promised the model in the new year, which puts it on more devices than any competitor can reach.

Risk is the part that has followed every large model release of the past two years. Google's own researchers have documented the tendency of such systems to produce confident falsehoods, and the company's reputation for launching products that are later withdrawn made journalists cautious. There were also questions about training data, about the energy consumed by serving hundreds of millions of queries, and about what happens to publishers whose work is summarized in an answer rather than clicked in a results page.

What the launch settled was the shape of the competition rather than its outcome. Three American companies now sell access to models of comparable scale, with different strengths in distribution, price and enterprise trust, and the market is deciding faster than the technology is maturing. Google's bet is that the model matters less than where it sits, which is the bet a company with a search engine, an operating system and a cloud is uniquely able to make.
