Kenya's meteorological department declared in late August that the long rains had failed again across most of the country, the fifth consecutive season in which the rains came late, came short, or did not come at all. The consequence shows up first in the reservoirs, and then in the electricity bill. Hydropower, which supplied roughly a third of Kenya's electricity a decade ago, has been running well below its capacity for two years, and the gap has been filled mainly by steam.

Kenya's Rift Valley is one of the few places on earth where geothermal power is cheap enough to be boring. The country's first plant at Olkaria came online in the 1980s, and the fields have been expanded steadily since, with wells drilled into a volcanic system that reheats the water fast enough to sustain production. KenGen and the independent producers now operate more than a thousand megawatts of geothermal capacity, and the energy ministry said in August that geothermal supplied about thirty-five per cent of the country's electricity in the first half of the year, up from a quarter in 2023.

The appeal is precisely that the resource does not depend on rain. A geothermal plant runs at a high load factor, day and night, and its fuel is free once the well is drilled. In a country whose hydrology is becoming less reliable, that is worth more than the tariff alone suggests. The state utility has been able to keep the lights on through the drought without the rolling blackouts that neighbouring grids experienced, and the share of expensive emergency diesel generation has stayed low.

The costs are of a different kind. Geothermal wells are expensive to drill and uncertain: a dry hole costs tens of millions of dollars and produces nothing, which is why the sector has relied on development finance and risk guarantees. The fields also sit inside a landscape that communities use for grazing, and the steam operations consume water, a sensitive subject in a drought. The government has been trying to route a share of the revenue to county governments and to speed up compensation for land, with mixed results.

The bigger picture is regional. The East African Rift runs for thousands of kilometres through Kenya, Tanzania, Ethiopia and beyond, and the same volcanic geology that powers Olkaria underlies all of it. Ethiopia has built geothermal capacity, Tanzania has been drilling, and the African Development Bank has been funding exploration across the corridor. If the drought that has hit eastern Africa for five seasons is a sign of the climate shifting rather than a temporary failure of the rains, then the steam under the Rift is one of the few resources in the region that becomes more valuable as the climate changes.

Kenya still has a way to go. Demand is growing faster than any single resource can meet, the grid needs reinforcement to carry power from the Rift to the coast, and the country's industrial users pay tariffs that remain high by regional standards. But the direction is clear enough, and it is unusual: an African grid that has decided to build its baseload on a resource that the weather cannot take away.
