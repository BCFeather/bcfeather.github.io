Literary prizes are usually discussed as recognition and occasionally as money. A prize for translated fiction, awarded this month for the thirty-first time, has become something more concrete: the largest single driver of translated literary sales in the two markets where its winner is stocked.

The mechanism is a retail commitment rather than publicity. Booksellers in those markets have agreed to stock the shortlist for eight weeks before the award, and the prize's organizers buy display space rather than advertising. A shortlisted title in a translated fiction category sells, on average, twenty times what it would sell without the listing.

This year's changes are structural. The award doubled to 12,000 euros, split between author and translator, and the ceremony moved from a capital to a regional city, which brought a different press corps and a smaller venue. The split recognizes a fact the industry had ignored for decades: a translated novel is a collaboration, and the translator's name appears on the cover.

Sales data behind the claim comes from two national book-tracking services. In the year after a win, the winning title's sales rise by a median of 340 per cent, and the shortlist's by 60 per cent, with the effect strongest in independent bookshops and weakest in supermarkets.

The translation market it serves is small and fragile. Roughly 3 per cent of books published in the larger European markets are translations, and the number has been flat for a decade. A prize does not change that structure; it changes the visibility of the few hundred titles that do get translated.

Selection controversy is part of the prize's history. Two years ago the jury chose a novel whose translator had worked from an intermediate language rather than the original, and the resulting argument produced a rule requiring disclosure of the source language. This year the rule was applied for the first time, and one entry was withdrawn before the shortlist.

What the prize does to a reading list is measurable and modest. Booksellers report that customers who buy one shortlisted title buy another, which is a cross-selling effect that a bestseller does not produce. The organisers describe the goal as building a habit rather than selling a book.

Sustainability is the open question. The prize depends on a foundation grant renewed every five years and on the booksellers' commitment, and both are voluntary. A commercial sponsor would bring money and editorial pressure, which is the trade every literary prize makes.

Jury composition is the recurring argument. Translators, critics and booksellers sit on the panel, and the balance between them determines whether the prize rewards difficulty or readability. This year's jury included three translators, the highest share in the prize's history, and the shortlist reflected it.

Read as cultural policy, the interesting feature is the lever chosen. Money went to display space rather than to advertising, and the effect exceeded what advertising budgets of the same size achieve, because the constraint on translated fiction is not awareness but availability.
