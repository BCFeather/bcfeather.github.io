Strontium ruthenate, \(\mathrm{Sr}_{2}\mathrm{RuO}_{4}\), has been a test case for correlated-electron theory for three decades, because it superconducts at a few kelvin and its normal state behaves like a metal that is nearly magnetic. The pairing mechanism is still argued about, and the argument turns on which collective excitations could mediate it. The compound sits near a magnetic instability, and that proximity is what makes the fluctuations strong enough to matter.

Spins and orbitals are the two candidates. A spin fluctuation can carry momentum between electrons, and an orbital fluctuation can change the occupation of the ruthenium orbitals, which alters the interaction. A measurement that separates the two would tell a theorist which channel to keep. A zone-resolved map is what can provide the separation, since a single momentum point cannot distinguish two channels that overlap in energy.

Work posted to arXiv on 1 December 2022 provides the separation, using resonant inelastic X-ray scattering at the ruthenium edge to map the excitations over the entire Brillouin zone. The maps show several dispersive branches, and the branches fall into two groups at distinctly different energy scales, one identified with spin and the other with orbital character. A separation into two energy scales is the cleanest statement of it, since a single branch would leave the assignment ambiguous.

The assignment is supported by theory. Response functions computed within dynamical mean-field theory agree with the data for both groups, which is a stronger check than reproducing a single dispersion, since the two channels are computed from the same self-energy. Agreement on both channels is what rules out an accidental fit to one of them, and the calculation is not tuned to the data.

The agreement points at a Hund's metal. In that picture the local interaction between the three ruthenium orbitals is what sets the electronic structure, and the excitations inherit the same hierarchy. Whether that picture also explains the superconductivity is the question the paper hands to the next one. An orbital-selective picture would place both channels at one scale, and the data rule that out, which is a sharper statement than any claim about the strength of one coupling.

What the measurement does not do is resolve the momentum dependence at the scale of the superconducting gap, which is far smaller than the energy resolution of the technique. A reader should therefore take the maps as a statement about the normal state and about the bosons that live there. Normal-state maps are still the right comparison for a pairing calculation, since the pairing instability grows out of the normal state.

A theory that uses these response functions to compute a pairing strength is the immediate next step, and the maps are the input such a calculation would need. Whether the same response functions produce a pairing strength consistent with the measured critical temperature is the question that would settle the mechanism.
