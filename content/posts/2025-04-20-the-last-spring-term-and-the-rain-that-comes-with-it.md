Guyu, the solar term that falls on the twentieth of April, translates as the rain of the grain, and it marks the point when the spring sowing is finished and the summer crop begins. The name is a description of what the calendar expects, a period of steady rain that arrives when the seedlings need it, and the expectation is the oldest kind of weather forecast, a statement about an average written into the language.

The agricultural logic behind it is sound. The winter wheat in the north is heading and filling at this time of year and needs water, the rice in the south is being transplanted into the paddy and needs the fields to be wet, and the spring maize in the centre is emerging and needs the soil to stay moist. The three crops are in different states in different regions and they all want rain in the same fortnight, which is why the term exists.

The tea comes at the same moment and the calendar has a word for that too. The leaves picked before guyu are called the pre-rain tea, and they are the most expensive of the year because the spring growth is slow, the buds are small, and the flavor that the market pays for is produced in the cool weeks before the warmth arrives. A week of heat at the wrong moment will open the leaf and drop the price, and the picking is scheduled around a forecast.

What has changed is the reliability of the rain. The record of the last three decades shows the spring rainfall in the north becoming more variable, with the total roughly constant and the arrival less predictable, which is harder for a farmer than a dry year that everyone can see coming. The irrigation that has been installed over the same period covers part of the gap, and it costs money and water and a pump, and the farms that have it are the ones that can wait.

A change in the human calendar comes with the term as well. The spring that began at lichun in February ends here, and the summer begins, with the school term past its middle, the heat arriving in the south, and the first of the summer fruit in the market. The naming of the period is a way of noticing that the year has passed its halfway point in the agricultural sense, whatever the calendar on the wall says.

What the term is good for, in a city, is a reminder that the food arrives on a schedule that a supermarket hides. The grain that the rain is named for will be harvested in June, milled in July, and sold in August, and the price of a bag of flour in the autumn is decided by the rain that falls in this fortnight. The name of the term is a sentence about that chain, and the chain is longer than most shoppers think, and the rain of the grain is the first link in it.
