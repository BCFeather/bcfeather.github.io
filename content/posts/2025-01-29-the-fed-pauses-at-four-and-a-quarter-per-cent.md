description: "The Federal Open Market Committee left the target range at 4.25 to 4.50 per cent on 29 January, the first meeting without a cut since the easing cycle began, and dropped its language about progress towards the inflation target."
---

Three cuts in four months took the federal funds rate from 5.25 to 4.5 per cent between September and December, and the January meeting was the first in which the committee had nothing to do. The statement issued on 29 January kept the target range at 4.25 to 4.50 per cent and changed almost nothing else, which in the language of central banking is the message.

What it dropped matters more than what it kept. The December statement said that inflation had made progress towards the committee's 2 per cent objective. The January statement deleted that sentence and retained only the observation that inflation remains somewhat elevated, which is a downgrade of the committee's own assessment without a change in the policy setting, and a committee that stops saying progress has occurred is a committee that has noticed the last mile is the slow one.

Labour market language moved in the other direction. The December statement described conditions as having generally eased; the January statement said the unemployment rate had stabilized at a low level in recent months and that labour market conditions remain solid. A committee that removes a concern about employment and adds a concern about prices is a committee that has shifted its weighting.

Activity was described as expanding at a solid pace, which matches the data. Growth in the fourth quarter ran near 2.5 per cent, consumption held up through the holidays, and the unemployment rate ended the year below 4.2 per cent. An economy with solid growth, unemployment below 4.2 per cent and inflation above target gives a central bank no reason to cut and no strong reason to raise, which is the definition of a pause that could last several meetings.

Powell, at the press conference, said the committee was not in a hurry. The sentence was reported as a signal about the March meeting, and it was, but it also described the committee's position: with the policy rate still restrictive by most estimates, the cost of waiting is lower than the cost of easing into an inflation that has stopped falling.

Tariffs were the subject that the statement did not address and the press conference could not avoid. The new administration had announced measures in its first weeks, and the committee's projections assume that fiscal and trade policy will be known before the next set of forecasts. A tariff is a price shock that a central bank can look through if it is one-off and cannot if it is passed into wages.

The balance sheet continued to shrink, and the pace of runoff was unchanged. That is a second instrument of tightening operating in the background while the policy rate holds, and the committee has said it will slow the runoff before it stops it, which means the balance sheet will keep draining through the year even if the rate does not move.
