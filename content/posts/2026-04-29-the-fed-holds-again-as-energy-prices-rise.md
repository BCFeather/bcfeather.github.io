description: "The Federal Open Market Committee kept the target range at 3.50 to 3.75 per cent on 29 April, citing elevated inflation that it attributed partly to higher global energy prices, with one dissent in favour of a cut."
---

The March meeting held rates and described inflation as somewhat elevated. The April meeting, on 29 April, held them again and described inflation as elevated, in part reflecting the recent increase in global energy prices. The change of adverb is the whole story of the two meetings.

The target range stayed at 3.50 to 3.75 per cent, the level reached after three quarter-point cuts at the end of 2025. Economic activity was described as expanding at a solid pace, job gains as remaining low on average, and the unemployment rate as little changed, which is a labour market that is neither a reason to cut nor a reason to tighten, and a committee that describes it that way is a committee waiting for a signal from somewhere else.

A further sentence noted that developments in the Middle East are contributing to a high level of uncertainty about the economic outlook, which is the language a central bank uses when a shock is visible and its size is not. An energy shock is the one kind of inflation that a central bank cannot address without causing a recession, because the supply is constrained and the price is set outside its jurisdiction.

Governor Stephen Miran dissented again, preferring a quarter-point reduction. A dissent that repeats across meetings is a dissent that has become a position, and the position is that restrictive policy is doing damage to the labour market that the committee will only see after the fact.

Voting was otherwise unanimous among Jerome Powell, John Williams, Michael Barr, Michelle Bowman, Lisa Cook, Philip Jefferson, Anna Paulson and Christopher Waller. A committee that agrees on a hold while disagreeing about why is a committee that will move quickly once the data settle the question.

What the hold does to expectations is the part markets read first. A statement that names energy prices as an inflation source is a statement that the committee is not going to look through the shock, and a committee that will not look through a shock is a committee that is further from cutting than the previous meeting's dot plot implied.

Asymmetry is worth noting. If energy prices fall back, the case for a cut returns and the committee can move. If energy prices stay high and feed into wages and services, the committee is stuck between inflation above target and an economy that is slowing, which is the worst position a central bank can occupy. The next meeting falls in June, and between now and then the data that matter are the energy price path, the employment reports and the inflation prints that exclude energy. Those three series will decide whether the pause was patience or the beginning of a longer hold, and the statement itself leaves both readings open, which is the kind of ambiguity a committee produces when its members agree on the decision and not on its rationale.
