description: "A graph network trained to explain which parts of a molecule carry the activity pointed chemists at a scaffold outside the known families, and the resulting compounds worked in two mouse infection models."
---

A graph neural network trained on chemical structures has found a family of antibiotics unlike anything in the current arsenal, and the paper that reports it is as interesting for how the search was run as for what it found. The work began with the observation that most new antibacterial candidates are variations on scaffolds discovered in the 1950s, and that a model trained to recognise those scaffolds will keep proposing them. The authors therefore trained their network on a different question: given a structure, does it inhibit the growth of a bacterium, and if so, which parts of the structure seem to be responsible. That reframing is what made the result possible, because a model trained to recognise known antibiotics reproduces the habits of the field, and the habits are the problem.

The model is a message-passing network, which means it treats a molecule as a graph of atoms and bonds and learns which local arrangements matter for the property it is predicting. The version used here was built to explain itself, and the explanation takes the form of a set of substructures that the network weights most heavily when it makes a prediction. That matters because a hit with no explanation cannot be optimised: a chemist needs to know which part of the molecule to keep and which part to vary. The explanation is not a proof, since a highlighted substructure says where the model looked and not why the bacterium died, but it shortens the list of analogues worth making from hundreds to a few dozen.

Screening ran through a commercial library of millions of compounds, and the output was ranked by predicted activity, with the top few per cent selected for testing in the laboratory. The compounds that came back active showed a common core that was not present in the training set of known antibiotics, which is what the authors mean by a structural class, and the class was then explored by synthesis around that core. That the core had been sitting in a commercial library all along is the uncomfortable part of the result: the compounds existed, and the search was what had been missing. The screen covered a library of millions of molecules, and the few hundred ordered and tested came from the top of the ranking rather than a random draw.

Activity was measured against a panel that included methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus, and the best compounds held at concentrations that a clinician would recognise as plausible. Resistance was tested by serial passage, the standard method of growing a bacterium in increasing drug concentrations to see whether it adapts, and the frequency of resistant mutants was low, which is the property that makes a new class worth pursuing. The concentrations sit in the range that medicinal chemists treat as a starting point, and the next round of synthesis usually gains a factor of ten before a compound is worth an animal.

Animal work followed the usual sequence: a mouse model of a systemic infection and a mouse model of a skin infection, with the compound given at doses the pharmacokinetics could support. Survival improved in both, and the compound could be measured in the blood at concentrations above the minimum that inhibited the bacterium in the dish, which is the minimum a paper of this kind has to show before anyone talks about a drug. The doses came from a pharmacokinetic study run in parallel, and the concentrations in the blood stayed above the inhibitory level for several hours. Neither model is the one a trial would use, and the survival differences were modest, which is normal at this stage.

The caveats are the ones that apply to every antibiotic candidate at this stage. Two mouse models are not a trial, the chemistry has not been optimised for solubility or for half-life, and toxicity in a mammal over weeks is not the same as toxicity in a mammal over days. The larger point is about the search and not the molecule: a model that explains its predictions can be pointed at chemical space that the field has not been mining, and the first result from that direction was a class nobody had looked at. Scope limits it as well: the model was trained on data for one organism, and a compound that works against Staphylococcus aureus says nothing yet about the Gram-negative pathogens that the World Health Organization ranks as the priorities. The compounds also need testing against human cells, since a molecule that disturbs a bacterial membrane can disturb a red cell, and that check is the one that most often removes a candidate.
