Chromosome organization depends on loop extrusion, in which a protein complex grips a DNA duplex, reels it in and forms a growing loop, and most accounts of the process stop at how it shapes the sequence. The mechanical side has been left aside: a nucleus is full of entangled polymer, and what extrusion does to that entanglement decides how freely anything inside can move.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 30 January 2024 simulates that side. The authors model entangled solutions of linear polymers under generic loop-extruding factors, in a scheme that keeps the topological constraints of the chains and the rule that an extruding factor and the DNA cannot pass through one another, so the loops created are loops in a real tangle and not in an idealized chain.

The result is fluidification. Extrusion drives the formation of bottle-brush-like structures, in which loops sit along a backbone, and these structures lower the number of entanglements per chain and therefore the effective viscosity of the whole system, which is the quantity that sets how fast the interior can deform. There is an optimum: the viscosity falls most sharply when there is roughly one extruding factor per 300 to 3,000 basepairs, and it falls less at higher and lower densities, since too few extruders leave the tangle intact and too many crowd the backbone without adding new loops.

The scaling changes with the architecture. In a melt of linear chains the viscosity grows steeply with polymer length, and in the extruded system it grows linearly with length instead, which is why the total effect reaches a thousandfold reduction at the longest chains simulated. The reduction is not a function of density alone, since the architecture of the bottle brush sets how the loops are spaced.

Mechanism and result are not the same claim. A bottle brush is thinner than the coil it replaces, so it has fewer neighbours to be entangled with, and the linear scaling follows from the way the loops space out along the backbone; the simulation reproduces both, which is why the optimum can be attributed to structure rather than to a coincidence in the parameters.

What the model leaves out is the cell. It treats the medium as a solution of linear polymers, while a real nucleus contains chromosomes that are themselves organized into loops, and it takes the extruding factors as generic, not as the specific motors of a given organism, so the density that is optimal here is a prediction to be tested rather than a measured property.

A measurement that could test it would be a rheological one on a reconstituted system in which the density of extruders can be titrated while the viscoelastic spectrum is recorded. If the viscosity minimum sits where the simulation puts it, loop extrusion is doing mechanical work on the nucleus and not only on its sequence, and that would change how the motor's processivity is treated in models of chromosome motion.
