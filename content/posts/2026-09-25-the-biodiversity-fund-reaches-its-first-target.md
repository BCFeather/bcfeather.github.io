The biodiversity fund established under the Kunming-Montreal framework announced this month that it has reached its first capitalization target, the threshold that was supposed to be met in 2025. The announcement was made at a meeting of the fund's council, and it came with the caveat that a substantial share of the total is pledged rather than deposited. That caveat is the story.

Multilateral environmental funds have a characteristic life cycle. Governments promise money at a conference, the promise is counted as progress, and then the money arrives over several budget cycles, sometimes in a different form, sometimes not at all. The biodiversity fund has followed that cycle closely: pledges came in quickly after the framework was agreed in 2022, disbursements lagged, and the fund's first years were spent on governance and on persuading countries that it was worth funding rather than on projects.

The projects themselves are small by the standards of the problem. The fund's design directs money towards developing countries with high biodiversity and limited fiscal space, and towards projects that can demonstrate a measurable outcome: a protected area with a management plan, a subsidy reform that removes a perverse incentive, a corridor that connects two fragments of habitat. Those projects are cheap relative to the scale of the crisis and expensive relative to the administrative capacity of the countries that need them, which is why the fund has spent as much on capacity-building as on land.

The pledge-versus-payment gap is not only a matter of tardiness. Money that arrives late arrives in a different political context, and a project that was designed for a three-year budget may find itself competing with a new government's priorities. Several recipient countries have reported that the delay forced them to sequence projects differently, starting with the ones that could be financed from domestic budgets and using the fund for the parts that could wait. That is a rational response and a poor one, because the parts that wait are often the monitoring that proves the outcome.

The broader question is whether the fund's model can scale. The framework's target is a flow of billions per year, and the fund's first capitalization is a fraction of that. The gap is supposed to be filled by redirecting subsidies that harm nature, which is a domestic political problem in every country and has nothing to do with international generosity. No fund can solve it, and the fund's defenders say so openly: the money is a catalyst, not the mechanism.

What the announcement does establish is that the institution works. A fund with a council, a secretariat, an accreditation process and a track record of disbursement is a machine that can absorb more money if more money appears. That sounds like a low bar, and it is the bar that most multilateral environmental initiatives fail to clear. The next test is the replenishment, where the pledges will be made again, and where the gap between what is promised and what is paid will be the number to watch.
