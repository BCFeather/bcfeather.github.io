NVIDIA reported results for the quarter ended 29 January on 22 February, and the revenue figure was 6.05 billion dollars. That was 21 per cent lower than a year earlier and slightly above the guidance the company had given in November. Data centre revenue reached 3.61 billion dollars, a record for the segment and a fifth above the previous quarter. Gaming revenue fell 46 per cent to 1.83 billion, and the professional visualization line fell 65 per cent. The quarter was the fourth in which revenue fell, and the decline was smaller than in the previous three.

Two halves of the business moved in opposite directions for the third quarter running. Gamers had bought heavily during the pandemic, and the channel was still holding inventory that had been ordered in 2021. Data centre customers were buying the chips that train language models, and the orders came from a small number of large buyers. Management said the concentration was a risk it accepted for the growth. Inventory at the company stood at 5.9 billion dollars, and it had been 4.4 billion a year earlier.

Gross margin was 66.1 per cent on a reported basis, down from 67 per cent a year earlier, and the company took a charge of 1.1 billion dollars for inventory. That charge was largely for gaming cards that had been built for a market that did not arrive. Operating expenses rose 27 per cent as hiring continued through the year, and the company said it would slow the pace of additions. Headcount passed 26,000, and the company said the additions would slow to a few hundred per quarter.

Guidance for the following quarter was 6.5 billion dollars, above the 6.0 billion consensus. The company said data centre demand would stay strong through the year and that gaming would improve in the second half as inventory cleared. Analysts noted that the guidance implied a return to growth in the summer, which is earlier than the industry had expected. The guidance implied a sequential increase of 7 per cent, the first rise since the previous spring.

Shares had fallen 50 per cent from the 2021 peak and rose 8 per cent after the release. Investors were watching the inventory line more than the revenue, because it decides when the gaming business returns to normal. The company also said it had resumed shipments of a chip that had been held back by export rules, without giving details. Short interest in the stock was about 2 per cent of the float, and it had been higher in the autumn. The export rules covered the most advanced accelerators, and the company designed a variant to fit under the threshold.

For the full year revenue was 26.9 billion dollars, roughly flat with the previous year, and data centre passed gaming as the largest segment for the first time. The shift had been under way since 2020, when the first large language models were trained on the company's hardware. Management said the installed base of such systems would matter more than the quarterly cycles from then on. Data centre revenue for the year was 15 billion dollars, and gaming 9 billion. The company said it would publish a separate line for automotive chips from the next quarter.
