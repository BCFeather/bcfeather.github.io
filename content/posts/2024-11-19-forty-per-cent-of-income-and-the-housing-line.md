In 2023, 8.8 per cent of the European Union's population lived in a household that spent 40 per cent or more of its disposable income on housing. The share was 10.6 per cent in cities and 7.0 per cent in rural areas, and it ranged from 2.6 per cent in Cyprus to 28.5 per cent in Greece, with Denmark at 15.4, Germany at 13.0, Luxembourg at 11.5, Bulgaria at 11.1 and Sweden at 10.9. The union's house price-to-income ratio stood at 100.9 against a long-run average of 100, which says that prices had barely outpaced incomes in the aggregate. Both statements are true, and the distance between them is where the argument lives.

A ratio is easy to misread, and this one divides two survey answers. Housing costs include the rent or the mortgage interest, and the utilities that come with occupancy; they are net of housing allowances, and the income is net too. An owner who has paid off the mortgage registers a low cost and therefore a low ratio, which is why countries with high ownership rates look comfortable in the statistic while their private tenants do not. Imputed rents complicate comparisons further, since a household that owns outright consumes a housing service worth something even if it pays nothing, and the survey assigns it a value in some analyses and not in others. The forty per cent line is a convention that travels badly.

Housing is the large expenditure that cannot be traded down without moving. A household facing a rent rise in a tight market has three options: pay, move further out and accept the commute, or leave the city, and each of those is a decision about a job, a school and a network and not about a monthly line. Supply cannot respond within a year, because building takes land, permits, materials and labour, and the labour is scarce in exactly the places where demand is concentrated. The result is that a demand shock arrives as a price shock, and a price shock arrives as a distributional one.

Demand subsidies are the instrument that governments reach for first, and they have a known defect. A rent allowance paid to every eligible tenant raises the amount a landlord can ask, particularly where supply is fixed and the waiting list is long, so part of the subsidy is capitalized into the rent and the tenant's net position improves by less than the transfer. The magnitude of that effect depends on the elasticity of supply and on how the allowance is designed, and the empirical literature disagrees about both, but the mechanism is not in dispute. Whether the answer is to build, to regulate, or to pay is therefore a question about which constraint is binding.

On the other side sits rent regulation. A cap protects the sitting tenant and produces a two-tier market, in which the protected tenancy is cheap and the new tenancy carries the difference; the longer the controls last, the wider the gap between the two. Germany's rent brake has been evaluated repeatedly, and the finding is consistently modest: rents in regulated areas rose a little more slowly than they would have, exemptions and loopholes absorbed part of the effect, and new construction in the regulated segment slowed. The trade-off is real, and it is a trade-off between the tenant who is already inside and the household that is trying to enter.

Social housing is the third instrument, and its share varies by an order of magnitude across the union, from a few per cent in some member states to more than a fifth in others. Where the stock is large, it damps the private market at the bottom and gives key workers a foothold; where it is small and targeted at the poorest, it concentrates disadvantage in estates that the private market has abandoned. Public construction is expensive per unit, slow, and vulnerable to the same land and labour constraints as private building, which is why the countries that rely on it most also run the longest waiting lists.

No subsidy lifts the constraint that actually binds, which is the capacity to build. Bricklayers, electricians and project managers are scarce in the cities where the waiting lists are longest, and a permit that takes three years to clear does not respond to a rent rise this year. Materials costs rose through 2022 and 2023, financing costs rose with them, and a developer who cannot fix a sale price cannot borrow against the project. Modular methods shorten the schedule at the margin and need a pipeline of orders large enough to keep a factory open. The countries that build most are the ones with a standing programme and a planning system that answers within a known period, not the ones with the largest subsidy.

The counter-case for the market is stronger than its critics allow. Housing markets are not competitive in the textbook sense, since land is fixed and planning is a public decision, but the last two decades offer natural experiments. Spain built at a rate that no regulator would have permitted and then absorbed a collapse that left a generation with negative equity; Ireland did the same on a smaller scale. Credit conditions moved prices more than any regulation did, and the countries that suffered least were those where supply kept up with household formation. Blaming landlords for a shortage that planning law created is a misdirection that survives because it is convenient.

Tax treatment pulls against every policy above. Relief on mortgage interest raises what a household can bid and, where supply is fixed, raises prices in proportion; exemptions on capital gains favour the owner who holds and the investor who trades; transaction taxes discourage moving and lock households into the wrong size of dwelling. A property tax levied on the value of the land rather than on the structure falls on the owner of a vacant site and encourages building. The tenure neutrality that economists recommend, taxing owner-occupation and renting alike, is politically unattractive because the owner-occupier votes and the owner of a vacant plot does not.

Two households can hold the same tenure and absorb the same rate rise over very different periods. Households on variable rates absorbed the rate rises of 2022 and 2023 within months, and the share of income going to interest rose without any change in the rent; households on long fixed rates absorbed them over years. That difference shows up in the overburden rate as a temporary spike in the countries with variable-rate lending, and it will fade as the loans are refinanced, which is a reminder that a one-year statistic cannot distinguish a structural shortage from a monetary cycle.

Short-term letting has removed a slice of the private rented stock from the reach of residents, and the effect is concentrated where rents are already high. Caps on nights and local registration schemes have been passed in several cities, and enforcement depends on platforms with little interest in it. The landlords who remain in the long-let market are increasingly small owners with a single flat and a mortgage of their own, which makes them sensitive to rate rises and quick to sell, and a sale removes a tenancy. The structure of ownership therefore decides how a rent shock is absorbed, while the statistics record the result.

Across countries the distributional pattern is stable. Young households, private tenants, single parents and recent migrants carry the highest ratios; older owners with equity carry the lowest; and the households in the middle move between the two as they age. Homelessness, measured on a given night, is the visible end of the same distribution, not a separate problem, and the countries with the lowest overburden rates are not always the ones with the shortest homelessness counts, because the two statistics measure different points on the queue.

Limits of the evidence are worth naming. The survey is self-reported, the samples are large but the top and bottom of the income distribution are thinly covered, and the treatment of housing allowances differs by country. A country that pays a large allowance and taxes it back shows a different ratio from one that pays nothing, without any difference in what households actually pay. Comparisons across a decade are further complicated by the change from paper to online collection in several member states. A household that rents from a relative, or occupies a dwelling provided by an employer, sits outside the survey, and those arrangements are common in the segments where the ratio is highest.

What the numbers cannot settle is the question the politics keeps asking: whether housing is an asset whose price should be allowed to clear the market, or a service whose price should be set by what a household can pay. A country can choose the first and accept that some households will be priced out of the city they work in, or choose the second and accept that capital will move elsewhere, that maintenance will lag and that the queue will lengthen. Most have chosen a mixture of subsidies, controls and construction, and the mixture is judged each year by a single ratio. That ratio is not a law of nature. It is a line drawn at forty per cent, and the line is a decision.
