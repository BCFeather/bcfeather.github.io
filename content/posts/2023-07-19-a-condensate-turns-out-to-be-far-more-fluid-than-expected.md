A biomolecular condensate is a droplet of protein and RNA formed by phase separation, and its interior has been pictured as a mesh. A mesh would slow diffusion, and a slow interior would explain how a condensate confines a reaction. In *Nature* on 19 July 2023, that picture is complicated. The authors track single molecules inside a condensate and find diffusion coefficients far higher than the mesh picture predicts.

Tracking is the advance. A labelled molecule's trajectory is recorded at high time resolution, and its statistics give local viscosity and the presence of obstacles. Time window is the key parameter, because a trajectory shorter than a binding event's lifetime reports a bound molecule as immobile while a longer one averages over escapes, and a range of windows is what resolves the distribution.

Mobility turns out to be a spectrum. Some molecules move almost as freely as in dilute solution, others are transiently trapped, and the distribution is what a mesh would not produce. Transient network is the interpretation. Interactions holding the condensate together are brief, and a molecule's path samples many binding events rather than a fixed cage. Probe size matters for the comparison, since a small molecule samples the gaps between obstacles and a large one does not, and a range of sizes turns a single number into a picture.

Composition is the variable. A droplet with more RNA carries more charge and more interaction, and mobility falls as the RNA fraction rises. Functional consequence is a different idea of what a condensate does. A droplet fluid inside can host fast reactions, and selectivity would come from local concentration rather than confinement.

Gel offers the contrast. A gel's crosslinks are long-lived and a condensate's are not, and the difference is what the numbers establish. Boundary is the other measurement, since a condensate's surface has a tension, and a low tension allows a droplet to deform and fuse.

System sets the limitation, because one condensate's interior is not every condensate's, and one held together by a different interaction may well be a mesh. Effect on a reaction is what the paper does not show, since a fluid interior changes the diffusion-limited rate and that rate inside a droplet is the next step.

Cell biology gains a correction to a metaphor, because the droplet has been drawn as a distinct phase with a defined boundary, and the measurement shows a sharp boundary with an interior that is not structured. A stress granule is the biological example, since its interior must let a protein enter and leave, and a mesh trapping one permanently would make the granule a sink. A reaction run inside the droplet and outside it, with the same substrates, would show whether the fluid interior changes the rate that the concentration alone cannot explain, and that comparison is the functional claim the paper leaves open. Whether a condensate can be engineered to be more or less fluid is the practical corollary, since a droplet's viscosity is set by its sequence composition and could be tuned for a reaction a cell needs to speed up.
