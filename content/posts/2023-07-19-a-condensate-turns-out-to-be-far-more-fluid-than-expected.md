description: "Single-molecule tracking inside a biomolecular condensate shows that its interior is far more dynamic than a gel-like picture implies, according to work published in *Nature* on 19 July 2023."
---

A biomolecular condensate is a droplet of protein and RNA that forms by phase separation, and the picture of its interior has been a mesh. A mesh would slow diffusion, and a slow interior would explain how a condensate confines a reaction.

The measurements in *Nature*, 19 July 2023 complicate the picture. The authors track single molecules inside a condensate and find diffusion coefficients that are far higher than the mesh picture predicts.

The technique is the advance. A labelled molecule's trajectory is recorded at high time resolution, and the trajectory's statistics give the local viscosity and the presence of obstacles.

The result is a spectrum of mobilities. Some molecules move almost as freely as in dilute solution, and others are transiently trapped, and the distribution is what a mesh would not produce.

The interpretation is a transient network. The interactions that hold the condensate together are brief, and a molecule's path samples many binding events rather than a fixed cage.

The functional consequence is a different idea of what a condensate does. A droplet that is fluid inside can host fast reactions, and its selectivity would come from local concentration rather than from confinement.

The comparison with a gel is the useful contrast. A gel's crosslinks are long-lived, and a condensate's are not, and the difference is what the paper's numbers establish.

The limitation is the system. One condensate's interior is not every condensate's, and a condensate held together by a different interaction may be a mesh.

What the paper does not show is the effect on a reaction. A fluid interior changes the diffusion-limited rate, and the rate's measurement inside a droplet is the next step.

For cell biology, the value is a correction to a metaphor. The droplet has been drawn as a distinct phase with a defined boundary, and the measurement shows a boundary that is sharp and an interior that is not.

The probe's size matters for the comparison. A small molecule samples the gaps between obstacles and a large one does not, and the authors' range of probe sizes is what turns a single number into a picture.

The condensate's composition is the variable. A droplet with more RNA has more charge and more interaction, and the mobility falls as the RNA fraction rises.

The boundary is the other measurement. A condensate's surface has a tension, and a tension that is low allows a droplet to deform and to fuse, which the authors observe.

The biological example is a nucleolus or a stress granule. A granule's interior must allow a protein to enter and to leave, and a mesh that trapped a protein permanently would make the granule a sink.

The theory's adjustment is the paper's conceptual contribution. A phase-separated droplet has been modelled as a concentrated solution, and the measurement says the concentration is real and the structure is not.
The tracking's time window is the key parameter. A trajectory shorter than a binding event's lifetime reports a bound molecule as immobile, and a longer one averages over escapes. The authors' range of windows is what resolves the distribution.
