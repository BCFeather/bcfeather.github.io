Cryo-electron microscopy has transformed structural biology by freezing molecules in a thin film of vitreous ice and imaging them with electrons. The method works best on purified, rigid particles, and the proteins that matter most in drug discovery are often neither: they sit in membranes, in complexes, or inside cells. Synchrotron and free-electron laser sources have been used to improve diffraction for years, and the same physics applies to imaging with electrons.

A method reported in *Science* on 11 June boosts the signal with laser excitation, which raises the number of electrons emitted per incident beam and improves contrast. In practice that means images can be collected from thicker, more crowded specimens, which is what a cell is. Laser excitation is a way of increasing the number of electrons that escape the specimen per unit of beam, which is the physical route to contrast without dose.

The payoff is context. A purified protein is a protein without its neighbours, and the conformation it adopts alone may not be the one it adopts in a membrane or in a complex. Structures solved in situ are closer to the object a drug has to bind. A structure solved inside a cell also shows the protein in the presence of its binding partners, which is the state a drug has to compete with. A drug that has to reach a protein inside a membrane is designed against the state that the structure shows, and the closer that state is to the native one, the fewer surprises in the clinic.

Better contrast also reduces the dose needed, and dose is the limiting factor in cryo-electron microscopy because electrons damage the specimen as they image it. Less damage per unit of information means either higher resolution or more images from the same sample, and both are scarce resources. The method also changes what a sample has to be: a thin, purified particle becomes optional, and a frozen cell section becomes usable.

Drug discovery is the application the authors emphasize. Membrane proteins such as transporters and channels are the hard cases for crystallography and for cryo-electron microscopy alike, and a method that works on them inside a lipid environment removes one of the largest remaining gaps between a structure and a target. The method's resolution in a crowded cell remains lower than what purified particles achieve, and the authors are explicit about that.

The caveats are technical and practical. Laser excitation adds a component that most cryo-electron microscopy facilities do not have, and the alignment and timing of the laser with the electron beam is a source of artefacts that takes expertise to exclude. Whether the method travels beyond the laboratories that built it depends on how easily the optics can be replicated. The replication question is the usual one for a method paper, and the answer depends on a laboratory that did not build the instrument.
