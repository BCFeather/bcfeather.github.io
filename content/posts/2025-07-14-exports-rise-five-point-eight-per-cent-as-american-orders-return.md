A month in which exports accelerate after a tariff truce is a month in which importers restock, and June's customs data show shipments up 5.8 per cent on the year against 4.8 per cent in May, with imports turning positive at 1.1 per cent and a monthly surplus of 114.8 billion dollars, the second largest on record.

The American line improved without recovering. Exports to the United States fell 16 per cent, which is less than half the 34.5 per cent drop in May, as retailers that had stopped ordering shoes, toys and furniture resumed shipments to beat the August deadline, and that resumption is a timing effect that will not repeat in September.

Diversification is doing the structural work. Shipments to Southeast Asia rose 13 per cent in the first half, with Thailand up 22 per cent, Vietnam up nearly 20 per cent and India up more than 18, and part of that is final demand and part is transhipment through countries that face a lower rate. A route that runs through a country with a lower rate is a route that adds a factory and a certificate of origin, and it is the arrangement that makes a decoupling expensive for everyone involved.

Imports turning positive is the more interesting number. A 1.1 per cent increase after months of decline suggests that domestic demand is stabilising and that firms are buying components again, and it also suggests that the front-loading of the spring has run its course and that inventories are being rebuilt at a normal pace.

Prices are absorbing part of the duty. Export prices have fallen in some categories and the volume has held, which means producers are sharing the cost with their American buyers, and the share each side bears is the number that the tariff literature argues about and that the customs data can only hint at. A tariff that is split between producer and buyer is a tariff that distorts less than the headline rate suggests, and it is still a tariff that raises the price of a container and lowers the volume of trade.

The half-year total is a record and the composition is a warning. Trade with the United States fell while trade with everyone else rose, which is what a decoupling looks like from the Chinese side: the same volume of goods, routed through more countries, with more of the value added at home and more of the paperwork abroad. A customs statistic cannot say where a good was made, only where it was declared, and that limitation is the reason the trade numbers look better than the industrial ones.

What would settle the second half is whether the August deadline produces another rush or a cliff. A rush would make July strong and August weak, and no rush would mean that importers have already found their alternative suppliers, which is the outcome that a tariff is designed to produce and that a trade statistic cannot distinguish from a slowdown.
