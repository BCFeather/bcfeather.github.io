Gene editing with a bacterial nuclease requires the patient to tolerate a protein that no human immune system has seen, and the assumption has been that the protein is immunologically invisible because it is delivered briefly. A survey of healthy donors challenges the assumption. A therapeutic protein that comes from a bacterium is a protein that the immune system may already know, and the survey of donors is the test that the field had not run.

Investigators measured antibody and T cell responses to RfxCas13d in healthy donors with ELISA and T cell culture assays, and compared them with responses to the Cas9 proteins of *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Streptococcus pyogenes*. The paper appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 6 June 2022. A comparison with the Cas9 proteins is the right control, because those enzymes have been in development for a decade and the same question was asked of them.

RfxCas13d-reactive antibodies and CD4 and CD8 T cell responses appeared in most donors, at magnitudes comparable to those against the Cas9 proteins. The responding T cells produced interferon gamma, tumour necrosis factor and interleukin 17 when stimulated. A T cell that produces interleukin 17 is a T cell that has been primed, and the cytokine profile is the evidence that the response is adaptive and not innate.

Pre-existing immunity is the consequence of exposure to the bacterial species from which the enzyme was derived, and the exposure is common because these bacteria colonize skin and mucosa.
