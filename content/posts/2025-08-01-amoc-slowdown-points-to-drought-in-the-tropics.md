Carrying heat northwards, the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, or AMOC, has mostly been discussed as a system whose effects stay in the North Atlantic: cooler seas, altered European winters, a different hurricane season. Tropical rainfall is less obviously connected, which is why model projections of its future have stayed uncertain. A study in *Nature*, 30 July 2025, traces the route by which a slowdown in the high latitudes reaches the tropics, then checks that route against a past interval in which the circulation is known to have weakened.

Pedro DiNezio, Timothy Shanahan and colleagues use a combination of climate model simulations and palaeoclimate records from Heinrich Stadial 1, a period of pronounced AMOC weakening. In the models, a cooler tropical and subtropical North Atlantic sends cold air east and west through the prevailing easterly and westerly winds. Air-sea interaction carries that signal across the Pacific and Indian oceans, and the rainfall response reaches Indonesia, the tropical Andes and northern Australia. Under warming scenarios, the same teleconnection produces a consistent multi-model pattern, with a stronger drying signal in Mesoamerica and West Africa than in the models' unconstrained projections.

A second study, published this month in *Communications Earth & Environment*, identifies a complementary fingerprint. Mid-depth warming between 1,000 and 2,000 metres in the equatorial Atlantic is shown, through an MITgcm ocean model, to be coherent with the AMOC on decadal and longer timescales; baroclinic Kelvin waves carry the signal from the subpolar North Atlantic along the western boundary to the equator. Observations since 1960 show a trend that emerges from natural variability only in the early 2000s, implying that the slowdown began late in the last century.

The stadial record is what gives the proposed route weight. Heinrich Stadial 1 provides an observed, rather than modelled, example of a weakened circulation, and the tropical rainfall changes in the records align with what the models produce. The direction is widespread drying across Mesoamerica, the Amazon and West Africa, all of them regions where a small shift in rainfall changes what can be grown and who has water. The agreement between model and proxy does not prove causation within the stadial, but it does make the proposed route the leading candidate.

For people, drought is the channel through which circulation change becomes loss. A cooler North Atlantic is remote from a farmer in West Africa; the rainfall field is not. If the models and the stadial agree, the risk is not merely a cooler ocean but a rearranged tropical hydroclimate, with consequences for agriculture, ecosystems and the populations that depend on both.

Against that, the agreement is between models and one palaeoclimatic interval. The stadial had other drivers besides a weak AMOC, so attribution within that period is not exact. The models also do not agree on every regional detail, and the future warming state differs in structure from a glacial stadial. The result constrains tropical rainfall projections and is not a forecast; it strengthens the case for drought risk in those three regions without specifying when it will arrive.
