description: "In Denmark's DANFLU-2 trial, the extra protection of a high-dose influenza vaccine against hospitalization grew with frailty risk, though categorical frailty subgroups were too imprecise to confirm the pattern."
---

Influenza vaccines are licensed on the basis of antibody responses in healthy adults, and the people who most need them are the ones whose immune systems respond least. A standard dose given to an eighty-five-year-old with heart failure produces a smaller and shorter response than the same dose given to a healthy fifty-year-old, and the resulting hospitalization rate is correspondingly higher in every winter that the virus circulates widely. High-dose formulations were licensed to close that gap, and the evidence for them has been strongest in the healthiest subset of older adults.

DANFLU-2 randomized older adults in Denmark to high-dose or standard-dose influenza vaccine, and a prespecified analysis examined whether frailty modified the effect. Frailty was scored continuously, using a deficit-accumulation index built from routine health data, so that the analysis could treat it as a gradient rather than a category. The results appeared in *The Lancet Healthy Longevity* on 17 September. Denmark's national registers made the continuous score possible, since every citizen's contacts with the health system are recorded and linked.

Relative vaccine effectiveness against hospitalization for influenza or pneumonia rose as frailty risk increased. The pattern is the one the biology predicts, since protection is lowest where immune responsiveness is weakest, and an extra antigen load should matter more in those people. The authors conclude that the findings might support considering high-dose vaccination for frail older adults.

The prespecified categorical subgroups did not show the same pattern, and the confidence intervals crossed the null in every one of them. That is the honest tension in the paper: a continuous analysis suggests effect modification, and the categorical analysis that clinicians would actually use to make a decision cannot confirm it. Imprecision, not absence of effect, is the likely explanation, and it is still a limit on what the trial can claim. Clinicians choosing a vaccine have to act on categories, and the paper cannot tell them which category to use.

Indices built from administrative data are coarse instruments. They capture diagnoses and prescriptions and miss the things that make an older person fragile in practice, and a patient's position on the index can change within a year. An interaction that depends on such a measure is suggestive and will need replication in a trial that stratifies on frailty at enrolment.

Policy implications are modest and real. High-dose vaccines cost more, and a recommendation targeted at the frailest would concentrate spending where the absolute risk of hospitalization is highest. Denmark's registers make this kind of analysis possible at a scale that few countries can match, and the result is a template for how vaccine policy could use routine data. Targeting the frailest also means accepting that some of the extra doses will go to people who would have been protected by the standard one.

None of this changes the basic advice, which is that older adults should be vaccinated against influenza every autumn. It refines the question of which formulation, and it shows that the answer depends on a characteristic that health systems can measure without asking the patient anything.
