---

The soy harvest in Rio Grande do Sul, the southern state that has anchored Brazilian grain production for two generations, has failed to reach its average for the third year running. The state's farmers planted less, the rains came late and uneven, and the yields in the western municipalities that depend on a single summer crop came in well below the levels of the early 2020s. The response is visible in the planting data for the coming season, and it points north.

The northward shift is not new, but it has accelerated. Soy area in the Cerrado, the savanna that covers the centre of the country, has been expanding for twenty years, and the newest frontier is the Matopiba region, the acronym for the four states of Maranhão, Tocantins, Piauí and Bahia where the rainfall is adequate and the land is cheap. Planting intentions reported by the agriculture ministry show another increase there this season, alongside a small decline in the south, and the two trends are now large enough to change the national total.

The economics are straightforward. Southern farmers face a combination of drought risk, high land prices and a stronger currency that makes exports less attractive, while the frontier offers flat land, cheaper credit and a growing set of grain terminals on the northern coast. The port of Itaqui and the terminals near Barcarena have cut the distance to Europe and Asia compared with the southern ports of Santos and Paranaguá, and the freight saving is enough to offset the longer haul from the interior.

The costs are less visible and harder to price. Clearing the Cerrado releases carbon stored in deep root systems and soil, and it changes the hydrology of a region that feeds several of the country's major river basins. The frontier also arrives without the infrastructure of the south: roads that become impassable in the wet season, storage that is insufficient for a good harvest, and a shortage of the agronomists and mechanics that a mature farming region takes for granted. Yields per hectare in Matopiba remain below the southern average, and the gap has narrowed only slowly.

The market consequence is a Brazil that is less vulnerable to a single drought and more dependent on the rainforest boundary. Analysts have noted that the national crop is now spread across a wider band of latitude, which reduces the risk of a total failure and increases the risk of a logistics bottleneck, since the grain must travel further on worse roads to reach ports that are themselves newer and less tested. The 2026 harvest is expected to be large; where it will be stored and how it will get to the coast are open questions.

For the south, the shift is a slow squeeze rather than a collapse. Rio Grande do Sul still produces more soy than any other state, and its cooperatives remain among the most efficient in the world. But the area is shrinking, the younger farmers are leaving for the frontier, and the state's agricultural establishment has begun to talk about irrigation and crop insurance as survival measures rather than improvements. A grain belt that moves is not a disaster; it is a bill that arrives over decades.
