description: "A quarter point, a promise of more, and a chairman who has to explain how a soft landing works when the price of oil is set by a war."
---

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate by a quarter of a percentage point on 16 March, to a range of 0.25 to 0.5 per cent, the first increase since December 2018. The committee had spent the previous year calling the inflation transitory and then watching it reach 7.9 per cent in February, the fastest pace since 1982. The move was the smallest it could make and still be called a beginning.

Language in the statement did the work that the number could not. The committee said it expected ongoing increases in the target range, which is a phrase that tells the market to price a series rather than a single step, and it said it would begin reducing the size of its balance sheet at a coming meeting. The projections published alongside showed most officials expecting the rate to reach about 1.9 per cent by the end of the year, a forecast that the year itself would make look timid.

The balance sheet mattered as much as the rate. The Fed had bought trillions of dollars of Treasury and mortgage securities during the pandemic, and the runoff that began in June was capped at 47.5 billion dollars a month for the first three months and then raised to 95 billion. A central bank that stops buying mortgages and starts letting them run off puts upward pressure on the cost of a thirty-year loan, which is how the policy reached the housing market without a rate rise.

Jerome Powell, at the press conference, was asked whether the committee could bring inflation down without a recession. He said the economy was strong enough to absorb higher rates, that the labour market had more vacancies than workers, and that the historical record offered examples of soft landings as well as failures. He also said that the war in Ukraine and the shutdowns in China were supply shocks that the Fed could not fix, which is the sentence that a central banker uses to lower expectations about the outcome.

Markets had priced the quarter point and reacted to the projections. The yield on the ten-year Treasury fell on the day and then rose through the spring, mortgage rates passed 4 per cent for the first time since 2018, and the stock indices rallied for a week before falling back. The dollar strengthened against most currencies, which imported inflation into countries whose central banks could not raise rates as fast and whose debts were denominated in the currency that was rising.

By the end of the year the committee had raised the rate seven more times, four of them by three quarters of a point, to a range of 4.25 to 4.5 per cent. Inflation peaked at 9.1 per cent in June and fell to 6.5 by December, and the unemployment rate stayed below 4 per cent, which is a soft landing in the only sense that matters to a central bank. The first quarter point of March looked, twelve months later, like the smallest of the decisions that followed it.
