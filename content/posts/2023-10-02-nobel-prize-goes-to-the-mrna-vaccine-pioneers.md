The Nobel Assembly at the Karolinska Institute awarded the 2023 prize in physiology or medicine to Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman on 2 October, for work published in 2005 that most of the field had ignored for years. Their finding was narrow and technical: replacing uridine with a modified nucleoside in synthetic messenger RNA stops the molecule from triggering the body's innate immune alarms, which had been the main obstacle to using mRNA as a medicine. Without that change, injected mRNA is read as an infection and destroyed before it can instruct any cell.

Karikó spent the 1990s at the University of Pennsylvania trying to interest colleagues in mRNA, and she was demoted from a tenure-track post when the grant money did not arrive. The university later sold its patent rights on the modified nucleoside chemistry to a small company, and the returns from those rights have been estimated in the hundreds of millions of dollars. Weissman, an immunologist at the same institution, began working with her on dendritic cells and vaccines, and the two built the experiments that produced the 2005 paper in Immunity.

Moderna and BioNTech both licensed the approach, and BioNTech's partnership with Pfizer produced the first authorized vaccine in late 2020, delivered in more than 13 billion doses worldwide by the end of 2023. The platform's advantage is speed: the sequence of a new pathogen can be turned into a designed molecule within weeks, and manufacturing is a chemical process rather than a biological one, which means the same plant can be repurposed. The Covid vaccines demonstrated that on a scale no previous technology managed.

The prize also settles an argument about credit. Several groups contributed to mRNA delivery, including the lipid nanoparticle carriers developed by Pieter Cullis and others, and those researchers were widely tipped for a share. The assembly instead recognized the specific chemical insight that made the platform viable, which is the modification of the bases rather than the packaging around them.

What the award does not change is the distribution problem. The technology costs what it costs, and the countries that waited longest for Covid doses in 2021 were the ones with the weakest manufacturing and the least bargaining power. Africa still imports most of its vaccines, and the mRNA hub that the World Health Organization set up in Cape Town has been slow to reach production. A platform that can be designed in weeks still depends on factories, regulators and contracts that take years.

Karikó learned of the call in the early morning and told Swedish radio that she had been repeating herself for a decade about mRNA to anyone who would listen, and that the recognition mattered because it would let her say it once more with authority. Her career is a case study in what happens to unfashionable work, and the prize is the first one given for a technology that is still being deployed. The platform is now being tested against influenza, shingles and cancer, and the same chemistry underlies all of those programmes.
