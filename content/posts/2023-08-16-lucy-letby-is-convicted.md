The jury at Manchester Crown Court convicted Lucy Letby on 18 August of murdering seven babies and attempting to murder six others, after a trial that lasted ten months and produced a verdict on most of the charges against her. She was thirty-three, a neonatal nurse who had trained at the hospital where she worked, and she is the most prolific female serial killer in modern British history. The judge sentenced her to a whole-life order three days later, which means she will never be released.

A chart built the case. Consultants at the Countess of Chester noticed that deaths and collapses on the unit had risen sharply in 2015 and 2016, and that a single nurse was on duty for most of them. Prosecutors used the unit's own records, the insulin results in the blood of two twins, and the testimony of colleagues who described a nurse who stood watching when a baby's alarm sounded and told a doctor that the child was dying.

Defence arguments were about statistics and about doubt. Letby's lawyers said the hospital was understaffed and the unit was badly run, that the deaths had other causes, and that a coincidence had been turned into a pattern by consultants who needed someone to blame. They pointed to the testimony of a senior doctor who had also been on duty for several of the collapses, and to the absence of direct evidence of harm in most cases. The jury rejected those arguments over twenty-two days of deliberation and convicted on all but a few counts, on which it could not agree and which the prosecution has said it will seek to retry.

The hospital's conduct is now the subject of a separate inquiry. Executives had been warned about the mortality figures and delayed removing Letby from the unit, and they asked consultants to stop raising concerns in writing. Two doctors who persisted were disciplined, and one of them left the trust. The inquiry, which began its work in the autumn, will examine how a hospital manages a suspicion of this kind, and its findings will be read by every trust in the country.

Conspiracy theories have attached themselves to the case, as they do to every case of this sort, and they travel faster than the evidence does. Some of them rest on genuine questions about the reliability of the expert evidence, and some of them deny that the deaths happened as the court found. The families of the victims have asked for the questioning to stop, and the attorney general has warned that comments outside the courtroom may prejudice a retrial on the counts that the jury could not decide.

A nurse has been convicted of killing the babies in her care, and a hospital is being asked how it let her continue. The verdict ends one trial and opens another, and the second one concerns not a person but a system that took eighteen months to act on what its own consultants had told it.
