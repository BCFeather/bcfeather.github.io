Shipping carries about eighty per cent of world trade and emits roughly three per cent of global greenhouse gases. It is also the only sector with a genuinely global regulator, the International Maritime Organization, which makes it the natural place to put a carbon price on fuel. The negotiations have been running for years, the technical design is mostly settled, and the measure has not been adopted. The reason is not engineering.

The mechanism under discussion is a levy on the carbon content of bunker fuel, collected at the point of bunkering, with the revenue going into a fund. A fuel standard sits alongside it, requiring ships to reduce the intensity of their emissions over time, and the two instruments are designed to work together: the levy makes the standard cheaper to comply with, and the standard gives the levy a purpose beyond revenue. On paper, this is the cleanest carbon price in the world, because it applies to every ship on every route and there is no way to move production to a jurisdiction with a weaker rule.

Money is what the argument is about. A levy collected on fuel burned in international waters raises a large sum, and the fund that receives it has to decide how to spend it. Developing countries with long coastlines and island economies want compensation for the higher freight costs, and they want the money to finance their own transition. Countries with large fleets and shipbuilding industries want the revenue to subsidize their operators. Countries that import most of their goods want the cost passed to the consumer to be small. Every one of those positions is reasonable, and they cannot all be satisfied by a rule that is simple enough to administer.

Evasion is the second obstacle. A levy at the bunkering port can be avoided by refuelling in a jurisdiction outside the scheme, and the transshipment routes that already exist make that easy for some trades. The scheme's designers have proposed a distance-based charge rather than a bunker-based one for that reason, which is harder to evade and harder to compute. The compromise under discussion applies the levy to voyages between participating ports and treats others differently, which reintroduces exactly the distortion the global approach was meant to remove.

Industry opinion is split. Large operators with modern fleets support a levy, because it penalizes their older competitors and because they have already ordered dual-fuel ships. Small operators and tramp trades oppose it, because a fixed cost per tonne of fuel is a larger share of their margin. Charterers, who pay for fuel under many contracts, want the cost to fall on owners, and the contracts are being rewritten to decide that.

Order books show the consequence of the delay. Shipowners are ordering vessels that can run on methanol or ammonia, and they are paying a premium for the capability without knowing the price of the alternative. A levy would make that bet pay off. Its absence makes it a hedge. The longer the negotiation runs, the more the industry builds a dual-fuel fleet on the assumption that a carbon price is coming, which is a strange way to reach a decision that the regulator has not made.

What is likely, and what is not, is worth separating. The technical framework will probably be adopted in some form, because the alternative is regional schemes that the industry finds worse. The distribution of the revenue is likely to be settled by a formula that satisfies nobody fully and lets everyone claim a win. The timeline is the part that nobody can forecast, and it is the part that decides how much of the transition happens on schedule.
