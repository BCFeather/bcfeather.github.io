England won the women's European championship on Sunday at Wembley, beating Germany two goals to one after extra time, and it is the first major tournament that an England team has won since 1966. The crowd was eighty-seven thousand and one hundred and ninety-two, which is the largest for a final in the history of the competition, men's or women's. The equaliser came from a substitute in the second half and the winner came from another substitute in the second period of extra time. The match was watched by a television audience that the broadcasters said was the largest of the year.

From the first minute it was the game the tournament had promised. Germany pressed from the start and England defended with a back three that had been the base of the campaign, and the first half produced few chances. Germany took the lead in the second half through a shot from the edge of the area, and England responded within ten minutes when a cross from the left was turned in at the far post. The winner came from a corner that the defence failed to clear, and the substitute who scored it had come on six minutes earlier.

Four years of work went into the side that won. The manager, who was appointed in 2018, changed the formation and the staff and brought in a group of players who had won at the youth level. The team won all of its matches in the group and beat Spain and Sweden in the knockout rounds, and it conceded two goals in the tournament. The squad included players who had been playing in the American league and in the clubs of the Women's Super League.

The organizers will remember the attendance. The tournament was played in nine stadiums and the total attendance passed half a million, which is more than double the figure for the previous edition. The final sold out within hours and the tickets on the secondary market were selling for more than ten times their face value. The television figures in Britain were the largest for a women's match, and the newspapers put the team on the front pages.

Politics ran through the tournament from the first match. The tournament was the first since the government had promised to review the governance of the women's game, and the players had spoken about the difference in the prize money and in the facilities. The European governing body raised the prize fund for this edition, and it is still less than a tenth of the men's. The captain said after the match that the team wanted the legacy to be the access of girls to the sport in the schools.

English football has a trophy it can point to at last. The men's team reached the final of the European championship last year and lost on penalties, and the women's team has now gone one step further. The next tournament is the World Cup in Australia and New Zealand next year, and the team will go there as one of the favourites. The crowd at Wembley sang the anthem at the end, and the players said the noise was the thing they would remember.
