title: "Designed proteins disarm snake venom in mice"
date: "2025-01-15T20:40:00+08:00"

description: "Proteins built by computational design bound the three-finger toxins that make cobra and adder venom lethal and protected mice from doses that would otherwise kill, a route to antivenoms that does not depend on immunizing large animals."
---

Antivenom has been made the same way since the 1890s. Horses or sheep are injected with venom, they produce antibodies, and the antibodies are purified into a serum that is given to a bitten patient. The product works, and it carries the properties of its manufacture: it is expensive, it needs a cold chain, it is specific to the species used to make it, and it can cause serum sickness in the people who receive it.

Computational protein design offers a different starting point. Instead of screening antibodies from an immunized animal, the designers specify a target site on a toxin and build a small protein that binds it. The method has produced binders against influenza haemagglutinin and against several bacterial toxins, and the paper in *Nature* on 15 January applies it to the three-finger toxins that dominate elapid and viperid venoms, a family with enough structural similarity between members to make cross-species coverage a realistic design target.

These toxins are small proteins with a distinctive fold, and they kill by binding to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors or to other targets on nerve and muscle. A binder that occupies the same surface blocks the toxin from reaching its receptor, which turns a lethal molecule into one that circulates and is cleared. The authors designed proteins against several toxins in the family, measured their binding constants in vitro and then tested protection in animals.

In mice, the designed proteins prevented death when given with venom or shortly after it, at doses of venom that killed every untreated animal. Protection extended across venoms from different species, which is the property a clinical product needs, because a bitten patient rarely knows what bit them and because one product covering several elapid venoms would replace a shelf of species-specific sera that most clinics cannot afford to stock.

Manufacture is the argument for the approach. A designed protein can be produced in a fermenter by a standard microbial or mammalian cell process, which removes the herd of immunized horses, the variability between batches and the dependence on refrigeration that antivenom programmes in low-income countries struggle with. The product is chemically defined, so a batch can be characterized and a dose can be calculated, and the cost per dose falls with scale in the way that a fermented product does and a serum does not.

The distance to a clinic is still long. The mouse experiments used a single challenge dose and a defined timing, while a real bite delivers a variable amount of venom over minutes and the patient often arrives hours later. Designed proteins are small enough to be cleared quickly by the kidney, which sets a limit on how long one dose protects, and the immune response of a human to an engineered protein is unknown until a trial measures it, which is the step that has ended more design programmes than any binding assay has.
