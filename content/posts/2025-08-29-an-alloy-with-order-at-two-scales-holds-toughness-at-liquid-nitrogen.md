Metals usually buy strength at the cost of ductility, and the trade becomes severe at cryogenic temperatures, where dislocation motion stiffens and a small defect turns into a crack that runs. Alloys for liquid hydrogen tanks and for superconducting magnets therefore need a combination that most strengthening strategies cannot deliver: yield strength above a gigapascal while the specimen still stretches by tens of per cent before it breaks. The usual fix is to add obstacles to dislocation motion, and the usual cost is that those obstacles also stop the processes that let a metal deform without cracking.

A paper in *Nature* on 27 August 2025 reports an alloy that manages it by arranging order at two scales instead of one. Its authors built a cobalt-nickel-vanadium system in which short-range order is dense and long-range order is sparse, and quantified both by atom-probe tomography: about \(2.4\times10^{26}\) short-range ordered regions per cubic metre, against roughly \(4.5\times10^{25}\) of the long-range kind. Neither population is large enough to dominate the microstructure on its own, which is what makes the combination a design, not a mixture.

The two populations do different jobs. Dense short-range order obstructs dislocation motion at the scale of a few atomic distances, which raises strength without creating the planar slip bands that make an ordered alloy brittle. The rarer long-range particles pin grain boundaries and provide the obstacles that make a crack change direction as it travels. The short-range regions are too small to appear in a diffraction pattern and too numerous to be avoided by a passing dislocation, which is why they thicken the resistance without embrittling the grain.

At 87 K, close to the boiling point of nitrogen, the alloy reached a yield strength near 1.2 GPa with a strength-elongation product of 76 GPa per cent, a figure that combines how hard the metal resists deformation with how far it deforms before failing. Room-temperature values were lower but followed the same ordering, which suggests the mechanism is the same at both temperatures rather than a low-temperature accident. The product is the measure this literature uses because either term alone can be pushed to a record by sacrificing the other.

Comparison is made with alloys that carry only one kind of order. Those reach similar strength and lose elongation, or keep elongation and give up strength, and the two-scale design is presented as the reason this one does neither. That is a claim about microstructure, not about composition, since cobalt, nickel and vanadium are ordinary ingredients. The paper's control is a series of alloys along the same composition line, which holds the chemistry fixed while the order changes.

What remains is the engineering. A laboratory alloy tested in compression or tension on small specimens is not a plate, and the paper reports no welding, forming or fatigue data, all of which matter for a pressure vessel. The processing route that produces the two-scale structure needs a narrow window of temperature and time, and a production line has to hold it. Fatigue at 87 K is the test that would expose a two-scale structure, since cyclic loading rearranges short-range order in ways a single tension test cannot.

A large specimen, joined and cycled at cryogenic temperature, with toughness measured by a standard method instead of inferred from elongation, is the test that would matter. Until that exists, 76 GPa per cent is a laboratory property looking for a component. The processing window is the first thing a manufacturer would try to widen, since a narrow one raises the cost of every part, and a widened window usually changes the microstructure the paper measured.
