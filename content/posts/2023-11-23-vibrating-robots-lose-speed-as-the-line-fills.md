Traffic models usually begin with drivers who look ahead, brake early and keep a distance that shrinks with speed, so the fundamental diagram, the curve relating flow to density, encodes anticipation as much as mechanics. Strip the driver out and the curve might be expected to change shape, because a platoon that interacts only by contact has no way to prepare for the vehicle in front. Whether anything of the familiar diagram survives the removal is what the experiment sets out to test, and the answer turns out to depend on how the platoon was assembled.

An experiment posted to arXiv on 2 November 2023 puts vibration-driven vehicles into a one-dimensional array and measures the diagram directly. Each vehicle moves by buzzing against the floor, so its free speed is set by a mechanical parameter and its interaction is a collision, with no sensing and no following rule. The array is built vehicle by vehicle, and the order of insertion is one of the variables rather than a fixed condition of the apparatus.

At low and medium lineal density the mean speed stays nearly level. As the density approaches the inverse of the distance a vehicle occupies, the speed falls by between 25 and 40 per cent, and the fall is a consequence of contacts rather than of any control law. The curve therefore has a shoulder that car traffic does not show, where speed holds before dropping, and the loss at the end of the shoulder is large enough that a platoon delivering goods would need to plan for it.

History enters the curve as well. When the vehicles are introduced in order of their free speeds, slow ones first or fast ones first, the speeds at low and medium density differ substantially, though the two curves converge once the line is saturated. The state of a jammed platoon depends on the route it took to get there, and a diagram drawn from one ordering does not describe the other.

Once the line is saturated the platoon moves slower than the slowest vehicle would move on its own. Contacts cost more than the free-speed distribution predicts, because a fast vehicle behind a slow one transfers momentum in a way that neither would choose, and the saturated state is a collective one that no single agent's parameter explains. A slow vehicle at the head of the line sets the pace, and every vehicle behind it inherits that speed through the chain of collisions.

What one dimension does not capture is the second axis. A two-dimensional floor allows overtaking and lane formation, and a platoon that can pass will not have the same shoulder or the same dependence on entry order. Whether the ordering effect survives when vehicles can steer around each other is the experiment that would connect this result to pedestrian and vehicle flow, and the preprint leaves it for a floor with more room.
