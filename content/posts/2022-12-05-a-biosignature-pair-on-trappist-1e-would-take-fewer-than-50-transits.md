A biosignature is a pair of gases that should not coexist at the observed abundances unless something is replenishing one of them. Carbon dioxide and methane are such a pair, because chemistry alone drives the mixture towards a single equilibrium, and an atmosphere that holds both is out of balance. Disequilibrium of that kind is what a metabolism would maintain, so the pair is a sign of life rather than a sign of geology.

Detecting the pair on a rocky planet is the goal of several observing programmes, and TRAPPIST-1e is the natural target, since it is close enough for many transits and small enough to be rocky. Work posted to arXiv on 9 December 2022 asks what the James Webb Space Telescope would actually see, using a general circulation model rather than a one-dimensional atmosphere. Transit spectroscopy is the technique, and the number of transits is the currency, since each one costs telescope time.

Simulations run the model with carbon dioxide partial pressures from \(10^{-4}\) bar to 1 bar against a background of 1 bar of \(\mathrm{N}_{2}\). Water, carbon dioxide and methane are traced through the climate, and the transmission spectrum is computed both for a modern Earth-like methane abundance and for an atmosphere without one. A three-dimensional model is needed because the climate of a tidally locked planet is not uniform, and a one-dimensional model would misplace the abundances.

A transit budget is the answer. In the optimistic case, haze-free and with \(1\) bar of \(\mathrm{N}_{2}\) and \(10\) mbar of \(\mathrm{CO}_{2}\), all three molecules could be detected in fewer than 50 transits with the near-infrared spectrograph, provided the noise floor stays near 10 parts per million. Fifty transits is a small budget for a planet at this distance, and the noise floor is the condition that decides whether it can be spent.

Temporal variability turned out not to matter much. A tidally locked planet has a permanent day side and night side, and the climate that develops is not uniform, but the authors find that the variability does not significantly change the spectral features that the measurement depends on. Features that change with the seasons would be harder to interpret, and the paper's finding that they do not change much simplifies the interpretation.

What the model does not include is clouds and haze, which it treats by choosing the optimistic case in which they are absent. Clouds raise the noise floor and mute features, and a reader should treat the transit budget as a best case rather than as a schedule. An atmosphere with clouds is the realistic case, and muting is what clouds do to every feature in the spectrum.

The result does not claim that the pair will be found, only that the observation is feasible within the telescope's lifetime, and the 50-transit figure is the number a proposal would have to argue against. A proposal would have to show that the noise floor can be reached in practice, which is an instrument question rather than a modelling one.
