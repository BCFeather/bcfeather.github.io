Most expensive tariff in modern history was reduced on a Sunday afternoon in Geneva. A joint statement issued on 12 May committed both governments to act by 14 May: the United States would remove the duties imposed on 8 and 9 April, suspend 24 of the 34 points of its reciprocal tariff for 90 days and keep 10, and China would do the same in reverse and suspend its non-tariff countermeasures since 2 April.

Arithmetic explains the headline. The American rate on Chinese goods had reached 145 per cent once the 20 per cent levy justified by fentanyl was added to the reciprocal schedule, and the Chinese rate on American goods had reached 125 per cent. Removing 91 points and suspending 24 leaves 30 per cent on one side and 10 per cent on the other, which is a fall of 115 points on both, and it is the largest one-day reduction in tariffs since the Smoot-Hawley era went into reverse.

Markets had been waiting for a signal of this kind since the April escalation, and they moved on the news: American stocks rose, the dollar strengthened, and the yuan and Hong Kong equities rallied. The relief was less about the level of the tariff than about its direction. A rate that stops rising is a rate that firms can plan around, and planning is what investment requires.

Shipping data had already shown the damage. Container bookings from China to the United States collapsed in the weeks after the April announcement, and then surged in the week before the truce as importers tried to move goods under the lower rate. A 90-day window produces exactly that behaviour: a rush at the front and a cliff at the end, and the shipping schedules for July were already being written around the deadline.

Retained tariffs are the part to watch. Section 301 duties, Section 232 duties on steel, aluminium and cars, and the 20 per cent levy linked to fentanyl all remained, so the average rate on Chinese goods stayed far above its pre-2025 level. A truce that leaves tariffs in place is a pause in an argument, not a settlement of it, and the average rate on Chinese goods stayed near 40 per cent once the suspended points were counted.

The mechanism the two sides established is thin. A consultation channel led by He Lifeng on one side and Scott Bessent and Jamieson Greer on the other commits the parties to keep talking, without a schedule or an agenda beyond the phrase economic and trade relations. Thin mechanisms are what governments build when they need time rather than agreement, and both sides had reasons to want time: one faced a midterm calendar and the other faced a growth target.

What would settle the truce is the 90-day deadline in August. An extension would show that both sides found the pause useful; a lapse would restore the rates that caused the April collapse in trade, and the second escalation would be harder to reverse than the first.
