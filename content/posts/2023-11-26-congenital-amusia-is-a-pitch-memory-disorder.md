Congenital amusia is a developmental condition in which music perception and production are impaired despite normal hearing and normal exposure to music. Around one person in twenty is affected, the deficit is not explained by training, and the affected listeners describe music as pleasant but poorly differentiated. The condition is a natural experiment for asking what pitch processing requires, and for asking which of its stages can fail separately.

A review of the behavioural and neural literature, posted on 27 November 2023, organizes the findings around three stages instead of a single deficit. Pitch information must be encoded, held in short-term memory, and recalled, and the evidence points to impairments at each stage, with the memory component carrying more of the explanation than discrimination alone.

Discrimination deficits are real but modest, and they do not account for the experience of the disorder. Where amusic listeners diverge most clearly is in retaining a pitch across a delay and in comparing it with a later one, which is the operation that melody recognition depends on. A listener who can hear a difference but cannot remember the tone has a memory problem, not a hearing problem.

The deficits extend beyond music. Pitch cues in speech carry prosody, emphasis and some lexical distinctions, and emotional prosody depends on pitch contour as well. Amusic listeners show weaker performance on those tasks, which is why the condition is described as an auditory disorder with musical symptoms and not as a musical curiosity.

Imaging work locates the changes in a distributed fronto-temporal network, and some of the differences are visible at rest, which suggests a difference in functional connectivity rather than in a single structure. Connectivity patterns within the network and between it and other regions differ from those of controls, and the pattern is consistent with a system that integrates pitch information less efficiently across the stages the review separates.

One finding cuts against the simple deficit account. Some studies report spared implicit pitch processing: amusic listeners perform poorly when asked to judge a pitch explicitly, and yet their brain responses or their indirect behaviour carry information about the same pitch change. Implicit cognition appears to operate where explicit access fails, which is a dissociation worth pursuing for rehabilitation.

Training and rehabilitation follow from that dissociation, and the review sketches approaches that exploit audiovisual integration and implicit tasks instead of direct discrimination drills. Whether those approaches produce durable gains is untested at the scale a clinical trial would need, and the condition is rarely presented to a clinician at all. What the review establishes is a framing: amusia is neither a lack of musicality nor a fault in the ear, and it is a disorder of pitch representation whose memory component can be measured. That framing matters for education as well as for neuroscience, since a child who cannot sing in tune is often assumed to be unwilling rather than unable.
