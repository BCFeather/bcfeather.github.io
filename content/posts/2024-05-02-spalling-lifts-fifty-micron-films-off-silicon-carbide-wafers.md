Silicon carbide substrates are grown slowly and yield badly, and a wafer of the size a power device wants costs accordingly. The properties that make the material worth the trouble, a wide bandgap and high thermal conductivity, let a thin film do the electrical work, so the expensive part is the thickness that only carries the film through processing and contributes nothing to the device itself.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 30 April 2024 reports controlled spalling of single-crystal 4H silicon carbide, lifting films of 10 to 50 microns from bulk substrates. Spalling is a fracture running parallel to the surface beneath a stressed overlayer, and controlling where the crack starts and how thick the stressor is turns it from a damage mechanism into a transfer technique.

A stressor is a film deposited cold, and its residual stress drives the crack; the thickness of that layer sets the depth at which the fracture runs, which is why the paper spends its effort on the deposition and not on the crystal. Get the depth wrong and the film is either too thin to handle or too thick to have saved anything at all.

The authors call the result the highest fracture toughness material spalled so far, which is the claim that matters, since a tougher crystal resists the very crack the method depends on. They also show reuse of the substrate after the film is removed, bonding of the lifted film to a carrier, and spin control of neutral divacancy ensembles inside the transferred film. Toughness is where silicon carbide usually beats silicon, and a lift that gave it up would save the wafer and lose the reason for it.

The qubit measurement carries the argument. Ensembles of the neutral divacancy, written \(VV^{0}\), keep a spin coherence time of about 79.7 microseconds in the spalled film, close enough to bulk values that the fracture has not wrecked the defect environment. A film that can be lifted and still hold coherent spins is doing more than surviving the process, and that is the result the authors lead with when they argue for reuse.

Preliminary is the authors' own word for the spin work, and the numbers are for ensembles and not single defects. Nothing is said about yield across many films, about how the lifted surface behaves after bonding, or about the cost of the stressor and the etch that removes it, and those three decide whether this is a laboratory result or a process. A process claim would need all three numbers, and the paper asks for them openly.

A reusable substrate changes the economics of a device family that is otherwise limited by crystal growth. Power electronics and the quantum work that shares the material both want large areas of good crystal; if a film can be lifted repeatedly, the area available stops being the area that was grown. That is the claim to test next: lift a film, regrow on the scar, and lift again.
