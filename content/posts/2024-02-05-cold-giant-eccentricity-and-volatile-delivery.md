Whether a planetary system can keep a temperate surface is partly a question about its outer planets. Water and other volatiles have to reach the inner system, and the usual carrier is a giant planet beyond the snow line whose gravity throws ice-bearing bodies inward. The solar system's Jupiter does this gently, on a nearly circular orbit. A preprint posted to arXiv on 5 February 2024 asks whether that arrangement is typical or rare.

The authors collect eccentricities for giant planets on both sides of their systems' snow lines. Cold giants, the ones outside, come out with a median eccentricity of 0.23. Warm and hot giants inside the line sit on far more circular orbits. That asymmetry is the starting point, and the rest of the paper is about what it does to delivery.

Simulation supplies the comparison. The authors run scattering experiments with a Jupiter analog and with a Jupiter whose eccentricity is raised to the median they measured, and count the bodies that end up crossing the orbit of an Earth-like planet. The eccentric version delivers a substantially larger volume of Earth-crossing material, since a planet that swings between radii sweeps a wider annulus and pumps eccentricity into the planetesimals it meets.

Those numbers imply that many known systems with cold giants may host water worlds inside the snow line, or at least planets with more volatile inventory than a solar-system analog would suggest. That is a statement about probability, not about habitability, since a delivery mechanism that brings water also brings impacts, and the same scattering that supplies a surface can strip an atmosphere.

Two caveats sit inside the result. Eccentricity measurements carry errors that grow with semimajor axis, and the median is drawn from a heterogeneous set of surveys with different detection limits, so part of the 0.23 could be noise. The simulations also assume a disk of planetesimals massive enough to scatter, and a system that has already cleared its belt delivers little regardless of the giant's orbit.

Observational selection explains part of the pattern. Eccentricity is easier to measure for giants on wide orbits when the orbit is eccentric, and radial-velocity surveys find the close-in, circular planets that are easiest to detect. Whether those tails dominate the delivery budget is a question for the next survey, since a few eccentric giants can outweigh a majority of circular ones. The authors correct for what they can and note the remainder. A median is also a summary that hides bimodality, and the sample contains both nearly circular cold giants and strongly eccentric ones, which matters if the delivery rate depends on the tail.

What the paper changes is the treatment of architecture as a variable. Models of volatile delivery have long used the solar system as the template, and a median eccentricity of 0.23 says the template is the exception, which changes how much water a typical terrestrial planet should be expected to hold.
