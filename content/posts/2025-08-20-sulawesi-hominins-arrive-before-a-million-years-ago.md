Crossing to Wallacea, the islands between the Asian and Australian faunas, required a sea voyage at a time when no one had a boat with a sail. The record of who made that crossing and when has been thin: stone artefacts at Talepu on Sulawesi dated to about 194,000 years, hominins on Flores from around a million years, and *Homo luzonensis* in the Philippines from a few hundred thousand. Modern humans reached the region between 73,000 and 63,000 years ago, so everything older belongs to someone else.

A paper in *Nature* on 6 August 2025 reports a much older site on the same island. At Calio, in the Walanae Depression of South Sulawesi, stone artefacts sit in fluvial sandstone of the Beru Member, with the lowest artefact in a layer below a fossil-bearing horizon that yielded a partial suid maxilla of *Celebochoerus*. The artefacts are in place, not reworked, and the sandstone layers can be dated directly. The layer that holds the lowest artefact sits beneath the fossil-bearing horizon, which fixes the order of events at the site independently of the numerical ages.

The dating rests on two methods. Palaeomagnetic measurement of the sedimentary sequence places the artefact-bearing layers in a reverse-polarity interval between the Olduvai and Jaramillo subchrons, which brackets them between 1.787 and 1.070 million years ago. Uranium-series and electron-spin-resonance dating of two teeth from the maxilla returned a modelled age of 1.26 ± 0.22 million years, with 1.20 ± 0.18 for the upper molar and 1.43 ± 0.21 for the premolar. Taking the stratigraphy into account, the artefacts have a minimum age of 1.04 million years and possibly as much as 1.48 million.

The implication is that Sulawesi was occupied at least as early as Flores, and possibly earlier, which makes the crossing to Wallacea older than the first records of seafaring anywhere else. It also loosens the assumption that early hominins stayed on continental shelves: reaching this island meant a voyage of tens of kilometres of open water, whatever craft was used. A voyage of that length needs a craft and a plan, and neither leaves a trace in a sedimentary record, so the crossing has to be inferred from what was carried across it.

What limits the claim is the absence of a body. The evidence is stone and stratigraphy, with no hominin fossil at the site, so the species that made the artefacts is unknown and could be any of the candidates then present in the region. The uranium uptake model behind the ESR age carries assumptions, and a closed-system reading would allow an age as old as 2.1 million, which shows how wide the bracket is.

Tests that would tighten it are excavation rather than reanalysis: more artefact-bearing layers at Calio, a technological comparison with the Talepu assemblage, and faunal remains that show what the makers were eating. Any hominin tooth from the same member would settle the identity question, and none has been found. A single hominin tooth from the same member would also settle whether the makers belonged to the population already recorded on Flores.
