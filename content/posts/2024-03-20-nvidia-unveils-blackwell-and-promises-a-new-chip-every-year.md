NVIDIA announced the Blackwell architecture at its developer conference in San Jose on the eighteenth of March, and the centrepiece was a processor the company calls the B200, which contains 208 billion transistors and is built from two dies joined together. Company said the chip delivers about two and a half times the performance of its predecessor on the workloads used to train large language models, and that systems built around it would be available from partners later in the year.

Architecture behind the numbers is a change in the way the company builds processors. Blackwell pairs two of the largest dies that the manufacturer can produce and connects them with a link that allows them to work as one, which avoids the yield problems that come with a single very large chip. It also moves to a higher-bandwidth memory and adds a dedicated engine for the compression and decompression that inference requires. Company said the design was aimed at both training and at the cheaper inference that most customers will need as models move into production.

Cadence promised at the same event was the more consequential announcement. Chief executive Jensen Huang said the company would release a new architecture every year, with a successor to Blackwell in 2025 and another in 2026, and he presented a long-term plan that included chips, networking, software and systems sold together. That schedule compresses a cycle that had been closer to two years, and it puts pressure on competitors who are already behind on the current generation.

Customers described in the keynote are the largest buyers of computing in the world. The company listed Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta and Oracle among those that had committed to Blackwell systems, and it said that demand exceeded supply through the year. Cloud providers have been building data centres at a pace that has strained power grids and construction capacity, and the new chips are more efficient per unit of work, which is the argument that the company makes to customers who cannot get more electricity.

Competition is real but distant. Advanced Micro Devices has an accelerator that some buyers describe as competitive on inference, and the large cloud companies are designing their own chips for internal workloads. Intel and several start-ups are also building accelerators. None has the software ecosystem that CUDA provides, which is the advantage that the company's customers cite most often when they explain why they keep buying.

Consequence of the eighteenth of March is a company that has become the supplier of a strategic input rather than a vendor of hardware. Its products determine how fast models can be trained and how cheaply they can be served, and its roadmap now sets the pace for the industry. Whether that position can be maintained against custom silicon and against export controls that limit its sales in China is the question that the next two years will answer. What the conference showed was that the company intends to compete on speed of iteration, which is a contest that it currently wins.
