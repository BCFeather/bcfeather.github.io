Summer time ended in Europe on Sunday morning, and for the first time since March the clocks were put back at two, which meant that the night lasted an hour longer than the calendar said it should. For most people the change passed without notice, and the phone adjusted itself. For anyone awake at the moment, the hour repeated, and the same minute arrived twice. The newspapers ran their annual pieces about the farmers and the schoolchildren, and the argument about abolishing the practice was made again in three countries and settled in none.

An hour is an odd thing to be given. It arrives without a plan and is usually spent on the same things that would have been done anyway, which is why nobody feels richer after it. The value depends on what it interrupts, and there is a difference between an hour that lengthens a night of sleep and an hour that lengthens an evening of work. Somebody in an office in Brussels once calculated what the change costs the economy, and the number was large enough to be quoted and small enough to be ignored.

In this hemisphere the change has no legal force, since the clocks here stay where they are, and the only sign of it is a message from a friend in another time zone. The distance to Europe grows by an hour for six months of the year, and then shrinks again. Anyone who telephones colleagues on the other side of the world learns to check the offset twice a year, and anyone who forgets it ends up on the line at a strange hour.

The last day of October fell on a Monday this year, which is the worst arrangement a month can have. The month has thirty-one days and the term has about ten weeks, and the two counting systems begin to converge in the last week of the month, when the deadlines that looked comfortable in September become fixed points. What was an abstraction becomes a date on a page. The library fills in the evening, and the coffee machine is repaired, and somebody stops going to the gym.

The World Cup opens in twenty days, and the season will pause in the middle for it. Football is the one thing that makes the calendar feel shorter, because the matches arrive on a schedule that nobody can postpone. The competition has been moved to winter, which means the usual summer tournament will happen in a country where the summer is unbearable, and the supporters who travel will find that the weather is the least of it. The tickets were sold before the schedule was known, which is how these things work now.

November begins on Tuesday, and the year has two months left. The hour is gone, and it was spent on nothing that can be named. The light will keep leaving earlier until the solstice, and the plane trees on the river walk will lose the rest of their leaves in a week. There is a kind of satisfaction in watching a season do what it is supposed to do, even when the schedule that governs it is one that nobody voted for.
