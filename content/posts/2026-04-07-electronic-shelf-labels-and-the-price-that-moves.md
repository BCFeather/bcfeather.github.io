description: "A supermarket chain that installed electronic shelf labels found prices changed four times as often, and the labour saved went into analytics rather than into fewer staff. Dynamic pricing arrived through the label, not through the checkout."
---

Electronic shelf labels replace paper tickets with a display that a store's system updates over a radio link. A chain that installed them in 180 stores reported this month a 4 per cent improvement in gross margin, and the mechanism is not the labour saving.

Price change frequency is the visible effect. A store changes 900 paper tickets a week, and each change is a task with an error rate of 3 per cent. With electronic labels the chain changes 3,600 prices a week, and the error rate is zero, because the label displays what the system sends.

The economics of the change is where the margin comes from. A markdown that is applied two days earlier clears 12 per cent more of a perishable line, and a price that tracks a competitor's within an hour captures the customers who compare. Both require frequent changes.

Labour was redeployed rather than removed. The chain's staff hours rose 1 per cent, because the tasks that remain, replenishment and service, are the ones the labels did not automate. The chain's statement is explicit that nobody was made redundant.

Customer response is the regulatory question. Dynamic pricing by time of day is legal in most jurisdictions and requires disclosure in some, and the chain has restricted itself to markdowns and competitor matching rather than to surge pricing.

Technology cost is falling. A label costs 4 dollars and lasts five years, and a store of 12,000 labels costs 48,000 dollars plus 6,000 a year in infrastructure. That is cheap against the margin gain.

The comparison with online retail is the strategic frame. An online store has always changed prices continuously, and a physical store's paper tickets were the last friction in the channel. Removing that friction makes the two channels comparable, which is what the chain says it wanted.

Privacy is the emerging constraint. A label that displays a price can also display a message, and two chains are testing a personalised offer shown at the shelf, triggered by a loyalty card. Consumer groups have objected, and one jurisdiction has asked for an opinion on whether that constitutes profiling.

Failure behaviour matters for a store. A label whose battery dies shows the last price, which is legally the price at the till, and the chain has added a battery check to the weekly routine rather than a monitoring system.

The labour arithmetic is why the labels spread. A store with 12,000 labels spends 40 hours a week on paper tickets, and a label system removes almost all of it. The payback is 18 months on labour alone, before any pricing benefit.

For a reader, the interesting point is that a display technology changed pricing behaviour rather than labour cost. The label is a small device and its effect is on how a store thinks about price, which is a larger change than the labour it saved.
