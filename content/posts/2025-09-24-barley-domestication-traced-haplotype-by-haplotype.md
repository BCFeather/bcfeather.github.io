Barley is one of the oldest cultivated crops, and its domestication history has resisted a simple story. Earlier genomic work rejected a single origin and supported a mosaic, in which domesticated lines draw ancestry from several wild populations. That answer raised the next question: where the pieces of the mosaic came from, and whether some wild populations contributed more than others. Modern cultivars carry the domestication traits, and the wild relatives that contributed them remain identifiable in the Levant and the Zagros.

A paper in *Nature* answers it with haplotypes, the blocks of sequence that inheritance passes on intact. The authors analyse whole-genome sequences from 682 genebank accessions and 23 archaeological specimens, trace where each haplotype occurs in space and time, and identify the wild contributors during domestication and during later gene flow. A haplotype is a run of variants inherited together, so its geographic distribution records a migration that a single marker cannot. The authors treat each block as a unit of inheritance and compare its distribution with the range of its wild source.

A founding domesticated population emerges in the Fertile Crescent, and it formed over a long period of pre-domestication cultivation instead of in one season of selection. The haplotype structure shows the founding population receiving contributions from wild barley in its neighbourhood before domestication traits fixed, which is what a prolonged cultivation phase would produce. Domestication traits include a non-shattering rachis, and their fixation marks the end of the phase instead of its beginning.

Ancient DNA supports the inference from modern samples. The archaeological specimens carry haplotypes that the modern analysis assigns to the founding population, so the model is not an artefact of the genebank collection. Later gene flow then layered additional wild ancestry onto regional lineages, which is why a single origin was never going to fit. The archaeological specimens come from sites across the Fertile Crescent, and their ages bracket the period the model identifies.

The practical insight is a warning. Haplotype differentiation between barley populations is high, and it arises independently of selection or on top of it, which makes association mapping for adaptive traits harder than in a crop with a homogeneous background. Breeders searching for a locus in a panel that mixes lineages will see structure that mimics a signal. Mixed panels also inflate the significance of any locus that differs between lineages, which is the failure mode the authors describe.

The limits are those of sampling. Genebank accessions represent what collectors saved, and 23 archaeological specimens are few for a continental history. The dates of the archaeological material constrain the timeline, and a denser series from the Levant and Zagros would test the founding-population claim directly. Genebank collections also favour lines that breeders found useful, which biases the sample towards the domesticated end. The analysis corrects for part of that bias, and the correction is described in the supplement. Sampling density is the binding limit, and the paper says so.
