Sweden's election on Sunday produced a result that fits in three seats. The four parties of the right, led by the Moderates and including the Sweden Democrats, together won a hundred and seventy-six seats in a chamber of three hundred and forty-nine. The governing Social Democrats and their allies took a hundred and seventy-three. The Sweden Democrats, a party with roots in the neo-Nazi movement of the 1990s, took about twenty per cent and became the second largest party in the country for the first time. The prime minister, Magdalena Andersson, conceded on the night and said she would resign.

The campaign was fought almost entirely on three subjects. Crime and the shootings between gangs in the suburbs of Stockholm and Gothenburg dominated the television debates, and the parties of the right argued that the government had lost control of them. The cost of electricity, which had risen fourfold in the south of the country, was the second. Immigration and the integration of the arrivals of 2015 was the third, and the Sweden Democrats had campaigned on it since the party entered the chamber in 2010.

Eight years of Social Democratic government end with this result, and with it a longer period in which the party set the terms of the argument. Andersson had taken office only a year earlier, after her predecessor resigned, and she had built a coalition with the Greens and a working arrangement with the Left Party. That arrangement held through the pandemic and through the energy crisis, and it lost the votes of the suburbs where the shootings happened.

The Sweden Democrats will not be in the cabinet, at least at first. The Moderates' leader, who is expected to become prime minister, said during the campaign that he would seek to govern with the party's support rather than with its ministers, and the Sweden Democrats accepted the arrangement. That gives the government a majority on budgets and a partner that can withdraw it, which is a weaker position than the arithmetic suggests. The four parties have also disagreed about energy policy and about defence spending.

Effects on the rest of Europe reach beyond Stockholm. Sweden has been a stable member of the union and a consistent voice on climate and on free trade, and it is in the middle of an application to join NATO that has to be ratified by two members with reservations. A government that depends on a party that has been sceptical of immigration will also be watched in the European Parliament, where its allies sit with the far right rather than with the centre-right.

What the count shows is how narrow a majority can be and how much it can carry. A hundred and seventy-six seats gives the right bloc the power to pass a budget and the need to keep four parties together for three years. The Sweden Democrats have spent a decade being told they would never hold influence, and the count on Sunday night gave them the ability to bring a government down. The next test is the vote on the budget in the autumn, when the parties have to agree on numbers rather than on slogans.
