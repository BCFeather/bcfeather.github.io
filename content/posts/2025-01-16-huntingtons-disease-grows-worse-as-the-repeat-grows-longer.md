title: "Huntington's disease grows worse as the repeat grows longer"

description: "Brain cells from people with Huntington's disease show that the inherited CAG repeat keeps expanding through life, and that neurons carrying the longest repeats are the ones that die, which puts the timing of the expansion at the centre of the disease."
---

Huntington's disease is caused by a CAG repeat in the huntingtin gene, and the number of repeats predicts the age at which symptoms begin. Forty repeats typically means onset in midlife, sixty means onset in adolescence, and the relationship has been known since the gene was cloned in 1993, when the repeat was found to be the cause and the length of it was found to set the clock. What the relationship does not explain is why a mutation present from conception produces no symptoms for forty years and then produces them quickly.

A paper in *Cell* on 16 January offers an explanation that has been suspected and not shown. Working with post-mortem brain tissue and with cell models, the authors measured repeat length in individual cells and found that the inherited repeat expands in somatic tissue over decades, particularly in the striatum, which is the region the disease damages first. Some neurons carried repeats several hundred units long by the time the patient died.

Distribution across cells is the finding. Expansion is not uniform across cells, and the cells with the longest repeats are a minority that grows with age. The authors relate that minority to the pathology: the neurons with the largest expansions showed the gene expression changes that precede death, and the pattern suggests that a cell crosses a threshold of repeat length and then fails, which turns a genetic disease into a progressive one that has a rate as well as a cause, and a rate is something a drug can change.

That reframes the therapeutic target. A drug that lowers mutant huntingtin protein addresses the product of the gene. An intervention that slows somatic expansion addresses the process that makes the product worse, and the two are not the same, because a cell with forty repeats produces far less toxic material than a cell with two hundred.

DNA repair supplies the mechanism of expansion. The repeat forms secondary structures during replication and repair, and the mismatch repair proteins that correct errors in other genes are the ones that extend the repeat in this one. Genetic studies in patients have already implicated those genes as modifiers of onset, and the cell-level data connect the modifier genes to the process they modify, which gives the geneticists a mechanism for an effect they had measured only as a shift in age of onset.

Clinical translation is the hard part. Measuring somatic expansion in a living patient means sampling the brain, and the trial endpoints that regulators accept are clinical scores and imaging rather than repeat lengths. The paper supplies a rationale for drugs that target mismatch repair in the striatum, and it supplies a warning that such drugs would have to act on a process that every cell uses to keep its genome intact, which is a narrow target with a wide set of consequences and a long preclinical interval in which to watch for them.
