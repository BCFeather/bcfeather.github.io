Xinjiang is the corridor between the steppe, the Central Asian oases and the Tarim Basin, and its people have been described by historians, archaeologists and linguists without a genomic account of how the populations actually mixed. The region's dry climate preserves DNA well, which is why the material for such an account existed. The Tarim Basin sites in particular have yielded mummies that were described a century ago, and the genomic identity of those people has been a question ever since.

A study sequenced 201 ancient genomes from 39 archaeological sites and modelled their relationships to one another and to modern populations. It appeared in *Science*, 31 March 2022. The sites span the Bronze Age through the historical period, which is what allows the study to describe a process rather than a snapshot.

Bronze Age Xinjiang populations carried four major ancestries, related to early Bronze Age cultures of the central and eastern steppe, to Central Asia and to the Tarim Basin. That is a more complex picture than a single migration from one direction, and it fits the archaeological record of multiple material cultures arriving at different times. The four ancestries are not a list of separate arrivals but a mixture that is already present in the earliest Bronze Age samples, which means that contact predates the material cultures that archaeologists use to date it.

Admixture between middle and late Bronze Age steppe cultures continued through the late Bronze and Iron Ages, alongside an inflow of East and Central Asian ancestry. The mixing was therefore a process lasting centuries and not a set of discrete replacements. The inflow of East and Central Asian ancestry during the Iron Age coincides with the period in which the Silk Road corridors were most active.

Historical era populations showed ancestry as mixed and diverse as the present-day population of Xinjiang. The continuity is the result that matters for the region's self-understanding, because it indicates that diversity is the historical condition and not a recent arrival. Continuity into the historical period is the least surprising result for historians and the most consequential one for anyone who reads the region as a place of recent migration.

Ancient DNA from a dry region is biased towards the individuals whose remains happened to preserve, and 201 genomes cannot represent every community that lived there. The ancestry models also depend on reference panels that are unevenly sampled across Eurasia. The reference panels are denser in Europe than in Central Asia, which biases the assignment of ancestry towards the populations that are best represented.

This study is a demographic history and not a political statement, and its finding is that the corridor was always a place where populations met and mixed. That is what the archaeology, the languages and now the genomes all describe. The genetic record is one line of evidence among several, and its value is that it can be dated independently of the texts that describe the same region.
