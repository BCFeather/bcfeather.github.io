The north China rainy season begins when the monsoon front reaches the latitude of Beijing, which usually happens in the first week of July. In the first week of July the front that had sat over the Yangtze moved north, and the plain received its rain in three days.

The front's northward progress is paced by the subtropical high. As the high extends west and north, the moisture that has been sitting over the Yangtze valley moves with it, and the rain belt follows the high's western edge. A high that stalls produces a wet July in one province and a dry one in another, and the difference is often a few hundred kilometres.

Consequences are asymmetric in a different way from the south. North China is dry for nine months and receives most of its water in two, so the reservoirs, the aquifers and the river channels are sized for a short, intense season. Groundwater is the hidden variable. The plain has been pumping from the aquifer for decades, and the water table has fallen by tens of metres in places. A good monsoon recharges a fraction of what has been withdrawn, and the recharge is the only part of the deficit that the climate can address.

Storms have become more intense. The total rainfall has not changed much, and the number of days with heavy rain has risen, which means the same water arriving in fewer, larger events. A city whose drains were designed for a steady July has to rebuild them for a July that delivers a month's rain in a day. A city of a million people can absorb a hundred millimetres in a day if the drains were built for it, and most were built for less.

What the beginning of the season does practically is end the drought watch. The agriculture ministry issues its summer forecasts, the reservoirs begin to fill, and the farmers in the plain plant the second crop. The date is not a festival, and it is the most important date in the northern year. The plain is also the place where the groundwater that feeds Beijing is recharged, and recharge happens in weeks like this.
