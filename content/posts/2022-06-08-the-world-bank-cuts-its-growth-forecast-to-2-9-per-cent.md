Its forecast for global growth this year went to 2.9 per cent from 3.2, the World Bank said in a report on 7 June. It said the war and the lockdowns had hit every region, and that 70 countries had seen their forecasts cut. It warned that the world faced a period of stagflation like that of the 1970s. The bank said the war had cut growth by 1 point in the region and by 0.5 in the world.

Growth in advanced economies was forecast at 2.6 per cent, and in emerging ones at 3.4. The bank said the figure for emerging economies was below its average of the last decade, and that it would leave 100 million people in poverty. It cut China's forecast to 4.3 per cent from 5, and India's to 7.5. It expected the euro area to grow 2.5 per cent and Japan 2, and it cut Russia by 8 points.

Inflation was the reason. The bank said prices would rise 6 per cent in advanced economies, which is the highest since 1982. It said the war had raised the price of energy and of food, and that the two together accounted for 40 per cent of the increase. It said central banks would have to raise rates even as growth slowed. Its president said the world had not faced a combination of weak growth and high inflation since 1979.

He said the policy of the time had allowed inflation to become entrenched, and that the cost had been a recession. The bank's president said the danger was a repeat of the 1970s. He said the current cycle was different, because the labour market was stronger, and that it could be managed.

Debt was the other risk. The bank said 60 per cent of low income countries were in debt distress, and that 10 were in default. It said the interest payments of those countries would rise 30 per cent this year, and that the cost would crowd out spending on health and on education. The bank recommended three policies. It said governments should cut subsidies on fuel and target relief at the poorest. It said central banks should be allowed to raise rates, and that the fiscal space should be used for supply. It said the rich countries should open their markets to the poor. Its debt report said 40 countries had asked for relief, and that 12 had received it.

Forecasts proved too pessimistic on the year. Actual growth was 3.1 per cent, because the American consumer held up. The reason was the strength of the American consumer and the reopening of China, which the bank had not expected. The bank's next report in January 2023 forecast growth of 1.7 per cent for the year. Its president said the risk of a global recession had risen, and that the poor countries would bear the cost. The actual figure for 2023 was 2.6 per cent.
