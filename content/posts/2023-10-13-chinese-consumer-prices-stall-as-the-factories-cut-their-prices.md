Chinese consumer prices were unchanged in the year to September, the statistics bureau said on 13 October, down from a rise of 0.1 per cent in August. The producer prices fell 2.5 per cent, the third monthly decline. The figure was below the forecast of 0.2 per cent, and the measure has now been below one per cent for a year, which the economists describe as a deflationary drift. The month was the second in a year in which the index was flat or negative, and the deflation has been the longest since the Asian crisis of the late 1990s.

Food is the drag. The pork prices fell by about 22 per cent from a year earlier as the farmers had rebuilt the herds after the swine fever, and the vegetables fell after a good harvest. A pig that is plentiful is a price that falls for a year. The food prices fell by about 3.2 per cent over the year, and they carry about a fifth of the basket, which is the reason the headline is so low. The pork price is about a third below its level of two years ago, and the pig farmers have been losing money for six quarters.

Core weakness stands out. The measure excluding the food and the energy rose by about 0.8 per cent, which is the slowest since the start of the pandemic, and the prices of the household goods and the cars fell. A core that is below one per cent is a demand that is not there. The travel and the hotel prices rose over the summer and then fell back, and the clothing prices were little changed as the retailers discounted. The prices of the household appliances fell by about 2 per cent, and the carmakers have been cutting the prices of the electric models to defend the share.

Producer prices show the excess capacity. The output of the steel, the cement and the solar panels exceeds what the builders and the world can absorb, and the prices at the gate have fallen for a year. A capacity that is not shut is a price that keeps falling. The producer prices have fallen for twelve months, and the profits of the industrial firms have fallen by about 9 per cent over the year. The steel mills have been cutting the output by about 3 per cent, and the cement producers have been running at about 60 per cent of the capacity.

Policy has been aimed at demand. The central bank has cut the rates twice since the summer and the government has promised the support for the property, but the households have been saving rather than spending. A rate that is cut is a rate that the borrower must still want. The property investment fell by about 9 per cent in the first nine months, and the sales of the new homes by about 5, which is the source of the weak demand for the materials. The households have been saving about 30 per cent of their income, and the survey of the central bank shows the confidence about the future income is below its level of 2019.

What the month shows is that the deflation is being generated in the factories and the shops, and the inflation that is missing is a symptom of confidence rather than of money. A price that falls in the shops is a debt that becomes heavier, and the weight is the reason the policy has been so cautious about the demand. A deflation that is mild and persistent is a debt that is paid in a currency that is worth more, and the worth is the reason the borrowers are cautious.
