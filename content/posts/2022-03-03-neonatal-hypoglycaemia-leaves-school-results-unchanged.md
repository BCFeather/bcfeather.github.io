Neonatal hypoglycaemia is the most common metabolic problem of the newborn period, and it is treated with dextrose gel, feeding and, in stubborn cases, intravenous glucose. The justification for treating it is neurological: glucose is the brain's fuel, and prolonged low levels were assumed to damage developing white matter. The threshold for treatment has fallen over the past decade, and cohorts assembled under older protocols contain children whose glucose was lower and for longer than a contemporary clinician would allow.

The evidence behind that assumption came from cohorts of preterm infants and from children with severe and prolonged episodes. Whether a moderate episode in a late preterm or term infant leaves a lasting trace has been tested less often, and a New Zealand cohort addressed it directly.

The study followed infants born at risk of hypoglycaemia, measured blood and interstitial glucose for up to seven days, and assessed 480 of 587 eligible children at a mean age of 9.4 years. The primary outcome was low educational achievement, defined as performing below or well below the curriculum level in standardized reading or mathematics tests. The findings appeared in *JAMA*, 1 March 2022. The cohort was assembled at a single national centre, and the children were born moderate to late preterm or term with at least one risk factor, which describes the group that neonatal units screen most often.

Among children who had been exposed to hypoglycaemia, 138 of 304, or 47 per cent, showed low achievement, against 82 of 176, or 48 per cent, of those who had not. The adjusted risk difference was -2 per cent, with an interval from -11 to 8, and the adjusted relative risk was 0.95, with an interval from 0.78 to 1.15.

That is a null result with a reasonably tight interval, and its value lies in what it excludes. A doubling of risk would have been visible in this sample, so the study rules out a large effect of moderate neonatal hypoglycaemia on school performance at nine years. A null result of this kind is easy to overstate, and the authors do not claim that hypoglycaemia is harmless, only that its effect on schooling is too small to detect in this sample.

Secondary outcomes told a more mixed story. Some measures of executive function and visual-motor coordination differed between groups, which is consistent with earlier reports and inconsistent with a completely benign course. The authors are careful to describe these as associations in a cohort rather than as causal effects.

Treatment still matters for the individual infant, because a glucose of 1.5 millimoles per litre is an emergency regardless of what a school test will show in a decade. What the study changes is the counselling: a parent whose newborn was treated for low glucose can be told that the evidence does not predict poor schooling. A clinician reading the paper should treat the negative finding as reassurance about the population studied and not as a licence to relax monitoring in a newborn whose glucose continues to fall.
