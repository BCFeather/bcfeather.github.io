description: "A small museum in Jiangsu has published photographs and records of its entire collection, including the objects that never leave storage. The catalogue is imperfect and unfinished, and it is a more interesting document than the exhibitions the museum is known for."
---

The museum in a county town west of Nanjing has about four thousand objects in its collection and room to display perhaps three hundred. The rest live in a store room with a dehumidifier, a set of steel shelves and an inventory book that has been kept, in one form or another, since the 1980s. This month the museum published all of it: a photograph, a description, a provenance note and a location code for every object, on a website that looks like it was built by two people with a budget and a deadline.

The catalogue is not a polished product. Some photographs are taken on a phone against a grey card, the provenance notes are inconsistent, and a few entries admit that the object's origin is unknown. The museum's director said in an interview that the imperfections were deliberate: the alternative was another five years of cataloguing before anything was published, and the objects that matter most to local families are precisely the ones with thin documentation. A descendant looking for a grandmother's wedding jacket is better served by an imperfect photograph than by a promise.

What the catalogue reveals is a collection shaped by accident. The museum was assembled in the 1980s from objects confiscated during the Cultural Revolution, donations from families leaving the county, and a handful of archaeological finds from the riverbank. The published entries show all three streams, and the result is a portrait of a place that no exhibition would attempt: a stone tablet, a set of agricultural tools, a stack of letters, a porcelain bowl with a repaired crack, a bicycle licence plate from 1972.

Digitizing a store room is not the same as making it accessible. The website allows a search by keyword and by location code, and the museum has begun to run monthly tours of the storage itself, limited to fifteen people, in which a curator opens the cabinets and talks about what is inside. Those tours sold out for the autumn. The director's argument is that the store room is more honest than the gallery, because the gallery is a narrative and the store room is an accumulation.

The project also raises the question that every small museum faces when it publishes its collection: what happens to the things that should not be public. A few objects have donors who asked for discretion, and the museum has honoured those requests by publishing a description without a photograph. A few have unclear ownership, and the catalogue flags them. The decisions were made quickly and will probably be revisited, which is the ordinary condition of institutional memory.

For the county, the effect is already visible in a small way: families have written in with photographs, letters and stories that fill gaps in the entries, and the museum has added a field to the catalogue for corrections received from the public. That is the part of digitization that the technology companies never promise and that museums keep discovering: publishing a collection turns it into a conversation, and a conversation is a better catalogue than a database.
