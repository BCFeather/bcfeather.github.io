description: "Observations of LTT 9779 b reported in *Nature Astronomy* describe an atmosphere that has kept its volatiles despite a dayside hot enough to vaporize rock, in a region of parameter space where planets are rare."
---

The planet orbits a star slightly smaller than the Sun once every 0.79 days, which puts it at a distance where the irradiation is about two thousand times what Earth receives. A gaseous planet at that distance should lose its envelope to the stellar wind within a few hundred million years, and the region of the mass-period diagram where such planets would sit is called the hot Neptune desert because almost nothing occupies it.

LTT 9779 b is one of the few exceptions, with a radius about five times that of Earth and a mass about thirty times, and its survival is the question that the observations were designed to answer. The measurement used the phase curve, the variation in the infrared flux as the planet rotates through its orbit, which separates the dayside emission from the nightside and shows how the heat is redistributed.

Daytime side is extremely hot, near the temperature at which refractory minerals condense, and the nightside is hundreds of kelvin cooler. That gradient is smaller than a model with no atmospheric circulation would produce, which means that winds carry heat from the day side to the night side efficiently, and the presence of an atmosphere at all, after billions of years of irradiation, is the first conclusion.

Spectrum of the dayside shows a continuum that is consistent with an atmosphere poor in hydrogen and helium, which is what a planet that has lost its primordial envelope would have. A rocky core with a thin envelope of heavier gases is the picture, and the metals that the star has in abundance are the ones that appear in the spectrum.

Night side carries clouds. The emission at wavelengths where clouds of silicate would absorb shows a dip, and the temperatures implied are low enough for such clouds to condense, which means that the night side of a planet at 2300 kelvins on the day side is raining rock. That asymmetry is the most striking result in the paper, and it is the kind of phenomenon that only a phase curve can see, because a transit measures the atmosphere at the terminator and never the face that faces away.

The survival mechanism is the part that remains open. A magnetic field would shield the atmosphere from erosion, and a planet that is tidally locked with a strong day-night circulation can keep an envelope on the night side where the clouds form. The alternative is that the planet is young enough that the loss has not finished, which would make it a transient state and not a stable configuration.

What the object does for the field is to populate a desert. A single planet does not establish a population, and the value of the measurement is that the atmospheric properties of a desert dweller are now known, which gives the models something to fit and the next surveys a template to look for.
