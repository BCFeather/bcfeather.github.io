Nigerians voted on 25 February in the largest election the continent had held, with eighteen candidates and more than ninety million registered voters. The contest was between the governing party's candidate, Bola Tinubu, a former governor of Lagos; the main opposition candidate, Atiku Abubakar, who had run four times before; and a third candidate, Peter Obi, whose campaign had drawn on young voters in the south-east. The president, Muhammadu Buhari, was completing his second term and could not stand again.

Mechanics were the first problem. The electoral commission had promised to publish results from each polling unit as they were counted, and the system that was supposed to do it failed in a large number of units. Observers described long queues, late openings and polling stations where the card readers did not work, and the commission extended voting into a second day in some areas. Card readers failed in the units where the queues were longest, and the commission's own staff described the day as improvised.

Three questions shaped the campaign. The first was the economy, which had grown slowly and had produced inflation above twenty per cent and a currency that had lost most of its value against the dollar. The second was security, with kidnapping in the north-west, an insurgency in the north-east and separatist violence in the south-east. The third was the record of the outgoing government, which each of the three main candidates described as a failure and each of them promised to reverse.

The result came on the morning of 1 March, after a count that took five days. Tinubu won 8,794,726 votes, or about 36 per cent, Atiku took 6,984,520 and Obi 6,101,533, and the turnout was low enough that the winner's mandate was described as a plurality of a minority. Tinubu also carried the largest number of states, which satisfied the constitutional requirement that a winner have a quarter of the vote in two-thirds of them. Tinubu's margin in the north-west was large enough to offset Obi's sweep of the south-east and the south-south, which is the geography that decided the result.

Disputes began before the result was announced. Obi's campaign said the count had been manipulated and promised a challenge, Atiku's party described the process as a sham, and the international observers said the election had been competitive and that its administration had fallen short. The commission's chairman said the failures were technical, and the opposition said the failures were the point.

Orderly transition followed the pattern of Nigeria's previous handovers. Tinubu was sworn in on 29 May, the outgoing president handed over, and the losers went to court, where the petitions would be heard for months. The election had produced a president with a third of the vote, an opposition with two-thirds between them, and a system that had been tested and had bent without breaking. The petitions would be heard in the spring, and the tribunal's calendar was the part of the election that nobody had campaigned on.
