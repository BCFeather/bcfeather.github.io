Pita Limjaroenrat walked into the chamber on 13 July with a coalition of eight parties, 312 of the 500 elected seats, and a mandate that had been delivered at the ballot box two months earlier. He walked out with 324 votes from a joint sitting of the lower house and the appointed Senate, fifty-one short of the 375 he needed. Of the 749 members eligible to vote, most of those who did not support him abstained rather than voted against, which is the Thai way of saying no without saying anything a court can examine.

The arithmetic had been visible for weeks. Move Forward's coalition commanded a majority in the elected house and nothing close to one across both chambers, because the constitution written after the 2014 coup gives the 250 appointed senators a vote for prime minister and no obligation to respect an election result. Senators argued that Pita had promised to amend the law on royal insult, that his party was a threat to the monarchy, and that they were defending an institution rather than defying voters. Some simply abstained and let the numbers do the work.

The legal pressure on Pita ran alongside the political pressure. The election commission had referred him to the Constitutional Court over shares he held in a media company at a time when candidates are barred from owning them, and on 19 July the court suspended him from parliament while it considered the case. He was also the subject of complaints about a pledge to revise the lese-majeste law, which the court is still weighing. His party's supporters read the sequence as an attempt to remove an inconvenient winner by procedure.

A second vote was held six days later and failed by an even wider margin, after which Move Forward stepped aside and its coalition partner Pheu Thai took the lead. Pheu Thai, which had campaigned against the generals and then agreed to govern with parties that had served in their cabinets, eventually produced Srettha Thavisin, a property developer with no electoral record, as prime minister. The shift has cost the coalition its reforming wing and given it a prime minister acceptable to the Senate.

What the affair has shown is a system that can hold an election and refuse its result without appearing to overturn it. The Constitutional Court has dissolved parties before, the Senate has blocked candidates before, and both acts were available here without any soldier leaving barracks. For the millions who voted for a party that promised to curb the generals, the lesson is about the difference between winning an election and being allowed to use it.

Pita said he accepted the outcome and would keep working, and his supporters have gone back to the streets and the courts. The next hearing will decide whether the party survives at all, which is a sharper question than who sits in the prime minister's chair, and one that will shape whether the next election is fought on the same terms as this one.
