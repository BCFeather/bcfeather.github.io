Paris opened the Olympics on the evening of 26 July with a ceremony staged along the Seine, in which delegations were carried past the city's monuments on boats rather than marched around a track. The format was new and it put the river at the centre of the games, which is also where the open-water swimming and the triathlon were to be held. Paris had promised the games would be held in the city rather than in a purpose-built park on its edge, and the promise shaped the budget and the security plan.

River is the risk. Swimming in the Seine had been banned for a century because of the bacteria in the water, and a cleanup programme costing more than a billion euros was supposed to make it safe. A rainstorm can overwhelm the sewers and raise the counts within a day, which is why the organizers had a reserve plan. Tests were run every morning, and a decision on whether to swim was taken hours before the race, which meant the athletes trained without knowing whether they would compete.

Security was the other constraint. An opening ceremony on open water requires a perimeter that a stadium does not, and the country deployed tens of thousands of police and soldiers along the route. A ceremony that is held in public space is a ceremony that cannot be searched in the way a stadium can. The perimeter extended for kilometres along both banks, and residents inside it needed passes to reach their own homes for the duration of the ceremony.

Cost is the familiar argument. A budget of several billion euros for a three-week event is a budget that a city spends on transport, housing and cleanup that it would not otherwise have funded, and the question is whether the spending was for the games or for the city. A cleaning programme that outlasts the event is the version of the argument that wins. The transport spending included a new metro line and a extension of the regional network, and those assets will be used long after the closing ceremony.

Politics of the ceremony was not subtle. A country holding an election in which a party that wants to leave the European Union was expected to do well used the event to present itself as a capital of the continent, and the tableau included references that the organizers described as a message of openness. A ceremony is a political statement whether or not it is intended as one. The tableau that drew the most comment was a sequence on a bridge that mixed fashion, dance and a reference to the city's history, and the organizers described it as a celebration of diversity.

What the games will be measured on is not the ceremony. A city that has built a transport network and cleaned a river has produced assets that outlast the medals, and the medals will be forgotten before the river is. A river that is clean enough to swim in is a standard that the city can keep, and the standard is the part of the games that will still be visible in ten years.
