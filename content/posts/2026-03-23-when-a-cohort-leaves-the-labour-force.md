description: "Participation among men aged 25 to 34 has fallen 4.2 points in a decade, and the decline is concentrated among those without a post-secondary qualification. The statistic is a composition effect as much as a behavioural one."
---

Labour force participation is a ratio, and a ratio can move because the numerator changed or because the denominator did. The decline of 4.2 percentage points among men aged 25 to 34 over the past decade has been read as a behavioural story, men withdrawing from work; the decomposition published this month suggests that composition explains more than half of it.

Composition here means education. A cohort with a larger share of graduates has a higher participation rate at every age, and a cohort with a smaller share does not. Adjusting for educational attainment, the participation decline in this age band shrinks to 1.8 points, which is smaller and still real.

The second adjustment is geography. Participation fell most in regions where the manufacturing share of employment fell most, and the regional variation is larger than the age variation. A national average masks a 9-point gap between the strongest and weakest labour markets.

Behavioural explanations that survive the adjustment are specific. Men without a qualification in regions with declining manufacturing are less likely to be employed and less likely to be looking, and the survey data show a rise in the number who report not wanting work rather than not finding it. That is a discouraged-worker story with a clear geographic footprint.

Measurement issues matter here. The participation rate counts people who are looking, and someone who has retired early, gone to prison, or moved into informal work is out of the numerator. The survey's coding of informal work has improved, and the improved coding accounts for about 0.6 points of the apparent decline.

Policy responses proposed in the report are conventional and expensive. Wage subsidies for the long-term unemployed, expansion of short-cycle vocational training, and childcare support for the 25 to 34 group, where the participation story differs for women. The report estimates the cost of the first at 0.2 per cent of GDP and the effect at 1.1 points of participation.

The report's caution is the useful part. A participation rate is a headline number that invites a story about culture or motivation, and the decomposition says that education, geography and measurement explain most of the movement. The residual behaviour is real and narrower than the headline.

Policy cost estimates deserve a caveat. The report prices a wage subsidy at 0.2 per cent of GDP for an effect of 1.1 points of participation, and that effect is drawn from evaluations of programmes in other countries with different labour markets. The estimate is a benchmark, not a forecast.

Regional policy is where the report ends. The authors recommend that any national programme be allocated by region rather than by demographic group, because the variation between the strongest and weakest labour markets is larger than the variation between age bands.

For a reader, the operational lesson is to ask for the adjusted series. Statistics agencies publish participation by age and education, and the cross-tabulation usually resolves the question faster than any interpretation of the aggregate.
