Crowds began gathering before dawn on the first of March outside the Church of the Icon of the Mother of God in the Maryino district of Moscow, where a funeral service was held for Alexei Navalny, the opposition leader who died in a prison colony above the Arctic circle on the sixteenth of February. Queue stretched for hundreds of metres along the street, and people waited for several hours to be admitted. Church held a closed ceremony for family and close friends, and the coffin was then taken to the Borisovskoye cemetery for burial.

Attendance was possible only because the authorities allowed it, and they made the cost visible. Police were deployed along the route, and officers detained people who chanted slogans or carried signs. At the cemetery, mourners who tried to gather in larger groups were pushed back, and journalists were told to move away from the grave. Human rights groups counted dozens of detentions in Moscow and in other cities, where people had laid flowers at makeshift memorials.

Death of Navalny was reported by the prison service on the sixteenth of February, which said he had lost consciousness after a walk and could not be revived. His allies and several Western governments said the timing and the circumstances were suspicious, and they noted that he had been moved to a colony in Yamalo-Nenets in December and had been held in conditions that his lawyers described as punitive. Russian officials said the cause was natural and refused to release the body for eight days.

Family had fought for the release of the body and for a funeral that the state would permit. His mother, Lyudmila Navalnaya, said officials had threatened to bury him in the prison colony without ceremony, and she recorded a video appeal to President Vladimir Putin that was viewed millions of times. Body was eventually handed over, and the family announced the service two days before it took place, which gave the authorities less time to prepare and gave mourners less time to travel.

Funeral became the largest opposition gathering in Russia since the death of Boris Nemtsov in 2015. Crowds were smaller than at the protests of 2021, and there were no chants that could be heard on the main street, which analysts read as a measure of how much the space for opposition has narrowed. Many of those who came said they had not supported Navalny's politics and were there to mark the fact that a man had died in custody.

Succession is the question that the funeral left open. Navalny's organization was designated extremist in 2021 and its regional offices were shut, and several of his associates are in prison or abroad. His widow, Yulia Navalnaya, said in a video a few days after his death that she would continue his work, and she asked supporters to treat Putin as the head of a criminal organization. Whether an organization can be rebuilt around her is uncertain, and the queues of the first of March were the last large public gathering that the movement is likely to produce for some time.
