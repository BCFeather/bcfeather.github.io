---

Antlers are the fastest growing bones in mammals. A stag grows a set of them in a few months, sheds them, and grows another set the next year, and the tissue that does it is a stem-cell population that has no counterpart in the rest of the skeleton.

The study in *Science*, 3 March 2023 characterizes that population. Tao Qin and colleagues isolate stem cells from the antler's growing tip, show that they produce bone and cartilage at a rate that ordinary periosteum does not, and demonstrate that the cells can drive antler-like growth when introduced into mice.

The rate is the striking number. A antler can extend by centimetres per day during the growth season, and the tissue is bone rather than cartilage scaffold. A mammalian bone that grows that fast would be a model for repair.

The mechanism is a programme. The stem cells are a distinct population with a transcriptomic signature that combines skeletal progenitors with regulators of rapid growth, and the signature is what distinguishes them from the mesenchymal stem cells that bone marrow contains.

The mouse experiment is the proof of principle. Implanted cells produce antler-like structures in a host that does not grow antlers, which shows that the programme is cell-autonomous rather than a property of the deer's environment.

The regenerative medicine angle is the ambition. A human fracture heals slowly, and a large defect heals incompletely. A cell population that builds bone quickly is a candidate for a therapy, and the species barrier is the obstacle.

The obstacle is real. Deer cells in a mouse are xenogeneic, and a human therapy would need human cells with an equivalent programme. Whether such a programme exists in humans at all is the question the paper raises rather than answers.

The developmental question is about the antler's origin. Antlers are cranial appendages, and their stem cells derive from a periosteal niche. The paper's lineage analysis places them in that niche, which connects an extreme regeneration to an ordinary tissue.

The shedding is the other puzzle. A bone that is shed every year must be resorbed, and the resorption is coordinated with the growth. A system that grows and removes bone on schedule is a system with a control loop that the paper does not resolve.

What the study does not show is the trigger. Day length sets the cycle, and the hormonal signal that starts the growth is known to involve testosterone, and how that signal reaches the stem cells is the next step.

For the field of bone regeneration, the value is a natural experiment. Evolution has produced a tissue that regenerates at a speed no laboratory has matched, and the cells that do it are now available to study.

The antler's blood supply is a further component. A growing antler is covered in velvet, a skin rich in vessels, and the bone beneath it is highly vascular. A growth rate of centimetres per day requires a blood flow that a normal periosteum does not support, and the velvet is the structure that supplies it.
