description: "Stem cells from the growing tip of a deer antler build bone at a rate no other mammalian skeleton reaches, and the cells induce antler-like growth when placed in mice."

Antlers are the fastest growing bones in mammals. A stag grows a set in a few months, sheds them, and grows another set the following year, and the tissue that does it is a stem-cell population with no counterpart in the rest of the skeleton.

In *Science* on 3 March 2023, that population is characterized. Tao Qin and colleagues isolate stem cells from the antler's growing tip, show that they produce bone and cartilage at a rate that ordinary periosteum does not, and demonstrate that the cells can drive antler-like growth when introduced into mice.

Rate is the striking number. An antler can extend by centimetres per day during the growth season, and the tissue is bone rather than a cartilage scaffold, so a mammalian bone that grows that fast would be a model for repair.

Because the cells carry a distinct transcriptomic signature that combines skeletal progenitors with regulators of rapid growth, the mechanism is a programme, and that signature is what separates them from the mesenchymal stem cells that bone marrow contains. Vascular supply follows from the same argument, since the growing antler is covered in velvet rich in vessels and the bone beneath it is highly vascular, and a growth rate of centimetres per day needs a blood flow that ordinary periosteum does not support.

Proof of principle comes from the mouse experiment, because implanted cells produce antler-like structures in a host that does not grow antlers, which shows that the programme is cell-autonomous rather than a property of the deer's environment. Development places the cells in a periosteal niche through lineage analysis, and that placement connects an extreme regeneration to an ordinary tissue.

Regenerative medicine supplies the ambition. A human fracture heals slowly and a large defect heals incompletely, so a population that builds bone quickly is a candidate for therapy. The species barrier is the obstacle, though, because deer cells in a mouse are xenogeneic and a human therapy would need human cells with an equivalent programme, and whether such a programme exists in humans at all is a question the paper raises rather than answers.

Shedding is the other puzzle, since a bone lost every year must be resorbed and the resorption is coordinated with the growth, which implies a control loop the paper does not resolve. The trigger stays open, because day length sets the cycle and the hormonal signal is known to involve testosterone, while how that signal reaches the stem cells is the next step. Bone regeneration gains a natural experiment, since evolution produced a tissue that regenerates at a speed no laboratory has matched, and the cells that do it are now available to study. A cell population isolated from a wild animal is also a practical tool, since it can be expanded in culture and its programme probed with the same assays used on human progenitors. What a wild species offers is a reference programme against which the repair of a human bone can be judged, and the comparison is the useful part of a result that no human tissue will match.
