Most potassium channels of the six-transmembrane family close by pinching the pore with a bundle of crossing helices, and the structure of that gate is visible in crystal forms. The large-conductance calcium-activated channel lacks the bundle, which raised the question of how it can be closed at all without a physical obstruction. The channel instead carries a large current at high voltage and closes when calcium is removed, so the closed state is the resting state of a pore that has to open quickly.

A structure of the non-conductive state confirmed the absence of a steric gate, and left the mechanism unexplained. Accessibility experiments had already hinted that the closed pore admits small molecules at some positions, which rules out a simple plug and points towards something that depends on the state of the pore interior. A steric gate would have to sit somewhere in the pore, and no such constriction appears, so the block has to come from the solvent or from the membrane.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 7 May 2024 answers with simulations. The authors run molecular dynamics and free energy calculations on the BK channel, starting from a calcium-free state and following the sequence of events that ends in conduction once calcium binds. The calculations resolve the pore at atomic detail and follow both the water and the lipid tails, which is what allows a mechanism to be proposed instead of guessed.

Lipids turn out to be part of the machinery. The channel has fenestrations that connect the pore to the surrounding bilayer, and in the calcium-free state the methyl groups of the annular lipid tails occupy the closed pore, sliding in through those openings. The tails enter only when the pore is closed, and their position depends on how the transmembrane helices are tilted in that state.

That occupancy seeds a vapour bubble. A lipid-filled cavity is hydrophobic enough that water cannot stay in it, and the bubble nucleates in the inner cavity and blocks ion conduction without any protein atom standing in the way. A vapour bubble is a region with no liquid water at all, and ions cannot cross it, so the pore is blocked without a protein residue in the way.

Calcium reverses the arrangement. Binding displaces the lipids out of the inner cavity, the region becomes less hydrophobic, the pore hydrates, and conduction follows, which is a gating mechanism written in water and lipid rather than in helix packing. The displacement is small, a few angstroms, and it is driven by the change in the electrostatic environment that calcium produces at the intracellular mouth.

The mechanism explains the awkward observations. Blockers that reach the pore only in the open state, and the state dependence of accessibility, both follow from whether lipids or water fill the cavity, and the authors use that to reconcile experiments that a steric gate could not. Whether the same mechanism operates in other channels with fenestrations is the obvious next question, and an answer would connect a family of pores that have been treated separately.
