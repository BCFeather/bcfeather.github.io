A Formula One season is twenty-four races in nine months, and each race moves about a thousand tonnes of equipment across a border. The cars, the garages, the hospitality units and the tyres travel on a chartered freighter fleet, and the cost of that logistics is a fixed cost that the sport has to recover from the races it sells. A freighter that carries the cars is a 747 with the seats removed, and there are only a few of them in the world.

The calendar is designed around freight rather than around weather. Races are grouped into regional blocks so that the equipment can stay in one continent for several weeks, and a block of three races in the Americas is cheaper per race than three races on three continents. The grouping is the reason the season opens in Australia and ends in Abu Dhabi rather than following a natural summer. A triple header in three time zones is a schedule that a mechanic survives on adrenaline and a spreadsheet.

Host fees are the revenue line that decides the calendar. A race that pays a sanctioning fee of tens of millions of dollars is a race that can be scheduled in a market where the sport has few fans, and a historic race that pays little is a race that survives on its share of the broadcast revenue. The balance between the two is negotiated every few years. A new race in a market with no circuit requires a circuit to be built, and the builder is usually the government.

Sprint weekends add cost and reduce practice. A sprint format compresses three practice sessions into one and adds a race on Saturday, and the teams that adapt bring a car that is already sorted. A team that arrives with a new floor and no time to test it loses a weekend, and the format rewards preparation over improvisation. A sprint weekend reduces the time a team has to understand a new tyre, and the teams that test in simulation adapt.

The people are the constraint that the calendar ignores. A mechanic who works twenty-four weekends plus testing is a mechanic who is away from home for two hundred days, and the teams have begun to rotate staff and to hire locally. A sport that expands to twenty-four races is a sport that has to solve a labour problem that it created. A locally hired crew that does the setup and the pack-down reduces the travel, and the training takes a season.

What the calendar sells is a global product, and the cost of being global is the freight. A race in a new market adds a fee and a fan base, and it adds a flight. The arithmetic of that trade is the thing that decides whether the season grows to twenty-five races or settles where it is. A race that pays a fee and draws no audience is a race that the sport keeps for the fee.
