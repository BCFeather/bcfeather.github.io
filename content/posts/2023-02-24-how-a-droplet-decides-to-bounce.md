A raindrop that hits a leaf either sticks or bounces, and the decision is made in about ten milliseconds. The drop arrives at a speed of a few metres a second, flattens into a disc against the surface, and either drains the energy it brought into the impact or returns it to the drop as it retracts. A surface that lets the drop retract without pinning its edge gets the energy back and the drop lifts off, and a surface that pins the edge keeps the energy in a pancake that eventually sits still.

The parameter that decides the regime is the Weber number, which is the inertial energy of the moving drop divided by the surface energy that holds it together. A slow drop has a low number and spreads gently, and a fast one has a high number and spreads into a thin sheet whose rim is unstable. Above a threshold the sheet breaks into smaller drops, and the outcome is splash rather than bounce.

Texture is what a real surface uses. A hydrophobic coating on a flat surface gives a contact angle near the maximum that chemistry allows, and the drop still pins at the rim because the contact line has to move over a defect. A surface with roughness at two scales traps air under the drop, and the drop rests on the tops of the asperities instead of on the solid, so the contact area is small and the edge is free to retract. The same structure fails when the pressure of the impact is high enough to force the liquid into the texture.

The contact time is set by the size of the drop and not by its speed. A drop that spreads to a radius and retracts to a point does so in a time that scales with the square root of the density times the radius divided by the surface tension, and the scaling means that a smaller drop touches for less time. A surface designed to reduce contact time works by splitting the retraction into several paths, so that the drop breaks into pieces before it has time to send a wave back to the centre.

Applications are about ice and heat. A surface that shortens the contact time reduces the heat transferred in a single impact, and a surface that makes drops bounce at low temperature delays the freezing that starts at the contact line. Aircraft icing and heat exchangers both care about the same number, and both have found that a coating that works in a laboratory at room temperature can fail at a surface temperature below freezing, because the condensate that forms first fills the texture.

Limits come from wear and from scale. A texture a few micrometres deep is destroyed by a finger, a gasket or a grain of sand, and a surface that has lost its texture is a surface with a high contact angle and a pinned edge. The demonstrations are on samples a few centimetres across, and the question of how to make a square metre of the structure is the question that separates a paper from a product.
