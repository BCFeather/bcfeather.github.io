Europa's ice shell covers an ocean, and the question of whether the ocean's chemistry reaches the surface is central to the moon's habitability. Carbon dioxide at the surface is a clue to that exchange.

The analysis in *Science*, September 2023 concludes that the carbon dioxide comes from the ice. The authors map the molecule's distribution with a space telescope's spectra and compare it with the surface's geology.

The distribution is the finding. The carbon dioxide concentrates in regions where the ice has been disrupted, and the concentration is consistent with a source in the shell.

The mechanism is radiolysis. A charged particle splits a molecule in the ice, and the fragments recombine into carbon dioxide, and the process is what produces the molecule without an ocean.

The ocean's role is the open question. An ocean that exchanges with the shell would leave a signature that the paper does not see at the surface.

The implication is a limit on the ocean's chemistry. A surface that reflects the ice rather than the ocean is one that a lander cannot use to sample the water.

The measurement is the achievement. A moon's surface at a distance of hundreds of millions of kilometres yields a spectrum, and the authors' mapping is a chemical map.

The limitation is the resolution. A telescope's beam covers a region, and a feature smaller than the beam is averaged away.

What the paper does not establish is the subsurface. A molecule at the surface says nothing about the ocean below, and a radar sounder is the instrument that could tell.

For planetary science, the value is a constraint on a mission's target. A lander that seeks the ocean's chemistry must find a place where the shell exchanges with it, and the map is the guide.

The radiolysis is the chemistry. A Europa's ice sits in Jupiter's radiation belt, and the particles that hit it break water into radicals that recombine into new molecules.

The chaos terrain is the geological context. A region where the ice has been disrupted exposes material from below, and the authors' correlation of carbon dioxide with such terrain is the argument.

The salts are the second clue. A surface with magnesium salts and a carbon dioxide that appears with them suggests a chemical link, and the authors' spectral comparison is the test.

The telescope's resolution is the limit. A spectrum averages a footprint of kilometres, and a patch of a hundred metres is invisible.

The comparison with Ganymede and Callisto is the frame. Those moons have ice and no ocean, and a carbon dioxide that appears on all three points to a process in the ice.

The mission's instrument is the follow-up. A radar that penetrates the shell would test whether the ocean communicates with the surface, and the measurement is planned.
The sulphur is the companion. Europa's surface carries sulphur compounds from the same radiolysis, and the ratio of carbon to sulphur is a fingerprint of the process.
