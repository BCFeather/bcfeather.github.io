Europa's ice shell covers an ocean, and whether that ocean's chemistry reaches the surface is central to the moon's habitability. Carbon dioxide at the surface is one clue to the exchange.

Read in *Science* in September 2023, the clue points to the ice itself, with the molecule's distribution mapped from telescope spectra and compared against the surface's geology. Distribution carries the finding. Carbon dioxide concentrates where the ice has been disrupted, and that concentration is consistent with a source inside the shell rather than below it. Radiolysis supplies the mechanism. Charged particles from Jupiter's belt split water into radicals, the fragments recombine into new molecules, and carbon dioxide appears without any ocean reaching the surface.

Ocean's role stays open, because an ocean exchanging with the shell would leave a signature the surface fails to show. Implication limits the ocean's chemistry, since a surface reflecting the ice rather than the water is one a lander cannot use to sample it. Measurement is the achievement. A moon hundreds of millions of kilometres away still yields a spectrum, and mapping it produces a chemical map.

Resolution sets the limitation, because a beam covers kilometres and a patch of a hundred metres disappears into the average. The subsurface is what the paper does not reach, since a molecule at the surface says nothing about the ocean and a radar sounder is the instrument that could tell.

Planetary science gains a constraint on a mission's target, because a lander seeking ocean chemistry must find where the shell exchanges with it and the map is the guide. Chaos terrain is the geological context, since disrupted ice exposes material from below and correlating the gas with such terrain is the authors' argument. Salts provide a second clue, because magnesium salts appearing alongside the gas suggest a chemical link, and the spectral comparison is the test.

Telescope resolution is the limit, since a footprint of kilometres averages everything inside it. Ganymede and Callisto offer the frame, because both carry ice and neither carries an ocean, and a molecule appearing on all three points to a process within the ice.

A higher-resolution observation of one chaos region would show whether the gas sits in patches or spreads evenly, and a patchiness at a finer scale would argue for a local source rather than a global one. A laboratory experiment irradiating ice with the same sulphur and magnesium mixture would test the recombination chemistry directly, and an experiment is what turns the spectral association into a mechanism. A radar sounding across the shell would show whether liquid communicates with the surface at all, and a null result there would confine the ocean's chemistry to the deep interior. A model of the ice shell's overturn would show whether material from the ocean can reach the surface within the shell's lifetime, and a timescale shorter than the moon's age would reopen the exchange the map appears to close.
