title: "IEA finds emissions growing more slowly than the economy"
date: "2025-03-24T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [economy]
description: "The International Energy Agency's Global Energy Review, published in March, reported that energy-related carbon dioxide emissions rose 0.8 per cent in 2024 while the world economy grew by more than 3 per cent, a decoupling that the agency credits to clean energy deployment."
---

Energy demand grew by about 2.2 per cent in 2024, faster than in the previous year, and the growth was concentrated in electricity instead of in transport or heating. That is a shift in the shape of demand, because electricity can be supplied by sources that do not burn fuel, and the report makes the point that the growth of the grid is now the main variable in the emissions equation.

Emissions rose by 300 million tonnes, which brought the total to a record, and the agency attributes about half of the rise to weather. 2024 was the hottest year on record, and a hot year increases the demand for cooling, which is met by electricity, and reduces the demand for heating, which is met by gas. The counterfactual in the report is worth reading: if the weather had matched 2023, about half of the increase would not have happened.

Clean energy did the rest of the work. Solar photovoltaic additions reached a record, wind and nuclear output rose, and electric cars accounted for more than a fifth of new car sales, and the agency estimates that the deployment since 2019 now avoids about 2.6 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide each year. That is roughly 7 per cent of global emissions, and it is the number that supports the argument that the transition is displacing fuel and not adding to it.

Geography of the increase matters more than the total. Most of the growth came from emerging economies outside China, and China's own emissions growth slowed, which is the first sign that the world's largest emitter has passed the point at which industrial expansion and emissions move together. A country that adds solar and nuclear at the rate China has added them can grow its economy while its emissions flatten, and 2024 was the year in which that became visible in the data.

Electricity demand is where the argument will be tested. Data centres, electric vehicles, heat pumps and air conditioning all add load, and the agency projects that the growth of electricity demand will outpace the growth of total energy demand for the rest of the decade. Whether that load is met by clean generation or by coal depends on the grid that each country builds, and the report is explicit that the outcome is a policy choice and not a forecast.

Agency's own record on that choice is mixed. Its projections have understated the growth of solar for a decade, because the cost of a module has fallen faster than its models assumed, and a report that projects the grid from the current cost curve carries the same risk in the other direction. A reader should treat the demand forecast as a range and the deployment record as a fact.

What the review provides is the annual audit. Emissions rose by less than the economy grew, the gap came from clean energy and from weather, and the record total was set in a year in which the deployment of clean generation also set a record. Those two facts sit together, and the argument about whether the transition is fast enough is an argument about the rate and not about the direction.
