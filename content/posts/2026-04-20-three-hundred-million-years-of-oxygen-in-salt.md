Rock salt traps air. When halite crystallizes from a brine, tiny pockets of the surrounding atmosphere are sealed inside the crystal, and they stay sealed because salt is impermeable and chemically inert. A study published in *Nature Geoscience* on 16 April used those pockets, from five basins spanning the Permian and Triassic, to reconstruct atmospheric oxygen across about 300 million years.

The technique is a refinement of an old idea. Crush a crystal under vacuum, release the trapped gas, and measure its composition by mass spectrometry. The refinement is sample selection: the team screened thousands of inclusions under a microscope, kept only those that were primary, unbroken and free of daughter crystals, and then ran the same measurement on three separate aliquots per sample to check reproducibility.

Oxygen came out between 15 and 22 per cent through the late Permian, rising towards 25 per cent in the Early Triassic before declining again. That is not what the standard box model predicts. The model, built from carbon and sulfur isotope records, has oxygen peaking in the Carboniferous and falling through the Permian into a low-Oxygen Triassic, and the halite data disagree with it at the level of several percentage points.

The disagreement has a plausible explanation, and the authors are careful to state it as one. Isotope-based models infer oxygen from the burial ratio of organic carbon to pyrite and from the isotopic composition of carbonate, both of which integrate over ocean chemistry and lag the atmosphere. Salt records the atmosphere directly at the moment of crystallization, but samples a handful of points in time and can be biased by the environment of deposition, since halite forms in restricted basins where the local air may not represent the global mean.

Fires provide a cross-check, and an imprecise one. Charcoal abundance in coal seams is used as a proxy for atmospheric oxygen, because below about 15 per cent fires do not propagate and above 30 per cent they become too frequent for forests to persist. The new values sit comfortably inside that window, which supports the reconstruction without confirming it.

For the Permian-Triassic boundary, the implication is specific. If oxygen was already rising during the latest Permian, then the end-Permian extinction, dated to 251.9 million years ago, occurred against a backdrop of comparatively oxygen-rich air rather than the oxygen-poor atmosphere that the box model implies. That does not explain the extinction, which is attributed to warming, ocean anoxia and volcanism, but it changes the baseline against which the kill mechanism is measured.

A further complication is the brine itself. Halite crystallizes from evaporated seawater in a basin that is partly isolated from the ocean, and the trapped fluid is not pure air: dissolved gases from the brine, including carbon dioxide and traces of hydrogen sulfide, mix with the atmosphere in the inclusion. The authors correct for this using the composition of fluid inclusions in the same crystal that formed later, when the basin was more concentrated, and the correction moves their values by under one percentage point.

Timing is the other soft spot. Crystallization dates come from the same strata as the inclusions, and the resolution is a few hundred thousand years at best, so a rapid excursion lasting a million years could be missed entirely between two sampled beds.

Readers should note the sample geography. All five basins sit in the Northern Hemisphere subtropics, and one of them, in the Zechstein, is famous for its cyclic evaporation. A global mean reconstructed from restricted basins is an estimate with an unquantified error bar, and the paper says so in its second paragraph rather than its last.
