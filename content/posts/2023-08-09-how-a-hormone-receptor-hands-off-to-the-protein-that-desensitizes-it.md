A G-protein-coupled receptor signals by activating a G protein, and it stops signalling by binding an arrestin. The hand-off between the two decides how long a hormone's effect lasts. In *Nature* in August 2023, that hand-off is resolved for the glucagon receptor. The authors determine cryo-electron microscopy structures of the receptor bound to beta-arrestin 1, and the complex shows how the arrestin recognises the activated state.

Recognition is the finding. The arrestin binds the receptor's phosphorylated tail and its intracellular face at once, and the two contacts together give the complex its stability. Size of the interface is the source of affinity, since a broad contact with many small interactions produces a complex persisting long enough to be measured.

Phosphorylation is the switch. A kinase adds phosphates to the tail after activation, and the arrestin's positively charged surface reads the pattern, so the tail acts as a code in which several phosphates bind strongly and fewer bind weakly. Competition follows. A receptor cannot bind a G protein and an arrestin in the same way at once, and the structures show the overlap that makes the hand-off a replacement.

Physiology supplies the motivation. Glucagon raises blood glucose, and a drug prolonging or shortening the receptor's signalling is a candidate for diabetes therapy. A biased ligand is the application, since a molecule favouring one downstream pathway is a drug with fewer side effects.

Comparison across receptors frames it. Arrestins bind many receptors, and one complex is a template for the others; the beta-adrenergic complex has been solved, and a comparison shows which contacts are conserved.

Construct sets the limitation. A stabilised receptor and a modified arrestin are what cryo-electron microscopy requires, and those modifications may bias the conformation. The tail's flexibility is the technical obstacle, because a disordered tail does not produce a sharp image, and stabilisation is what makes the complex resolvable.

Kinetics is left unmeasured, because a structure is a state and the rate of the hand-off is a separate measurement. Pharmacology gains a target's shape, since a drug blocking the arrestin without blocking the G protein would bias the receptor's output, and this structure is the basis for that design. Dimerisation stays a complication, because some receptors act as dimers and others as monomers, and the monomeric state in the structure is the premise of the model. A time-resolved measurement of arrestin recruitment would put a number on the hand-off, since the structures define the endpoint of a process whose duration decides how long a hormone acts, and a live-cell assay with a fluorescent arrestin is the experiment that would supply it. Whether the same interface explains arrestin binding at other family members is the generalisation the field will test, and a set of complexes across the family is what would turn one structure into a rule about biased signalling. The structural work also gives a medicinal chemist a surface to dock against, and that is the practical value of a complex resolved at this resolution.
