China adopted its one-child policy in 1979 and 1980, and the demographic response arrived a decade later. Fertility changed little at the start, then fell by more than a third during the early 1990s and passed below replacement. Social change, contraceptive supply and the policy itself have all been proposed as the cause, and the timing has made it hard to separate them.

Meng, Yang and Li use the staggered adoption of a bureaucratic rule to isolate one channel, in *Nature*, 11 November 2026. The one vote veto made promotion for local officials conditional on meeting birth targets, and provinces adopted it on different dates until coverage reached nearly the whole population by 1996. Birth and contraceptive histories come from the 2001 National Family Planning and Reproductive Health Survey, a retrospective instrument that records each pregnancy and each method used.

The sample holds 36,375 women and 1,906,333 woman-years. Between 1987 and 1999 the total fertility rate fell by 0.98 births per woman, and the authors attribute about 48 per cent of that decline, or 0.48 births per woman, to the veto. Reductions concentrated at second and higher parities and among women aged 20 to 29, and sequential multi-decrement life tables convert the estimated hazards into actual and counterfactual rates. Each province serves as its own control before adoption, which is how the event study avoids leaning on a single national pre-trend.

Two results carry the argument. Provincial adoption dates line up with breaks in annual birth probabilities, and the effect runs through contraception: use of intrauterine devices rose, with a larger relative increase where local officials recommended the method than where women took it up voluntarily. That pattern is consistent with administrative pressure working through the clinic, and it explains why a rule about careers moved births rather than intentions alone. Intrauterine devices were the most common modern method in China at the time, so a shift in their use moves the aggregate.

Provincial timing is not random. Urbanization, income growth and the end of the commune system all moved through the 1990s and could correlate with adoption dates, and provinces differed in age structure before the rule arrived, which weighting by female population addresses without removing. Birth histories are retrospective and self-reported, and the counterfactual is a model prediction, so 48 per cent is an estimate of the rule's contribution under stated assumptions. Fertility was already falling before the veto reached a province, and the authors do not claim that the rule created the decline.

County-level records would show whether the break in births sits at adoption or earlier, and administrative documents would show how targets were set and audited. Comparisons with other bureaucracies that tie promotion to demographic targets would show whether the mechanism is general or specific to this administration. A decomposition that includes migration and marriage timing would show how much of the remaining half the model leaves unclaimed.
