Venous thromboembolism became a recognized complication of COVID early in the pandemic, and the question for clinicians after the acute phase passed was how long the risk persists and how it compares with the baseline risk of the same person. Registries with complete national coverage are the instrument for that question. A patient who develops a clot two months after a mild infection is not obviously a patient with long COVID, and the diagnosis is easy to miss when the illness is in the past.

A Swedish study linked all cases of COVID in the country to diagnoses of deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism and bleeding, and used a self-controlled case series in which each patient is compared with themselves outside the exposure period. The results appeared in *The BMJ*, 6 April 2022.

Pulmonary embolism was 36 times more common during the first week after COVID and 46 times more common during the second, and the ratio fell over the following weeks. Deep vein thrombosis was six times more common during the first month, and bleeding was 2.5 times more common over the same period. A ratio of 36 in the first week is a comparison with the same person's own baseline, and the baseline rate in a 70-year-old is already appreciable.

Absolute numbers are small relative to the ratio. Pulmonary embolism occurred in 0.17 per cent of patients with COVID during the first 30 days, deep vein thrombosis in 0.039 per cent and bleeding in 0.101 per cent, which are rates that a clinician can put beside the risk of prophylactic anticoagulation.

Ratios stayed elevated for 70 days for deep vein thrombosis, 110 days for pulmonary embolism and 60 days for bleeding, and the authors describe a period of roughly two to three months in which the risk is abnormal. That interval is longer than the thromboprophylaxis given in hospital and shorter than the follow-up of most long-COVID clinics. The bleeding signal is the other side of the same coin, because the inflammatory state that promotes thrombosis also activates platelets and the endothelium in ways that can destabilize a vessel.

Severity mattered. Patients with critical COVID had the highest ratios, and the first pandemic wave produced higher ratios than the second and third, which suggests that the variant and the treatment protocol both influence the risk and not the virus alone. Patients with critical disease are also the ones who receive the most intensive thromboprophylaxis, and the remaining excess suggests that prophylaxis does not cover the whole period of risk.

A registry cannot tell whether a patient received anticoagulation, and the analysis adjusts for long-term anticoagulation at baseline but not for prophylaxis during the acute illness. The implication for practice is a period of vigilance after discharge rather than a change in inpatient protocols. The message for a general practitioner is to consider a recent infection when a patient presents with leg swelling or breathlessness, and the message for a guideline committee is that the window for prophylaxis may need extending.
