NATO's leaders met in Ankara on 7 and 8 July for a summit that the alliance had billed as a delivery check, and the secretary general, Mark Rutte, opened it by asking every member to arrive with a clear, concrete and credible plan for raising defence spending to five per cent of national output. The venue was itself a statement, since Türkiye had spent the previous year arguing that the alliance's southern flank was the part that mattered. Leaders left with a communiqué that repeated the target and described the plans as work in progress.

Arithmetic of the target is what makes the argument hard. Five per cent of output is roughly double what most European members spent before 2022, and the money has to come out of budgets that also pay for pensions and health care. Rutte's formula asks for most of the increase to go to core military capability and the rest to infrastructure that armies use, and governments have interpreted the split differently enough that comparisons between them have become a diplomatic exercise.

Disagreements ran along two lines. The large spenders, including Poland and the Baltic states, wanted a binding schedule and argued that the alliance had already spent a decade missing softer targets. The older members, including Germany and Italy, wanted the target defined by capability and by industrial capacity rather than by a number, and their finance ministries pointed out that orders cannot be placed faster than factories can deliver.

Turkish government had its own agenda for the summit. Ankara wanted the alliance to treat the Kurdish groups it fights in Syria as a security question for everyone, and it wanted the sale of fighter aircraft and munitions to move faster than the export bureaucracies of its partners had allowed. Turkish officials also used the meeting to press for a bigger role in the alliance's command structure, which several European members resisted.

Industrial base is the constraint nobody at the summit could legislate away. European factories that produce shells and propellant have been running at capacity for two years, and the companies say that new lines take three to five years to build and staff. A target measured in output share can be met on paper by writing a budget line, and the equipment it is meant to buy arrives on a different timetable. Alliance officials have also begun to talk about multi-year contracts that would let factories plan beyond a single budget cycle.

Alliance summits are judged by whether they change behaviour, and this one changed the paperwork. Every capital will now produce a plan, and the plans will be compared by the alliance's staff and briefed to journalists, which is a form of pressure that works slowly. Whether that is enough will be tested by the next crisis rather than by the next summit, and the alliance has had several reminders that crises arrive on their own schedule. Rutte said the alliance would publish a scorecard in the autumn, which gives the argument a date.
