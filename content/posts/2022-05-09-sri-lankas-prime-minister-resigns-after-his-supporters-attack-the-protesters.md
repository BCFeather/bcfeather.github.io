Mahinda Rajapaksa resigned as prime minister of Sri Lanka on 9 May, hours after a march by his supporters ended in violence against the protesters camped outside his office. The crowds had come to Colombo from the districts around the capital, and by the afternoon they had attacked the tents at Galle Face and at the entrance to Temple Trees, the prime minister's residence, with sticks and helmets. Seven people were killed, including two governing-party members of parliament, more than two hundred were injured, and the government imposed a curfew across the country.

The resignation letter went to the president, who is the prime minister's younger brother, and it said the decision was taken because the president had asked for it at a cabinet meeting on 6 May. The office had been the family's for most of two decades, and the man leaving it had been president from 2005 to 2015, when he built the wartime command that defeated the Tamil Tigers and the peacetime apparatus that his critics say turned the state into a family business. A new prime minister, Ranil Wickremesinghe, was sworn in on 12 May, his sixth time in the post.

The violence was a collision between two mobilisations. The protest movement that calls itself the struggle had been camped at Galle Face since early April, demanding that the president resign over an economy that had run out of fuel, medicine and foreign currency; the crowds that marched on 9 May had been bussed in by the ruling party to demand the opposite. Once the fighting started, the police stood back, the army was deployed with orders to shoot anyone who damaged property, and the country spent the night under curfew listening to reports of houses burned in the suburbs.

Fuel, medicine and foreign currency had been failing for months before the politics caught up with them. Sri Lanka suspended payments on its foreign debt in April, with about fifty-one billion dollars outstanding, and its reserves had fallen below a hundred million dollars, which buys less than a week of imports. Inflation was near thirty per cent, Diesel and petrol queues stretched for kilometres, hospitals ran out of anaesthetics, and schools closed because there was no paper for exams.

Talks with the International Monetary Fund were under way, and India had kept the island afloat with credit lines and shipments of fuel that totalled several billion dollars, while China's loans from the port and highway years remained on the books. Whoever governs has to negotiate a restructuring with creditors while keeping the lights on, an exercise that usually requires a government able to sign its name to terms and a parliament able to ratify them. The resignation removed one obstacle to that and installed another: a prime minister whom the protesters regard as part of the same family arrangement.

By the middle of the month the camps were still standing, the president was still in office, and the curfew had become the instrument of daily government. Mahinda Rajapaksa left his post with a letter and a police escort, and the crowds that had marched to defend him left behind a country that has run out of the things a government is expected to provide.
