Japan's currency fell to 161.27 per dollar on 28 June, its weakest level since 1986, and it has lost about 12 per cent this year. The finance ministry has spent an estimated sixty billion dollars on intervention since April. The move came in the London session when the liquidity is thin, which is the time of day when the officials have intervened most often. The move came a day after the American data showed the prices slowing, which the market read as a reason for the gap to narrow rather than widen.

Interest gap is the cause. The American rate is above five per cent and the Japanese rate is near zero, and a saver who can earn the difference by holding dollars does so, which is a flow that no amount of official buying can offset for long. A gap that is structural is a gap that the market will keep testing. The rate differential is the widest since the 1990s, and the hedging costs that the exporters pay have become a line in their results.

Intervention changes the speed rather than the level. The purchases in April and May slowed the fall for a few weeks and the currency resumed its decline once the effect wore off, which is what the officials have said they expected. A policy that buys time is a policy that has to be repeated. The ministry has declined to confirm the amounts, and the estimates come from the changes in the accounts at the central bank.

Imported prices are the cost. The food and the energy that the country buys abroad become more expensive, and the households that already spend a larger share of income on them feel the effect first. A weak currency is a tax on the people who buy what the country does not make. The wage increases that the unions won this spring are the only offset, and they have covered about half of the loss in purchasing power. The retailers have begun to raise the prices of the imported goods, and the surveys show the households noticing.

Only the central bank can change the gap. It has raised the rate once this year and has said it will do so again, and the market's expectations for the next move have been pushed back by the weakness of the data. A bank that waits is a currency that falls. The bank's next meeting is in July, and the market has priced a small probability of a rise, which is higher than it was before the currency moved.

What the month shows is that a currency can be defended with reserves only until the reserves are seen to be finite, and the market has begun to price that limit. A country that intervenes without changing the cause is a country that is buying time from the market, and the market sells it back at a price.
