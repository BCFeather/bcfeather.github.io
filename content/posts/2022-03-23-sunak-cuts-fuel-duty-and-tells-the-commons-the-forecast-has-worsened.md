Rishi Sunak delivered the spring statement on 23 March, and he spent the first ten minutes of it describing a forecast rather than announcing a policy. The Office for Budget Responsibility had revised its projections since October: inflation was expected to average about seven and a half per cent this year and to peak near nine in the autumn, growth was cut to below four per cent, and the real incomes of households were expected to fall by the largest amount in any year since the 1950s. The chancellor said that he could not protect every household from the shock that the war had produced.

The measures that followed were small and targeted. Fuel duty was cut by 5 pence a litre for twelve months, the threshold at which national insurance begins was lifted to 12,570 pounds, the rate of VAT on solar panels and heat pumps was cut to zero, and a fund for household support was increased. The chancellor said the package was worth about 6 billion pounds, and the opposition pointed out that the rise in national insurance that had been announced in the autumn was still going ahead in April.

Arithmetic was the politically difficult part. The energy price cap was due to rise by more than 50 per cent at the beginning of April, the war had pushed the price of petrol and food up, and the Bank of England had raised rates for the third time in four months. The Office for Budget Responsibility said that the tax burden would reach its highest level since the 1950s, which is a phrase that no chancellor of a low-tax party wants to read out in the chamber.

Criticism came from both directions. Labour said the statement took less from working households than the national insurance rise took back, and that a one-year cut in fuel duty was a subsidy to drivers rather than a policy on energy. Conservative backbenchers said the chancellor had room to cut taxes further and had chosen not to use it, and they pointed to the receipts from inflation, which raises the yield of a tax system that is indexed only in part.

The spring statement had been designed as a minor occasion, a mid-year update with no red box and no set-piece announcements. The war turned it into the only fiscal event before the autumn, and the chancellor used it to set out a plan to cut the basic rate of income tax to 19 per cent before the end of the Parliament, a promise that the autumn statement would have to either keep or abandon. The Office for Budget Responsibility published the tables that made the promise look expensive, and the two documents sat beside each other in the same hour.

What the statement revealed about the year was the gap between the shock and the instrument. A war that raised the price of energy by a third could be met with a cut of 5 pence on a litre of fuel, and a household whose energy bill doubled in April received about 350 pounds of support through the council tax rebate and the fund. The chancellor's reputation for competence survived the afternoon; his reputation for generosity did not, and the autumn statement in November was written to answer for both.
