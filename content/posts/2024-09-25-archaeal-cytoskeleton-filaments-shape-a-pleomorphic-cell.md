---

Bactofilins are cytoskeletal filaments that bacteria use to connect cellular processes to the curvature of their membrane, and homologues had been found in archaea without being studied. A paper in *PNAS* on 25 September 2024 characterizes two of them in *Haloferax volcanii*, an archaeon whose cells are pleomorphic, meaning that their shape varies. The bacterial versions establish the family, and the archaeal homologues had been annotated without being examined.

Two proteins are involved, halofilin A and halofilin B. Both polymerize in vitro and assemble into straight bundles, which is the behaviour expected of a bactofilin, and neither requires a membrane to form filaments. Polymerization in the absence of a membrane separates the filament from its anchoring, which is a property the bacterial proteins share.

Their behaviour in cells differs. HalA polymers are highly dynamic and accumulate at regions of positive membrane curvature, the convex edges where a cell bulges outward, while HalB forms more static foci that localize to regions of negative curvature on the outer surface. A dynamic polymer can remodel within a cell cycle, while a static focus marks a site that has to stay put.

Division of labour is spatial. A filament that follows convex edges and another that marks concave ones together describe the geometry of a cell whose shape is not fixed, and a cytoskeleton that reads curvature can maintain a shape without prescribing one. Curvature is the only geometric variable that a membrane-bound polymer can read without a receptor, so a filament that follows it is a shape sensor.

Deletions test the function. Strains lacking the halofilins lose morphological integrity during growth, which indicates that the filaments are required for the cell to keep a consistent outline rather than for any single morphogenetic event. A strain that cannot keep its outline is one that has lost a structural function rather than a metabolic one.

Archaeal cytoskeletons are sparse compared with their bacterial counterparts, and the discovery that bactofilin homologues do real work in archaea extends a family that had been defined by its bacterial members. The finding narrows the gap between the two domains, since a family assumed to be bacterial turns out to be universal.

The paper does not resolve how the filaments sense curvature, since a polymer that binds a membrane has no obvious way to distinguish convex from concave geometry except through the proteins that anchor it. Nor does it establish what determines the difference between a dynamic and a static polymer, which is the property that separates the two halofilins and would be the target of a structural comparison. Anchoring proteins are the obvious candidates for the curvature sensor, and their identification is the next structural step. A third halofilin family, if it exists, would show whether the dynamic and static roles are a general division or a peculiarity of this halophile. Live imaging at higher resolution would show whether the filaments move along the membrane or are anchored while the membrane deforms around them.
