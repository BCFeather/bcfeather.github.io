description: "A regional landslide warning system has sensors on 12 per cent of its slopes, and its own audit found that 8 of 11 fatal events in five years occurred on a slope it was not monitoring."
---

Landslide warning depends on knowing where the ground is moving, and a regional system with sensors on a fraction of its slopes has to decide how to spend its budget. An audit of five years of operation found that 8 of 11 fatal landslides occurred on an unmonitored slope.

The sensors are inclinometers in boreholes and GPS monuments on the surface, and each costs 40,000 dollars to install. The region has 4,000 slopes that could fail and a budget for 500. Steepness and proximity to a road set the selection criterion, and the audit found that it missed the events that mattered. Seven of the eight misses occurred on slopes with a history of small failures, which the criterion treated as safe because they had not failed catastrophically.

Rainfall thresholds are the second instrument. A threshold of 120 millimetres in 24 hours triggered alerts, and the fatal events had 80 to 100 millimetres. The thresholds were set for a different soil type. A slope's history is what an instrument records, and a slope without one has no baseline against which a movement can be judged.

Satellite interferometry is the alternative. A radar satellite can measure a slope's movement to a millimetre a month across a whole region, and the data's resolution is 20 metres. That method would cover 4,000 slopes for the cost of 200 boreholes. The constraint is latency and cloud. A satellite pass is every 12 days and a radar signal penetrates cloud, so the method gives a monthly snapshot and not a real-time alarm. The two methods are complements. A regional inventory of slopes is the input a siting decision needs, and most regions do not have one.

The audit's recommendation is a hybrid. Satellite screening identifies slopes that are moving, and boreholes are installed on the ones that matter. That doubles the effective coverage for the same budget. A sensor network is placed where a hazard is known, and that is the audit's structural problem. A slope that has already moved gets an instrument; a slope that has not is unmonitored. The 8 of 11 failures were on slopes without instruments, which is a statement about siting rather than about instruments.

Satellite interferometry changes the siting constraint. A radar satellite can measure a slope's movement without an instrument on it, and its limitation is the revisit interval and the vegetation. A forested slope in a cloud-prone region is invisible to both. A sensor's warning requires an evacuation, and an evacuation requires a decision. A slope that moves two millimetres a day may fail in a week or in a decade, and a local authority that evacuates on the first signal spends its credibility on false alarms. The audit's failure is as much institutional as instrumental.

Designed hazard and occurring hazard are different things, which is what the audit exposes. A sensor on the wrong slope is an instrument that reports the ground is stable while the ground elsewhere is not.
