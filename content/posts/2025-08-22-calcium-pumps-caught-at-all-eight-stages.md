Cells keep their calcium under tight control, because the ion is used to carry signals rather than to build tissue. The pump that removes it, known as PMCA, sits in the plasma membrane and pushes calcium out against a gradient that is roughly ten thousand times steeper outside than inside. It does this at a rate measured in kilohertz, which is fast for a protein that has to change shape four times per cycle. The pump works against a gradient that would otherwise pull calcium back into the cell within milliseconds, so speed is not a refinement here but the whole function.

A paper in *Nature* on 20 August reports structures of the mammalian PMCA2 pump in eight states of its transport cycle, with and without its auxiliary subunit neuroplastin. The work, from the groups of Bernd Fakler and Stefan Raunser at Freiburg, used cryo-electron microscopy and was paired with whole-cell recordings that measured how quickly calcium leaves a live cell.

The striking detail concerns a lipid. Phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate, a membrane lipid that is present in most eukaryotic membranes, binds at a site that moves during the cycle and behaves as a latch: it holds the pump in a conformation that allows calcium to be released quickly, and it opens a path for the counter-ion that has to come in as calcium goes out. Remove the lipid and the pump still works, but slowly.

That explains an old puzzle. PMCA had been known to depend on acidic lipids for activity since the 1970s, but the dependence had been treated as a cofactor rather than as part of the mechanism. The structures put the lipid inside the machine, at a site that is empty in the resting state and occupied during turnover.

It also explains some disease mutations. Several inherited mutations in the PMCA genes cause hearing loss or neurological problems, and a number of them map to the interfaces that the structures now resolve: the site where the lipid binds, the hinge that closes around the calcium, and the surface where neuroplastin attaches. The subunit turns out to stabilize a particular angle between two domains, which is why its presence changes the rate rather than the destination of the cycle.

The therapeutic angle is speculative but concrete. If the lipid site can be occupied by a small molecule, then calcium handling could be adjusted without touching the pump's catalytic core, which is a much harder target because it is conserved across many enzymes. The authors note that the site is unusually accessible from the membrane, which is where drugs can reach it. For cell biology the result is a reminder that membranes are not a backdrop. A pump that moves ions across a bilayer is regulated by the bilayer's composition, and the composition changes with diet, with inflammation and with age. The eight structures make that regulation visible, and they give the field something it has lacked: a set of coordinates for the states that the kinetic data had only inferred.
