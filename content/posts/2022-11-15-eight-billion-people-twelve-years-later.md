The figure is an estimate, and the United Nations says so each time it publishes one. No government counts its people on the same day as its neighbours, and some governments have not held a census for decades. Even so, the projection for 15 November 2022 was eight billion, and the date had been chosen months in advance so that the world could mark it. The announcement came with a report, a press conference and a set of charts showing how the curve had risen and where it would go next.

Arithmetic behind the number is the story. The human population took one hundred and twenty-five years to grow from one billion to two, and twelve years to grow from seven to eight. The curve flattened at the same time as it rose, because birth rates have fallen almost everywhere outside a handful of countries in Africa and in parts of Asia. A woman alive in 1950 would have expected to bury several of her children, and her granddaughter expects to raise two. Vaccination and clean water did most of that work, and the rest came from schools and from clinics that let parents choose.

Falling birth rates do not mean fewer people. They mean an older population, and a population that keeps growing for a while because the children of the previous generation are still alive. The United Nations projects a peak of about ten point four billion in the 2080s and a slow decline afterwards, which would be the first sustained fall since the Black Death. Most of that remaining growth will happen in countries whose hospitals and schools are already stretched, and whose cities are growing faster than their pipes.

Geography has shifted as well. India is expected to overtake China as the most populous country during the following year, and more than half of the increase between now and the middle of the century is expected in eight countries, most of them in Africa and Asia. Europe and parts of East Asia are already shrinking, and their schools are closing in the villages where the youngest classes have emptied. Japan has been losing people for more than a decade, and its towns have begun to merge their services and to close their branches.

The milestone arrived with arguments attached. Some pointed at consumption rather than numbers, and noted that the richest tenth of the population produces most of the emissions and most of the waste. Others pointed at the countries where women still lack access to contraception and to schooling, and said the remaining growth would be fastest where resources were thinnest. Both arguments are correct in their own terms, and they lead to very different policies. Which one a government takes seriously depends on whether it is counting people or counting what those people consume.

A date on a calendar is a device for getting people to look at a trend they already know. What the trend says is simple enough. The century in which the number doubled is over, the century in which it will age is beginning, and the policies that matter now concern schools, pensions and the price of energy.
