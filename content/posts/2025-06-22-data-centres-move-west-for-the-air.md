A data centre is a building full of heat, and the heat has to go somewhere. On the coast, it goes into chillers that run all year and consume a third of the electricity the servers use. In the highlands of the west, it can go into the outside air for most of the year, which is why the national computing programme has been directing new capacity towards Gansu, Ningxia, Guizhou and Inner Mongolia. The physics is simple. A server hall runs at around 25 degrees Celsius, and the air in Yinchuan in January is well below zero, so a fan and a heat exchanger do the whole job. In July the air is warm, and the hall falls back on evaporative cooling, which uses water rather than electricity. The annual power saving is large enough that a site can pay back its construction cost from the electricity bill alone, which is not true of a hall in Shenzhen.

Officials call the scheme the east-west computing programme, with processing in the east and storage in the west. The idea is that the latency-sensitive work, from payments to video, stays near the users, and the work that tolerates delay, from model training to archival storage, moves to where the power is cheap.
