title: "Adult mice regrow a molar, 40 per cent too small"
date: "2025-11-01T10:15:00+08:00"

description: "A dental stem-cell population that had been assumed to be exhausted in adults has been reactivated in mice, and the regrown tooth has the correct layers but not the correct size."
---

Teeth are replaced once in humans and continuously in some fish, and the difference is a population of stem cells that persists in the dental lamina. A study reported this month that this population exists in adult mice, dormant, and that a signal can restart it.

The signal is a Wnt pathway activator delivered by a gel applied to the gum. Two doses four days apart produced a new tooth in 60 per cent of the treated mice, and the tooth erupted through the gum in six weeks.

Anatomy is the encouraging part. Micro-computed tomography shows enamel, dentine, a pulp chamber and a root, and histology confirms the layers are in the correct order with the correct cell types.

Size is where the disappointment lies. The regrown molars are 40 per cent smaller than the originals and have a thinner enamel layer, and the defect traces to a smaller initial stem-cell aggregate. Histology shows the enamel and the dentine in the right order, which a size measurement alone would not establish.

Control experiments separate regeneration from inflammation. An untreated group and a gel-without-activator group produced no new teeth, and a group that received a Wnt inhibitor produced none either, which fixes the pathway as the mechanism.

The human translation is a long way off. Human dental lamina loses its stem cells after the second dentition, and the paper's own discussion states that the equivalent population has not been found in adult human tissue. Function is the test that follows size, and a regenerated tooth that cannot bear a load is a structure rather than a tooth.

An implant is the comparison that matters. An implant is a metal post in bone with a crown, and it works for decades and does not remodel. A regrown tooth would attach through a periodontal ligament and move with the jaw, which is the property that makes regeneration worth pursuing.

A tooth's size is set by the number of divisions a stem cell population completes before it mineralizes, and a regenerated molar stops dividing early because the signal arrives after the enamel organ has formed. The 40 per cent shortfall is a timing defect rather than a supply one.

Timing is what a clinical version would have to fix. A gel applied to a gum reaches the dental lamina within hours, and the window in which a mouse's lamina is receptive is a few days. An adult human's equivalent window is not known and may not exist.

A regenerated tooth's enamel is the second gap. A molar's crown is enamel over dentine, and a regenerated tooth that mineralizes 40 per cent small also mineralizes thin, which a histological section shows. A tooth that a mouse can use is not a tooth that a person could.

Dormancy, once established, can be reversed, and this result is the demonstration. The size gap is the part that shows how much of tooth development is about the number of cells at the start rather than about the signals that follow.
