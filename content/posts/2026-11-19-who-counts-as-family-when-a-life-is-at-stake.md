'Are you family?' The question at the desk of an intensive care unit is usually put that plainly, and it decides what happens in the next hour. Nobody asks whether the visitor has cooked for the patient for eleven years, paid half the rent or sat through two remissions. The answer has to be a category, and the category comes from a list.

Life and love are the two words a ceremony joins, and the ceremony asks nothing of them beyond the saying. An institution has to be more exact, because a life at stake becomes a decision about a body, and the love around it becomes a claim that somebody has to test.

What stands behind that list is the assumption that a formal relationship certifies care. Spouse, parent, child and sibling are the entries, with a grandparent or a stepchild added by some statutes; each one says that the person at the desk has a stake in the patient's life and can be trusted to speak for it. Most of the time the assumption holds, because most people who love someone that much have married them, or raised them, or been raised by them. The failures are ordinary: cohabiting couples, the divorced, the emigrated, the never-married. Where the list works, it works because the person it names is usually the person who would have been chosen anyway; where it fails, the mistake is quiet, and the relative asked to decide is often the relative who inherits.

English law never defines next of kin, and it gives that person no power to decide anything. A decision about an adult who cannot make one falls under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 and is taken in that adult's best interests, by the clinician in charge, after consulting the people close to the patient and anyone named in a lasting power of attorney. A relative who disagrees has a voice in that consultation and no veto. The statute protects the right object, the patient's interests, and it leaves the person who has done the caring with the standing of a witness.

Where the patient cannot be told, Chinese law directs the doctor to the near relative. Article 1219 of the Civil Code requires an explanation of the condition and the proposed measures to the patient, and to a near relative where telling the patient would be inadvisable; article 1127 then orders the succession, spouse, children and parents first, siblings and grandparents second. Both articles are silent on the partner who is not a spouse, however long the household has lasted. Ordering is the mechanism the code trusts: it fixes a queue in advance so that nobody has to negotiate at the bedside, and a queue is easier to defend than a judgement about who was loved.

The American route reaches the same answer by different machinery. Federal privacy rules permit a hospital to tell family members involved in a patient's care what is happening; who may consent when the patient cannot is left to state statutes, and the usual ladder starts at the spouse, descends through adult children and parents, and reaches siblings last. Again the assumption is that kinship predicts agreement with the patient's wishes, and again the proxy is usually right and occasionally wrong.

Built for a country of married households, these ladders no longer describe the households people live in. In England and Wales, 30.2 per cent of households, 7.5 million of them, held one person at the 2021 census. Marriages registered in China fell from 13.47 million in 2013 to 6.84 million in 2022, recovered to 7.68 million in 2023, and fell again to 6.1 million in 2024. Couples who never register, adults whose children have moved to another province, and the divorced who have repartnered without a ceremony all stand outside the first rung, and a list written for the majority now serves a minority of the arrangements people actually keep.

Registration does the same work at the other end of a life, assigning a child to the adults who will be answerable for it. A birth is registered with a mother and, where the rules allow, a father; in England and Wales an unmarried father who jointly registers the birth acquires parental responsibility by that act, and without the registration, an agreement or an order he has none, so the certificate that records a birth also decides who may consent to a school trip or a vaccination. Registration is not a formality appended to a family. It is the step that makes the family visible, and the state's categories arrive with it.

Most of what love does is absent from every form. It is the dressing changed at six in the morning, the appointment remembered, the food eaten because someone sat there until it was. In England and Wales the 2021 census counted 5.0 million unpaid carers, 9.0 per cent of residents aged five and over, against 11.4 per cent in 2011; the fall measures the census rather than need, because the question changed between the two counts and pandemic rules kept households apart. A health system that leans on this labour buys care at a price the carer pays, in hours and in a pension that will not be there. Carer's Allowance in England counts 35 hours of care a week and pays a weekly rate that works out well below the minimum wage for those hours.

From the first hour, a human infant depends on care that no statute can compel. A newborn cannot roll over or feed itself, does not walk for about a year, and stays dependent far longer than any other primate of comparable size. Cooperative breeding is the usual human arrangement: mothers, fathers, grandmothers, older siblings and neighbours take turns, and the post-menopausal decades that look wasteful under a model of individual fitness are one explanation for why the arrangement holds. Oxytocin belongs to this account, though the shorthand that names it the love hormone outruns the trials, which report effects small and uneven enough to keep the claim modest.

Reciprocity is a poor test of love, and care for the very old shows why. Dementia removes the person who could answer, and the spouse keeps arriving with the laundry; the arrangement continues on one side for years after it has stopped being a relationship in the ordinary sense. At the other end of a life the imbalance is plainer still, since an infant returns nothing for a long time and is loved anyway. A rule that looked for return on both sides would find little to recognize in either case, which is why the law settles for a formal category and lets it stand in for something it cannot measure.

Older than any ceremony is the bond the ceremony recognizes. Marriage entered the record as an instrument of property and legitimacy, a way of fixing which children inherited and which widow was provided for; the attachment was there before it and has continued outside the register as well. What marriage supplied was legibility, a certificate that told a landlord, a court, a priest and a hospital clerk which two people belonged together. For most of the centuries in which the institutions were built, that certificate was an accurate summary of how people lived.

Designation is the instrument that fits the present case, and several jurisdictions have it. A lasting power of attorney names the person who decides when capacity goes; an advance directive records the treatment wanted and refused; China's Civil Code lets an adult appoint a guardian in writing in advance; and in Scotland the Family Law (Scotland) Act 2006 permits a cohabitant to claim a share of an intestate estate, provided the claim is brought within six months of the death. France attaches a narrower set of consequences to its civil partnership, including a right to the shared home, and nothing from the estate without a will.

Each of these instruments depends on the paper reaching the ward. A directive left in a drawer, a proxy held by a solicitor or a copy in a surgery that closed at six changes nothing at three in the morning, and the staff fall back on the family in the room.

Death is where the two words meet most directly, because the loss registers in the survivor's body. Mortality rises in the months after a spouse dies, most sharply among older men, and settles back within a year or two; part of the excess is shared exposure, the same house and the same air, and part is the disappearance of the person who noticed that the medication had not been taken. Read as physiology, grief says that another presence had been holding something in place.

The week after a death is when the categories do their damage. A partner of twenty years may have no right to the flat, no share of the estate and, in some places, no right to arrange the funeral; the family that arrives from another city has all three. Grief then acquires an administrative layer, and the survivor learns that a relationship the neighbours recognized does not exist for the bank. Who may collect the body, keep the tenancy, draw on the joint account or open the laptop is settled by rules written before any of it happened.

Some losses fall between the entries on the list. Two widows who have shared a flat for fifteen years, dividing the bills and the hospital visits, are each other's closest attachment and, to the registrar, two unrelated names; the second death repeats the first one's paperwork with a stranger's name on it. Households of this kind are counted by the census and not by the statutes, and the count is rising while the statutes stay where they were.

A registry of affection would be a poor instrument, and nobody who has watched a family litigate a will would want one. Designation can be signed under pressure, and a form completed at twenty-five may name someone the signer no longer speaks to. The people with nobody to name stay where they were, since three households in ten hold one person. A form settles who may speak. It does not supply the person who sits through the night.

Some of the care that keeps a person alive is paid work, and a rule that recognizes a chosen person should cover the agency nurse and the home assistant as well. What the ward is trying to establish is whom the patient would have named, which is a question about the patient and not about the warmth of whoever is standing at the desk.

It can be written down while the person is well: a name, a signature, a date, a copy in the file. The ward's question then has an answer that does not depend on a clerk's reading of a family tree, and the person who will be believed is the one the patient chose.
