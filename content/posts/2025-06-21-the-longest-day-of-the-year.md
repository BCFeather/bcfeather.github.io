The solstice fell on 21 June, and in the northern part of the country the evening did not end until well past eight. This is a modest fact and it is the only day of the year when anyone notices it. For the rest of June, sunset drifts by a minute every few days, and by July it has started to retreat, and nobody marks either movement. On the solstice the movement stops, and for about a week the light stays where it is.

Why it can be hard to feel: the date is not centred in the season. Midsummer in the northern hemisphere is still three weeks away, and the hottest part of the year has not arrived. The astronomical event is about geometry rather than temperature. The earth's axis is tilted towards the sun as far as it goes, the sun climbs to its highest noon position, and then the whole arrangement reverses. The heat that follows is a separate process with its own lag, which is why the longest day is usually a pleasant one and the hottest day comes later.

Places with long evenings use them differently. In cities, the parks stay open later and stay fuller. The evening market opens around six and does its best trade at eight, when the light is flat and the heat has gone. Children are still outside at nine. Adults who work indoors go home in daylight for the first time since April and find the experience mildly disorienting.

Not everyone would call the solstice a good day. Anyone who lives far enough north to have a solstice also has a December in which the sun sets at four in the afternoon, and the long day in June settles a balance rather than offering a gift. The two seasons are two halves of one arrangement, and nobody who lives through both experiences them as separate events. Southern China has neither extreme. The day is longer than usual and the difference is modest, and the solstice passes there as an unremarkable Thursday or Saturday, in heat, without comment.

There is a version of midsummer that belongs to folklore rather than astronomy. Bonfires, maypoles, the Swedish and Finnish celebrations, the Chinese traditions attached to the sun and the harvest, the various excuses people have found over the centuries for staying up all night. Most of those have been detached from the farming calendar that produced them and survive as festivals, which is what happens to agricultural rituals in countries that no longer farm much.

What the day is good for now is something simpler. It is a marker, a point at which the year is at its maximum and about to start turning back, and it arrives without an advertisement attached to it. There is no obligation, no gift, no dinner reservation. The sun is up for a long time and the evening is warm, and if nothing else happens that is the whole of the ceremony.
