description: "A model fitted to some 2,600 coral reef sites in 44 ecoregions estimates that existing protection adds roughly 10 per cent to reef fish biomass relative to a counterfactual without it."
---

Marine protected areas are counted in square kilometres and judged by outcomes, and the two numbers have never been connected at a global scale. A paper in *PNAS* on 7 October 2024 does the connection for coral reefs, using a model that predicts how local conditions shape what protection achieves. Counting area is cheap and counting fish is expensive, which is why the two metrics have been reported separately for so long.

The data are about 2,600 reef sites across 44 ecoregions, each with its own fishing pressure, habitat and management regime. A predictive model of local conditions is fitted to those sites and then used to construct a counterfactual in which no protection exists. A counterfactual is the only way to attribute an outcome to a policy, since protected sites are not randomly assigned and the choice of site reflects its value.

The estimate is that existing protection adds roughly 10 per cent to reef fish biomass. That figure is a global average, and averages of this kind hide the variation that matters to a manager, since some sites gain far more and others gain nothing. Ten per cent is the average of a distribution, and a manager who needs to know what a new reserve will deliver needs the distribution rather than the average.

In the clinic, the method is what allows the average to be computed at all. Without a model that accounts for local conditions, a comparison between protected and unprotected sites confounds protection with the reasons a site was chosen for protection in the first place. Matching on habitat and depth removes part of the confounding that site selection introduces, and it does not remove all of it.

Assumptions carry the estimate. The counterfactual is modelled rather than observed, the biomass measure depends on survey methods that differ between programmes, and compliance with protection is not recorded in most of the data. Compliance is the variable that no global data set records, so the estimate is best read as what designation achieves rather than what enforcement achieves.

On the modelling side, the result still answers a question that has been asked repeatedly. Ten per cent is a modest return for the area that has been set aside, and it is consistent with the finding that many protected areas are paper parks in which fishing continues. A modest average is consistent with a literature that has found large gains in well-enforced reserves and negligible ones in reserves that exist only on paper.

Implications for policy are that expansion should be paired with enforcement. A site that is protected on a map and fished in practice contributes to the area total and little to the biomass, and the model can be used to identify where enforcement would buy the most. For the next assessment the value is a baseline, since a number can be compared with a later number, and a comparison is what a conservation budget needs. The policy conclusion is that the marginal reserve matters less than the marginal enforcement officer, which is an uncomfortable conclusion for a budget.
