description: "Russian drones and missiles struck the ports of Odesa overnight on 17 and 18 July, damaging grain terminals and stores, a day after Russia withdrew from the Black Sea grain initiative."

The explosions began shortly after midnight and continued for two hours. Russian forces attacked Odesa with Iranian-designed drones and cruise missiles in the first hours of 18 July, hitting port infrastructure, grain silos and warehouses within a few hundred metres of the waterfront. Ukrainian officials said sixty thousand tonnes of grain were destroyed, along with storage capacity that had been rebuilt after earlier strikes, and that air defences had brought down most but not all of what was launched.

The timing was the message. Russia had announced its withdrawal from the grain corridor the previous day, and the first attack on the ports that had served it came within hours. Ukrainian officials called it a deliberate attempt to close the corridor permanently rather than a military operation with a military purpose, and they pointed out that the targets were grain terminals rather than warships or barracks. Russia's defence ministry said the targets were military, an assertion that photographs of burning silos did little to support.

Odesa's economy had been rebuilt around the corridor. The city's terminals handled millions of tonnes a year, its port workers had returned after the early months of the war, and the shipping companies that insured and scheduled the voyages had only recently resumed normal business. Traders holding cargoes for August were told to expect delays nobody could price, and rail links into the port were cut in several places. The strike hit that machinery at the point where it is most expensive to repair: silos, loading equipment, the rail yards behind them, and the confidence of the traders who book the cargoes.

Damage to the city has accumulated alongside the damage to the port. Missiles have hit residential buildings, a cathedral in the historic centre was struck later in the month, and UNESCO has listed the centre as an endangered site. The Orthodox Transfiguration Cathedral, consecrated in the eighteenth century and rebuilt in the nineteenth, lost part of its roof, and the city's museum collections were moved again. Its bell tower had survived the Soviet campaign against the church, and it took a missile to open the roof beneath it.

Ukraine's response has been to promise a new corridor and to press its allies for more air defence. Its navy has no ships to speak of, its ports are within range of Russian missiles, and the insurance premiums for any voyage will reflect what happened on 18 July. Officials have said convoys will be escorted by military tugs and that foreign hulls will be welcome, a plan that depends on the confidence of the flag states. The agriculture ministry has said the harvest will be stored and moved by rail and by road, at a cost that the country can ill afford.

A day of explosions has undone a year of arrangement. The corridor's future will be decided by whether ships sail in the next weeks without a Russian signature, and by whether Ukraine's partners are willing to guarantee the hulls that do, none of which has been promised yet.
