title: "Care policy switches its yardstick from beds built to places filled"
date: "2026-11-24T10:35:00+08:00"
categories: [society]
description: "A plan approved by the State Council sets 2030 targets for elderly care and childcare that measure the nursing share of beds and the enrolment rate instead of the number built."
---

The plan published on 23 November sets three targets and each of them is a ratio: nursing beds at 73 per cent of the total in elderly care institutions, community facilities for older people covering 70 per cent, and an enrolment rate for children under three six percentage points above its 2025 level. That shift, from what is built to what is used, is the change worth noticing. The document was approved by the State Council and issued by the National Development and Reform Commission with the ministries for civil affairs, health and education.

Behind the ratios sits a baseline. At the end of 2025 the country had 395,000 elderly-care institutions and facilities holding 7.679 million beds, and 126,000 childcare institutions offering 6.66 million places, or 4.73 places per 1,000 people. County-level coverage of childcare had reached 98.8 per cent. Capacity counted in beds and places was no longer the binding constraint, and the plan is written as though its authors know that a ratio and a count answer different questions.

Its measures follow the same logic. Two thousand publicly run homes that were built long ago are to be renovated and refitted, in order to raise their standards of care and safety management, and townships are to gain regional service centres. Childcare is to be organized into a quarter-hour circle, with city-level service centres, kindergarten places converted for children of two and three, and half-day and hourly care for families that need less than a full week.

Ratio targets carry problems the document names only in part. An enrolment rate needs a denominator, and the plan gives the 2025 level as its base without publishing the figure, so six points cannot be converted into a number of children by any reader. A nursing share can rise by reclassifying beds as easily as by building them, and the safeguards against that are inspection and evaluation, which report on compliance instead of on care. A share met on paper can leave the residents who need nursing in homes that offer none.

Money, land and staff are the parts that decide whether the ratios move. The plan names central investment, local revenue and special-purpose bonds as the channels, and the county and village level as the destination, which is where the shortage of trained staff is sharpest. It also asks local governments to fold the targets into their own plans and project lists, a step that turns a national document into a budget line or leaves it on a shelf.

Statistics will be the test. They have counted beds and places for a decade, and the count will need to include who is in them, at what price and with what staff. A country with 7.679 million beds and 6.66 million childcare places can meet a coverage target without improving much; a nursing share and an enrolment rate take longer, and they show up in tables that record use rather than supply, which is a harder thing to move and a better thing to know.
