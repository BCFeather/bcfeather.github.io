---

Swiss National Bank cut its policy rate on the twenty-first of March to 1.5 per cent from 1.75, the first reduction since the bank began raising rates in 2022 and the first cut by a major central bank since the inflation surge of 2021. Decision was a surprise to most economists, who had expected the bank to wait until June, and it was announced with a statement that cut its inflation forecast for the year to 1.2 per cent from 1.8.

Justification given was arithmetic rather than drama. Swiss inflation had been below two per cent for eleven months, and the bank said that the strength of the franc, which had risen against the euro and the dollar, was doing part of the work of tightening monetary conditions. It also lowered its growth forecast and said that the economy would expand by about one per cent in 2024, which is weak for a country that has historically grown slowly anyway.

Appreciation of the franc has been a persistent constraint on the economy. Currency rose to its strongest level against the euro since 2015 in the months before the decision, which makes Swiss exports more expensive and reduces the revenue of the country's large pharmaceutical and machinery industries. Bank has intervened in the currency market in the past to hold the franc down, and it said its balance sheet had shrunk as it sold some of the foreign currency it had accumulated.

Timing relative to the European Central Bank is the interesting part. The euro area's inflation was falling more slowly, and the ECB was still signalling that it would wait until the summer. Switzerland trades heavily with the euro area, and a rate cut before the ECB's would tend to weaken the franc, which is one of the effects the bank wanted. Its policy rate is also much lower than the ECB's, so the gap between them narrowed rather than reversed.

Market consequence was a fall in the franc and in Swiss bond yields. Currency weakened by about one per cent against the euro within a day, and yields on ten-year government bonds fell, which is what the bank's rate cut would predict. Shares of Swiss exporters rose, since a weaker currency improves their reported earnings, and the country's main index reached a record in the following weeks.

What the twenty-first of March means is that the global cycle had turned. Central banks in emerging markets had already begun cutting in 2023, and the Bank of Japan was moving in the opposite direction, but a European central bank with a strong currency and low inflation was the first of the large ones to reduce rates. If inflation stays near target elsewhere, others will follow, and the question is whether they will move as early as the data would allow. The Swiss decision suggested that a central bank confident about its currency can act before its neighbours, which is a freedom that larger economies do not have.
