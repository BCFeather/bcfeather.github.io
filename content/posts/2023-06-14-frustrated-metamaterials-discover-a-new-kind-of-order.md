description: "A mechanical metamaterial whose elements cannot all satisfy their preferred orientation develops a non-orientable order, and its response depends on the order of operations."
---

Frustration is a familiar idea in magnetism, where spins that cannot all align produce a state with no simple pattern. A mechanical analogue has now been built, and its behaviour is more peculiar than the magnetic version. In *Nature* on 14 June 2023, that analogue is described. The authors construct a lattice of hinged units whose orientations are coupled, so a unit's preferred angle conflicts with its neighbours', and the conflict cannot be resolved everywhere at once.

Non-orientable order is what emerges. A path carrying a local frame around a closed loop returns it rotated, which means the lattice has no globally consistent assignment of orientations, and experiment and simulation both demonstrate it. The hinge is the essential part, since a joint resisting rotation stores energy while one allowing it transfers the frustration to its neighbours, and a compliant hinge lets the lattice relax without breaking.

History-dependent response follows. A sequence of deformations does not commute with another, and the material's final shape records the path producing it rather than only the final load. Energy landscape underlies the picture, because a frustrated lattice has many configurations of similar energy and a path between them crosses a barrier set by hinge compliance.

Measurement supplies the demonstration. Markers on the lattice are tracked through a cycle, and the rotated frame appears in reconstructed positions. Readout is optical, since a camera records the markers and a reconstruction gives each unit's orientation at every step, and a lattice that could not be observed would be a model without a test.

Topological classification is the theory. A lattice's frustration can be described by how frames transform around loops, and that classification predicts which configurations are possible. Geometry is the platform, because the unit can be changed and a family of lattices with different loop rotations follows from one principle.

Mechanical memory is the application. A material recording the order of its deformations stores a sequence in its shape, which is memory without electronics. Origami offers the comparison, since a folded pattern's shape is determined by its creases while this material's shape is determined by its path, which is a looser relation between structure and form.

Scale sets the limitation, as the lattice is a tabletop object made by printing or assembly, and bulk manufacture would need a different process. Robustness stays unestablished, because a lattice with defects may lose the non-orientability the ideal structure has, and a real material always has defects. Mechanics gains a concept borrowed and returned, since frustration came from condensed matter and the metamaterial shows it has a mechanical expression with phenomena of its own. A lattice with a deliberately placed defect would show how the non-orientability survives disorder, and the answer decides whether the concept reaches materials that anyone can make. A cyclic loading test would show whether the memory survives repeated use or fades as the hinges fatigue, and fatigue is the failure mode a device would meet first.
