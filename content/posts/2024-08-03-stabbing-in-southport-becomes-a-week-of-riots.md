Three children were killed and several others injured at a dance class in Southport on 29 July, and within days the town and then a dozen others across England were the scene of riots in which shops were burned and police were attacked. The violence began with a demonstration outside a mosque and spread through social media. The attack took place at a holiday club for children, which is the detail that made the story travel beyond the town.

Misinformation is the mechanism. A name and a set of claims about the attacker circulated before the police had confirmed anything, and the claims were repeated by accounts that had built audiences on the same kind of content. A rumour that arrives before the facts fills the space that the facts would occupy. The suspect was a teenager who had been born in Britain, and the correction was published by the police within a day and travelled a fraction as far as the rumour.

Policing was the second issue. Forces that had been reduced in numbers over a decade were deployed to protect mosques and hotels housing asylum seekers, and the disturbances moved faster than the units could. A service that has lost a fifth of its officers since 2010 is a service that responds to the loudest incident and not to the pattern. Officers who were injured in the disturbances numbered more than a hundred, and the courts sat through the night to process the arrests.

Politics was immediate. The government described the riots as criminality and promised that those who had travelled to join them would be prosecuted, and the opposition argued that the sentences were being handed down before the cases were heard. A disorder that becomes a sentencing argument is a disorder that has entered the political system. The government had been in office for five weeks and had promised to restore confidence in the police, which made the disorder its first test.

Social media companies were named by the regulator. The platforms were asked to explain how the false claims had spread, and the answer that the content was permitted under their rules was not one that a government facing a week of arson wanted to hear. Moderation is a policy that a platform sets in advance and cannot adjust during an emergency. The companies argued that the content was posted by users and that removing it at speed risks removing the truth as well, an argument that the regulator rejected.

What the week shows is that a community's information environment is part of its security. A town that believes a rumour about its neighbours is a town that is one demonstration away from a riot, and the correction arrives after the damage. The repair bill for the shops that were burned will be paid partly by insurers and partly by a fund that the government announced within a month.
