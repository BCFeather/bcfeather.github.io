Engineers at the Joint European Torus in Culham, south of Oxford, reported on 9 February that their machine had produced 59 megajoules of energy from a fusion reaction and held it for five seconds. The experiment used a fuel of deuterium and tritium, the mixture that a power station would burn, and the power released averaged about 11 megawatts over the five seconds. The previous record, set in 1997 by the same machine, was 21.7 megajoules.

Fusion is the reaction that lights the sun, and it needs temperatures of about 150 million degrees to happen on earth. At Culham the fuel sits inside a doughnut-shaped magnetic field, a tokamak, and the field holds a plasma that no material container could touch. Heating comes from radio waves and beams of particles, and the whole event lasts seconds because the magnets and the walls can take only so much. A tokamak that ran for an hour would need superconducting coils cooled to a few degrees above absolute zero and a way to remove the helium that the reaction produces.

The result matters because of what it says about the fuel. Tritium is radioactive and scarce, and keeping a plasma stable with it is harder than with deuterium alone, which is what earlier experiments mostly used. The team showed that the tritium survived the reaction, that the walls of the vessel coped with the neutron flux, and that the heating systems could be tuned to a mixture rather than a single gas.

Nobody at Culham claimed that fusion was close to producing electricity. The heat leaves the plasma as neutrons, and the walls of the vessel absorb it and are cooled by water, and no turbine turns that warmth into current. The heating systems consume far more electricity than the reaction returns, and the machine is a research instrument rather than the front end of a power station. The number to watch is the ratio of output to input, and this experiment stayed far below one.

ITER, the international reactor under construction at Cadarache in France, is designed to reach that ratio and to sustain a plasma for minutes rather than seconds. Its first experiments are expected in the middle of this decade, and the design borrowed heavily from what Culham learned, including the shape of the field and the materials that face the plasma. Britain's exit from the European fusion programme left the future of the Culham site open, and the government has since promised a domestic programme of its own.

What the five seconds establish is that the physics scales. A larger machine with better magnets should hold a hotter plasma for longer, and the private companies that have raised money for smaller tokamaks read the result as evidence that their models are worth building. Electricity from fusion remains a promise without a date, and the step that would turn it into one is a machine that holds the plasma for minutes rather than seconds. The people who make that promise now have a measured number behind them.
