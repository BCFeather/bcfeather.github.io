Central banks describe the end of a tightening cycle as a pivot, and the word hides a decision that is genuinely difficult. A pause says that policy is restrictive enough for now; a pivot says that the next move will be a cut. The distance between the two is an estimate of the neutral rate, the level at which policy neither restrains nor stimulates, and that estimate is not observable. The estimate is a range, published with wide confidence bands, and committees disagree about it without saying so in public.

The standard framework compares a policy rate with inflation and with the output gap. A real policy rate above the neutral real rate is restrictive, and the question is how far above it policy should sit and for how long. Estimates of the neutral rate have moved a great deal since 2008, and the pandemic changed the components that feed them, from the saving rate to the investment demand for AI infrastructure. A central bank that responds to an unreliable series will overreact to noise, and one that ignores it will lag a turning point.

Data arrive with a lag that matters. Inflation is measured monthly and revised, employment is measured monthly and revised twice, and GDP is measured quarterly and revised for years. A central bank that waits for confirmation before cutting will cut late, and one that cuts on the first sign of weakness may cut into an inflation that has not finished falling.

History offers two templates. The 1995 and 1998 cuts followed pauses that lasted months and were followed by continued expansion, and both were justified by a slowdown in demand rather than by a fall in inflation. The 2001 and 2007 cuts were rapid, and both came after the labour market had already turned. The difference between the two cases is not the speed of the response but the state of private balance sheets when it arrived. Household debt service ratios matter more than the level of rates, because a mortgage fixed at three per cent does not reprice when the policy rate moves.

Balance sheet policy adds a second instrument that complicates the signal. A central bank that is shrinking its holdings is tightening even while it holds the policy rate, and a central bank that stops shrinking is loosening at the margin. Markets read the two instruments together, and the combination has produced episodes where the rate was unchanged and financial conditions moved. Quantitative tightening has been run in the background, and its effect on long-term yields is smaller than the effect on bank reserves.

The practical lesson for investors is that the terminal rate is a range rather than a number. Policy that sits at the top of that range for a year produces a different economy from policy that sits at the bottom, and neither is visible in the announcement. What is visible is the language, and the language has been deliberately vague because the committee itself does not know where the neutral rate is. Language that commits to a path is a promise the committee may have to break, which is why guidance has become shorter.
