Mitochondria fuse and divide constantly, and the textbook account of fusion is a story about proteins: mitofusins on the outer membrane, OPA1 on the inner one, and cardiolipin in the membrane where the two organelles meet. A paper in *Science* on 8 October adds a third party to that account. Sho Aki, Mayuko Segawa and colleagues in Julien Prudent's laboratory at Cambridge report that vesicles derived from the Golgi, carrying the lipid phosphatidylinositol 3,4-bisphosphate, are recruited to fusion sites and are required for the membranes to merge.

Lipids have been the neglected half of the problem. A membrane cannot fuse unless its composition allows the two bilayers to bend and meet, and the enzymes that remodel lipids sit in the membranes rather than in the cytosol. Phosphoinositides are the signalling lipids that mark which membrane is which, present in small amounts and turned over quickly, and \(\mathrm{PI}(3,4)P_2\) had been associated with endosomes and with the later stages of endocytosis rather than with mitochondria.

The finding is about where the vesicles go. Golgi-derived carriers reach sites on the mitochondrial surface that are defined by the mitofusins and by contact with the endoplasmic reticulum, and the arrival of the lipid precedes fusion. Blocking the vesicles, or removing the lipid from them, leaves the mitofusins in place and the membranes close together but stops the merge, which separates the machinery that holds mitochondria together from the chemistry that actually joins them.

That separation is the conceptual gain. Fusion has been studied as a protein-driven process with a lipid garnish; here the lipid is the limiting component, delivered from a different organelle by a vesicle that has its own trafficking rules. Mitochondrial dynamics therefore inherit the state of the secretory pathway: a fragmented Golgi, which happens in mitosis and in several diseases, changes the supply of the lipid and therefore the rate at which the mitochondrial network remodels. That coupling had not been on the field's diagram, and it fits observations that had no explanation: cells with normal fusion proteins sometimes fail to rebuild a mitochondrial network after stress.

Diseases of mitochondrial dynamics are usually blamed on the fusion proteins, and the genes behind them are indeed the mitofusins and OPA1. If lipid delivery is a second, independent input, then some patients whose fusion proteins are intact may still have a fusion defect, and the secretory pathway becomes a place to look. The same logic applies to neurons, where mitochondria travel long distances, have to fuse at specific places, and are supplied by a secretory pathway that is itself under strain in several neurodegenerative diseases.

Work in cultured cells is where lipid traffic is easiest to manipulate and least representative, and that is the main limit of the paper. Which effector reads the \(\mathrm{PI}(3,4)P_2\) at the fusion site, whether the vesicles carry other lipids that matter equally, and how the Golgi decides when to send them are the questions the next papers will answer. What the result already does is remove a simplifying assumption from the field's standard diagram.
