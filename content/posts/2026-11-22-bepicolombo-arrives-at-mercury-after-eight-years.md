BepiColombo reached Mercury on 21 November, when the planet's gravity captured the pair of orbiters that had been flying towards it since October 2018. The mission's operators confirmed contact with the spacecraft a day later, and the joint project of the European Space Agency and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency marked the end of an eight-year cruise.

The mission carries two spacecraft stacked on a transfer module. The Mercury Planetary Orbiter, built by ESA, will map the surface and measure its composition. Mio, built by JAXA, will study the magnetic field and the thin magnetosphere, the part of Mercury's environment that has puzzled physicists since Mariner 10 found a field around a planet too small and too slowly rotating to explain one. The transfer module that carried them across the inner solar system separated on 3 September after the last trajectory correction.

Arrival used a technique that relies on Mercury as much as on the engines. The composite spacecraft flew a weak stability boundary approach, letting the planet's gravity do most of the work of capture, then fired chemical thrusters to close the orbit. The Planetary Orbiter will settle into a polar orbit running from about 480 kilometres at the closest point to 1,500 at the farthest; Mio takes a higher orbit with a longer period.

The orbiters separate on 10 December, after which Mio jettisons its sunshield on 16 December. The Planetary Orbiter reaches its final orbit in March 2027, and routine science begins in the spring, for a primary mission of one Earth year with an option to extend. Until then the instruments run through commissioning and calibration, which for a mission this late in its cruise is a short list compared with the years already spent in flight.

Mercury is the hardest of the inner planets to reach, a fact that surprises people who notice how close it is to the Sun. A spacecraft falling towards the Sun gains so much speed that it must shed most of it to enter orbit, and the planet is small and its gravity weak, so the braking has to be spread across flybys and ion thrust. ESA describes the journey as harder than a trip to Saturn, and only two spacecraft have been there before, Mariner 10 on three flybys in the 1970s and MESSENGER, which orbited the planet from 2011 to 2015.

The questions waiting are concrete. Radar found bright deposits at the poles that appear to be water ice in craters never touched by sunlight, on a world where the surface reaches several hundred degrees. Mercury's hollows, shallow bright depressions that look fresh, have no agreed explanation. The planet's iron core fills most of its volume, and the ratio of core to rock is the largest in the solar system, which makes it the natural test case for theories about how the inner planets assembled and what stripped its outer layers.

BepiColombo's answer to those questions will arrive slowly, as orbital missions do, in maps, spectra and field measurements accumulated over a year. The mission is named for Giuseppe Colombo, the engineer who worked out the gravity-assist trajectory that Mariner 10 used and who later pressed for a Mercury orbiter. Fifty years after that first flyby, the third mission to the planet is in position and waiting for its instruments to be switched on.
