Airborne transmission of influenza has been accepted for decades and measured badly. The standard evidence comes from outbreaks in rooms and from experiments with animals, and the missing measurement is the one that matters: how much infectious virus an infected person actually puts into the air, and how much that varies from person to person.

A study published in *Cell* on 19 March supplies it. The Emory-led team, with Seema Lakdawala as senior author, ran a controlled human infection study, infected volunteers with influenza in a contained ward, and sampled the air they exhaled with a purpose-built collector. The collector captured live virus and not viral RNA, which is the difference between measuring particles and measuring infectious particles.

Variation was the finding. Some participants expelled orders of magnitude more infectious virus than others, and the spread across a dozen volunteers covered a range that no animal model reproduces, which is the pattern that outbreak epidemiology calls superspreading and that individual-level studies usually miss. A tenfold difference changes the arithmetic of a ward, and a thousandfold difference changes the arithmetic of a pandemic.

The second finding is more useful clinically. Nasal viral load did not predict how much infectious virus was expelled into the air, which means the measurement clinicians use to gauge how contagious a patient is does not measure contagiousness. A patient with a high nasal load and little shedding is less of a hazard than a patient with a moderate load and a cough that produces fine aerosol.

Symptoms tracked the shedding loosely. The people who expelled the most virus were not reliably the sickest, and the timing of peak shedding did not match the timing of peak symptoms in every participant. A transmission model driven by symptom onset therefore misplaces the infectious period in a fraction of cases, which is the fraction that drives outbreaks.

The genetics add a dimension. The team traced the diversity of viral genomes from the respiratory tract into the air and found that the population leaving a person is not a sample of the population inside the person. Some variants are filtered out on the way, which means the virus that a contact receives is a selected subset and not a random draw.

Controlled infection studies are small and artificial, and this one is no exception. Volunteers are young and healthy, the dose and the strain are fixed, and the ward is not a bus. The strengths that compensate are that the timing is known exactly, the sampling is repeated, and the measurement is of infectious virus and not of RNA. What the study supports is a shift in emphasis from the amount of virus to the kind of breathing that carries it. Measuring what people exhale is difficult, and it is the measurement that a hospital, a school or a pandemic response actually needs. The equipment now exists, and the argument for using it is no longer theoretical.
