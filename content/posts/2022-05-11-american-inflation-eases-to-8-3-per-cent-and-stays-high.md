Consumer prices in the United States rose 8.3 per cent in the year to April, the labour department reported on 11 May, down from 8.5 per cent in March and the first decline in the annual rate since August. Measured against the previous month, prices rose three tenths of one per cent, which annualizes to a pace that would keep inflation above the target for a long time. Strip out food and energy, the two categories that move on their own schedules, and core prices were 6.2 per cent higher than a year earlier.

Almost all of the improvement came from petrol. The average price of a gallon of unleaded had passed four dollars in March and retreated during April as crude oil fell back from the peaks reached after the invasion of Ukraine, and used cars, which had been the emblem of the pandemic inflation, finally began to fall in price. Food, rent, airline tickets and hospital services all kept climbing, and the shelter category, which lags actual rents by months, recorded its fastest monthly rise in years.

The White House described the figure as unacceptably high and pointed to the decline as evidence that the peak had passed. Republicans described the same number as proof that the administration had lost control of prices, and both were reading a statistic that had already been overtaken by events: petrol prices began climbing again in the second week of May and set records through the rest of the month, which meant the May reading would likely reverse the April improvement.

For the Federal Reserve the report settled nothing. The committee had raised rates by half a point a week earlier and had signalled more of the same in June and July, and core inflation at 6.2 per cent is the number that decides whether those moves were enough. Wages were rising at about five and a half per cent, productivity had fallen, and unit labour costs were climbing at a rate that firms could only absorb by passing it on to customers.

Inflation of this kind is slow to leave a service economy. Goods prices had begun to normalize as supply chains untangled and consumers shifted spending back towards services, while services prices depend on wages and on rents, and both move with contracts signed months earlier. The categories still rising fastest in April were the ones with the longest lags: rent, insurance, tuition, and the imputed cost of housing that the index calculates rather than observes.

Political arithmetic is simpler than the economic one. Prices were the issue that voters named first in every survey of the year, the president's approval had fallen with them, and a midterm election was six months away. A single tenth of a percentage point in the annual rate was being read as a turning point by one party and as a rounding error by the other, and the petrol stations that set the numbers on their signs were the most reliable poll of the summer.
