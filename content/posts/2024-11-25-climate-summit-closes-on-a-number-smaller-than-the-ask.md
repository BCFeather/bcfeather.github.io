Baku's conference ended in the early hours of 24 November with an agreement that developed countries would provide at least three hundred billion dollars a year by 2035, and with a second figure of one and a third trillion described as an aspiration for all sources. The recipients had asked for more than a trillion in public money and called the result inadequate. A conference that runs over its schedule is a conference whose closing text was written under pressure. A deal reached on the final morning is a deal that the presidency wrote and the plenary accepted.

Structure of the deal makes the number ambiguous. The three hundred billion counts public money and money mobilized by public guarantees, and the larger figure counts private investment that is expected to follow. A recipient that has to borrow at market rates is not helped by a target that treats a loan as a transfer. A guarantee that mobilizes private money is a guarantee that may never be called. A fiscal transfer and a commercial loan are both counted, and the difference is the burden on the recipient's budget.

Politics of the negotiation were about who counts as developed. A group of large emerging economies objected to being asked to contribute, and the text that emerged keeps the obligation with the historical emitters while inviting others to give voluntarily. That compromise is the only one available and also the one that leaves the pool smaller than the need. A group that is asked to contribute and refuses is a group that has the votes to block a stronger text.

Carbon market rules were the other outcome. A mechanism that allows a reduction in one country to be bought by another had been waiting for a text since the Paris agreement, and the rules agreed here create a registry and a process for authorizing transfers. A market that works is a market that shifts investment to where a tonne is cheapest to avoid. A registry that tracks a transfer is a registry that makes double counting visible.

Credibility of the number depends on delivery. The previous pledge of a hundred billion a year was met two years late, and the shortfall was documented by an independent panel. A promise that has missed its date once is a promise that the next round of negotiations will discount. A panel that documented a shortfall is a panel that gave the next negotiation its opening argument. A recipient that has been promised money and received a loan is a recipient that has learned to read the annexes.

What the summit produced is a floor rather than a solution. A floor is enough to keep the process alive and not enough to fund the adaptation that the countries with the least capacity need, and the argument about the difference will define the next conference. A floor that keeps the process alive is a floor that the next presidency will try to raise.
