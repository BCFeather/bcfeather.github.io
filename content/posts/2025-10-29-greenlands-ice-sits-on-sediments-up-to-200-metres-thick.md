What an ice sheet slides on decides how fast it flows, and the bed beneath Greenland is a patchwork of hard rock and softer material that has never been mapped directly. A soft, deformable bed speeds sliding; a rigid one slows it. Radar sees the ice but struggles at the interface, and drilling gives a point measurement at a cost that makes a grid impossible, so the bed has stayed one of the least known surfaces in the northern hemisphere. Soft beds let the ice slide on a deforming layer, which is why the question matters for sea level.

*Geology* published a seismic analysis on 28 October 2025. The authors applied the receiver function technique to decades of recordings from stations on and around the ice sheet. That method was developed for earthquakes and has been used to image the crust, and applying it to the ice sheet base turns old records into a survey that no campaign could afford to repeat. Receiver functions had been used on ice before at isolated stations; the contribution here is a systematic application to an archive rather than a new deployment.

Compared with synthetics computed for ice resting on hard bedrock, the observed receiver functions show a consistent delay in the converted arrival. A delay of that kind indicates a low-velocity layer between the ice and the rock, and the authors interpret it as sediment up to about 200 metres thick. The layer appears widely but not uniformly, so the bed is heterogeneous at the scale of the station spacing. Two hundred metres is the upper end of the interpretation, and most of the delays imply less, so the layer is thinner than the headline figure almost everywhere it appears.

The physics is a conversion. A compressional wave arriving at an interface produces a shear wave whose travel time depends on the thickness and on the velocity contrast, so a delay maps to a layer of given properties. Because the ice is well characterized, the unknown is the material below it, and sediments have the low shear velocity that produces the delay the authors see. A lower shear velocity in the sediment than in the ice or the rock produces the delay, and the size of the delay then scales with thickness.

Resolution limits the claim. Receiver functions average over a region around each station, so a thin layer and a patchy one can produce a similar signal, and the velocity of the sediment is assumed rather than measured. The stations are sparse and concentrated where logistics allowed them, which leaves interior basins sampled thinly, and no drill core confirms the interpretation at any site. An assumed velocity also means an assumed thickness, since the two trade against each other in the conversion.

Two checks would strengthen it. A denser array across a suspected basin would test whether the delay varies at the scale the interpretation implies, and combining the seismic result with radar that penetrates the interface would constrain thickness independently. A core would settle composition, and that matters because till and marine clay deform very differently under an ice sheet. Radar that penetrates the interface has been used on ice shelves, where the geometry is simpler, and extending it to the bed is the natural second measurement.
