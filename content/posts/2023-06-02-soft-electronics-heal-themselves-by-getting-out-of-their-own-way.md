description: "Multilayer soft electronic devices made from immiscible dynamic polymers align and repair themselves after damage, an approach that keeps stacked functional layers working."
---

A soft device is a stack of layers with different jobs: a conductor, a dielectric, a sensor. Each layer has to bond to its neighbours and keep its own properties, and the two requirements pull against each other. In *Science* on 2 June 2023, the conflict is resolved by refusing to mix. The authors build each layer from a dynamic polymer that heals through reversible bonds, and choose chemistries so adjacent layers are immiscible and therefore do not blur into one another.

Healing is autonomous. A cut closes when the pieces are brought together, because reversible bonds re-form across the interface without heat or solvent, and the process works at room temperature. Immiscibility is a thermodynamic choice, since two polymers with different solubility parameters separate, and the useful pair bonds at an interface while resisting interdiffusion.

Alignment is the second property. A damaged multilayer stack can misregister, and a device whose layers slide out of position fails even if every layer heals. Function is what makes the multilayer case hard, because a single healing layer is a materials problem while a stack is a device problem, and the healing has to preserve the registration the device depends on.

Chemistry drives the design. A dynamic bond exchanging quickly heals quickly and creeps under load, while one exchanging slowly is stable and slow to repair, so the selection is a compromise the application dictates. The bonds themselves are supramolecular, since hydrogen bonds or metal-ligand contacts that break and re-form give the network mobility, and that mobility lets a crack's faces find each other.

Electrical test supplies the demonstration. A healed device conducts and holds a dielectric layer's insulation, and recovery is measured as a fraction of the original performance. Optics follows, because a soft display or a sensor window needs transparency, and immiscibility keeps a healed interface from scattering light. Mechanics carries the cost, since a healed interface is weaker than the original and repeated damage accumulates a loss the paper quantifies. Environment sets the limitation, because a device in air heals differently from one in water, and an implantable device would face both.

A product is what the paper does not show, since a laboratory device healing under a microscope is a demonstration, and a manufacturing route keeping layers immiscible is the next problem. Soft robotics gains a materials strategy, because a robot that can be cut and keep working can go where repair is impossible, and the strategy is a set of rules rather than a single material.

Hydrogels offer the comparison, since a gel heals because it is mostly water and its chains are mobile, while an electronic device needs dry layers with electronic function, and these polymers are dry and still mobile. A cyclic test would separate the chemistry's contribution from the geometry's, since a device cut and healed in the same place tests the bond while one damaged in a new place tests the registration.
