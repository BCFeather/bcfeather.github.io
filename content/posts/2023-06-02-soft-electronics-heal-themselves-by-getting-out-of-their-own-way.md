description: "Multilayer soft electronic devices made from immiscible dynamic polymers align and repair themselves after damage, an approach reported in *Science* on 2 June 2023 that keeps stacked functional layers working."
---

A soft device is a stack of layers with different jobs: a conductor, a dielectric, a sensor. Each layer has to bond to its neighbours and keep its own properties, and the two requirements pull against each other.

The polymers in *Science*, 2 June 2023 resolve the conflict by refusing to mix. The authors build each layer from a dynamic polymer that heals through reversible bonds, and they choose the chemistries so that adjacent layers are immiscible and therefore do not blur into one another.

The healing is autonomous. A cut closes when the pieces are brought together, because the reversible bonds re-form across the interface without heat or solvent, and the authors show that the process works at room temperature.

The alignment is the second property. A multilayer stack that is damaged can misregister, and a device whose layers slide out of position is a device that fails even if each layer heals.

The chemistry is the design. A dynamic bond that exchanges quickly heals quickly and creeps under load, and one that exchanges slowly is stable and slow to repair. The authors' selection is a compromise that the application dictates.

The electrical test is the demonstration. A healed device conducts and holds a dielectric layer's insulation, and the recovery is measured as a fraction of the original performance.

The optical application follows. A soft display or a sensor's window needs transparency, and the polymers' immiscibility keeps a healed interface from scattering light.

The mechanical cost is the caveat. A healed interface is weaker than the original, and repeated damage accumulates a loss that the paper quantifies.

The limitation is the environment. A device in air heals differently from a device in water, and an implantable device would face both.

What the paper does not show is a product. A laboratory device that heals under a microscope is a demonstration, and a manufacturing route that keeps the layers immiscible is the next problem.

For soft robotics, the value is a materials strategy. A robot that can be cut and continue is a robot that can work in places where repair is impossible, and the strategy is a set of rules rather than a single material.

The immiscibility is a thermodynamic choice. Two polymers with different solubility parameters separate, and a pair whose parameters are close enough to bond at an interface but far enough to resist interdiffusion is the pair the authors are looking for.

The dynamic bond is a supramolecular one. Hydrogen bonds or metal-ligand contacts that break and re-form give the network its mobility, and the network's mobility is what allows a crack's faces to find each other.

The stack's function is what makes the multilayer case hard. A single healing layer is a materials problem, and a stack is a device problem, because the healing has to preserve the registration that the device's function depends on.

The comparison with self-healing hydrogels is instructive. A gel heals because it is mostly water and the chains are mobile, and an electronic device needs dry layers with electronic function. The authors' polymers are dry and still mobile.
