One month does not make a trend, and the May inflation release was the first month in which the April jump looked mechanical. Consumer prices rose 3.4 per cent on the year, down from 3.5 per cent, and 0.2 per cent on the month, and the annual rate stayed well above the 2 per cent target while moving in the right direction.

Services are the number that matters. Services inflation went from 5.4 per cent to 4.7 per cent, back to where it had been in March, as airfares and package holidays gave back the Easter distortion that had inflated April. A rate that returns to its March level is a rate that has not fallen, and a board that wants to cut has to explain why it will.

Core inflation is the puzzle. The measure excluding energy, food, alcohol and tobacco was 3.5 per cent, higher than the headline for the first time in years, because the regulated prices that pushed the headline up are excluded and the services that pushed core up are not. A core rate above the headline is a warning about domestically generated inflation.

Goods were the disinflationary part. Goods inflation was 2.1 per cent, up from 1.7 per cent, and core goods 1.6 per cent, which is where a weaker pound and tariff-affected supply chains would show first. A goods rate that stays near 2 per cent while services stay near 5 is the shape of an economy with a labour shortage and no spare capacity in the sectors that set prices.

The Bank of England had expected the fall. Its May report had projected a peak around 3.5 per cent in the third quarter, and the peak arrived early and left early, which is a better outcome than the reverse. The committee's August meeting is the next opportunity, and the vote in June had already shown three members willing to cut.

Wages are the last support for the hawks. Private sector regular pay growth had slowed to about 5 per cent, vacancies had fallen below their pre-pandemic level, and the unemployment rate had drifted up, all of which point to a labour market that is loosening without breaking. A loosening labour market is the condition under which services inflation can fall.

What would settle the argument is whether services inflation holds below 5 per cent for three prints. A single month can be an airfare; three months is a labour market, and the board has said so in every statement since February. A core rate above the headline is the statistical signature of a country whose regulated prices and whose service wages are moving in opposite directions. What would make the autumn easier is a wage settlement round in the public sector that comes in below the private one, and the pay review bodies report in July. Whether the August meeting cuts will depend on a single services print that arrives in the middle of the holiday season, when airfares behave badly.
