Vaccination reduces the severity of the acute illness, and the question that followed was whether it also reduces the chance of sequelae. Answering it required a cohort large enough to measure events that occur in a small fraction of a mostly mild population.

Data from the US Department of Veterans Affairs assembled 33,940 people with a breakthrough infection and three control groups: contemporary, historical and vaccinated people without infection. The paper appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 25 May 2022. A database that covers hospital, pharmacy and laboratory records for a national system is the kind of source that can count events in a population of millions, and the trade is that the diagnoses are codes rather than adjudicated findings.

At six months after infection, and beyond the first 30 days of illness, people with a breakthrough infection had a higher risk of death than contemporary controls, a hazard ratio of 1.75, and a higher risk of incident post-acute sequelae, a hazard ratio of 1.50. The excess spanned cardiovascular, coagulation, gastrointestinal, kidney, mental health, metabolic, musculoskeletal and neurological disorders. A hazard ratio of 1.50 describes an excess spread across organ systems, with no single organ carrying the burden, and that pattern is the signature of a systemic insult and not of one damaged organ.

Compared with 113,474 people who were infected without having been vaccinated, those with a breakthrough infection had lower risks of death and of sequelae, with hazard ratios of 0.66 and 0.85. Vaccination therefore reduces the post-acute burden without eliminating it, and the two comparisons answer different questions.

A patient wants the comparison with the unvaccinated group, while a health system wants the comparison with the uninfected controls. Both are reported, and the two are not interchangeable. Age and comorbidity also matter here, because a veteran population is older and sicker than the general one, so the absolute risks are higher than a young and healthy cohort would experience. A comparison with uninfected controls captures the whole burden of the infection, including the acute phase, and a comparison with unvaccinated cases isolates the effect of vaccination.

Residual confounding remains a real concern, because people who vaccinate differ from those who do not in ways that statistical adjustment cannot fully capture. The contemporary and vaccinated control groups go some way towards it, and the consistency of the result across the three comparisons is the reassurance.

Vaccination before infection confers partial protection in the post-acute phase, which cannot be the only mitigation strategy. A health system that relies on vaccines alone will still need clinics for the sequelae, and those clinics are the part of the response that most systems have not built. Building them takes staff who are already scarce, and the shortage will outlast the pandemic that created the demand. A cohort defined by attendance at a health system is a cohort defined by access, and the people who never came to the system are absent from both the cases and the controls.
