A magnetic storm is a disturbance of the Earth's field that lasts for hours to days, and it is driven by the solar wind. Two sources matter: a coronal mass ejection drives a storm with a shock, and a fast stream from a coronal hole drives a storm by compressing the field ahead of it. The distinction is what a statistician has to separate. Separating them matters because the two sources respond differently to solar activity, and a trend in one is not a trend in the other.

Work posted to arXiv on 1 December 2022 separates them across the space age, from 1957 to 2021, using two storm indices. The counts come to 2,743 storms in one index and 2,526 in the other, and the difference is the part of the record that each index sees. Splitting by strength gives 45 per cent weak, 40 per cent moderate, 12 per cent intense and 3 per cent major. The split by strength is what makes the record comparable across cycles, since a weak storm and a major storm are different events.

Decline is the headline. Storm numbers follow the slow decrease of sunspot activity, and the solar magnetic structure changed with it, which is a statement about the field rather than about the spots. The authors quantify the relation between sunspots and ejection-driven storms over the five cycles of the space age. A sunspot number is a proxy for the field, and the proxy is what the field does, so a trend in the number is a trend in the structure behind it.

The current sheet is the mechanism. Solar polar fields tilt the heliospheric current sheet, and stronger polar fields make it thinner, so a fast stream meets a sharper boundary. Polar fields peaked in cycle 22, the sheet was exceptionally thin, and the 1990s produced a record of large stream-driven storms. Thinner means the field reverses over a shorter distance, and the reversal is what a fast stream crosses in a few hours.

The widening came later. Weak sunspot activity since cycle 23 weakened the polar fields and widened the sheet, which lowered the count of large and moderate stream-driven storms. A wide sheet also means the Earth sits in slow wind more often, and the paper puts the fraction at half the time since cycle 23. Wind speed is the variable a spacecraft measures directly, and the width of the sheet is what a model has to infer from it.

What the study does not do is attribute any single storm to a cause, since the indices are global and the drivers are not resolved storm by storm. A reader should treat the shares as a climatology rather than as a forecast. Forty years is long enough to be a climate and short enough to still be a record, and the paper treats it as both.

A climatology of this kind matters for anyone who designs for the radiation environment, because the environment a satellite meets is the average of these storms rather than their worst case. An engineer who multiplies the average by the length of a mission gets the fluence, and the fluence is what damages a solar cell.
