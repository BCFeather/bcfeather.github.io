The European Council granted Ukraine and Moldova the status of candidate countries on 23 June, at a summit in Brussels that had been preceded by weeks of argument about whether the gesture was premature. Georgia was offered what the conclusions called a European perspective, with the conditions that would allow it to apply. Heads of government described the decision as historic, and the word was accurate in the narrow sense that the union had never before opened accession to a country at war.

Status carries procedure rather than membership. A candidate must adopt the union's body of law, which runs to tens of thousands of pages, and must satisfy criteria on democracy, courts, corruption and market regulation. The European Commission then assesses progress and recommends whether to open negotiations, and each of the twenty-seven governments must agree at every stage. Countries that started this path in the 1990s took between eight and fifteen years to finish it.

The politics behind the decision were dominated by the war. Ukraine's government had applied four days after the Russian invasion and had spent the spring arguing that a country fighting for European values should be treated as part of Europe. The commission's president, Ursula von der Leyen, said that Ukraine had already adopted about seventy per cent of the union's rules, and several leaders from eastern member states pressed for a signal that would be visible in Kyiv.

Costs were discussed more quietly. Ukraine is a country of forty million people with a large agricultural sector, and joining the union would entitle its farmers to subsidies on a scale that would require rewriting the common agricultural policy. Reconstruction after the war is estimated in hundreds of billions of dollars, and the union has begun to sketch a fund that would pay for part of it.

Moldova's candidacy raised different questions. The country of two and a half million has a frozen conflict on its border, a breakaway region where Russian troops are stationed, and a government that has spent two years fighting corruption in its judiciary. Western officials described the candidacy as a reward for reform and as a hedge against Russian influence, and Moldova's president called it a day that her country had waited for since independence.

Other applicants watched the decision closely. Albania and North Macedonia had been candidates for years without negotiations beginning, largely because of vetoes from member states, and Bosnia and Kosovo remained further back. The summit's conclusions promised to unblock the western Balkans, and the promise was received in those capitals with the skepticism that a decade of similar promises had earned. What the decision buys is time and a stake. Ukraine will not join while the war continues, and the reforms required will outlast the governments that promised them, but the candidacy changes the terms of the reconstruction, because money and technical help now come with a membership track attached; the union has said what it is willing to become, and the next decade will decide how much of that sentence it meant.
