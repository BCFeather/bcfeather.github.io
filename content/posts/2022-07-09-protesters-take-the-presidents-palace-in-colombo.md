A crowd broke through the police barriers around the president's official residence in Colombo on Saturday morning and occupied the building, and the president had left the city before they arrived. The protesters swam in the pool, sat at the desk and went through the rooms, and the television cameras followed them. The prime minister's private house was set on fire in the afternoon, and the prime minister said he would resign. The police fired tear gas and the curfew was imposed in the evening.

Six months of shortage had already worn the country down before Saturday. The foreign reserves ran out, the government stopped paying for fuel and the queues at the filling stations run for kilometres. The power cuts last for hours, the schools have closed and the hospitals have run out of medicine. The government has asked the International Monetary Fund for a programme and it has been negotiating with India and China for credit. The negotiations have been slow because the lenders want a plan and the government has not produced one.

Within two months a second prime minister had resigned. Ranil Wickremesinghe took office in May after the previous prime minister left, and he has been the public face of the negotiations with the fund. The president, Gotabaya Rajapaksa, has stayed in office and has blamed the opposition and the pandemic for the crisis. The family that has dominated the country's politics for two decades is now the target of the anger, and the protesters have said that they will not leave until the president goes.

No day since the protests began has been as violent as Saturday. The hospitals in Colombo said they had treated more than a hundred people, and the police said they had used tear gas and water cannon at the palace gates before the crowd broke through. The buses stopped running and the petrol stations that had fuel closed. The foreign ministry asked the citizens who were abroad to delay their return, and several embassies advised their nationals to stay indoors.

Nothing in the politics can be separated from the balance of payments. The country borrowed to cut taxes in 2019 and the pandemic then removed the tourists who paid for the debt. The government banned the import of chemical fertilizer in 2021 to save foreign exchange, and the harvest fell, which raised the price of rice. The rupee has lost more than half its value this year, and the price of the fuel that is imported has risen in the same period.

The state has lost the means of keeping order. The president has no money to pay the public servants and no majority in the parliament, and the crowd that took his house knows it. The negotiations with the fund will decide whether the country can restart the imports, and the fund will not sign while the government is being changed. The next few days will decide whether the president leaves and who replaces him, and neither question has an easy answer.
