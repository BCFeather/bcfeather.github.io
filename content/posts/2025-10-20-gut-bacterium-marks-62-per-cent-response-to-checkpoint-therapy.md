Checkpoint inhibitors work in a third of patients and the field has spent a decade looking for a predictor. A cohort of 420 patients reported this month that the abundance of one bacterial species explains a meaningful share of the variation in response, and the result is a correlation that three groups are now trying to turn into a mechanism.

The species is a butyrate producer that colonizes the colon's mucus layer. Patients in the cohort's highest tertile of abundance had a 62 per cent response rate, against 28 in the lowest, and the association survived adjustment for tumor type, prior treatment and antibiotic use. Antibiotics are the natural experiment. Patients who received a broad-spectrum antibiotic within 30 days of starting therapy had a 21 per cent response rate, and the effect was largest for the antibiotic that depletes anaerobes. That is evidence that the microbiome matters, and it does not identify which member. A stool sample also stores badly, and a cohort that collects at home and freezes on arrival has a sample whose composition may already have shifted.

Mechanism candidates are two. Butyrate can suppress the cytotoxic T cells that the therapy is meant to unleash, or the bacterium can stimulate the dendritic cells that prime them. The two predictions point in opposite directions, and the cohort's data cannot separate them. Dose, timing and the tumor's own genetics each modify the association, and a cohort of 200 patients cannot separate those effects from the bacterium's.

The mouse work is the test. Two groups have gavaged the species into germ-free mice and report opposite effects, which suggests the effect depends on the background community rather than on the species alone. Fecal transplant trials are the clinical test and they have been inconsistent. Three small trials reported responses between 20 and 60 per cent, and the variability correlates with the donors' own communities rather than with the recipients' disease.

A marker is not a mechanism. The bacterium's abundance correlates with response across a cohort, and the causal test is a germ-free mouse colonized with a responder's and a non-responder's flora. The cohort's result is the correlation, and the mouse work is the mechanism's first step.

Antibiotics confound the association. A patient who received a broad-spectrum course before treatment has a flora that a stool sample records and cannot date, and the cohort's analysis adjusts for the exposure rather than for its timing. A marker's clinical use would be a stool test before treatment, and its value depends on what a clinician does with the answer. A patient with a low abundance has no alternative therapy that works better, and a marker that cannot change a decision is a research result rather than a test.

A 62 to 28 split is a signal worth pursuing, not a test worth ordering. A predictor that works needs a mechanism, and the mechanism here is contested by the field's own animal data.
