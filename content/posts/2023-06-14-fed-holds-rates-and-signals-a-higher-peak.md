Its policy rate was left at 5 to 5.25 per cent on 14 June, the first pause after ten consecutive increases. The statement said the committee had decided to hold to assess the effects of the tightening, and it kept the reference to the additional policy firming that may be needed. The market had expected the pause. The vote was 11 to 1, with one governor preferring another quarter-point increase. The balance sheet continued to shrink by about 95 billion dollars a month, and the runoff is the other part of the tightening.

Projections were the surprise. The median of the nineteen officials pointed to a rate of about 5.6 per cent by the end of the year, which implies two more quarter-point rises. That is up from the 5.1 per cent in the March projections, and it is the part that the market did not expect. The officials also raised the forecast for the core inflation to about 3.9 per cent this year from 3.6. The dot plot has been the subject of the criticism from the market, and the committee has said it is not a promise.

Growth forecasts were raised. The committee now expects the economy to grow by about 1 per cent this year instead of 0.4, and the unemployment to peak at about 4.1 instead of 4.5. The forecast is the one that the soft landing rests on, and the officials said the banking strains had not hurt as much as they feared. The forecast for the inflation was cut to about 3.2 per cent this year, and the one for the next year to about 2.5.

Its chair spoke about the credit. He said the tightening of the credit conditions would do some of the work of the rates, and the committee would take it into account. He declined to say when the next increase would come, and he said the meeting in July was live. He said the labour market was still very tight, and the wage growth was running at about 4.3 per cent.

Markets moved both ways. The two-year yield rose by about 15 basis points after the statement and fell back as the chair spoke, and the ten-year was little changed. The traders priced about a 70 per cent chance of a rise in July, up from about 60 before. The dollar fell by about 0.3 per cent against the basket after the press conference, and the stocks were little changed.

This decision is the one that the committee had signalled in May. A pause that comes with the higher projections is a pause that is not a pivot, and the distinction is the one the chair spent the press conference making. The next meeting is in July, and the inflation report of the week before it is the one the market will trade.
