title: "A fourth dose cuts Omicron infection by half and severe disease by more"
date: "2022-04-05T09:40:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "Among 1,252,331 Israelis aged 60 or over during the Omicron wave, a fourth dose was associated with about half the rate of confirmed infection and roughly four times less severe COVID than three doses."
---

Israel began giving a fourth dose to people aged 60 and over on 2 January 2022, at the start of the Omicron wave, and the decision was made on the basis of waning immunity rather than on trial evidence. The Ministry of Health database allowed the decision to be evaluated within weeks. A country that had vaccinated its older population early was the first to see the waning, and the ministry had to choose between waiting for trial data and acting on the epidemiology.

Its analysis covered 1,252,331 people who were eligible for a fourth dose between 10 January and 2 March 2022, and compared those who received one with those who had three doses and with a group that had been vaccinated three to seven days earlier, before the fourth dose could have taken effect. The study appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 5 April 2022.

Rates were estimated from eight days after the fourth dose, with adjustment for age, sex, demographic group and calendar day. The rate of confirmed infection was roughly half that in the three-dose group, and the rate of severe COVID was lower by a larger factor, close to four. The comparison group that had been vaccinated three to seven days earlier is an internal control, and it removes the confounding that a simple comparison between vaccinated and unvaccinated groups would introduce.

A within-person comparison, in which people act as their own controls before and after vaccination, removes the differences between people who choose to be vaccinated and those who do not. That design is the strongest argument the authors have, and it also means the estimate is vulnerable to changes in testing behaviour over the study period.

The severe outcome is the one that matters for policy, and a two-thirds to three-quarter reduction is large enough to justify a fourth dose for an older population during a wave. The infection outcome is smaller and shorter-lived, which is consistent with what every study of this vaccine has found: protection against infection wanes faster than protection against disease. Whether a fourth dose should be offered to younger adults is a different question, because the absolute risk of severe disease in a healthy 40-year-old is low enough that the benefit is small in absolute terms.

Omicron had been circulating for only a few weeks when the data closed, and the follow-up is short by the standards of vaccine effectiveness studies. A benefit measured over six weeks may decay over six months, and Israel later extended eligibility to younger groups on the strength of these and similar estimates.

This study is observational, and the authors note that people who took the fourth dose earlier may have differed from those who waited. Residual confounding of that kind is the price of a real-world evaluation, and it is a smaller price than waiting for a trial that a pandemic does not allow. The Israeli data were used by several countries to justify a fourth dose, and the fact that the policy preceded the publication is a feature of pandemic decision-making rather than a defect of the study.
