Astrocytes in the striatum were known to influence dopamine transmission indirectly, by governing the extracellular concentration of GABA and adenosine. A paper in *Nature Communications* on 19 November 2024 reports a faster route, through cholinergic interneurons. An indirect route through extracellular transmitters is slow because diffusion sets the timescale, and a synaptic route is fast because it does not.

The anatomical finding is cell-type specific. Striatal astrocytes sit in a defined spatial relation with cholinergic interneurons rather than distributed uniformly among striatal cells, and proximity is what makes a fast interaction possible. Spatial partnership is the anatomical claim that makes the physiological one plausible, and the two are reported together for that reason. Defined spatial relation is a claim about position as well as about contact, and the imaging supports the position.

The functional finding is excitation. Astrocytes excite the cholinergic interneurons rapidly, a departure from the slow neuromodulatory action the earlier literature described. Fast excitation of an interneuron is a stronger claim than modulation of its environment, and the experiments are designed to distinguish them. Rapid excitation is measured in time, and the time is what separates a synaptic action from a diffusional one.

Dopamine release is the downstream variable. Cholinergic interneurons regulate striatal dopamine, so a fast excitatory input to them is a fast control on dopamine, and the timescale is the contribution. Dopamine is the output that makes the circuit interesting clinically, since every drug for Parkinson disease acts somewhere on that pathway. A control on dopamine is a control on striatal output, and output is where the clinical interest has always been.

Circuit-level control of a neurotransmitter by a glial cell is the general claim. The glia are not passive support here but a node in a pathway that ends at dopamine. A glial node in a neurotransmitter pathway changes what a circuit diagram should contain, and diagrams are how the field thinks. A diagram with a glial node is a diagram that predicts a fast effect of a glial perturbation on behaviour, and the prediction is testable.

On the modelling side, the striatum is where motor, reward and associative signals meet, and a glial node in that circuit is a node that could be targeted in disorders of movement or of reward. Targeting a glial node is a pharmacological strategy with few precedents, and the precedents that exist are mostly in inhibitory transmission. A target with few precedents is a risk for a drug programme, and the risk is the reason glial pharmacology has advanced slowly.

Whether the same arrangement exists in other regions is open, and the answer depends on whether astrocytes there also show cell-type-specific partnerships. Cell-type-specific partnerships elsewhere would make the arrangement a general principle, and a general principle is what a field can build on. Generality would require a survey of regions, and a survey is the kind of study that a single laboratory can initiate.
