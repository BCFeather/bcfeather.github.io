Israel killed a senior commander of Palestinian Islamic Jihad in a strike on a building in Gaza City on Friday afternoon, and the group answered with volleys of rockets towards cities in southern and central Israel. The commander was Tayseer al-Jabari, and the army said he had been planning an attack. The operation was named Breaking Dawn and it was the first major exchange since May of last year. The first rockets were fired within an hour of the strike, and the air raid sirens sounded in Ashkelon before the evening.

The fighting lasted three days and it stayed inside a narrow frame. Israel said it was targeting the organization and not the Hamas government, and the strikes hit apartments, a tower and a tunnel shaft. Islamic Jihad fired several hundred rockets, most of them at towns near the border, and the Iron Dome intercepted the ones that were aimed at built-up areas. Two girls were killed by a rocket in the West Bank, and a Palestinian labourer from Gaza was killed by a rocket inside Israel.

The casualties were mostly Palestinian. The health ministry in Gaza put the dead at forty-nine, including fifteen children, and the army said some of them had been killed by rockets that fell short. The United Nations and the human rights groups said they could not verify that claim, and the photographs from the strikes showed whole floors of residential buildings removed. A commander of the group's southern division was killed on the second day, and the organization's leadership went into hiding.

Egypt brokered the ceasefire, as it has done in each of the previous rounds. The truce took effect on Sunday night at half past eleven, and the terms stayed unpublished. Israel said it would stop if the rockets stopped, and Islamic Jihad said it would stop if the assassinations stopped. The crossing points into Gaza were closed for the duration, and the fuel that runs the power station was stopped, which left the strip with a few hours of electricity a day.

At the same time, the politics of the round are the part that observers watched. The Israeli caretaker government is in an election campaign, and the prime minister is a former general who has been criticized by his opponents for being soft on Gaza. Islamic Jihad had been outside the last round of fighting, and it had been trying to raise its profile against Hamas. Egypt wanted a quiet border during its own economic crisis, and it wanted to show the Americans that it can deliver one.

What the ceasefire buys is time rather than a settlement. The blockade remains, the crossings reopen for goods and people and the rockets are still in the tunnels. The reconstruction of the buildings that were hit will take months, and the money for it comes from Qatar, as it has in each of the previous rounds. The next escalation will begin with the same ingredients, and the only variable is which group is the target.
