description: "ZF2001, an adjuvanted RBD-dimer given as three 25-microgram doses, prevented symptomatic COVID in 75.7 per cent of adults across four countries and severe disease in 87.6 per cent, with a reactogenicity profile close to that of placebo."
---

Protein subunit vaccines have been the slowest of the COVID platforms to reach efficacy results, because they depend on producing a antigen in a bioreactor, purifying it and pairing it with an adjuvant that a regulator will accept. The platform is also the one with the longest record of use in other diseases, which is why its results matter beyond the pandemic. Attenuated or killed whole-cell vaccines take years of process development, and a subunit vaccine built from one piece of the spike is the version that a manufacturer can specify, release and assay with precision.

ZF2001 is a dimer of the receptor-binding domain of the spike protein, designed to present two copies of the antigen to the immune system, and it is given with an aluminium hydroxide adjuvant. A phase 3 trial ran at 31 centres in Uzbekistan, Indonesia, Pakistan and Ecuador, with three 25-microgram doses 30 days apart. The paper appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 4 May 2022.

The trial randomized 28,873 participants in a 1:1 ratio, and the updated efficacy analysis covered 25,193 people who completed the three-dose course, with about six months of follow-up. Symptomatic COVID at least seven days after the third dose occurred in 158 of 12,625 vaccine recipients and 580 of 12,568 placebo recipients, an efficacy of 75.7 per cent with an interval from 71.0 to 79.8. The participants were adults with no exclusion for comorbidity, and the four countries were chosen because their infection rates were high enough to accumulate cases within a reasonable follow-up.

Severe to critical disease occurred in 6 vaccinated participants and 43 placebo recipients, an efficacy of 87.6 per cent. Death from COVID occurred in 2 and 12 respectively, an efficacy of 86.5 per cent, and the interval around that estimate is wide because the numbers are small.

A three-dose schedule is the design choice that sets this vaccine apart from the mRNA products, and the interval between doses is a full month. The advantage is that a protein vaccine is stable at refrigerator temperature and cheap to make at scale; the disadvantage is that three visits are needed to complete a course. A three-dose regimen also has a practical cost in a campaign, because each additional visit loses a fraction of the people who had the first one.

Reactogenicity was modest, with 98.5 per cent of adverse reactions graded 1 or 2, and serious adverse events were balanced between groups. A vaccine that produces little fever is easier to deploy in a campaign where a second or third visit has to be earned.

The trial's limitation is the period. It ran before Omicron and included variants that were mostly descendants of the original strain, so the efficacy measured here describes a virus that no longer circulates. The result establishes the platform and leaves the variant question to the immunogenicity studies that followed. The authors report the efficacy against a variant mix that no longer exists and, wisely, present the immunogenicity data as the part of the result that will travel further.
