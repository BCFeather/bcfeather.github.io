Falling to 1.035 dollars in the Asian session on 26 September, the pound reached a level last seen in 1985. It had closed at 1.08 on the previous Friday and lost 5 per cent in a few hours. The move came after the chancellor's budget on 23 September, which cut taxes by 45 billion pounds and did not say how they would be paid for.

Announcing the budget without an independent forecast was the chancellor's choice. The fiscal watchdog was due to publish its assessment in November, and the chancellor chose to go ahead before it. The markets read that as a signal that the numbers had not been checked, and the reaction was immediate. The ten-year gilt yield rose 0.5 points in two days, and mortgage lenders withdrew products.

Sterling had been falling all year. It began 2022 at 1.35 and had lost 15 per cent before the budget, mostly because the Bank of England was raising rates more slowly than the Federal Reserve. The budget added a fiscal risk to a monetary one, and the combination was what took the currency below its 1985 level.

Intervention came from the Bank of England on 28 September, in the gilt market rather than in the currency. It bought 65 billion pounds of long-dated bonds over thirteen days to stop pension funds from being forced to sell. The funds used liability-driven investment strategies that required collateral, and the rise in yields had created a spiral. The bank said the operation was financial stability, not monetary policy.

Mortgage rates passed 6 per cent for a two-year fix, and the average rate had been 2.3 per cent a year earlier. About 1.8 million households were due to refinance in the following two years, and the monthly payment for a typical loan rose by 400 pounds. House prices fell for the first time in two years in October. The government reversed most of the budget on 3 October, and the pound recovered to 1.13. The chancellor was dismissed on 14 October and replaced by a former chancellor who undid the rest. The prime minister resigned on 20 October after 44 days in office, and her successor kept the tax rises.

Sterling ended the year at 1.20 and stayed in a narrow range through 2023. The episode cost the country money. The bank's purchases were sold at a loss, and the Treasury covered it, at a cost estimated at 3 billion pounds. The fiscal hole in the November statement was 55 billion pounds, and half of it came from the reversal. The independent forecast published with that statement showed the economy shrinking in 2023. The lesson that officials drew was that a budget needs a forecast attached and that a currency can punish a government faster than a bond market can. The lesson that markets drew was that the bank would defend financial stability but not a particular exchange rate.
