---

Standard accounts of recent human evolution were built on modern genomes. Populations in Europe, Africa and East Asia resemble each other closely, the argument went, so the last fifty thousand years must have been quiet, and the differences that exist are mostly the residue of older migrations.

Ancient DNA has changed the arithmetic, because change over time is visible only if you have samples from different times. A study reported in *Science* on 16 April, using data published in *Nature*, assembled close to 16,000 ancient genomes spanning 18,000 years, mostly from Europe and the Middle East, and added about 10,000 previously unpublished genomes to the few thousand that had been analysed before.

What emerges is a genome under heavy selection. Hundreds of variants changed frequency measurably in a relatively short window, and the shifts are not random noise, because random drift does not move hundreds of loci in the same direction across independent populations, and the signature of selection is coherence rather than magnitude.

Two of the traits that show up are resistance to tuberculosis and lower body fat, and a third cluster sits in genes that regulate the immune response to pathogens that crowded cities made common. Both make sense in the context of the period: tuberculosis became a crowd disease when people began living in dense settlements, and lower body fat is a plausible adaptation to food scarcity, although the same variant can be harmful in an environment of abundance.

It also bears on the shape of recent evolution, because the changes cluster in the last ten thousand years, which is when agriculture, urbanization and long-distance trade reorganised human life, and a genome under selection for a new way of living is a genome that had not finished adapting before the way of living changed again.

Selection detection has known failure modes, and the authors address them. A variant can rise because it is linked to a neighbour that is being selected, and ancient genomes have uneven sampling across space and time, which can make a real shift look sharper than it was. Methods that model the sampling structure are the difference between a discovery and an artefact.

The dataset is skewed geographically as well. Europe and the Middle East are where ancient DNA has been recovered in quantity, because the climate preserves bone and because the field has concentrated there, and a global picture built from a regional sample is a picture with a large blank area, which the field is filling at a rate set by permits, museum collections and the willingness of institutions to allow destructive sampling. What the study establishes is that the last ten thousand years were not a pause. People changed their genomes as they changed their diets, their diseases and their cities, and the record of that change is now long enough and dense enough to measure, which is a different kind of statement from a claim about a single gene.
