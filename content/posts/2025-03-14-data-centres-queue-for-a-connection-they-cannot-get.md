description: "The binding constraint on new computing capacity has moved from chips to the grid, and the queue in some American regions is now measured in years."
---

A data centre needs land, power and a connection, and in several American markets the third item has become the first problem. The queues for new transmission capacity in parts of the country stretch to four or five years, and a hyperscale operator that wants to energize a campus in 2027 has to file for interconnection now. A project that cannot get a connection does not get built, however many accelerators it has ordered. A campus that is announced with a gigawatt of demand is a campus that has already spoken to the utility, and the conversation is about the year 2030.

Grid operators design their queues around the assumption that a request will not all be built. A large share of projects that enter a queue withdraw before construction, and the studies that decide who gets capacity are expensive and serial. Several regions have moved to a cluster study that processes requests in batches, and the reform has shortened the queue without shortening it enough. A queue position can be sold, and several developers have built businesses around holding interconnection rights rather than around building anything.

Firm capacity is the second constraint. A data centre runs at a high load factor, and a grid that is built around a peak that lasts a few hours a year has to add generation that can run all the time. Gas turbines are the fastest option and the one with the longest permitting, and batteries can shift energy but cannot create it. A grid that was designed for a city of two hundred thousand people cannot absorb a load equal to that city without new lines, and new lines need rights of way.

Behind-the-meter generation is the workaround that large operators are using. A campus that builds its own gas plant or its own small reactor does not need a transmission upgrade, and it accepts a higher cost per kilowatt hour in exchange for a date it can plan around. The regulatory question is whether an operator that generates its own power is a utility, and the answer differs by state. A gas turbine ordered today is delivered in three years, and a turbine ordered with a combined-cycle package takes longer than the building that will house it.

Diesel is the backup that every data centre has and nobody talks about. A campus with fifty megawatts of load keeps a few dozen generators for the hours when the grid fails, and those generators run on fuel that has to be stored on site. A longer outage turns a backup system into a supply chain problem. Fuel storage is regulated as a hazardous material, which is a permitting problem that a campus in a rural county solves more easily than one near a suburb.

Where computing happens is what the queue has changed. Capacity is moving towards places with spare generation and cheap land rather than towards places with the fastest fibre, and the map of the industry is being redrawn by an infrastructure timetable that no software company controls. A region that can offer power in two years attracts the workload, and the workload moves with its customers.
