Foreign ministers of the Shanghai Cooperation Organization gathered in Goa for the grouping's Council of Foreign Ministers, an annual meeting that sets the agenda for the heads of state summit due in July. The two-day session took place under India's rotating presidency and produced a set of draft documents rather than binding decisions, which is how the body has worked since its founding.

All eight full members sent their top diplomats: China, India, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Pakistan, Russia, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan. On the margins, the secretariat signed memorandums granting dialogue-partner status to the United Arab Emirates, Kuwait, the Maldives and Myanmar, which brings the group's reach into the Gulf and across South Asia. Those four states join working groups on trade, energy, transport, security and cultural exchange without voting rights, and the arrangement gives the secretariat a reason to keep expanding its bureaucratic machinery.

Discussion kept returning to the founding vocabulary of the grouping, the set of principles its members call the Shanghai Spirit: mutual trust, equality, shared benefit and non-alignment. Ministers renewed their commitment to joint action against terrorism, separatism and extremism, and against cross-border drug trafficking and organized crime. They also exchanged views on Afghanistan, calling for an inclusive political structure and for sustained action against armed groups operating from its territory.

Economic connectivity took up much of the second day. Delegates backed work on transport corridors, regional supply chains and trade in agricultural goods, energy and digital services. The SCO Interbank Consortium and the Business Council were presented as practical vehicles for financing infrastructure, and several projects were described as compatible with the Belt and Road framework. Ministers warned against unilateral sanctions and protectionist measures that interrupt regional commerce. Shipping costs and insurance premiums across the corridor were mentioned more than once, which suggests that the practical complaints of traders are now reaching the ministerial agenda.

Formal accession for Iran and Belarus advanced as well, with both due to be admitted at the July summit in New Delhi. Enlargement of this kind changes the character of a body that began as a border-confidence arrangement among five states, and it strengthens the claim that middle powers are looking for forums outside the Western-led system. The presence of Pakistan's foreign minister, the first such visit to India in twelve years, drew attention for the same reason: the meeting offered a small step between two states that rarely speak.

The grouping still carries disagreements that no communique can hide. India and Pakistan hold rival claims over Kashmir, and members take different positions on the war in Ukraine and on Middle East conflicts, which is why officials repeat that the SCO does not function as a military alliance and does not require a single line on every international question. Before the session closed, ministers finalized drafts on food security, energy cooperation, climate resilience and rules for the digital economy. Adding partners is the easy part; keeping a widening club focused on regional stability is the harder test that the July summit will face.
