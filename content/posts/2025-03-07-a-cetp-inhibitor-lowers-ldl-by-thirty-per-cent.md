Cholesteryl ester transfer protein moves cholesterol between lipoprotein particles, and blocking it raises HDL cholesterol and lowers LDL. The logic attracted three large programmes in the 2000s and 2010s, and all three ended badly: torcetrapib raised mortality and blood pressure, dalcetrapib showed no benefit on outcomes, and evacetrapib was stopped for futility.

What separates the new molecule from its predecessors is potency and selectivity. Obicetrapib lowers LDL by about 30 per cent as a monotherapy and by more when combined with a statin, which is a larger effect than the earlier compounds produced, and it appears not to raise blood pressure or aldosterone in the way that torcetrapib did.

A trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 7 March enrolled patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or heterozygous familial hypercholesterolaemia who were on the maximum tolerated dose of a statin, and it randomized them to obicetrapib or placebo for 84 days. The least-squares mean change in LDL was minus 29.9 per cent with the drug, against plus 2.7 per cent with placebo, a difference of about 33 points.

Population enrolled is the one that matters commercially. A patient who has had a heart attack and is already on a statin and ezetimibe has a residual risk that the existing oral drugs do not remove, and the injectable options, which are the PCSK9 inhibitors and inclisiran, are effective and expensive. An oral drug that adds thirty points of LDL lowering fits between those two tiers, and the size of that tier is large. A patient who cannot tolerate an injectable, or who declines one, has had no oral option beyond ezetimibe.

Safety was unremarkable at the doses tested, with no increase in blood pressure and no signal in liver enzymes, and the discontinuation rate matched placebo. Liver fat, a concern for a class that moves cholesterol between particles, did not change over the twelve weeks. A 84-day study cannot detect the rare events that ended the earlier programmes, and the outcome trial that the company is running is the one that will decide the class's fate.

Field has learned from three failures that a surrogate endpoint is a promise and not a result. A drug that lowers LDL by thirty per cent should reduce events by a predictable amount, and the prediction has failed before in this class, which is why the outcome trial matters more than the lipid data and why the company has enrolled several thousand patients rather than a few hundred.

Cost is the remaining question. An oral CETP inhibitor would be priced against inclisiran and against the generic statins, and a health system that already pays for a PCSK9 inhibitor for its highest-risk patients will ask whether the oral drug replaces that spend or adds to it. The answer depends on the event rate in the outcome trial, which is the number that the whole class has been waiting for.
