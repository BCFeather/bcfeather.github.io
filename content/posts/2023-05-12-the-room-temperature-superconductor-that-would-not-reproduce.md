---

A claim of superconductivity at room temperature and at modest pressure would be a discovery of the century. Such a claim was made in March 2023 for a lutetium-hydrogen-nitrogen compound, and the field's response has been the response such claims always attract: attempts to repeat it.

In *Nature* on 11 May 2023, one such attempt is reported. A group specialising in high-pressure synthesis made more than thirty samples following the original recipe and found no superconductivity. The samples' colours changed with pressure, which the original paper had cited as evidence, and the change came without the electrical signature.

Signature is the test. Superconductivity means zero resistance and an expelled magnetic field, and a claim lacking those measurements concerns a colour rather than a state. Presentation of the data is the crux of the dispute, because a resistance drop can come from a percolating metallic path, and a measurement failing to separate bulk from surface can mislead.

Pressure is the second problem. The original claim required about ten kilobars, far below the diamond-anvil pressures dominating the field, and a measurement at that pressure is easier to make and easier to check. Failure to replicate is not proof of fraud, since a synthesis sensitive to trace impurities can produce a superconducting phase in one laboratory and not in another, and the honest conclusion is that the claim stands unreproduced.

History supplies the context. A series of high-pressure superconductivity claims have collapsed under scrutiny, and the pattern has made journals and referees more cautious about resistance data. Incentives are the underlying problem, because a room-temperature superconductor would transform power transmission, and that prospect creates pressure to publish before the evidence is complete. Preprint culture belongs to the story as well, since a claim circulating before review reaches the public without the field's filter, and the gap between the two is where a field's reputation is made or lost.

Readers learn what a claim is worth. A single paper with an extraordinary result is a report, and a result other groups reproduce is a fact. Closing the question is what the rebuttal does not do, because a different composition or route might still produce the phase, and the search continues with the original claim as one hypothesis among several.

Materials science gains a demonstration of self-policing, since a replication that fails is itself a result, and publishing it keeps an extraordinary claim from becoming an accepted one. A synthesis paper owes its readers the exact conditions under which the reported phase appears, and a recipe that cannot be followed is a recipe that cannot be tested, which is the practical lesson the episode leaves for high-pressure chemistry. What would settle the matter is a sample whose resistance falls to zero in a measured magnetic field, and until one exists the literature holds a claim rather than a result. The episode also shows how quickly a remarkable number travels, since a claim reaches readers as a value long before it reaches them as a measurement.
