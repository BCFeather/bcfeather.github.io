title: "The Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant is cut off from the grid and then reconnected"

The nuclear power station at Zaporizhzhia lost its connection to the electricity grid on Thursday, and the operators said the shelling had damaged the switchyard that links the plant to the transmission lines. The plant is the largest in Europe and it has six reactors, and the last two that were running shut down when the connection failed. The power that the station needs to cool the fuel came from diesel generators for several hours, and the operators said the supply was restored on Friday. It was the first time in the war that the plant had been taken off the grid.

The station has been occupied by Russian forces since March, and it has been operated by Ukrainian staff under Russian orders. The two sides have accused each other of shelling the site, and the United Nations has said it cannot verify who fired. The plant sits on the southern bank of the reservoir, and the Ukrainian positions are across the water, which puts the site inside the area that the Russian army has fortified. The inspectors from the international agency have been asking for access since June, and the requests have gone unanswered for most of the summer.

At the same time, the risk that the engineers describe is a loss of cooling. A reactor that has shut down still produces heat from the decay of its fuel, and the pumps that carry the water have to run for days. The diesel generators have fuel for about ten days, and the tanks have been hit in previous attacks. The agency's director general said in a statement that the situation was grave and that a failure of the external power supply would be a serious danger. The Ukrainian energy minister said the plant was one step from an accident.

Meanwhile, the diplomatic effort has been slow. The agency's mission was announced in August and it has been assembled in Kyiv, and the route to the plant crosses the front line. The Russian government said it would allow the inspectors in and then said the timing was wrong, and the talks have involved the United Nations and Turkey. The Ukrainian government has asked for a demilitarized zone around the site, and the Russian government has rejected that. The agency has said it will not send its people into a combat zone without guarantees.

Beyond the plant, the wider electricity system is also under strain. The plant supplied about a fifth of the country's power before the war, and the grid has been patched with imports from the west and with the plants that remain. The shelling of the substations has become a pattern, and the repairs take weeks. The operators have said that the winter will be difficult, and the officials have asked the population to buy generators and to store water.

What the incident shows is a nuclear facility inside a war that neither side can end quickly. The reactors are built to withstand earthquakes and aircraft, and they were never designed for a battle in which the switchyard is a target. The inspectors will eventually arrive and their report will describe the damage rather than assign the blame. The plant will keep running or it will keep shutting down, and the people who live downwind will keep watching the wind.
