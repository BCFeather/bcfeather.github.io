Zoom reported results for the quarter ended 31 January on 27 February, and revenue was 1.1 billion dollars. That was 4 per cent above the previous year and the slowest growth the company had recorded since listing. Enterprise customers rose 12 per cent to 213,000, and the share of revenue from them passed 70 per cent. The quarter was the eighth in which growth slowed, and the company had stopped giving monthly usage figures. Revenue from online customers fell 10 per cent, and that segment is now less than a fifth of the total.

Guidance for the year was 4.5 billion dollars, which implies growth of about 1 per cent. Analysts had expected 4.7 billion, and the shares fell 8 per cent in extended trading. The company said the uncertainty in technology spending and the strength of the dollar would both weigh on the numbers. The company said it would not give quarterly guidance, and that it would report billings instead.

Its difficulty is structural as much as cyclical. Zoom became a household name in 2020, when meetings moved online, and it has been trying to sell more per customer since. The company added a contact centre product in 2022 and a email service, both of which require sales people rather than self-service. Those products grew, though from a small base. The contact centre product was bought rather than built, and the deal closed in 2022.

Costs were the other part of the story. The company had hired aggressively through 2021 and cut 15 per cent of staff in February, about 1,300 people. It took a charge of 100 million dollars in the quarter and said the savings would show from the spring. Operating margin on a non-standard basis was 34 per cent, which is high for a software firm of its age. The cuts fell hardest on the sales organisation, and the company said it would hire engineers instead. Share-based compensation added 400 million dollars to costs for the year.

Free cash flow for the year was 1.4 billion dollars, and the company held 5.4 billion in cash and securities. That balance sheet is why the guidance was read as caution rather than distress. The company bought back 500 million dollars of shares in the quarter and said it would continue. The company had no debt, and its cash had fallen by 300 million over the year because of the buyback.

Competitors had a harder year. The large platforms bundled video calls into their office suites, and the smaller specialists either merged or cut costs. Zoom's share of the paid market was about 55 per cent, according to one research firm, and it had not fallen much during the year. Whether it can grow again depends on selling the new products, and that takes time. Microsoft bundled its video service into the office suite at no extra charge, which removed the reason to buy separately.
