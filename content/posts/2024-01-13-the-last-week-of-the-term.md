Revision week has its own weather. Sky stays grey all day and never quite rains, the light in the classroom is the colour of an old envelope, and the only sound outside the window is the compressor of the air conditioner in the office next door, which starts and stops at intervals that no one has ever worked out. Inside, the sound is paper. Thirty-odd people turning pages, writing lines, closing books and opening them again, and underneath it the low murmur of the teacher at the front, going through the same problem for the third time because the same five people got it wrong twice.

The examinations in January do not count for as much as the summer ones, and everybody behaves as if they count for more. A term's worth of material is tested in three days, and the results arrive before the holiday does, which means the holiday is decided by what happens in that week. Boys who are safe in the top ten work differently from the boys who are not, and you can tell which is which by the way they hold their pens. One group writes quickly and then checks. Others write slowly and then leave things out, because leaving things out is a decision they have already made.

The teachers are in the same position, and it makes them easier to talk to. Their marking is finished except for what is not, they are tired in a way that shows around the eyes, and they answer questions more directly than they do in October, when there is still time to be careful. Our maths teacher has given the class a sheet of nine problems and told everybody that four of them will appear on the paper in some form, which is either a kindness or a piece of psychological warfare depending on how much of the four you can do.

Outside the school gates the town has started on the year's other business. Market road has more people on it in the evening than it did last week, and the shops that sell preserved meat and sweets have put their goods out on tables in the street. The migration of the swallows is finished and the migration of the family is beginning, in the sense that people in other cities are buying tickets. Somebody in the class has a cousin who is coming home on the twenty-eighth, and the date has been repeated in the corridor so often that people who have no cousin are asking about it.

What I notice in this week is that the year is closing in a way that the calendar does not quite describe. Nothing has ended yet and everything is about to, and the examinations sit between the two like a door that has to be opened before the rest of it can happen. When the last paper is finished and the results are read out, the term will be over in twenty minutes, the way terms always are, and the winter will begin with a suitcase and a train. Until then the work continues, one problem at a time, in a room where the light is grey and the paper does not stop.
