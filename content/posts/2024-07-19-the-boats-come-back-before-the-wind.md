A typhoon that forms in the western Pacific gives a fishing port three days to bring its fleet home. Marine authority issues the notice when the storm passes a line on the chart. Drawn to allow for the slowest boat is the line. The harbour that receives the fleet is smaller than the fleet. A port built for two hundred vessels takes four hundred. Extra ones moor three abreast against the quay. A crew that cannot find a berth anchors in the bay and keeps a watch through the night.

Crews sleep on board. A fisherman who has been at sea for a fortnight does not go home for two days of wind, because the boat has to be ready when the storm passes. Families come down to the quay with food instead. The market closes for the duration. A fish market that opens at four in the morning is shut when the storm is within a day. Catch landed the evening before is sold at a discount to whoever will take it.

Ice is the constraint on the second day. A boat that cannot ice its catch cannot go back out. Plant runs at full capacity for the week after the storm. Longer at the plant than at the fuel dock is the queue. The typhoon also brings the season's best fishing. Water that the storm stirs up carries nutrients. Boats that go out on the third day come back with a fuller hold than they had all month. Danger and profit arrive together.

Ports repair what they can before the next one. A quay that lost a section of railing is patched in the autumn. A breakwater that was damaged in the last storm is surveyed and scheduled. Schedules slip when the budget is short. The forecast has improved and the losses have not fallen as fast. A storm that is tracked accurately still closes a port for three days. Cost of three days is counted in a season that has forty weeks in it.

Insurance is sold by the season. A boat that stays in port for a storm collects nothing and pays nothing. Premiums are calculated on the days at sea. A skipper who goes out on the third day pays for the risk that he took. The cooperative keeps a list of the boats that are at sea when the notice is issued. A boat that does not answer the radio is reported to the marine police, and the police send a cutter. A hand-written board in the office carries the list, and the board is photographed and sent to the county. A port that has lost a season to a single storm remembers the date, and the board in the office is kept from year to year.

Boats go back out when the signal is lowered. The crews who stayed aboard for two days wash the decks, check the nets, and leave on the evening tide. By the second week of August the season has been repaired, and the accounts in the office show what the two lost days cost.
