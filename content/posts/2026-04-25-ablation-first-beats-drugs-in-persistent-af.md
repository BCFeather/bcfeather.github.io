Atrial fibrillation is treated in a sequence. Drugs come first, because they are tablets and ablation is a procedure, and ablation is offered when drugs fail or when a patient cannot tolerate them. The sequence is a convention and not a result, and for persistent fibrillation, where the arrhythmia has lasted beyond seven days, the convention has weak support.

A trial published in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 25 April tested the sequence by reversing it. Patients with symptomatic persistent atrial fibrillation who had not been treated with antiarrhythmic drugs were randomized to pulsed field ablation, a technique that kills arrhythmogenic tissue with short high-voltage pulses and spares adjacent structures, or to drugs, and the comparison was at twelve months.

Treatment success at twelve months was 128 of 207 patients in the ablation group, a Kaplan-Meier estimate of 56 per cent, against 40 of 103 in the drug group, an estimate of 30 per cent. The hazard ratio for the composite of treatment failure was 0.46 with a confidence interval from 0.33 to 0.65, and the *P* value was below 0.001.

Success was defined strictly. Freedom from recurrent atrial arrhythmia, from repeat ablation and from the need for antiarrhythmic drugs from ninety days to twelve months, which means a patient who returned to drugs counted as a failure. That definition favours ablation in one direction and penalizes it in another, and the net effect is a comparison of strategies and not of procedures.

Safety was acceptable. A device- or procedure-related serious adverse event occurred in 13 of 257 patients in the combined ablation group, 5.1 per cent, and serious adverse events of any kind occurred in 25 per cent of the ablation group and 21 per cent of the drug group. The difference is the price of a procedure, and it is a modest price by the standards of electrophysiology. The result will change the sequence for a subset. A patient with persistent fibrillation, a normal-sized atrium, no major comorbidity and a preference for a single procedure is now a candidate for ablation first, and a patient with a large atrium, long-standing disease and a high procedural risk is still a candidate for drugs.

Cost and capacity are the constraint on how far the finding travels. An ablation procedure costs more than a year of generic antiarrhythmic drugs and requires an electrophysiology laboratory and a waiting list, and a health system that adopts first-line ablation for everyone with persistent fibrillation is a system that has decided to spend its cardiology budget differently. The trial's design limits the argument in one respect. The drug arm received antiarrhythmic therapy without a repeat attempt at rhythm control, and the ablation arm could be repeated, so the comparison is between a strategy that escalates and one that does not. That is a real-world pattern and it is also a source of bias that a reader should hold in view.

What the trial establishes is that the conventional order is a choice and not a necessity. For a condition that damages the atrium a little more with every year of uncontrolled arrhythmia, doing the definitive treatment first is a defensible position, and it now has a randomized trial behind it.
