description: "The one-week ultimatum that the Economic Community of West African States gave Niger's officers expired on 6 August 2023 with the president still detained, the airspace closed and no intervention under way."
---

The deadline set in Abuja on 30 July expired on Sunday night, and nothing happened. Niger's airspace stayed closed, the officers of the presidential guard stayed in the palace they had seized, and President Mohamed Bazoum stayed in the rooms where he has been held since 26 July. The Economic Community of West African States had said it would take all measures necessary unless he was restored, and it had also said that it preferred a negotiated outcome, which is how a threat expires without being withdrawn.

The junta moved first. It closed the country's airspace on 6 August, citing the threat of intervention, and it replaced the heads of the army's regions with officers it trusts. It also named a cabinet of twenty-one members, several of them civilians from parties that had served Bazoum, which is the standard method by which a coup turns itself into a government. General Abdourahamane Tchiani said on state television that the council would not be deterred by sanctions or by threats.

Pressure has come through money rather than soldiers. Nigeria, which supplies most of Niger's electricity, cut off the power it had been providing, and Niamey has been running on generators and on imports from Burkina Faso. The bloc's sanctions froze assets and closed borders, and the World Bank suspended disbursements worth hundreds of millions of dollars, most of them for health and education programmes. Hospitals reported shortages, the price of rice rose, and the finance ministry said it would not be able to meet the payroll without help. The uranium mines have kept working, which is why the sanctions were drawn to spare them.

Mediation has been attempted and has not yet produced a meeting. The former Nigerian head of state Abdulsalami Abubakar travelled to Niamey and met the officers, and the bloc's commissioner said talks would continue. The junta has refused to receive delegations from some member states, has accused France of preparing an operation, and has said that any intervention would be answered. Diplomats who have met the general describe a man convinced that the region will accept the outcome once the money settles. Mali and Burkina Faso repeated their warning that an attack on Niger would be treated as an attack on them.

Niger is a test of whether the region can enforce its own rules, and the answer so far is that it can impose costs and cannot impose outcomes. The bloc has intervened before, in Liberia, in Sierra Leone and in Gambia, and each of those operations was short, welcomed by the population and paid for by Nigeria. This one would be long, contested and expensive, and the Nigerian senate has already declined to endorse it.

A week has passed and the president is still detained, and the conditions of his detention have become a diplomatic subject of their own. The officers have bought time, the bloc has spent credibility, and the mediation that everybody says they want has produced a room in Abuja rather than a result in Niamey.
