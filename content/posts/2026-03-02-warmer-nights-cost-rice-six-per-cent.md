description: "A nine-year trial across four sites found that a 1.5 degree rise in night-time minimum temperature reduced rice yield by 6 per cent, with the damage concentrated in the grain-filling stage rather than in flowering."
---Rice is grown in the tropics and has a narrow temperature optimum, and the night-time minimum matters more than the daytime maximum. A trial reported this month, running across four sites for nine years, measured the effect of a 1.5 degree rise in night temperature on yield, and found a 6 per cent reduction.

Design is what makes the result usable. Each site grew the same three varieties in paired plots, one under a passive night-time warming system that raises canopy temperature by 1.5 degrees and one at ambient, through six seasons. The warming is applied with infrared heaters controlled by a canopy sensor.

The Timing of the damage is the mechanism. The reduction appears when warming is applied during grain filling, the 25 days after flowering, and not when it is applied during flowering. Respiration at night consumes carbohydrate that would otherwise go to the grain, and a warmer night raises respiration by 12 per cent.

Varietal differences are modest. The three varieties lost 5.2, 6.1 and 7.4 per cent, and the difference correlates with their respiration rate rather than with their maturity group. That is a useful signal for breeding and not a solution.

The comparison with daytime warming changes the conclusion. In a companion experiment, the same 1.5 degree rise applied during the day reduced yield by 3 per cent, half the night-time effect. Most crop models weight day and night equally, which is the error this trial corrects.

Field validation is the next step. The trial's warming system is passive and site-specific, and the results are being compared with a network of 40 farm sites where growers recorded night temperature and yield. The correlation holds within 2 per cent.

Limits are the sites. All four are in irrigated lowland systems in one climatic zone, and upland rice and rain-fed systems respond differently. The authors state that the 6 per cent figure is a lowland estimate.

The warming system's artefacts were measured rather than assumed. Infrared heaters raise leaf temperature and slightly reduce humidity, and the team ran a sham heater in the control plots to separate the heating from the equipment's presence. The sham had no effect on yield.

Breeding implications are narrower than they look. Respiration rate varies among cultivars, and the three tested differ by 2 points of yield loss, which suggests a selection criterion rather than a large gain. A breeding programme would need a decade to fix it.

The region's exposure is larger than the experiment's single season. Night temperatures in the basin have risen 1.2 degrees in thirty years, and the experiment's 1.5 is within that range rather than beyond it. The loss is therefore a description of a trend rather than of a future.

For a reader, the relevance is in how a small temperature change becomes a large yield change through a physiological route that models underweight. A 6 per cent loss on a 1.5 degree night-time warming is the kind of number that turns a climate projection into a food-security calculation.
