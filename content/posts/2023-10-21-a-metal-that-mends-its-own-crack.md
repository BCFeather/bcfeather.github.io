description: "A fatigue crack in a metal can close again when the load is removed, and the atoms that fill it come from the grain boundary that the crack runs along."
---

A crack in a metal usually grows until the part breaks, and the reason is that every cycle of loading opens the crack a little more than the unloading closes it. A crack that runs along a grain boundary can behave differently: if the two grains are pressed back together, the boundary can bond again, and the crack disappears. The effect has been seen in a platinum alloy and in copper, in samples small enough that the surfaces can be brought into contact.

The mechanism is diffusion along the boundary. When the crack is open, the surfaces are exposed and the atoms at the tip are mobile. When the load is removed the surfaces close, and the atoms can rearrange into the lattice of the boundary if the temperature is high enough for them to move. A metal at room temperature does not diffuse fast enough to heal in a useful time, and a metal at a few hundred degrees does.

Crystal orientation decides whether the crack heals or grows. A boundary whose two grains are related by a low-index misorientation is a boundary with a regular structure, and the atoms on the two sides fit together after the crack closes. A boundary with a random misorientation has a structure that does not match, and the crack that runs along it does not close. The experiments that produced the healing used a boundary of the first kind, which is why the result is a demonstration rather than a general property.

The loading matters as much as the material. Healing occurred when the crack was loaded in a way that opened it and then closed it completely, and a load that leaves a residual opening leaves a crack that cannot bond. A real component in service sees loads that are not symmetric, and a crack in a turbine blade is opened on every cycle and never pressed shut. The condition for healing is therefore a condition on the design as much as on the alloy. A part that is loaded through a bolt pattern sees a compressive field around each hole, and those regions are where a crack would be closed.

Microscopy with the sample under tension is how the measurement is done, and the crack is watched while it grows and closes. The resolution is nanometres, so the displacement across the crack can be followed cycle by cycle, and the healing appears as a decrease of the opening displacement instead of as a change of the surface. The same experiment shows that the healed region carries load again, since the stiffness of the sample recovers.

Implications for engineering are narrow and real. A component that is loaded in compression as well as tension, such as a rail or a bearing race, is one where a small crack can close, and an alloy chosen for diffusion at the service temperature would heal some of the damage. A component that is loaded in one direction only would not benefit, and the design of a part that heals is a design in which the load path is made to close the crack it opens.
