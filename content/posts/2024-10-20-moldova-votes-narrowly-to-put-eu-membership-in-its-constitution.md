title: "Moldova votes narrowly to put EU membership in its constitution"

description: "A referendum held with the presidential election on 20 October approved a constitutional commitment to European Union membership by 50.35 per cent, while President Maia Sandu led the first round and faced a runoff."
---

Moldova held a referendum on European Union membership alongside its presidential election on the twentieth of October, and the yes vote won by a margin of less than one per cent, with 50.35 per cent of valid ballots in favour after the count was completed. Turnout was about fifty-one per cent. Question put to voters was whether the aspiration to join the union should be written into the constitution as a permanent commitment, which would make it harder for a future government to reverse.

Result arrived slowly and changed shape during the night. Early counts published after polls closed showed the no side ahead, and the yes vote took the lead only as ballots from the capital and from polling stations abroad were added. President Maia Sandu, who had organized the vote, said at a press conference that the result had been secured despite an attempt to buy votes on an industrial scale, and she thanked Moldovans abroad, whose ballots were overwhelmingly in favour.

Vote-buying allegations dominated the campaign and the aftermath. Prosecutors and the electoral commission investigated networks linked to Ilan Shor, a businessman and former politician who lives abroad and who has been convicted of fraud, and they described payments made to voters through messaging applications. Observers from the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe said the campaign had been competitive and that the vote had been administered well, and they also documented pressure on public employees and the use of state resources.

Presidential contest in the same ballot went to a runoff. Sandu took 42.45 per cent, short of the fifty per cent needed to win outright, and Alexandr Stoianoglo, a former prosecutor general backed by the Socialists, took about 26 per cent. Their second round was set for the third of November, and Sandu won it with about 55 per cent, after a campaign in which she presented the choice as one between a European future and dependence on Russia.

Russian involvement was the subject of statements rather than evidence. Kremlin said it was watching the vote and denied interference, and it criticized the referendum as an attempt to impose a foreign choice on Moldovan voters. Moldova's own security services said they had detected schemes to pay voters and to spread false information, and they attributed them to actors connected to Moscow. The country has applied for membership and opened accession negotiations, which will run for years whatever the constitutional text says.

Consequence of the narrow margin is political rather than legal. A constitution that commits a country to a course requires a broad majority to be credible, and half the voters rejected the commitment, which gives the opposition a platform at every future election. Government says the vote secures the direction of travel and puts the country beyond the reach of reversals of the kind seen in Georgia and in Ukraine before 2014. Critics say the country is divided in a way that a single article in the constitution cannot resolve, and that the next parliamentary election will test whether the margin can be widened or has to be defended.
