Dilated cardiomyopathy is diagnosed when the left ventricle dilates and stops contracting well without an obvious cause, and its inheritance has been studied mostly in White families attending specialist clinics. Cascade screening, in which relatives of an affected patient are offered echocardiography, follows from the assumption that a meaningful fraction of those relatives carry the same risk.

The assumption had never been measured across a diverse population. A consortium of 25 heart failure programmes in the United States enrolled 1,220 probands and screened 1,693 first-degree relatives between June 2016 and April 2021, defining familial disease as cardiomyopathy in at least one first-degree relative and expanded familial disease as cardiomyopathy or an isolated enlargement or dysfunction. The findings appeared in *JAMA*, 1 February 2022. The definition matters because a relative with an enlarged ventricle who does not meet the full criteria may still be at risk, and the expanded category captures that intermediate state.

Among the probands, the median age was 52.8 years, 43.8 per cent were women, 43.1 per cent were Black and 8.3 per cent Hispanic. The crude prevalence of familial disease was 11.6 per cent, but only a median of 28 per cent of living first-degree relatives had been screened, so a model was used to estimate what complete screening would find: 29.7 per cent, with an interval from 23.5 to 36.0. The estimate for expanded familial disease was 56.9 per cent.

Cumulative risk in first-degree relatives reached 19 per cent by age 80 for cardiomyopathy and 33 per cent for the expanded definition, and the hazard was higher in relatives of Black probands than of White probands, with a hazard ratio of 1.89 and an interval from 1.26 to 2.83. The prevalence difference between Black and White probands was 11.3 percentage points. A hazard ratio cannot be translated into a screening interval without knowing when the disease appears, and the age-specific data suggest that the risk accumulates steadily rather than in a single window.

Incomplete screening is the central weakness and the authors are explicit about it. A prevalence estimated from the relatives who chose to attend is biased in an unknown direction, since families with a history of sudden death are more likely to accept testing, and the correction assumes that the untested resemble the tested within each family. The modelling also assumes that a family's willingness to be screened does not depend on the result of the first relative who was tested, which is plausible but unverified.

Genetics explains part of the pattern and not all of it. Known pathogenic variants account for a minority of familial cases, shared environment and undiscovered variants for the rest, and the racial difference in risk suggests that the usual panels, built from European ancestry data, are missing variants that matter in other populations. The panels were designed for European ancestry, and the higher yield in Black families may reflect a higher burden of variants that the panels do not sequence.

The clinical implication is straightforward and cheap. A diagnosis in one patient should prompt an offer of echocardiography to parents, siblings and children, and the yield is high enough that the offer is worth making even where genetic testing is unavailable.
