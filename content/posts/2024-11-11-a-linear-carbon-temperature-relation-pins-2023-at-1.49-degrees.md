Whether the world has passed a warming threshold depends on a metric as much as on a thermometer, and the metrics in use differ in transparency and in uncertainty. A paper in *Nature Geoscience* on 11 November 2024 argues that a linear relation between carbon dioxide and temperature supplies a better one. A metric is a choice about how to attribute warming, and different choices produce different numbers for the same year and the same physical change.

Linearity is the premise. If temperature responds approximately linearly to cumulative carbon dioxide, a regression fitted over the instrumental period can be extrapolated to give the human-induced component. Extrapolating a regression is an assumption, and the authors state it instead of hiding it, which is the standard a metric has to meet to be auditable. Cumulative emissions are the predictor, which places the method among transient-response metrics rather than among energy-balance ones.

The estimate for 2023 is 1.49 degrees Celsius above a pre-1700 baseline, with an uncertainty of 0.11 degrees. That interval is at least 30 per cent narrower than the intervals that alternative methods produce. Pre-1700 avoids the industrial era, and a reader who prefers another baseline can recompute the number from the data without changing the method.

Certainty of that kind is what a compliance assessment needs, since a policy judged against a threshold requires an estimate precise enough to say whether the threshold has been crossed. Aerosols are the forcing that breaks linearity, so a period of rapid aerosol change is a period in which the estimate is least secure. A narrow interval allows a threshold to be declared crossed, and a wide one leaves the question open for another year.

The baseline is a judgement. Pre-1700 is a period before industrialization and before most land-use change, and a different baseline would shift the number without changing the physics. Precision matters because compliance is binary, and a threshold judged against an uncertain number is a threshold that can be disputed.

Linearity is an approximation that holds over the range in which it was fitted. Aerosols and other forcers break it, which is why the method attributes warming rather than predicting it. Adoption would make the method the number against which policy is judged, which makes the choice of method a political act as well as a scientific one. Attribution says how much of the observed change is human, and prediction says what comes next.

On the modelling side, the paper proposes a metric rather than measuring the climate, and the two should not be confused by a reader who takes the number for the answer to the policy question. A number another group can recompute from the same records is a metric, and one that it cannot recompute is an opinion with a decimal point. A decimal point does not make a judgement, and the method is judged by whether another group can reproduce it.
