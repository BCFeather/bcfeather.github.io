description: "A two-component signalling system rebuilt in an artificial membrane transmits a chemical signal across a lipid bilayer, according to work published in *PNAS* on 9 May 2023 that gives synthetic cells a route to sensing."
---

A cell's boundary is a filter, and a filter that only blocks is a wall. What makes a membrane a boundary of a living system is its ability to pass information: a signal outside changes behaviour inside.

The work in *PNAS*, 9 May 2023 rebuilds that ability in a synthetic membrane. The authors reconstitute a bacterial two-component system in lipid vesicles, so that a sensor kinase in the membrane phosphorylates a response regulator inside when it detects its ligand outside.

The architecture is the standard bacterial one. A sensor kinase spans the membrane, binds a signal, and autophosphorylates; a response regulator receives the phosphate and binds DNA. The two components are the minimum for a signal to cross a boundary.

The reconstitution is the achievement. Both proteins have to be inserted into a vesicle with the right orientation, and a sensor that faces the wrong side responds to nothing.

The output is measurable. The authors detect the phosphorylated regulator inside the vesicles, and the amount rises with the ligand outside, which is the definition of a transmitted signal.

The design question is the membrane's composition. A bacterial membrane has the right lipid environment for a bacterial protein, and a synthetic vesicle has to approximate it or the kinase misfolds.

The application is a synthetic cell. A protocell that senses a chemical and responds is a protocell that can be programmed, and a sensor is the input half of a circuit.

The limitation is the response. A phosphorylated regulator is an intermediate, and a behaviour would require a downstream gene or enzyme. The paper's system stops at the signal.

The comparison with lipid-only vesicles is instructive. A vesicle that contains enzymes but no sensor can run a reaction, and a vesicle that senses can choose when to run it.

What the paper does not establish is robustness. A reconstituted system in a batch of vesicles is a system with a distribution of behaviours, and a single vesicle's reliability is a harder measurement.

For synthetic biology, the value is a modular part. A sensor that works in a synthetic membrane is a part that can be combined with others, and a library of such parts is what a synthetic cell needs.
The phosphate's transfer is the step that a membrane makes possible. A kinase that sits in the bilayer can hold a ligand outside and an active site inside at the same time, and a soluble protein cannot span that distance. The reconstitution therefore tests the membrane's role rather than the proteins' alone.
The leak is the failure mode that the authors had to exclude. A vesicle that is permeable to the phosphorylated regulator reports a signal that never crossed, and the paper's control with an empty vesicle is what rules the artefact out.
The prospects for a synthetic cell are the reason the result matters. A cell that grows, divides and responds needs a sensor, and a sensor that functions in a lipid bilayer is the first of the three capabilities that synthetic biology has to assemble from parts.
