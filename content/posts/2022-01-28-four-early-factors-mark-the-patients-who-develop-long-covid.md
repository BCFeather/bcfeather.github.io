Long COVID has been described mostly by its symptoms, and symptom lists are poor guides to mechanism. Fatigue, breathlessness and brain fog overlap with a dozen other conditions, and a patient who reports them months after infection may have a persisting virus, an autoimmune process, damaged tissue or a deconditioned body. Distinguishing those paths requires measurements taken before the outcome is known.

A multi-omic study followed 309 patients from initial diagnosis through convalescence two to three months later, combining transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, single-cell immune profiling and clinical records. The analysis, published in *Cell*, 25 January 2022, looked for features present at diagnosis that anticipated later sequelae.

Four factors did. Type 2 diabetes, SARS-CoV-2 RNA detected in blood, Epstein-Barr virus viremia and specific autoantibodies each raised the risk of persistent symptoms, and their immunological associations weakened over time, leaving distinct convalescent immune states. Patients with gastrointestinal sequelae showed unusual dynamics of SARS-CoV-2-specific and cytomegalovirus-specific CD8+ T cells during recovery.

The design's strength is its timing. Measuring at diagnosis avoids the trap of comparing patients who are already months into an illness of unknown duration, where the immune signature may be a consequence of the symptoms rather than a cause. The presence of EBV viremia points to a mechanism that has been suspected since the first SARS outbreak, in which a herpesvirus reactivates under the pressure of a new infection and adds its own inflammatory load.

A cohort of 309 is large for multi-omic work and small for epidemiology. The patients came from a single health system, the follow-up ends at two to three months, and the autoantibody panel covers a fraction of the possible reactivities. The four factors also overlap, so the paper identifies markers of risk rather than four independent causes.

The clinical use would be triage: a panel run at diagnosis could identify the patients who need close follow-up, and the endotypes suggest that different sequelae may need different trials rather than one broad long-COVID study. Whether treating any of the four factors prevents the sequelae is the question the design cannot answer.
