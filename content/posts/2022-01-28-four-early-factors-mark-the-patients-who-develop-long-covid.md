Long COVID has been described mostly by its symptoms, and symptom lists are poor guides to mechanism.Cohorts assembled after recovery reverse the arrow of inference, because the measurement then reflects the illness rather than the conditions that produced it. Fatigue, breathlessness and brain fog overlap with a dozen other conditions, and a patient who reports them months after infection may have a persisting virus, an autoimmune process, damaged tissue or a deconditioned body. Distinguishing those paths requires measurements taken before the outcome is known.

A multi-omic study followed 309 patients from initial diagnosis through convalescence two to three months later, combining transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, single-cell immune profiling and clinical records. The analysis, published in *Cell*, 25 January 2022, looked for features present at diagnosis that anticipated later sequelae. Recruitment began at diagnosis, so every sample preceded the outcome it later predicted.

Four factors did. Type 2 diabetes, SARS-CoV-2 RNA detected in blood, Epstein-Barr virus viremia and specific autoantibodies each raised the risk of persistent symptoms, and their immunological associations weakened over time, leaving distinct convalescent immune states. Patients with gastrointestinal sequelae showed unusual dynamics of SARS-CoV-2-specific and cytomegalovirus-specific CD8+ T cells during recovery. That pattern points to a local mucosal process, although the cells were sampled from blood rather than from tissue.

The design's strength is its timing. Measuring at diagnosis avoids the trap of comparing patients who are already months into an illness of unknown duration, where the immune signature may be a consequence of the symptoms rather than a cause. The presence of EBV viremia points to a mechanism that has been suspected since the first SARS outbreak, in which a herpesvirus reactivates under the pressure of a new infection and adds its own inflammatory load.The autoantibodies were not a generic sign of immune activation; they bound named targets, which makes them a candidate mechanism rather than a marker.

Clinical and molecular variables sit on the same footing in the analysis, which is why the authors treat the four as axes of a risk space rather than as four separate tests. A cohort of 309 is large for multi-omic work and small for epidemiology.Symptoms were self-reported, and the cohort came from patients who sought care, which selects for severity and misses the mildest cases. The patients came from a single health system, the follow-up ends at two to three months, and the autoantibody panel covers a fraction of the possible reactivities. The four factors also overlap, so the paper identifies markers of risk rather than four independent causes.

The clinical use would be triage: a panel run at diagnosis could identify the patients who need close follow-up, and the endotypes suggest that different sequelae may need different trials rather than one broad long-COVID study. Whether treating any of the four factors prevents the sequelae is the question the design cannot answer. Trials that enrol by endotype would test that claim directly.
