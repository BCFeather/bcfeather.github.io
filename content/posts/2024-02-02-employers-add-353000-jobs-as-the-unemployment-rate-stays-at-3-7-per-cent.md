American employers added 353,000 jobs in January, almost twice the 185,000 that forecasters expected, and the unemployment rate held at 3.7 per cent. The report published on 2 February also revised the previous two months up by 126,000. The figure was the largest in a year, and the revisions added to a picture in which the employment has grown faster than the population for six months. Hiring at this pace is not what a slowing economy produces. The gains were spread across health care, professional services and government, and the leisure sector added staff for a ninth month. A labour market that broadens is one that is still absorbing people rather than replacing them. The participation rate held, and the share of prime-age workers in work stayed near its highest since 2001, which leaves little room for further gains.

Wages rose 0.6 per cent on the month and 4.5 per cent on the year, the fastest since 2021, and the figure was the detail that moved the bond market. Pay that accelerates is pay that the central bank cannot ignore when it sets the rate. The wage measure that the committee prefers, the employment cost index, has been rising more slowly than the payroll figure, and the two have disagreed for a year.

Hours worked fell slightly, the one soft number in the report, and the average week is now shorter than it was before the pandemic. A shorter week with more jobs is a productivity question rather than a demand one. The survey of households told a different story, with employment falling and the number of people looking for work rising slightly. The average weekly hours fell to 34.1, and the economists read that as a sign that the employers are stretching the staff they have rather than hiring more.

Prices in the bond market fell within minutes. The two-year yield rose by fifteen basis points, the expected date of the first cut moved from May to June, and the shares that had rallied on the hope of lower rates gave back part of the gain. A strong report is a report that costs the holders of bonds. The economists who had forecast a slowdown said the report showed the economy was still growing above its trend, which is a reason for the committee to wait.

What the month shows is that the labour market has not responded to the highest rates in two decades, and the puzzle for the committee is why. A market that had priced six cuts in the year repriced to four within the session, and the repricing is the transmission that the committee relies on. The next report is in March, and the forecasters have said the pace cannot continue without a rise in the participation.
