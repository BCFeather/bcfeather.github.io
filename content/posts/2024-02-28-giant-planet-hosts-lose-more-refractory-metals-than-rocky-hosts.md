description: "Across 227 planet-hosting stars in APOGEE, the fall of refractory abundances with condensation temperature is weak overall but steeper for stars with giant planets than for stars with rocky ones."
---

A star that has formed planets carries a chemical record of the process, because elements that condense at high temperature are the first to be locked into solids and the first to be removed from the gas that becomes the star. Plotting the abundance of a refractory element against its condensation temperature gives a slope, and a star that has sequestered such material into planets should show a steeper slope than one that has not.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 29 February 2024 tests that expectation at scale. The authors take thin-disk stars from the seventeenth data release of APOGEE, cross-match them against the Kepler Input Catalog to keep only stars with confirmed planets, and end up with 227 planet-hosting stars that have reliable abundances for six refractory elements. The elements span a condensation temperature range wide enough to fit a slope, which is the measurement every comparison in the paper rests on.

The control sample is the part that makes the comparison work. Galactic chemical evolution moves abundances with radius and age, so the authors build a comparison set of stars without detected planets matched in evolutionary stage and stellar parameters, use it to subtract that trend, and then measure the slope of the corrected abundances.

The overall result is a null. Planet-hosting stars as a group do not differ significantly in slope from stars without planets, which is the answer that the earlier literature had left contested and which a matched sample of this size can now give. Each element contributes one point to the fit, and the fit is a straight line through them.

Splitting the sample by planet type changes the picture. Stars hosting giant planets are more depleted in refractory elements than stars hosting rocky planets, and within the giant-planet hosts the sub-Jovian systems show steeper depletion than the Jovian ones, which points to different formation routes for Neptune-like and Jupiter-like planets rather than to one shared pathway.

Among the rocky-planet hosts the depletion correlates, tentatively, with the radius and the multiplicity of the planets, and the authors present that as a trend to be followed up rather than a result, since the numbers per bin are small and the abundance errors are at the scale of the effect. A trend that survives a larger sample would be the first evidence that rocky planets, and not only giants, leave a chemical mark.

What the sample cannot do is separate formation from migration. A star's refractory slope records how much condensable material left the disk, not where the planets formed or how far they moved afterwards, and a giant planet that formed beyond the ice line and migrated inward would leave the same signature as one that formed in place. Spectroscopy of host stars at higher precision, and larger samples from the next generation of surveys, are what would turn the split by planet type into a test of formation models.
