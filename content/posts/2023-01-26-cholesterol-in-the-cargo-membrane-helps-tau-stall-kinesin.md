Molecular motors carry cargo along microtubules, and the cargo is usually wrapped in a membrane. The motor attaches to the membrane, the membrane has a lipid composition, and the composition differs between organelles and between healthy and diseased cells.

The study in *PNAS*, 17 January 2023 asks whether that composition changes how a microtubule-associated protein interferes with transport. Qiaochu Li, Jing Xu and colleagues reconstitute kinesin-1 motors on membrane-bound cargo and vary the cholesterol content of the bilayer, then measure how tau affects the motors' movement.

Tau is a microtubule-associated protein that binds the lattice and competes with motors. In solution it inhibits kinesin, and the inhibition is graded: more tau means slower transport. The paper finds that cholesterol in the cargo membrane amplifies that inhibition, so that the same tau concentration produces a larger slowdown when the membrane is cholesterol-rich.

The mechanism is mechanical rather than chemical. A motor that binds a membrane pulls against the bilayer's resistance, and cholesterol stiffens the bilayer and increases the force needed to deform it. A motor working against a stiffer cargo has less margin, and tau's competition consumes the rest.

The result connects two fields that usually talk past each other. Membrane biophysics describes how lipid composition sets stiffness and order, and motor biophysics describes how load changes stepping. A cargo whose membrane is cholesterol-rich is a cargo under load, even before anything binds to it.

Disease relevance follows from the components. In Alzheimer's disease tau aggregates, and cholesterol metabolism is altered in the brain; whether the two interact through transport is a hypothesis the paper makes testable rather than one it settles.

The reconstitution is the strength. In a cell, thousands of variables confound the measurement, and a supported bilayer with a defined lipid composition and a defined motor density isolates the variable that the paper cares about.

What remains is the in vivo test. A neuron's cargo membranes vary by organelle, and the cholesterol content of a synaptic vesicle membrane differs from that of an endosome. Whether tau's effect on transport in a neuron depends on which cargo is moving is a question that imaging in live cells could answer.

The broader implication is that transport defects in disease may be partly a membrane phenomenon. A therapy that changes lipid composition would be a strange kind of neuroprotection, and the paper's result makes that idea less strange.

The measurement that carries the argument is the motor's run length. A kinesin that detaches sooner travels less far per encounter with the microtubule, and the paper's distributions of run length show the effect of cholesterol even when tau is held constant.
Cholesterol also orders the bilayer, and an ordered bilayer binds the motor's tail differently. The paper separates the mechanical effect from the binding effect by varying the lipid composition without changing the motor's attachment chemistry.
For neurodegenerative disease, the practical question is whether lowering membrane cholesterol would restore transport. Statins lower plasma cholesterol and not membrane cholesterol, and the brain's lipid metabolism is largely independent of the liver's, so the pharmacological route is not obvious.
