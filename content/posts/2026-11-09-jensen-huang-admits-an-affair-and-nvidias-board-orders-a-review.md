Jensen Huang sent a letter to NVIDIA's employees on 8 November that admitted an affair, and the company published it on its newsroom the same day under the heading 'A Message to Our Company'. The chief executive wrote that he had been unfaithful to his wife, that the conduct had hurt her and their children, and that he offered no excuse for it. He accepted full responsibility and the criticism that would follow, and he asked that his family be given the privacy the situation deserved. He also wrote that leading NVIDIA had been the privilege of his life and that he intended to stay at the company and earn back the trust of colleagues, partners and shareholders. The letter named nobody else, and the family has made no public statement.

Within hours the board answered with a statement of its own. A committee of independent directors will oversee an independent review, and outside counsel has been retained to assist it. The directors said that Huang supported the review and had undertaken to cooperate fully, and that they retained their confidence in him and in his leadership team. Nothing in the statement suggested that his position was in question, and the board promised to say more when it could.

Reporting before the letter had already raised the question the review has to answer. *The New York Times* and others had described a relationship with a businesswoman whose company, a data-centre operator based in Singapore, buys NVIDIA's systems. A personal relationship with a customer's executive is a conflict of interest that a board has to test rather than ignore, and the disclosure in the letter made that test public. The letter itself dealt with the family and said nothing about the commercial side.

Traders treated the news as a governance event rather than an earnings one. NVIDIA shares fell more than three per cent in after-hours trading on 9 November, and the options market priced a wider range for the session in New York. The company sits at the centre of the largest capital-spending cycle the industry has seen, and its customers have committed to capacity years ahead. A board review can run for months while the orders continue, and the price of the shares has been set by the demand for the chips rather than by the character of the man who sells them.

Reaction from outside the company split along familiar lines. President Trump, asked about the letter, said Huang was a good person and that he had heard nothing to suggest the chief executive had done anything wrong. Elon Musk reposted an assessment that credited Huang with building the most important company in the industry while allowing that he had made mistakes, and said he agreed with it. In the trade press the argument turned quickly to whether a founder's private conduct changes the value of what he built.

What the review produces will matter more than the letter did. Its scope, the standard it applies and the moment it reports will tell investors whether the affair touched the company's business, and the board has promised to publish more when it can. The episode also tests a habit of founder-led firms, where the leader is the brand and is harder to separate from the company than any employee. Huang has run NVIDIA for thirty-three years and has become the public face of the artificial-intelligence boom, and the letter he wrote on 8 November was addressed to the people who depend on him rather than to the public. Whether that framing holds through the review, and whether the customers who buy the systems accept it, will decide how much of this is remembered as a family matter and how much as a governance one.
