---

Insulin is the reliable drug for type 2 diabetes and an incomplete one. A patient whose glucose stays high on a titrated basal insulin has usually exhausted the simple adjustments, and adding a second agent means choosing between weight gain, hypoglycaemia and cost. The incretin class offered a different trade-off, and a dual agonist promised more of it. The two incretin hormones act on different receptors, and their combined effect on appetite is greater than either alone.

Tirzepatide activates both the glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor and the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor, which is one receptor more than the drugs already on the market. The SURPASS-5 trial tested it as an addition to insulin glargine in 475 adults whose glycated haemoglobin remained high despite the insulin, randomizing them to 5, 10 or 15 milligrams weekly or to placebo for 40 weeks. The results appeared in *JAMA*, 1 February 2022.

At week 40, glycated haemoglobin fell by 2.40 percentage points at the 10-milligram dose and 2.34 points at 15 milligrams, against 0.86 points with placebo. The differences from placebo were 1.53 and 1.47 points, with 97.5 per cent confidence intervals that excluded zero. The trial was conducted at 45 centres in eight countries, and 94.9 per cent of participants completed it. The analysis reported results for the modified intention-to-treat population, which is everyone who received at least one dose.

Weight is the secondary endpoint that separates this class from insulin. Tirzepatide produced weight loss while insulin glargine usually produces gain, and the combination therefore addresses two problems that patients on insulin face together. The mechanism is incretin-driven: slower gastric emptying, better glucose-dependent insulin release and a reduction in appetite.

Discontinuation tells a less favourable story. Treatment stopped early in 10 per cent of the 5-milligram group, 12 per cent of the 10-milligram group, 18 per cent of the 15-milligram group and 3 per cent of the placebo group, which is the pattern of a drug whose gastrointestinal effects are dose-dependent and poorly tolerated by some patients. Nausea and vomiting drove most of the discontinuations, and they were most frequent during the weeks of dose escalation.

The trial lasted 40 weeks, which is long enough to measure glycaemic control and short enough to miss the outcomes that decide cardiovascular benefit. Participants were mostly White and middle-aged, the doses were escalated on a fixed schedule rather than by tolerance, and the comparison is against placebo rather than against an active second agent. A 40-week trial also cannot address the pancreatic and thyroid safety questions that regulators asked of this class.

For a patient already on insulin, the result is a reason to consider a dual agonist before escalating the insulin further. For a health system, the price of the class and the discontinuation rate are the terms that will decide how widely it is used. Whether the drug reduces heart attacks and strokes is the question that a dedicated cardiovascular outcome trial was designed to answer.
