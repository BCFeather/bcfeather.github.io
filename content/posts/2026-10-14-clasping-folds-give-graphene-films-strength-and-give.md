A paper in *Nature* on 14 October reports a graphene film that reaches 2.9 GPa in tensile strength, stretches 5 per cent before it breaks, and absorbs 84.5 megajoules per cubic metre on the way there. Wang Lidan, Liao Yangchao and colleagues at several Chinese and American laboratories built the film from folded flakes rather than flat ones, and the fold is the point. The route is mechanical rather than chemical, which is why it can be scaled on the film-handling equipment that manufacturers already own.

Flat sheets stack into films whose strength rises with the order of the stacking. Order also makes them brittle, because a film of neatly aligned platelets has little room to give before a crack runs through it. Bending individual sheets into controlled shapes would let a film store strain energy and slow that crack, but two-dimensional sheets resist being folded at production scale, and earlier routes to better films worked on the order of the stack rather than on the shape of each flake.

The route here starts from graphene oxide flakes whose surfaces already carry folds, made by a process the authors call modular assembly. Those folded flakes settle into a lamellar film in which the fold of one sheet clasps the sheet above it, so the layers cannot slide past each other under load. The authors describe the resulting transfer of stress as a shear lock, and they report a switch in the topology of the force chains that carry load through the film, from a pulling arrangement to a pushing one as the clasped layers engage.

That combination is what the numbers measure. A strength of 2.9 GPa puts the film near the top of the range reported for macroscopic graphene assemblies, and 5 per cent elastic strain is large for a material of that strength. Toughness of 84.5 megajoules per cubic metre is the energy the film absorbs before failure, and here the clasping structure does the work: the folds have to be pulled apart before the sheets can separate, which turns a fast fracture into a slower one.

Films that combine stiffness with elasticity are wanted for lightweight panels, flexible substrates and structural composites, where a material that fails without warning is the usual objection to carbon films. The wider claim of the paper is about design: if the spatial conformation of each flake can be set at the scale of a production process, then the properties of a two-dimensional material become a manufacturing parameter rather than a laboratory accident.

Scale is where the claim will be tested. These films are made through graphene oxide, which has to be reduced afterwards, and the reduction step is the one that usually leaves defects and shifts the mechanical numbers. The paper reports strength and toughness at the coupon level; fatigue, humidity and repeated bending are the questions a structural application would ask first. Until those are answered, the result stands as a demonstration that fold geometry can be engineered, not as a material with a datasheet.
