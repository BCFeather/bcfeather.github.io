Economists had expected 10.0. The Office for National Statistics published the figures on 19 October, and consumer prices rose 10.1 per cent in the year to September. The reading was up from 9.9 per cent in August, when the government's energy price guarantee had capped bills. Food was the largest contributor. Food and drink prices rose 14.6 per cent over the year, the fastest since 1980, and bread, milk and eggs rose more than 20 per cent. The war in Ukraine had raised the cost of wheat and of fertilizer, and the weak pound had raised the cost of imports. Supermarkets said they had held prices down longer than they could afford. The figure was the highest since 1982 and the fifth consecutive month above 9 per cent.

Energy did not fall as expected. The price guarantee capped the average bill at 2,500 pounds, and the statisticians had assumed it would cut the contribution of energy to the index. The actual cap was applied in October, and the September figure still reflected the old prices. The energy component rose 27 per cent over the year. The price of a pint of milk rose 40 per cent in a year, and a loaf of bread 20.

Core inflation, which excludes food, energy, alcohol and tobacco, was 6.5 per cent, unchanged from August. Services inflation was 6.1 per cent, and it was the measure the Bank of England said it watched most closely. The bank had raised rates to 2.25 per cent in September, and it was expected to raise again in November. The cap was set at 2,500 pounds for a typical household, and it replaced a guarantee of 3,549.

Import prices were still rising at 12 per cent over the year. The figures came a day after the fiscal statement was reversed and a day before the prime minister resigned. The political chaos had pushed the pound to a record low in September, and the currency had recovered to 1.13 by the time the data was published. Real wages were falling. Regular pay rose 5.4 per cent in the three months to August, and prices rose 10, so the real terms fall was 4.6 per cent. That was the largest fall since records began in 2001. Household consumption fell 0.6 per cent in the second quarter and again in the third. The bank's own survey of companies showed that they expected to raise prices by 5 per cent next year.

Rates went up by 0.75 points on 3 November to 3 per cent, the largest increase since 1989. It said it expected inflation to peak at 11 per cent in the fourth quarter and to fall to 5 per cent by the end of 2023. The forecast also showed a recession of eight consecutive quarters, which would be the longest since the 1920s.

Inflation peaked at 11.1 per cent in October and fell slowly through 2023. Food prices kept rising into the spring, and the rate was 8.7 per cent in April. The bank's forecast of a long recession did not materialize, because the labour market stayed tight and because energy prices fell faster than expected.
