Consumer prices rose 2.9 per cent in the twelve months to July, the first reading below three per cent since 2021, and the measure that excludes food and energy rose 3.2 per cent, the smallest increase since the spring of 2021. The report was published on 14 August and was the last inflation reading before the September meeting. Food prices rose more slowly than in June and energy was cheaper than a year earlier, which is the arithmetic that took the headline measure below the round number.

Composition is what the committee watches. Goods prices have been falling for a year, and the remaining pressure is in services, where wages are the largest cost and where the labour market is still tight. A report that shows goods deflation and services inflation is a report that says the last mile is a labour market problem. Wages in services are still rising faster than prices, and a business that has to pay more for staff passes the cost on or closes.

Shelter is the slowest component. Rents and owners' equivalent rent are measured with a lag of months, and the deceleration that the private data showed last year is only now arriving in the index. A component that is one third of the basket and is falling slowly is a component that sets the pace. The index that measures rents is computed from a survey of tenants that is repeated every six months, so a market that turned two years ago appears in the data now.

Market's reaction was to price more cuts. Futures moved to price a larger reduction in September, and the two-year yield fell, which is the market's way of saying that the labour market matters more than the price index. A market that prices a cut before the meeting is a market that has read the same data. The ten-year yield also fell, which means the bond market expects a slower economy rather than only a lower policy rate.

Political context was unusual. The report was published in the middle of a campaign in which prices were the central issue, and both candidates claimed it as evidence for their argument. A statistic that is used by both sides is a statistic whose credibility is being tested as much as its content. An incumbent who claims the number as a victory and a challenger who calls it a lie are both describing the same index, and confidence in it has fallen in surveys.

What the reading establishes is that the disinflation is real and incomplete. A rate of 2.9 per cent is close to the target by the standards of the past two years and far from it by the standard of the past two decades, and the committee has to decide which comparison governs. The committee meets in September with a labour report and one more price report in hand, and the size of the cut is the decision that the data will not make for it.
