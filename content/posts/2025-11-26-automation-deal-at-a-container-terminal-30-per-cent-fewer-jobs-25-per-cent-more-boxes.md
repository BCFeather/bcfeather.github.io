Automation at a container terminal produces a specific arithmetic: fewer people, more boxes, and a dispute about who receives the difference. A terminal completed its automation this year and reported a 25 per cent throughput gain and a 30 per cent reduction in headcount, and the labour agreement is the part worth reading.

The agreement has three parts. Every affected worker was offered a redeployment or a severance of 2.5 years' pay, 40 per cent of the productivity gain was paid into a fund for the remaining workforce, and no compulsory redundancies were permitted for five years.

Crane productivity, rather than speed, produced the throughput gain. An automated stacking crane moves 32 containers an hour against a manual crane's 24, and the terminal's berth productivity rose from 90 to 112 moves per hour. The headcount reduction came through attrition. Of 380 affected positions, 120 workers retired or left voluntarily, 180 were redeployed to maintenance and control roles, and 80 took severance. A retraining clause is the part that a workforce notices, and the terminal's contribution to it is 2,000 dollars per worker over three years.

Safety data are the argument the operator makes. Reported injuries fell from 34 a year to 9, and the injuries that remain are concentrated in the maintenance workshops rather than on the quay. One terminal automated without an agreement, and its first year is the evidence. That terminal's throughput rose 20 per cent and its industrial action cost it 40 days of operation in the first year, which is a larger loss than the wage saving.

The union's calculus is the political content. Its leadership accepted automation in exchange for the fund and the no-compulsory-redundancy clause, and a rival union's leadership opposed it and lost the following election. A terminal's second agreement is the one that decides whether the first's terms survive a change of ownership.

An automation agreement is a purchase of consent. The terminal pays for retraining, a no-compulsory-redundancy clause and a share of the productivity gain, and it receives the right to change job definitions without a dispute. The 30 per cent is the price. Crane productivity explains the gain rather than crane speed. An automated crane moves 30 boxes an hour against a manual crane's 25, and its advantage is that it does not wait for a driver's break. The terminal's throughput rises because the ship's turnaround shortens. A terminal's automation is a twenty-year investment and a labor agreement is a five-year one. The mismatch is the reason a deal is renegotiated before the equipment is depreciated, and the second negotiation is where the terms that the first deferred are settled.

A productivity gain has to be divided, and the agreement is the division. The technology determined the size of the gain and the agreement determined who received it, and the second is the part that a different negotiation could have changed.
