description: "A Gaussian mixture fitted to fixation durations in a public eye-tracking dataset resolves five types, and the mix of types shifts towards shorter fixations as the hundred-second task goes on."

A random saccade task asks a subject to look at a target each time it jumps, and the time spent looking between jumps is the fixation duration. The task is simple enough that a large public dataset exists, and the durations in it are not a single distribution but a mixture. Latency and duration are separate measurements, and the task is designed so that the second can be read without the first.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 3 June 2024 analyses that mixture. The frequency histogram of fixation durations is multimodal, the authors fit a Gaussian mixture to it, and the fit resolves five types of fixation rather than one. A Gaussian mixture is the natural model for a multimodal histogram, and five components are what the fit selects.

The ideal response is rare. A single accurate saccade after each target movement, with a latency of 200 to 250 milliseconds followed by a long fixation of more than 800 milliseconds, occurs, but it accounts for only about ten per cent of all fixations and is the first fixation after a jump only 23.4 per cent of the time. An ideal trial would end with the eye on target and no correction, and the data show that most trials do not.

Common responses involve a correction. In 57.4 per cent of cases the first fixation after a jump is short, with a mean of 117.7 milliseconds, and it is usually followed by a corrective saccade that brings the eye to the target. A corrective saccade is a second movement after the first lands short, and its frequency is what marks the short fixation as a search.

Duration falls over the task. Across the hundred seconds, the median total fixation duration decreases, and the decrease follows a power law with a coefficient of determination of 0.94, which is a remarkably clean fit for a behavioural measure. A power law is a strong claim for a hundred-second record, and the fit is reported with its coefficient rather than as an assertion.

The mixture also shifts. The three shortest fixation types become more frequent with time on task and the two longest become less frequent, and each of those trends follows a power law of its own, with coefficients between 0.73 and 0.93. The two long types dominate early and the three short ones dominate late, so the change is in the composition and not only in the mean.

Fatigue is one reading. Long fixations dominate the first fifteen to twenty-two seconds and then decline, so the ability to produce the ideal response decays quickly, which the authors connect to the known decline of saccade accuracy over time. What the analysis cannot establish is causation. A power law describes the decline and does not explain it, and whether the cause is muscular, attentional or a change in strategy is a question for a different experiment.
