Google published results from a superconducting processor called Willow, a hundred and five qubits made in its own fabrication line, showing that a surface code operated below the threshold at which error correction starts to work. The result is the first clear demonstration that adding qubits to a logical qubit suppresses errors instead of adding them. A chip that is made in-house is a chip whose process the company can tune without a foundry's schedule.

Threshold is the concept that matters. A surface code spreads one logical qubit across many physical ones and checks them for errors, and the scheme only helps if the physical error rate is below a critical value. Below that value the logical error falls exponentially as the code grows, and above it the code makes things worse. A physical error rate above the threshold is a rate at which more qubits make a worse computer. A physical error rate above the threshold is a rate at which more qubits produce a worse computer.

Numbers in the paper describe how fast that fall is. Increasing the code distance by two suppressed the logical error by a factor of about two in the smaller device and by about seven in the larger one, and both devices preserved quantum information for longer than their best physical qubit. A factor of seven is a suppression that grows exponentially, which is the difference between a demo and a design.

Engineering is as important as the physics. The processor was fabricated in a facility that the company built, the control electronics were designed with the chip, and the decoder that interprets the error signals runs in real time within the microsecond cycle of the machine. A quantum computer is a system, and the system is what the paper demonstrates. A decoder that runs in real time is a decoder that cannot be replaced by software run overnight.

Demonstration is also a landmark in the other direction. Google ran a random circuit sampling task in minutes that it estimates would take a classical supercomputer an astronomical time, and that claim has been contested before and will be contested again. A benchmark that cannot be checked is a benchmark that carries little weight. A benchmark claim that depends on a classical simulation is a claim that a rival can dispute with a better algorithm.

What the result changes is the roadmap. A company that can show error suppression by scaling is a company that can ask for the money to build a larger machine, and the next milestone is not more qubits but more logical ones. The distance from a hundred physical qubits to a useful computer is still measured in orders of magnitude.
