title: "The first clear carbon dioxide in an exoplanet sky"
date: "2023-02-23T23:50:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "Observations of the hot Jupiter WASP-39b with the James Webb Space Telescope, published in Nature on 23 February 2023, produced the first unambiguous detection of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere of a planet outside the solar system."
---

WASP-39b is a gas giant about the size of Saturn and the mass of Jupiter, orbiting a star seven hundred light-years away once every four days. It is close enough to its star that its atmosphere is inflated and its temperature is high, and it passes in front of the star from the point of view of the Earth, which makes it a good target for transmission spectroscopy. When the planet transits, a thin slice of its atmosphere is backlit by the star, and the wavelengths that the atmosphere absorbs appear as dips in the starlight.

The observation used the near-infrared spectrograph on the James Webb Space Telescope, in a mode that covers a wide range of wavelengths at moderate resolution. The paper reported water at very high significance across four separate bands, sodium at 0.58 micrometres, carbon monoxide at seven sigma, and a prominent absorption feature at 4.3 micrometres that belongs to carbon dioxide. The carbon dioxide signal was the first unambiguous detection of that molecule in an exoplanet atmosphere, and it was reported at twenty-eight sigma.

Hints of the molecule had come from older telescopes, which could not confirm it. The Spitzer measurements had produced bumps in the spectrum that were consistent with the molecule, but the wavelength coverage was too narrow and the noise too high for a claim that the field would accept. Webb's spectrum covered the feature with enough resolution to separate it from the neighbouring water and carbon monoxide bands, which is what turned a hint into a measurement.

Methane, which models predict should be scarce in an atmosphere this hot, did not appear in the spectrum. That absence is informative, because methane is destroyed by photochemistry at these temperatures, and its absence supports the picture in which the upper atmosphere is being cooked by the star rather than sitting in equilibrium. The same photochemistry produces sulfur dioxide, which a companion paper in the same issue identified at 4.1 micrometres.

The abundance pattern points to a formation story. The best-fitting models used a metallicity about ten times the solar value, which means the atmosphere is enriched in elements heavier than helium, and the enrichment is the kind that comes from accreting rocky material as well as gas. The carbon-to-oxygen ratio came out near or below the solar value, which is a constraint on where in the disk the planet formed and how far it moved afterwards. The models also assumed moderate cloud opacity and a temperature profile that the shape of the continuum could constrain.

What the measurement establishes is a method as much as a result. Carbon dioxide is one of the molecules that a biosignature search would look for in the atmospheres of smaller, cooler planets, and the demonstration that Webb can isolate it in a hot Jupiter is the step that makes the search credible. The planet itself is uninhabitable and uninteresting as a place, and the spectrum taken from it is the template for the ones that will matter later.
