The national carbon market expanded in March to cover steel, cement and aluminium smelting, adding about 1,500 installations to a scheme that had previously covered only the power sector. The expansion brings the market to more than 4 billion tonnes of covered emissions, roughly 60 per cent of the country's total. What changes is the number of firms and the diversity of the processes being priced, and both were the reason the expansion was delayed twice. The three sectors together account for about a third of industrial emissions, and they had been named as candidates since 2021.

Design follows the power sector's: free allocation based on historical intensity, a benchmark that tightens each year. A plant that beats the benchmark keeps the surplus, and a plant that misses it buys the difference. A carbon price that is high enough to change the technology is also high enough to close mills, which is a political decision rather than an environmental one.

Steel is the hard case. A blast furnace and a basic oxygen converter produce emissions that are difficult to abate, and the alternatives, hydrogen-based direct reduction and electric arc furnaces, cost more and depend on scrap or on green hydrogen that does not yet exist at scale. A carbon price that is high enough to change the technology is also high enough to close mills. Two-thirds of its emissions come from the calcination of limestone rather than from fuel, so a plant cannot fix the problem by switching to gas. Clinker substitution is limited by the standards that specify how much of it a bag of cement may contain.

Cement has a similar problem with a different chemistry. The abatement options are clinker substitution and carbon capture, and both need capital that the price of a permit will not cover at current levels. A low price is a deliberate choice: the scheme is designed to make firms measure and report before it makes them pay.

Prices in this market have been low. Allowances have traded at a fraction of the European price since the scheme began, because the cap is generous and because banking is limited. The cap, rather than the price, is where the policy sits, and the cap has been set with room to grow. Fixing the double counting is a technical problem with political consequences, which is why the expansion was announced with a two-year transition period. Banking of unused allowances is limited, so a plant cannot save a surplus for a year when output is high.

The next step is the link between the carbon market and the electricity market. A steel mill that buys electricity is paying for the power sector's permits through its tariff, and the free allocation it receives does not account for that. For now the market is a ledger with a price attached, and the ledger is doing more work than the price. A price that barely moves is a price that firms can ignore when they plan their next furnace.
