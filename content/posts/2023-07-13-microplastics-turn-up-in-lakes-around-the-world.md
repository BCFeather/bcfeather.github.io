A lake is a closed basin, and what enters it tends to stay. That makes a lake a record of its catchment's pollution, and a comparison across lakes a comparison across the ways people use land.

The survey in *Nature*, 13 July 2023 makes that comparison. The authors sample 38 lakes and reservoirs in 23 countries, filter the water, and count and identify the microplastic particles.

The result is universal contamination. Every sampled lake contains microplastics, and the concentrations vary with the catchment's population and with the lake's size.

The variation is the informative part. A lake in a densely populated basin carries more than one in a remote basin, and a large lake dilutes what a small one concentrates.

The size distribution is the method's window. A net with a mesh of a certain size catches particles above it, and the particles below it are counted by spectroscopy after filtration.

The sources are multiple. A tyre's wear, a textile's fibres and a package's fragments all contribute, and the polymer composition distinguishes them.

The ecological concern is the exposure. A lake's plankton can ingest particles in the size range that the survey measures, and ingestion is the route from water to food web.

The limitation is the method's comparability. Laboratories count particles differently, and a survey's value depends on a common protocol that the authors impose.

What the paper does not show is a trend. A single sampling cannot distinguish an accumulation from a steady state, and a repeated survey would.

For water management, the value is a starting point. A lake's microplastic load is a quantity that a regulator can measure, and a measurement is the precondition for a limit.

The reservoir's distinction matters. A reservoir's outflow is controlled, and a lake's is not, so that a reservoir's residence time is set by an operator and a lake's by its basin.

The catchment's land use is the predictor the authors test. Agriculture, urbanisation and industry each contribute a signature, and the correlation between land use and concentration is the paper's explanatory result.

The atmospheric input is a route that a survey of water alone misses. Fibres fall from the air, and a remote lake can receive them without a river.

The treatment's effect is an open question. A wastewater plant removes some particles and passes others, and a lake downstream of a plant is a test of the removal's efficiency.

The policy frame is the same as for marine debris. A limit needs a baseline, and a baseline needs a protocol that many laboratories can follow.
The particle's density determines its fate. A polymer denser than water sinks and one lighter floats, and a lake's mixing determines whether either stays in the water column. The authors' sampling depth is therefore part of the result.
The spectroscopy is the identification step. A particle's infrared spectrum names the polymer, and a count without an identification would describe debris rather than plastics.
The lake's size is the dilution variable the analysis uses. A small lake with a small inflow accumulates, and a large lake with a large inflow flushes, and the comparison across sizes is how the authors separate loading from retention.
