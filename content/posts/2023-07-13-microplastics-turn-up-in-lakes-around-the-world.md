A lake is a closed basin, and what enters it tends to stay, which makes a lake a record of its catchment's pollution and makes a comparison across lakes a comparison across the ways people use land. In *Nature* on 13 July 2023, that comparison is drawn from 38 lakes and reservoirs in 23 countries, where the authors filter the water and then count and identify the microplastic particles they retain.

Contamination turns out to be universal. Every sampled lake contains microplastics, and concentrations rise and fall with the catchment's population and with the lake's own size. Land use is the predictor the authors test, since agriculture, urbanization and industry each leave a signature, and the correlation between land use and concentration is the survey's explanatory result.

Variation carries the information. A lake in a densely populated basin holds more than one in a remote basin, and a large lake dilutes what a small one concentrates. Size also sets the dilution variable the analysis uses, because a small lake with a small inflow accumulates while a large lake with a large inflow flushes, and the comparison across sizes separates loading from retention.

Size distribution defines the method's window. A net of a given mesh catches particles above it, and finer particles are counted by spectroscopy after filtration, which in turn names the polymer and distinguishes debris from plastics. Density decides fate, since a polymer denser than water sinks and one lighter floats, and the sampling depth is part of the result.

Sources are multiple. Tyre wear, textile fibres and packaging fragments all contribute, and the polymer composition separates them. Atmospheric input is a route a survey of water alone misses, because fibres fall from the air and a remote lake can receive them without a river, while a reservoir's controlled outflow gives it a residence time set by an operator. Exposure is the ecological concern. Plankton can ingest particles in the size range measured here, and ingestion is the route from water into the food web. Comparability sets the limitation, as laboratories count particles differently and the survey's value depends on a common protocol the authors impose.

Trend is what the paper does not show, because a single sampling cannot separate accumulation from a steady state. Water management gains a starting point, since a load is a quantity a regulator can measure and a measurement is the precondition for a limit. Whether treatment removes or passes the particles remains open, and a lake downstream of a plant is the natural test of that efficiency.

A second survey five years later would turn the snapshot into a trend, and the protocol the authors standardized is exactly what such a repeat would need, which is the reason the comparability mattered more than the headline count. A reservoir's operator can also change the flushing rate within a season, which makes the managed lakes a natural experiment on retention that the free ones cannot provide, and the survey's spread of sizes already approximates it.
