A perovskite solar cell's efficiency has risen faster than any other photovoltaic technology's, and its durability has risen slower. Heat accelerates degradation, and a cell on a roof reaches temperatures a laboratory test at ambient does not replicate. In *Science* on 14 July 2023, the heat is addressed. The authors engineer the ligands passivating the perovskite's surface so their reactivity matches the defects they are meant to fix, and the resulting cells operate at elevated temperature with stable efficiency.

Passivation is the chemistry. A surface defect traps a charge and catalyses decomposition, and a ligand binding that defect can neutralize it, provided the ligand does not itself react with the perovskite. Design of the ligand is the paper's core, since a group binding strongly enough to stay and weakly enough to be replaced is the target, and a series of ligands charts the range.

Stability test supplies the evidence. A cell held at elevated temperature for many hours keeps its efficiency, and the control cell without the designed ligand does not. Temperature's role is kinetic, because a pathway slow at room temperature accelerates at sixty degrees, and a test at ambient misses the pathway that matters in the field.

Degradation mechanism frames the problem. Iodide migrates under heat and bias, and a surface resisting that migration is a surface slowing the cell's decline. Identity of the defect is the target, since a halide vacancy and an under-coordinated lead ion are the common sites, and a ligand binding the lead without donating a proton to the halide is the one that survives.

Efficiency is not sacrificed. A passivation improving stability at the cost of a little open-circuit voltage is a trade, and these cells avoid it. Band alignment constrains the design, because a passivating layer blocking extraction adds resistance, and a thin layer keeps the tunnelling barrier low. Tandem use supplies the motive. A perovskite on silicon must survive the silicon's processing and the module's operating temperature, and a heat-tolerant layer enables the tandem; a bulky ammonium salt passivates and degrades, while a designed ligand passivates and lasts.

Duration of the test sets the limitation. A thousand hours is not twenty-five years, and the acceleration factor connecting them is an assumption. Field performance stays unestablished, because a module in a humid climate meets moisture as well as heat, and encapsulation is the other half of the durability problem.

Photovoltaics gains a chemistry targeting a specific failure, since the field has improved stability by trial and error and a ligand with designed reactivity is a rational route. Protocol is the standardization issue still open, because a stability claim needs a defined temperature, bias and atmosphere, and the field's protocols are being written. A damp-heat test at eighty-five per cent humidity would ask the other half of the durability question, and the encapsulation that a module needs is where most field failures begin. A tandem built with this layer and measured under the same heat protocol would test whether the stability survives the extra processing, and that stack is the configuration the market would actually buy.
