A perovskite solar cell's efficiency has risen faster than any other photovoltaic technology's, and its durability has risen slower. Heat accelerates the degradation, and a cell on a roof reaches temperatures that a laboratory test at ambient does not replicate.

The approach in *Science*, 14 July 2023 addresses the heat. The authors engineer the ligands that passivate the perovskite's surface so that their reactivity is matched to the defects they are meant to fix, and the resulting cells operate at elevated temperature with stable efficiency.

The passivation is the chemistry. A surface defect traps a charge and catalyzes decomposition, and a ligand that binds the defect can neutralize it, if the ligand does not itself react with the perovskite.

The ligand's design is the paper's core. A group that binds strongly enough to stay and weakly enough to be replaced is the target, and the authors' series of ligands charts the range.

The stability test is the evidence. A cell held at an elevated temperature for many hours retains its efficiency, and the control cell without the designed ligand does not.

The mechanism of degradation is the frame. Iodide migrates under heat and bias, and a surface that resists the migration is a surface that slows the cell's decline.

The tandem application is the motive. A perovskite on silicon needs the perovskite to survive the silicon's processing and the module's operating temperature, and a heat-tolerant layer enables the tandem.

The efficiency is not sacrificed. A passivation that improves stability and costs a little open-circuit voltage is a trade, and the authors' cells avoid the trade.

The limitation is the test's duration. A thousand hours is not twenty-five years, and the acceleration factor that connects them is an assumption.

What the paper does not establish is field performance. A module in a humid climate faces moisture as well as heat, and the encapsulation is the other half of the durability problem.

For photovoltaics, the value is a chemistry that targets a specific failure. The field has improved stability by trial and error, and a ligand whose reactivity is designed is a rational route.

The defect's identity is the target. A halide vacancy and an under-coordinated lead ion are the common sites, and a ligand that binds the lead without donating a proton to the halide is the one that survives.

The temperature's role is kinetic. A degradation pathway that is slow at room temperature accelerates at sixty degrees, and a test that runs at ambient misses the pathway that matters in the field.

The interface's band alignment is a constraint. A passivating layer that blocks extraction adds resistance, and the authors' thin layer keeps the tunnelling barrier low.

The comparison with other passivants is the frame. A bulky ammonium salt passivates and degrades, and a designed ligand passivates and lasts.

The tandem's requirement is the push. A perovskite-silicon tandem's top cell absorbs blue light and heats, and a stable top cell is the tandem's bottleneck.

The measurement's protocol is the standardization issue. A stability claim needs a defined temperature, bias and atmosphere, and the field's protocols are still being written.
