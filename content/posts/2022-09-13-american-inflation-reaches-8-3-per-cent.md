---

On the month, prices rose 0.1 per cent, and core prices rose 0.6. The Labour Department published the report on 13 September, and consumer prices rose 8.3 per cent in the year to August. The forecast had been 8.1, and the reading was down only two tenths from July.

Core inflation, which excludes food and energy, rose 6.3 per cent over the year, up from 5.9 in July and the highest since March. Shelter rose 0.7 per cent on the month and 6.2 over the year, and rents were accelerating rather than slowing. Medical care services rose 0.8 per cent, and new vehicles rose 0.8 per cent as dealer markups persisted.

Energy fell 5 per cent over the month as gasoline came down from its June peak, and that was the reason the headline was lower. Food rose 0.8 per cent, and food at home rose 13.5 per cent over the year, the fastest since 1979. Electricity rose 15.8 per cent over the year, the fastest since 1981. The surprise was in services. Prices for transportation services rose 0.8 per cent, and airline fares rose 3.3 per cent after falling for two months. The category that officials watch most closely, services excluding shelter, rose 0.6 per cent, and it had not decelerated for four months.

Markets moved violently on the release. The S&P 500 fell 4.3 per cent, its worst day since June 2020, and the Nasdaq fell 5.1 per cent. The two-year Treasury yield rose above 3.8 per cent, its highest since 2007, and the dollar rose against every major currency. Futures priced a three-quarter point increase at the September meeting with a probability near 100 per cent, and they added a chance of a full point.

Both claims had some support in the data, and neither settled the question. The report changed the political argument as well as the market one. The White House said the numbers were unacceptable and pointed to the fall in gasoline, and the opposition said the stimulus of 2021 had caused the problem. The Federal Reserve met on 21 September and raised rates by three quarters for the third consecutive time. The statement said the committee would keep raising until inflation was on a path back to 2 per cent. Officials also raised their projection for the peak rate to 4.6 per cent, which the market had not priced.

Inflation peaked at 9.1 per cent in June and fell steadily from there, reaching 7.1 per cent in November. Shelter kept the core measure high into 2023, and the annual rate for that component peaked at 8.2 per cent in March. The August report was the last one that surprised the market to the upside in that cycle. The average thirty-year mortgage rate passed 6 per cent in the same week, its highest since 2008.
