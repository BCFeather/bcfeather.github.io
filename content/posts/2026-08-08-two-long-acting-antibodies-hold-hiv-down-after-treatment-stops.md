Analytical treatment interruption is the only way to test whether a strategy can replace daily antiretroviral therapy, and the field has spent a decade discovering that most strategies cannot. Broadly neutralising antibodies were the most promising candidate, since they bind the virus itself rather than a host receptor, and engineering them for a longer half-life through Fc modifications turned an infusion that lasted weeks into one that lasts months. The Fc modification is what buys the months, since the neonatal receptor recycles the antibody and extends a half-life from weeks to a quarter of a year.

The RIO trial randomised 68 adults who had started antiretroviral therapy in early infection and were virally suppressed, assigning 34 to two long-acting antibodies, 3BNC117-LS and 10-1074-LS, and 34 to saline, before interrupting treatment under double-blind conditions. Results appeared in *The Lancet HIV* in August, with viral rebound by week 20 as the endpoint that matters clinically.

Rebound occurred in eight participants in the antibody arm and 30 in the placebo arm. Three-quarters of the antibody group, 75 per cent with a 95 per cent confidence interval of 61 to 92, had not rebounded at 20 weeks, against 11 per cent in the placebo group, with an interval of 4 to 29. The hazard ratio for rebound was 0.09, with an interval of 0.04 to 0.21, and *P*<0.0001. That interval is wide because 34 participants per arm is a small trial, and the estimate would narrow in a confirmatory study of a thousand.

Sensitivity to the antibody is the entry condition, and that is the design's honest admission. Participants had no evidence of viral insensitivity to 10-1074 before enrolment, which selects for a virus the antibodies can actually bind. In a population where resistance to either antibody is common, the same regimen would fail differently, and the trial does not estimate how often that happens. Two antibodies are used rather than one because the virus escapes a single epitope quickly, and a combination raises the bar for escape while raising the cost of the regimen.

Safety tracked as expected for an infusion strategy. There were 326 adverse events in the antibody arm and 260 on placebo, including 19 treatment or procedure related events against 41, and none of the nine serious events was attributed to treatment. Fatigue and somnolence were the most frequent complaints, and two severe events, anal abscesses, occurred in the placebo arm. The asymmetry in procedure-related events may reflect the placebo arm's saline infusions being indistinguishable in frequency, so the difference is small enough to be noise.

The next question is durability rather than efficacy. A 20-week window is long for a virus that rebounds in weeks but short for a remission strategy, and the trial's optional second infusion suggests the investigators expect the effect to fade as antibody levels fall. Whether repeated dosing can extend control for years, and at what cost, is now the trial that matters.
