The Pacific warning system moved faster this week than the wave it was tracking, which is the whole point of the design. A seismic network detects the rupture, the message goes to a forecasting centre, the centre runs a model against a bathymetry grid, and a warning reaches a national agency within minutes. The wave takes longer. In the event of the twenty-ninth of July, the wave crossed open ocean at a speed no aeroplane could match, and the warning still beat it to the coastline.

The system is a chain, and each link has its own latency, and the total is what the person on the beach experiences as a single number of minutes. A seismometer in the north Pacific reports in seconds. The automatic centre that turns the reading into a preliminary magnitude takes a minute. The model that converts a magnitude into a wave height assumes a rupture geometry that will be revised, and the revision takes half an hour. The national agency that receives the message must decide whether to issue, and its decision is political as much as scientific, because an unnecessary evacuation costs money and credibility.

The final link is the one that fails most often, and the failures are usually organizational rather than technical, which is why the drills matter more than the software. A warning issued to a province must reach the town, and the town must reach the beach, and the beach must reach the person who is standing on it looking at the sea. The last part is handled by sirens, loudspeakers, text messages sent to every phone registered in the district, and by a few hundred people who have been trained to knock on doors.

What the public is expected to do with a warning depends on a piece of knowledge that is easy to state and hard to act on. When the sea pulls back, the wave is coming, and the right response is to move uphill immediately rather than to look at the exposed sea floor. The number of people who have watched that spectacle and then been caught by the following wave does not appear to be falling.

The engineering has improved year by year, and the improvement is measurable in the minutes saved rather than in the warnings issued. The buoys that detect a wave in deep water are more numerous than they were in 2004, the models are finer, the message routing is faster, and the coverage of phones in coastal districts is close to complete. None of that has solved the human problem, which is that a warning is a piece of information and an instruction at the same time, and people are much better at receiving information than at obeying instructions.

What the system is now testing, in every event, is whether the credibility it spends on unnecessary warnings will still be available when a necessary one arrives. That balance cannot be optimized, only managed, and it is the real content of the job.
