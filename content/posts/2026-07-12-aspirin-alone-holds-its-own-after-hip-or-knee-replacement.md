Blood clots after hip or knee replacement are common enough that every patient leaves hospital on some form of prophylaxis, and the choice of agent has been argued over for two decades. Aspirin is cheap, familiar and easy to take; direct oral anticoagulants are more effective in principle and carry a bleeding risk that matters when the operation itself is bloody. Guidelines have drifted between the two, and the drift has been driven more by opinion than by a trial of the size the question deserves. Both sides of the argument could point at observational data, and neither could point at a randomised comparison large enough to settle it.

That trial ran in 5,429 patients undergoing total hip or knee arthroplasty, and it reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 12 July 2026. Patients were randomised to aspirin alone or to rivaroxaban for a short period followed by aspirin, 2,718 and 2,647 respectively. The primary outcome was symptomatic venous thromboembolism, and the trial was designed as a noninferiority comparison with a margin set in advance.

Venous thromboembolism developed in 13 patients on aspirin alone, 0.48 per cent, and in 12 on the rivaroxaban-then-aspirin schedule, 0.45 per cent, a risk difference of 0.02 percentage points with a confidence interval from minus 0.34 to 0.39. Major or clinically relevant non-major bleeding occurred in 1.66 per cent and 2.04 per cent respectively, a difference of minus 0.38 percentage points. Noninferiority was met, and the bleeding difference pointed in aspirin's favour without reaching significance.

Interpretation turns on what the trial was powered to see. Event rates this low mean the confidence interval around the difference is wide in relative terms, and a margin that looks generous in absolute terms can hide a clinically real disadvantage. The population also matters: these were elective arthroplasties in patients selected by surgeons, and the results should not be extended to emergency hip fracture surgery, where clot risk and bleeding risk are both higher.

The practical reading is that the simple regimen is defensible, which matters because adherence to a drug that has to be taken for weeks after discharge is not guaranteed. Aspirin is also the cheaper option by an order of magnitude, and a trial that finds no difference in events and a hint of fewer bleeds is the kind of evidence that changes a formulary. What it does not do is show that anticoagulation can be shortened or skipped. Trial size also sets what a null result means: with events this rare, the study cannot exclude a difference of a few tenths of a percentage point either way.

The remaining question is who still needs the stronger agent. A trial this size cannot answer it in subgroups, and the patients at highest risk, those with previous thrombosis or active cancer, were largely excluded or present in small numbers. A risk-stratified design, with aspirin for the many and a direct oral anticoagulant for the few, is the next study worth running.
