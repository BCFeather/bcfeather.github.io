description: "In 5,197 adults who could not mount a good vaccine response, a single intramuscular dose of tixagevimab and cilgavimab reduced symptomatic COVID by 77 per cent over six months, with no excess of serious adverse events."
---

People whose immune system does not respond to vaccination have been told to rely on the immunity of others, which works until the people around them stop distancing. Monoclonal antibodies with an engineered long half-life offer a direct alternative, if they can be given as a single injection rather than an infusion. The group includes transplant recipients, patients on chemotherapy and people with primary immunodeficiency, and for them the vaccine campaigns have offered advice without protection.

PROVENT randomized 5,197 adults who were at increased risk of an inadequate response to vaccination or of exposure to the virus, in a 2:1 ratio, to 300 milligrams of AZD7442 or saline, given as two intramuscular injections. The primary analysis followed participants for up to 183 days. The paper appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 20 April 2022.

Symptomatic COVID occurred in a small number of participants in the antibody group and in a larger number in the placebo group, giving a relative risk reduction of 77 per cent. There were no cases of severe or critical COVID in the antibody group and several in the placebo group during the primary analysis. A 77 per cent reduction in symptomatic infection over six months is the kind of figure that a prophylaxis programme needs, though the absolute number of cases prevented depends on the incidence in the community.

Its half-life is about 90 days, achieved by modifications in the Fc region that reduce binding to the neonatal receptor and slow clearance. A single dose that protects for six months is the property that makes the approach practical for a patient who cannot attend monthly infusions. The mechanism is passive immunity: the antibodies neutralize the virus at the mucosal portal before it establishes an infection, which is a different mode of action from a vaccine.

Adverse events were similar between groups, and injection-site reactions were the commonest complaint. The trial was not powered to detect rare events, and the concern about cardiac events that surfaced later in the programme was not visible in this dataset. The intramuscular route is the logistical advantage, since an infusion requires a chair, a nurse and half a day, and a patient who needs protection every six months cannot do that repeatedly.

The trial ran during a period when Delta and then Omicron were circulating, and the antibody pair retains activity against Omicron in vitro at reduced potency. Whether the protection measured here holds against the variants that followed is a question for the surveillance data rather than for this trial.

For an immunocompromised patient, the practical question is access, the result offers a prophylaxis that does not depend on their own immune response, and it comes with a cost and a supply constraint that will limit it to the people at highest risk. Whether the protection extends to the variants that emerged later is the question that determines the drug's useful life, and the answer has been a steady erosion of potency.
