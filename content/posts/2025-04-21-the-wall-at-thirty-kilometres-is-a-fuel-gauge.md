A marathon is forty-two kilometres and a runner covers it in about two hours at the front of a major race, and the limit that decides the last ten kilometres is the amount of carbohydrate stored in the muscles and the liver. A trained runner carries perhaps two thousand kilocalories of glycogen, and a runner at race pace burns more than that before the finish. The wall is the point where the fuel runs out. A runner who has trained for a marathon has more glycogen than an untrained one, and the difference is the point of the training block.

Fat is the alternative fuel and it is abundant, and it cannot be burned fast enough to sustain a pace of under three minutes per kilometre. The body therefore uses a mixture, and the proportion of carbohydrate rises with intensity. Training at pace teaches the body to spare glycogen, and the adaptation is one of the reasons elite runners can hold a pace that a recreational runner cannot. A pace of four minutes per kilometre uses a mixture that is mostly carbohydrate, and a pace of six uses more fat.

Carbohydrate intake during the race is the other lever. The gut can absorb perhaps sixty grams of glucose per hour, and the transporters that handle fructose run in parallel, so a mixture of the two raises the limit to ninety or more. The practice of taking a gel every twenty minutes is a way of matching intake to expenditure, and the training that goes with it is training the gut to tolerate the load. A gel that is taken with water empties the stomach faster than one taken without, and the practice is rehearsed in training.

Supershoes changed the arithmetic. A shoe with a carbon plate and a compliant foam returns energy that would otherwise be lost, and the improvement in running economy is worth two to four per cent. A runner who is four per cent more efficient burns less fuel at the same pace, which pushes the wall further down the road. A plate that returns energy is a spring, and the spring stores energy during the stance phase and releases it during the push.

Pacing is the tactical version of the same calculation. A runner who goes out too fast burns carbohydrate at a rate that depletes the store before thirty kilometres, and the positive split that follows is not a collapse of will. The athletes who win majors run even or negative splits, and the splits are a consequence of a fuelling plan. A runner who fades in the last ten kilometres has usually made the mistake at ten, not at thirty.

What separates the elite from the good is the ability to run the last ten kilometres at close to the pace of the first. That ability is a function of glycogen, of efficiency and of the tolerance of discomfort, and the third is the only one that cannot be measured in a laboratory. A tolerance of discomfort is trained by running at a pace that hurts, and the training is the part that separates the prepared from the talented.
