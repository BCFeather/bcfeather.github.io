A Chinese carmaker put a driver assistance system on its cheapest models in February, and the announcement moved the feature from the premium segment to the mass market in a single week. The system combines a camera, a radar and a chip that runs a small model, and the version that was promised for the entry-level hatchback was the same version that the flagship sedan had received a year earlier.

The cost of the hardware is what changed. A camera module, a millimetre-wave radar and a computing board have all fallen in price as the volumes have grown, and the bill of materials for a basic assistance system is now a small fraction of the price of the car. The software is where the engineering cost sits, and the software is written once and shipped to every model.

The function that the system performs is narrower than the advertising suggests. It keeps the car in its lane, it follows the vehicle ahead, it brakes for a pedestrian in some conditions, and it parks. The driver remains responsible, the system disengages in the rain and at night, and the manual says so in a paragraph that the buyer does not read.

The regulatory frame is the part that varies by market. A car that steers itself on a highway is regulated differently from a car that steers itself in a city, and the approval for one does not cover the other. The Chinese approvals have moved faster than the European ones, and the difference is why the same feature is available on more models in one country than in another.

The competitive logic is a price war with a technical floor. When one maker offers the feature on a cheap car, the rival has to match it or explain why it will not, and the explanation is difficult to sell to a buyer who compares the specification sheets. The result is that a technology which was a luxury becomes a line item that the marketing department cannot leave out.

The data is the asset that the feature produces. A fleet of cars with cameras and radars collects a volume of driving that no test track can match, and the maker that sells the most vehicles has the largest set. The privacy rules that govern the collection are written by the regulator, and the consent that the buyer gives is a checkbox in a screen.

What the announcement shows is how a technology becomes ordinary. The research is done in a laboratory, the hardware is expensive, the first cars are premium, and then a cost curve and a competitor arrive together and the feature is standard. The cheap car that steers itself is the end of that sequence, and the sequence will repeat with the next feature.

What it also does is change the expectation. A buyer who has driven a car that centres itself in a lane will notice the absence of the function in the next car, and the market that treated the feature as a luxury in 2023 will treat it as a baseline in 2026. The specification sheet that a dealer hands over is a document about expectations, and the expectations are now set by the cheapest model on the lot.
