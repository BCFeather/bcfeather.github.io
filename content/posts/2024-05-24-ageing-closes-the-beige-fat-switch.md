---

Beige adipose tissue is white fat that has been persuaded to behave a little like brown fat, burning fuel as heat instead of storing it. The persuasion is cold, or a beta-adrenergic signal, and it is one of the few routes to raising energy expenditure that does not require exercise. What happens to that route in an ageing body is the subject of a paper in *eLife* on 24 May 2024.

The authors compared mice at two ages across a cold challenge, profiling the adipocyte stem and progenitor cells, the mature adipocytes and the transcriptional state of both. Ageing changes the progenitors before any cold arrives. Fibroblastic progenitors in old animals express *Cd9* and a set of fibro-inflammatory genes at higher levels, and they fail to differentiate into beige adipocytes in the tissue. The block is not intrinsic to the cells, since progenitors from young and aged mice differentiate equally well in culture, which points to the surroundings as the suppressor.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing adds a composition layer. The adipocyte population shifts with age, and cold exposure produces a distinct group of cells with high expression of de novo lipogenesis genes, a response that is strongly reduced in the old animals.

One gene stands out as a marker rather than a mechanism. *Npr3*, which encodes the natriuretic peptide clearance receptor, marks a subset of white adipocytes and rises with age in adipocytes, and the authors present it as a handle for that subset rather than as the cause of the decline. Exercise is the other comparison the field will want, since contraction releases signals that overlap with the cold response, and an aged niche that blocks one may not block the other.

Therapeutic reading follows the design of the experiment. If the block is environmental, then a drug that changes the niche might restore beige recruitment in older people, and drugs that activate beige fat directly would still fail if the progenitors never arrive. That distinction is the useful part of the paper for anyone thinking about obesity treatment in an ageing population.

Sex and strain both affect adipose plasticity, and those variables are held fixed here, which is normal for a mechanistic paper and a caution for anyone extrapolating to a clinic. Limits are those of a mouse study with one cold protocol. The authors do not show which factor in the aged niche suppresses differentiation, and the human relevance is inferred from conserved cell types rather than measured.

Method matters for how much weight the result can bear. Single-nucleus sequencing reads a snapshot, and a population that looks smaller in old tissue may be one that turned over faster rather than one that never formed. What the paper changes is where a therapy would have to act, and that is the shift a clinician would care about. Beige fat fades with age while the cells remain, and it fades because the tissue stops asking them to appear.
