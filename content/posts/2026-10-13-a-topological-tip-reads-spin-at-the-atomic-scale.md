*Nature* published on 13 October a scanning tunnelling microscope tip that is itself a topological material. Chen Xianhui and Wang Zhenyu of the University of Science and Technology of China, with Xiang Bin at the same university and Liu Junwei at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, made the tip from a single crystal of bismuth bromide and used it to read electron spin on a surface, atom by atom. The tip is not a coating or a functionalized probe; it is the bulk material itself, cut to a point.

Spin-resolved tunnelling normally needs a magnetic tip, and a magnetic tip is a compromise. The coating perturbs the surface it measures, loses its magnetization at modest fields and temperatures, and is hard to make sharp and stable at the same time. What the field wanted was a mechanism that selects spin without putting a magnet within a nanometre of the sample.

Topological materials offer one. Electrons travelling along a helical edge state carry opposite spins in opposite directions, so a conductor built from such an edge admits one spin and rejects the other. The group had already seen clean one-dimensional helical channels at step edges of bismuth iodide, and the new work turns that observation into a tool by shaping bismuth bromide into a tip and confirming that it passes current selectively with respect to spin.

The test case is potassium vanadium selenide oxide, a candidate altermagnet. Altermagnets order magnetically with no net magnetization, and their spin splitting depends on the direction an electron travels, which makes the order hard to see with probes that average over the surface. Tunnelling from the topological tip returned a signal that the authors, with theory support from their collaborators, read as two-dimensional d-wave altermagnetic order in the surface layer. Experiment and calculation agree on the sign of the splitting as the tip moves across the surface, which is the check that separates a real altermagnetic surface from a magnetic artefact.

Altermagnetism has been a materials-hunting problem more than a measurement problem: candidates are proposed from calculations faster than they can be checked in the laboratory. A tip that resolves spin without a magnet, and does so at atomic resolution, gives that hunt a detector. Whether the order survives at the surface, and how deep the reconstruction runs, are exactly the questions that a probe of this kind is built to answer.

Two limits are visible in the report. The measurements run at low temperature, which is where most spin-resolved tunnelling lives but not where devices would, and the tip is a fragile single crystal that has to be made and handled one at a time. The d-wave assignment rests on a comparison between experiment and calculation rather than on an independent structural measurement, so other groups will want to repeat it on the same and on different compounds. No measurement of the order at room temperature appears in the report, so the temperature at which it disappears is still open.
