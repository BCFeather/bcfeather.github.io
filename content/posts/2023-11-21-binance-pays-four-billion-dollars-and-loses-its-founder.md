Binance, the largest cryptocurrency exchange in the world by trading volume, pleaded guilty on 21 November to charges that it had allowed money laundering and had violated American sanctions, and agreed to pay 4.3 billion dollars in penalties and forfeiture. The settlement, announced by the Justice Department, the Treasury and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, is one of the largest corporate resolutions in American history, and it allows the company to keep operating under monitoring rather than face a shutdown.

Changpeng Zhao, known across the industry as CZ, pleaded guilty to a single count of failing to maintain an effective anti-money-laundering programme and agreed to pay a fifty million dollar fine. He resigned as chief executive in the same announcement, and Richard Teng, the company's head of regional markets, took over. Zhao had built the exchange from a startup in 2017 into a platform that at its peak handled most of the world's crypto trading.

Prosecutors described a company that had grown by ignoring the rules it was required to follow. Prosecutors said Binance had processed transactions for customers in Iran, Cuba, Syria and Crimea after sanctions were imposed, and that it had allowed accounts to be opened with minimal identification. The Treasury said the exchange had handled more than ten billion dollars in suspicious flows, including transactions linked to ransomware, darknet markets and terrorist financing.

Against Zhao himself the case turned on the gap between what he said publicly and what the company did internally. He had promised compliance in interviews while, according to the government, telling staff that the exchange was better off asking for forgiveness than permission. Two of his lieutenants had already pleaded guilty, and the company's compliance chief had been warned repeatedly that the programme was inadequate.

It leaves the exchange in an unusual position. Binance will be monitored by an independent compliance monitor for three years, will report to the government on suspicious transactions, and will have to exit businesses that do not meet the new standards. Customers did not run, and withdrawals continued through the day, which the industry read as evidence that the resolution had been priced in during months of speculation about the investigation.

For the crypto market, the effect is a consolidation of legitimacy. The two largest exchanges are now both under settlements or consent orders, and the firms that remain outside the American system are smaller and less liquid. Regulators in Europe and Asia have spent the year writing rules for the same risks, and the Binance case supplies the evidence they needed: an exchange can be too big to fail and too badly run to leave alone. Shares in Coinbase rose on the day and the price of bitcoin moved little, a reaction that analysts attributed to months of expectation after the investigation became public and the exchange's banking partners began to withdraw support. The monitor will report to the Justice Department, and the exchange has said it will publish a summary of each report.
