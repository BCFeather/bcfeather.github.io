description: "Four gunmen attacked the Crocus City Hall near Moscow on 22 March, killing at least 137 people and setting the building on fire, in an attack claimed by Islamic State and described by President Putin as the work of radical Islamists with a Ukrainian trail."
---

Four men armed with automatic rifles and incendiary devices entered the Crocus City Hall, a concert venue about seventeen kilometres from the centre of Moscow, on the evening of the twenty-second of March, and shot people who had come to hear the rock band Piknik. Attack began shortly before the performance was due to start, and the gunmen moved through the foyer and the auditorium for about ten minutes before setting fires that spread through the roof. Death toll reported by the investigative committee rose from 60 on the night to 137 and then to 144.

Number of dead makes it the worst attack in Russia since the siege of Beslan in 2004 and the deadliest in Moscow since 2011. Many of the victims died from smoke and from the collapse of the roof rather than from gunshots, and identification took weeks because of the condition of the bodies. More than 180 people were wounded, and hospitals in the capital treated burns and gunshot injuries for days afterwards.

Claim of responsibility came from Islamic State through its news channels, and the group published photographs and a video taken by the attackers that appeared consistent with the accounts of survivors. American officials said in the weeks before the attack that they had intelligence about a planned attack in Russia and had passed a warning to Moscow, which the government acknowledged in general terms. Russian officials initially said the warning was too generic to act on.

Attribution became a political question within hours. President Vladimir Putin said in a televised address on the twenty-third of March that the attackers had been detained while fleeing towards Ukraine, and he said the crime would be investigated as the work of radical Islamists, adding that the authorities were interested in who had ordered it. He did not repeat earlier suggestions that Ukraine was responsible, and the security service said it had detained eleven people, including four who had carried out the attack.

Detentions and the treatment of the suspects drew attention of their own. Video circulated on social media showing detainees being beaten and one having part of an ear cut off, and the government said it would investigate the circumstances. Courts remanded the four alleged gunmen in custody, and several of them appeared in court with visible injuries. Human rights groups said the treatment amounted to torture and that confessions obtained that way would not be reliable.

What followed from the twenty-second of March is a security debate that has been running since the war began. Russia has suffered attacks from groups based in the north Caucasus for two decades, and the Islamic State's branch in Afghanistan has recruited from Central Asia, which is where the suspects came from. The attack exposed the limits of a security apparatus that is focused on dissent at home and on the war abroad, and it did not produce a change in policy beyond promises of tougher measures. What followed was a wave of raids on migrant communities and a set of proposals to restrict entry, which fell on the people who had been victims of the attack as much as on those who carried it out.
