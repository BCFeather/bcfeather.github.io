Screening programmes are built on a compromise that nobody likes. Two radiologists reading each mammogram reduces the number of cancers missed and doubles the cost, and one reader with a heavier workload reduces the cost and misses more. Every health system has chosen a point on that line, and the choice is presented as a matter of resources rather than of technology. A trial published in *The Lancet* on 30 January suggests the line can be moved.

More than 100,000 women in Sweden were randomised in 2021 and 2022 to screening read by one radiologist assisted by an artificial intelligence system or by the standard double reading. The AI-assisted arm detected nine per cent more cancers and had twelve per cent fewer interval cancers, the cancers that appear between screens and that represent the failures of a programme. Recall rates and false positives were similar, which is the finding that matters most for the women who are asked to come back.

Those numbers describe a screening programme that finds more disease without generating more anxiety, which is close to the definition of a better screening programme. They do not describe a programme that saves more lives. Interval cancer is a proxy for missed disease and a proxy is not a mortality endpoint, and the trial is not powered to detect the difference in deaths that a nine per cent improvement in detection would eventually produce.

The commentary that accompanied the trial made the necessary cautions. One of the authors argued that adoption should be gradual and monitored, because a technology that performs well in a trial with a protocol, a training programme and a research team is a different product from a system deployed across a health service under financial pressure. The other noted that the interval cancer reduction was not statistically significant on its own, which is true and is a statement about arithmetic and about power, not about effect.

The economics are what will decide. If one radiologist plus software can match two radiologists, the saving is a salary per screen, and the software costs a licence. The arithmetic works in a system that cannot hire radiologists, which describes most of Europe, and it works differently in a system that can, which describes parts of Asia. Where the constraint is people, the technology is a way of doing more with the same workforce.

A risk that follows is the one that every screening technology has faced. Finding more small cancers that would never have killed anyone produces a rise in diagnoses, a rise in treatment and no change in mortality, and it is called overdiagnosis. A trial that reports more detected cancers has to demonstrate that the additional cancers are the ones that matter, and that requires a follow-up long enough to see which of them recurred.

What the trial establishes is that the compromise can be shifted and that the shift is not obviously harmful. What it does not establish is that the shift is worth the money, and the difference between those two statements is where the argument will be conducted for the next five years.
