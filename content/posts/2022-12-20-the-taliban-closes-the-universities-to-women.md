title: "The Taliban closes the universities to women, and the last door shuts"
date: "2022-12-20T23:00:00+08:00"

description: "The Taliban ministry of higher education suspends university education for women, closing the last partially open door."
---
The decision was issued by the ministry of higher education on 20 December and reported by the news agencies the same day. It suspended university education for women until further notice, and it instructed institutions to stop admitting them from that moment. Students who were sitting examinations that week were told to go home. The decree was short, and it carried no explanation beyond a line about the need to protect the national interest. The ministry's letter was circulated to universities by email, and several of them posted it on their notice boards the same afternoon.

Bans of this kind had come in a series that had been issued since the movement returned to power in August of the previous year. Secondary schooling for girls had been closed in the spring, and women had been barred from most government jobs, from parks and gyms, and from travelling without a male relative. Universities had been left partly open, with segregated classes and a limited set of subjects, and that partial opening was what the new decree closed. The earlier decrees had been issued by the same ministry and had been enforced through letters to provincial governors.

Foreign governments had argued for months that the bans were a barrier to recognition, and the argument had produced nothing. The United Nations mission in Kabul said that the decision was a breach of the country's obligations under the treaties it had signed. The human rights office described the exclusion as outrageous and called for it to be reversed. Officials in several capitals said the same thing on the same day, and the Taliban's spokesmen did not respond.

Practical effect falls on a generation of students who had already lost years to war, pandemic and closure. Many of them had studied at home, sat examinations in private rooms and travelled to a provincial centre for a place at a university. Families that had sold livestock to pay for a daughter's fees found that the fees had bought a place she could no longer occupy. Women who had nearly finished a medical degree lost the qualification that would have let them work.

Inside the country, the argument made by those who support the ban is about religion and about custom. Religious scholars who disagree have been silenced, and several have left the country. The interpretation that prevails in the movement is enforced by decree rather than by debate, and the ministry that issues the decrees answers to a leadership that does not hold press conferences. Scholars who had taught the subjects for decades said the curriculum had been designed to produce teachers and nurses as well as graduates.

Aid agencies said the decision would deepen a crisis that was already severe. Health care depends on women doctors, and the shortage of them is already acute in the provinces. The ban removes the supply at its source, and the effect will be measured in the years it takes to train a replacement. On the day the decree was read out, the lecture halls that had been full in November stood empty. Nursing and midwifery courses fell under the same ban, and the ministry confirmed that no exceptions had been made for them.
