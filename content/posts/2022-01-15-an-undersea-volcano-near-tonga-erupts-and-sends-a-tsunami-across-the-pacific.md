The Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai volcano erupted shortly after five in the evening on 15 January, sending a column of ash and water vapour into the sky and a tsunami towards the islands of Tonga. Witnesses in Nuku'alofa described the sky turning dark, waves washing through the waterfront and ash falling like rain, and the eruption was heard as a boom in Alaska, some nine thousand kilometres away. The ash also buried gardens and water tanks on the outer islands, which is why the government's first request was for drinking water rather than for food.

A shallow shelf of water under the vent shaped the size of the wave. Magma meeting seawater produces explosions of steam and ash that are far more violent than a comparable eruption on land, and the blast was among the largest of the satellite era. Instruments recorded the pressure wave circling the planet twice, and tide gauges in Chile, Japan and California registered the water that followed.

Communications failed at the worst moment. The single fibre cable that serves Tonga was damaged, and the islands were reachable only by satellite phones for more than a day, which left the government unable to report casualties and outside agencies unable to assess the damage. Ash covered the runway at the international airport, and ships could not approach until the harbour had been surveyed. Flights were suspended for days, and the government asked foreign navies for desalination units because the ash had contaminated the rainwater tanks that most households rely on.

Tsunami reached the other side of the Pacific within hours. Japan reported a wave of about a metre in some ports and damaged boats, Peru recorded two deaths on a beach, and the United States issued advisories from Hawaii to the mainland. Warning systems worked better than they did in 2004, and they still had to cope with a source that was itself underwater and that had knocked out the instruments closest to it.

Atmospheric effects will last longer than the damage. Water vapour injected into the stratosphere absorbs radiation and cools the surface, and the plume carried an amount of water that instruments had never recorded from a single eruption. Researchers spent the following months measuring a slight warming in the stratosphere and a cooling at the surface, an effect that will fade as the vapour circulates.

Tonga's government reported three deaths in the weeks that followed, and the reconstruction of the cable and the harbour took months. The eruption was a reminder that the hazards which matter most in the Pacific rarely arrive slowly, and that a warning system is only as good as the links that remain after the first wave. The islands' quarantine rules then delayed the arrival of aid workers, which is a familiar irony of disaster response in the Pacific. Scientists also had to recalibrate the models that assume an eruption of this size cools the climate, because the water vapour in this one did the opposite. The eruption also put a measurable amount of sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere, which will fade within a year.
