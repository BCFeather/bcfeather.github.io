American consumer prices rose 3.2 per cent in the year to October, down from 3.7 per cent in September, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on 14 November. The monthly increase was flat, the first time the index had shown no change in more than a year, and core prices, which exclude food and energy, rose 0.2 per cent on the month and four per cent on the year. The figures came in below the consensus forecast, and the reaction in markets was immediate.

Equities rallied, with the S&P 500 closing up more than two per cent and the Nasdaq gaining more, and Treasury yields fell sharply as traders moved forward their expectations for the first rate cut. The two-year yield dropped by more than fifteen basis points on the day, and the dollar weakened against most major currencies. Futures markets put the probability of another increase in December at close to zero, where a week earlier they had priced a small chance.

Composition mattered as much as the headline. Energy prices fell, as they had for months, and used car prices declined after a long stretch of increases. Shelter costs, which had been the largest single contributor to core inflation, rose more slowly than in previous months, and economists noted that the measure lags actual rents by six to twelve months, which means the disinflation in housing still had room to run. Airfares fell, and so did the prices of some services tied to travel.

The Federal Reserve had raised rates eleven times since March 2022, taking the benchmark to a range of 5.25 to 5.5 per cent, and had held at its two meetings in the autumn. Officials had said repeatedly that they would need to see a sustained decline before declaring victory, and the report was the strongest evidence yet that the tightening was working. Governor Christopher Waller said in a speech later in the month that he was encouraged by the trend, a comment that markets read as a signal about the direction of policy.

The economy's resilience had confounded the forecasters who predicted a recession in 2023. Growth in the third quarter ran at an annualized rate of nearly five per cent, powered by consumer spending, and employers added jobs at a pace that slowed but never stopped. Wage growth had eased from its peak while remaining above the rate consistent with two per cent inflation, which is the puzzle that the Fed has to resolve as it decides when to cut.

For households, the report described a slow improvement rather than relief. Prices are roughly twenty per cent higher than they were before the pandemic, and the annual increase is now close to the historical average rather than the four-decade high of 2022. Mortgage rates near eight per cent, and rents that had stopped falling, are the parts of the picture that a monthly inflation report does not capture, and they are the parts that voters in an election year are most likely to feel.
