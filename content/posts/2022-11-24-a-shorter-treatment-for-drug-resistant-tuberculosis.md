title: "Six months of pills cures drug-resistant tuberculosis"
date: "2022-11-24T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A trial reported on 24 November found that a six-month regimen worked as well as the standard eighteen-month course."
---

About 450,000 people contract drug-resistant tuberculosis each year, and the standard treatment cures about 60 per cent of them. The results were presented on 24 November at a conference in Paris and published in the New England Journal of Medicine. The trial enrolled 1,200 patients in South Africa and in India, and it compared a six-month regimen with the standard eighteen-month one. The new regimen used six drugs, and the old one used seven. The regimen requires daily injections for six months, and many patients stop before the end. The trial was designed to test whether a shorter course with oral drugs could do better. The regimen is known as BPaLM, and it combines bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid and moxifloxacin.

Cure rates were 91 per cent on the new regimen and 88 on the standard one. The difference was not statistically significant in the primary analysis, and the authors said the shorter course was at least as good. The new course had fewer side effects, and the number of patients who stopped treatment was half. The old course required an injection of an aminoglycoside, which damages hearing in a third of patients.

Cost was the argument for adoption. The old regimen costs about 2,000 dollars per patient including the injections and the hospital stays, and the new one costs 500. The Global Fund said it would buy the drugs at a fixed price, and it said the change could treat three times as many people for the same money. The new drugs are off patent in the countries that need them, and generic versions are available.

Running the trial was a partnership of universities and by the health ministries in the two countries. It took seven years and cost 30 million dollars, and it was funded by the American aid agency and by a foundation. The results were submitted to the World Health Organization, which updated its guidance in 2023. The countries involved were South Africa, India, Georgia and the Philippines.

A shorter regimen had been recommended in 2019 for a narrower group of patients. The new evidence covered a wider group, including people with HIV and with diabetes. The organization said in its update that the six-month course could be used for most patients. The logistics were the next obstacle. The drugs have to be taken daily, and the regimens need a stable supply chain. The partnership said it would train 5,000 health workers and would set up a monitoring system. The cost of that was 100 million dollars over three years. The organization's guidance said the regimen could be used without a drug-sensitivity test in some settings.

Cases fell slowly through the decade. The organization reported 10.6 million cases in 2022, the highest since it began counting, and 1.3 million deaths. The fall in deaths had stalled during the pandemic, and the new regimen was one of the few tools that had improved.
