description: "Accelerometer and GPS records from 163 wild barn owls show that landing force falls as the bird approaches prey, that the force predicts the next hunting attempt's success, and that perches in field boundaries allow the quietest landings."
---

Silent flight is the barn owl's reputation, and the feathers that produce it have been studied for decades. What the bird does with its silence when it is hunting has been harder to observe, since the relevant behaviour happens at night, at speed, and in the last second before contact. The feathers that soften the aerodynamic noise are well described, and what the bird does with that capacity at the moment of attack is less so.

A paper in *eLife* on 24 July 2024 measures it. The authors fitted 163 wild *Tyto alba* with GPS tags and accelerometers and analysed 87,957 landings, using the impact recorded by the accelerometer as a proxy for the noise a landing makes. Measuring impact rather than sound is an indirect route, and it works because the force of a landing sets the noise the landing makes.

Landing force falls as the bird approaches prey. The owls land more softly when they are close to a target, which is the opposite of what a simple perch-landing account would predict, since a bird in a hurry might be expected to arrive harder. An owl that decelerates before contact is trading speed for quiet, which is a cost that only pays when the prey is close.

The force predicts the outcome. Softer landings are followed more often by a successful hunting attempt, which links a mechanical variable to a behavioural one in the same record, and which makes the camouflage hypothesis testable rather than descriptive. The link runs in the expected direction, with quiet arrivals preceding captures more often than loud ones.

Substrate matters. Landings were softest on man-made poles at field boundaries, and harder on natural perches, which suggests that the birds choose structures that let them arrive quietly. The physical environment therefore sets the capacity for sound camouflage, and land use changes that capacity. A pole in a hedgerow is both a convenient lookout and a quiet platform, so the bird's perch choice carries acoustic consequences.

Whether a vole can hear the difference is not measured. The accelerometer records the force of impact, not the sound reaching a prey animal's ears, and the auditory threshold of a small mammal in grass is a separate problem. The study's reach comes from its scale. Nearly 88,000 landings across 163 individuals allow the authors to look at variation between birds and between nights, which a handful of captive trials could never supply. Hearing thresholds in grassland small mammals are measured in the laboratory, and extrapolating them to wind and vegetation is uncertain.

For conservation the result connects two fields that rarely meet. Predator-prey dynamics depend on the acoustic environment, and agricultural practice shapes that environment through the poles and strips it leaves behind. Hedgerows and field margins are the features that supply those poles, which puts an acoustic argument behind a familiar habitat prescription. Those margins are also where voles are densest, which is the coincidence that makes the acoustics worth studying.
