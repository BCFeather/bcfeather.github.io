Measles killed an estimated 72,000 people in 2025, 91 per cent fewer than in 2000 and the lowest number on record, the World Health Organization reported on 27 November. Vaccination against the virus is estimated to have saved more than 60 million lives since 2000, the figure that explains why a death count can fall by nine tenths inside a generation. The report is the annual accounting of a vaccine in routine use for more than four decades.

Infections ran to about 10 million. The geography changed shape over the same period: the Americas recorded 73,000 cases, roughly eight times its figure in 2000, and close to half of the world's cases occurred in South-East Asia, where one region's outbreak supplied the largest single share of the global total. Deaths fell while the case map spread, and the two movements are not in contradiction: the deaths are concentrated in the poorest cohorts, and the cases now include countries that had stopped expecting them.

Coverage held steady. Eighty-four per cent of children received a first dose and 77 per cent a second, broadly unchanged from the year before, but the averages conceal a split: low-income countries reported 69 and 57 per cent, high-income countries 93 and 91. Some 29 million children received no dose or too few to count as protected, and coverage in conflict and crisis settings runs below the national figures. A first dose protects about 85 per cent of children who receive it, so the 16 points missing from the first dose matter more than the 7 between the two.

Outbreaks followed the gaps. Fifty-nine countries had large or disruptive outbreaks in 2025, 18 of them low-income, 34 middle-income and seven high-income, and 11 of the 59 occurred in countries that had previously been certified as free of the disease. Income is a weak guide to where an outbreak lands: seven high-income countries are on the list, and several had let coverage slip below the level that holds a population immune. Certification records a past achievement.

Elimination status has to be defended annually. Ninety-five countries were verified as having eliminated measles or as keeping that status at the end of 2025, and the Pan American Health Organization announced this month that the United States, Mexico and Bolivia had lost certification. A certificate records interrupted transmission for three years; it lapses when surveillance finds sustained local spread rather than imported clusters. All three sit in one region, and coverage across the Americas fell below 90 per cent after the pandemic, having stood above it before.

Every figure here is an estimate. The death count comes from a model built on case reports, coverage surveys and mortality data, and surveillance is weakest where coverage is lowest, so the case count is a floor in some regions and a projection in others. Two doses in 95 per cent of a cohort is the level at which transmission stops, and the distance to it in low-income countries, 26 points on first dose and 38 on second, is what the next report will measure. A few points in one district can reopen transmission within a season.
