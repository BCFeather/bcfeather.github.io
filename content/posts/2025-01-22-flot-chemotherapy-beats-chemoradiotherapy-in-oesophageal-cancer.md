Oesophageal cancer that has spread to local lymph nodes is treated with a combination of surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and the combinations have competed for twenty years. The European standard has been perioperative chemotherapy with the FLOT regimen, four drugs given before and after the operation. The American standard has been chemoradiotherapy before surgery, on the strength of a trial published in 2012.

The trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 22 January set the two against each other. Patients with locally advanced adenocarcinoma of the oesophagus or the gastro-oesophageal junction were randomized to perioperative FLOT or to preoperative chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery, and the primary endpoint was progression-free survival. The trial was run in Germany, where both approaches were in use.

Three-year progression-free survival was 51.6 per cent in the FLOT group, with a confidence interval from 44.3 to 58.4, and 35.0 per cent in the chemoradiotherapy group, with a confidence interval from 28.4 to 41.7. The hazard ratio for progression or death was 0.66, with a confidence interval from 0.51 to 0.85, which is a difference that a trial of this size can support without qualification.

Toxicity separated the arms as well. Grade 3 or higher adverse events occurred in 120 of 207 patients who started FLOT, or 58.0 per cent, and the rate in the chemoradiotherapy arm was lower, because four drugs given for eight cycles produce more haematological and neurological toxicity than a course of radiotherapy with two drugs. The trade is survival against tolerability, and the trial's discontinuation rates show how many patients paid it before the fourth cycle, which is the number a patient wants before consenting to eight.

Surgical outcomes explain part of the result. The rate of complete resection was higher in the FLOT arm, and the rate of postoperative complications was not different, which means the survival difference comes from better control of systemic disease and from a higher proportion of patients who reached an operation able to remove everything.

The interpretation for practice is a preference for FLOT in adenocarcinoma. The trial enrolled adenocarcinoma and not squamous carcinoma, which behaves differently and responds differently to radiotherapy, so a patient with squamous disease is still treated with chemoradiotherapy in most centres. The result does not apply where it was not tested, and that distinction is the one that a tumour board has to make.

What the trial settles is a question that guidelines had answered by geography. A European oncologist and an American oncologist reading the same evidence reached different conclusions for a decade, and the trial was designed by investigators who thought the question deserved a direct answer. It now has one, and the answer is a regimen that was already the European default, which means the trial confirmed a practice rather than changed it for half the world.
