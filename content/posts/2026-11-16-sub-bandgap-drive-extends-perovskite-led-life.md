Perovskite light-emitting diodes have spent a decade being efficient and short-lived. Colour purity is excellent, and external quantum efficiency has climbed further than many expected this early in the technology's life. Endurance at the brightness a screen actually asks for has not followed. Measured half-lives for visible devices stayed under 1,000 h at nearly any fixed brightness, while the longer figures circulating in the field came from accelerated ageing tests and a curve drawn forward from them. That distance between the measured and the predicted is what made the promises hard to cost.

Yucai Yuan and colleagues at Zhejiang University, working with groups at the University of Science and Technology of China and the Hangzhou firm LinkZill Technology, have cleared that bar for a green emitter. Their paper ran in *Nature Communications*, 6 November 2026. The change is one layer wide: a self-assembled monolayer of a fluorinated molecule, inserted between the nickel oxide hole-injection film and the polymer hole-transport layer above it, so that holes cross the first interface with less resistance.

Measured at 1,000 nits of initial brightness, the device took 1,350 h to fall to 500 nits. That is the number the field has been chasing, and it is a measurement rather than an extrapolation. Voltage carries most of the explanation. Against a control built with the unfluorinated version of the same molecules, holding 1,000 nits needed about 2.6 V, and the fluorinated device managed it at about 2.1 V. Endurance did not cost efficiency: the best energy conversion reached 28.0 per cent, and peak external quantum efficiency reached 24.5 per cent.

Below 2.1 V the stack is being driven under the voltage that the emitter's own bandgap implies, and that is where ageing loosens its grip. Ion migration through the perovskite is the usual suspect behind brightness decay, and it is field-driven, so a weaker field across the layers slows it down. The authors report the interface pushing charge injection towards ohmic behaviour, which is what lets the same brightness arrive at a lower voltage in the first place. Cumulative photon output over a half-life then rises sharply once the drive falls under the bandgap.

Worth naming are the conditions this result sits inside. The paper claims green emission; the same 1,350 h is not claimed for red or blue, and a screen needs all three. The ageing run fixes brightness at one level, while a panel pulses pixels across many. Headline lifetime arrives as a single number, and device-to-device spread is what a reader will want first. The flexible active-matrix prototype displays patterns flat and bent, and carries no lifetime measured while bent. An interface tuned on single test pixels is also some way from a panel line.

Two tests would settle what the result is worth. Repeating the ageing run under a pulsed drive, across the several brightness levels a display actually mixes, would show whether the sub-bandgap margin survives once the field stops being steady. Rebuilding the same interface on red and blue emitters would show whether one molecular layer is a general fix or a lucky match to a single bandgap. What changes in the meantime is a habit of design: fix the voltage target first, and let brightness be the consequence.
