Premature ovarian insufficiency affects more than one per cent of women under forty and is treated as irreversible. The ovaries lose follicles, the tissue becomes fibrotic and the hormonal consequences include infertility, bone loss and cardiovascular risk. Hormone replacement manages the symptoms and does nothing for the fertility, and egg donation is the only route to a pregnancy for most patients.

Lin and colleagues screened a library of approved drugs for compounds that reduce ovarian fibrosis, and the screen returned finerenone, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist used to slow kidney disease in diabetes. The reasoning is that fibrosis is a shared process across organs, and a drug that reduces it in a kidney could reduce it in an ovary. Follow-up work in mouse models showed decreased fibrosis, improved follicular development and improved fertility, and other antifibrotic drugs produced similar effects. The paper appeared in *Science* on 5 February, with a Perspective by Duncan and Babayev. The tissue that remains can still contain follicles that have stopped maturing, which is what makes a treatment aimed at the stroma plausible.

The pilot clinical trial is the part that makes this more than a mouse result. Patients treated with finerenone showed improvement in ovarian function and follicle maturation, and some progressed as far as in vitro fertilization, without major side effects. The pilot was small and uncontrolled, which is the correct description of a first-in-indication study and the reason to be cautious.

Safety is unusually favourable for a reproductive drug. Finerenone is already licensed for chronic kidney disease, its safety profile is characterised in a population that is older and sicker than the patients who would receive it, and the fetal exposure risk that would dominate the development of a new molecule is partly characterised. A repurposed drug starts closer to the clinic than a new chemical entity.

Mechanism needs more work. Mineralocorticoid receptor signalling is involved in fibrosis in the kidney and in the heart, and its role in the ovary is plausible and less studied. The observation that other antifibrotics had similar effects suggests that the class of action matters more than the specific receptor, which weakens the mechanistic claim and strengthens the therapeutic one. That profile is why a repurposing strategy was worth trying for a condition that no company would develop a new molecule for.

The fertility endpoint is the hardest. A pilot that shows follicle maturation cannot show a live birth rate, and the patients who progressed to IVF are a selected subgroup of a small cohort. The natural history of premature ovarian insufficiency includes occasional spontaneous recovery, and a single-arm study cannot distinguish a drug effect from that variation. Recruitment for such a trial is feasible because the population is defined by a blood test and by age, and the endpoint would be ovarian response, not live birth.

What the paper offers is a hypothesis with a plausible mechanism, a licensed drug and a first clinical signal, which is the starting position for a proper trial rather than the ending position for a therapy.
