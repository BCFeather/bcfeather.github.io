description: "The Federal Open Market Committee left rates at 4.25 to 4.5 per cent on 7 May, and its statement judged that the risks of higher unemployment and higher inflation had both risen."
---

A central bank with one instrument and two risks normally chooses which one to lean against. The May statement chose neither, and said so in a sentence that had not appeared before: the Committee judges that the risks of higher unemployment and higher inflation have risen. That is a description of a shock that pushes prices and employment in opposite directions, which is what a tariff does.

The target range stayed at 4.25 to 4.5 per cent, where it had been since December, and the balance-sheet plan was unchanged. The Committee noted that swings in net exports had affected the data and that activity had continued to expand at a solid pace, which is a reference to a first quarter in which imports subtracted almost five points from growth while domestic demand held.

Powell's press conference supplied the numbers behind the wait. Payroll gains had averaged 155,000 over three months, unemployment was 4.2 per cent, and total PCE inflation was 2.3 per cent over the year to March with core at 2.6 per cent. He described the labour market as broadly in balance and not a source of inflation, and inflation as somewhat elevated relative to the 2 per cent goal.

Expectations were the part that mattered. Near-term measures of inflation expectation had risen in both market and survey data, and respondents named tariffs as the reason; longer-term measures remained consistent with the target. A central bank can look through a one-year rise in expectations, and it cannot look through a rise that becomes embedded in wage settlements, so the distinction is the whole argument. Powell noted that survey respondents named tariffs as the reason for the near-term move, which is a reason that can end.

The Committee also said the tariff increases announced so far had been significantly larger than anticipated. That admission is unusual in a statement, and it explains the pause: a policy that was sized for one tariff regime has to be recalibrated for another, and recalibration takes data that does not exist yet, and the Committee chose to wait for it and not to guess at it.

Markets read the meeting as a delay rather than a reversal. Fed funds futures after the statement priced the next cut in July and about three cuts for the year, down from more before the meeting, and Treasury yields rose slightly. The phrase in a good place, which Powell used repeatedly, is a phrase about optionality: the Committee is saying it can move either way and is not obliged to move now. Two governors would have preferred to hold the language tighter, and the minutes later recorded a discussion about how much of the tariff effect would reach prices.

What would settle the path is whether the tariff shows up in prices or in payrolls first. A rise in goods prices with stable hiring argues for holding; a fall in hiring with stable prices argues for cutting. The Committee has set itself up to react, and the cost of reacting late is the cost it has accepted.
