Volkswagen told its works council on 2 September that it could no longer rule out closing factories in Germany, which would be the first closures in the company's eighty-seven years of domestic production. The group also said that a job guarantee negotiated with the unions in 1994 would lapse at the end of the year. The works council was told at a meeting at the plant in Wolfsburg, which employs more people than the next three factories combined.

Arithmetic behind the statement is straightforward. The group has capacity for far more cars than Europeans are buying, its plants run at well under full utilization, and the cost per vehicle in Germany is roughly twice that of its plants in Spain and Slovakia. An industry that built for a market of sixteen million cars a year is now selling twelve million and carrying the fixed costs of the old number. The group's own forecasts assume that demand will stay near the current level for a decade, which is the assumption that the closures are designed around.

Electric transition is the second pressure. A battery car needs fewer parts and fewer hours of assembly than a diesel, so a plant designed for engines and gearboxes has work for fewer people even at full volume, and the company's own electric models have sold below its forecasts. A factory that is paid by the hour is a factory that a simpler product makes smaller. The company has delayed the launch of several electric models and has cut the shifts on the lines that build them, because the cars that were meant to fill them have not sold in the numbers planned.

Competition has moved east as well. Chinese manufacturers are selling electric cars in Europe at prices the German groups cannot match without losing money, and the tariffs that the European Union imposed in October were designed to slow that arrival rather than to stop it. A tariff buys time, and time is what the restructuring needs. Eurostat data for the first half shows that registrations of battery cars fell in Germany after the subsidy ended, which is a demand problem that a tariff cannot fix.

Politics are federal and regional. Lower Saxony holds a stake in the company and its premier sits on the supervisory board, and the state's election calendar makes any closure a campaign issue. A works council that has never negotiated a closure is a works council that has to invent a procedure. The state premier said that the government would look for alternatives to closures, and the alternatives that the unions have proposed are shorter hours rather than fewer plants.

Statement was a negotiating position as much as a plan. A company that announces it is considering closures before talking to the unions is a company that wants the unions to bring proposals, and the proposals will be about hours and pay rather than about which plant survives. The union has said that it will not negotiate plant closures, which is a position that both sides know will change once the numbers are on the table.
