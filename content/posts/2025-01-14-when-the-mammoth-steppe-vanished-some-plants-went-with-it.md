title: "When the mammoth steppe vanished, some plants went with it"
date: "2025-01-14T14:05:00+08:00"

description: "Ancient sedimentary DNA suggests that plant extinctions after the loss of the mammoth steppe ran at 1.7 to 5.9 species per million species years, above the background rate and below the modern one, with the largest losses lagging the vegetation turnover by thousands of years."
---

The mammoth steppe was a dry, cold grassland that stretched from western Europe to Alaska during the last glacial period, and it supported a community of large herbivores that no longer exists. When the climate warmed and the megafauna disappeared, the grassland contracted into fragments and was replaced by forest and tundra. Whether the plants that belonged to it went extinct or merely retreated is a question that bones cannot answer, because plants leave no bones.

Sedimentary DNA can. Lakes and ponds accumulate genetic material from the vegetation around them, and the layers of sediment date the material they hold. A study in *Nature Communications* on 14 January assembled such records from sites across the mammoth steppe and compared the plant taxa present before and after the transition, which allowed the authors to estimate extinction across a region instead of inferring it from pollen abundance at a single core.

The estimate is 1.7 to 5.9 extinctions per million species years. That range sits above the background rate that fossils give for the recent geological past and well below the rates estimated for the present, which is the comparison the authors emphasize. Two bursts of loss appear in the record, one around 17,000 years ago and another around 9,000 years ago, and both arrive after the main turnover in vegetation, which means the plants that were lost had already been declining for centuries by the time the record shows them going.

A delay of that length is the part of the result that carries information about how extinction works. A plant that loses its habitat does not disappear when the habitat does. It persists in pockets, in the wet hollows and the north-facing slopes where the old conditions survive, and it declines over thousands of years as those pockets shrink and as the species that replace it close in. The record shows that persistence and the slow end of it, not the instant loss that a simple habitat model would predict.

Which plants went is equally informative. Herbaceous taxa and taxa that contribute little to beta diversity were the more vulnerable groups, which means that the losers were the species with narrow ranges and few distinct communities to occupy. The survivors were the woody plants and the generalists that could move with the climate and colonize the ground the grassland had vacated, and that difference is the kind of prediction conservation biology uses when it asks which species a warming world will keep.

Uncertainty comes with the method itself. Reference libraries are incomplete, sediment records are patchy, and a taxon that vanishes from one core may survive in a basin that nobody has sampled. The claim the paper supports is that plants were more resilient than the mammals of the same ecosystem, which is a statement about relative tolerance in one transition and not a guarantee about the next one.
