The National Bureau of Statistics published the July figures on 15 August, and they describe an economy that is growing at a steady pace with an uneven internal balance. Industrial value added rose 5.7 per cent year on year, services output 5.8 per cent, and retail sales 3.7 per cent to 3.878 trillion yuan.

The composition of industrial growth is the interesting part. Equipment manufacturing rose 8.4 per cent and high-tech manufacturing 9.3 per cent, both well ahead of the headline. Output of 3D printers rose 24.2 per cent, industrial robots 24.0 per cent and new energy vehicles 17.1 per cent. These are the categories that policy has been pushing, and the numbers suggest the push is working at the level of production. Whether it is working at the level of profit is another matter: industrial profits fell 1.8 per cent in the first six months of the year, which is what happens when volumes rise and prices fall.

Consumption is more mixed. Retail sales grew 3.7 per cent, but the growth was concentrated where subsidies applied: household appliances rose 28.7 per cent, furniture 20.6 per cent, communication equipment 14.9 per cent, and cultural and office goods 13.8 per cent. Restaurant revenue rose 1.1 per cent, which is the weakest line in the table and the one that reflects how households feel rather than what they are being encouraged to buy.

Trade held up better than expected. Exports rose 8.0 per cent and imports 4.8 per cent, a surplus that continues to support growth while domestic demand stays soft. The caveat is that export growth of this size invites retaliation, and the policy discussion in the second half of the year will be about how much of it is sustainable.

Prices remain the puzzle. Consumer prices were flat year on year, and core inflation, which excludes food and energy, was 0.8 per cent, up a tenth of a point from June. Producer prices fell 3.6 per cent, the twenty-second consecutive month of decline, which tells you that factories still cannot pass costs on and that margins in the middle of the supply chain are thin. Deflation of this kind is good for buyers and bad for anyone carrying debt, and the balance sheets of local governments and developers are on the wrong side of it.

Investment grew 1.6 per cent, held down by property, where investment fell 12.0 per cent and new home sales fell 6.5 per cent by value. Infrastructure grew 3.2 per cent and manufacturing 6.2 per cent. The pattern is clear enough: the state is investing, households are saving, and the sector that used to absorb both is still shrinking. The savings rate has not fallen, which means the household sector is still rebuilding a buffer, and no subsidy has yet changed that. The trade-in programmes have pulled purchases forward, which is a real effect in July and a subtraction from next year.
