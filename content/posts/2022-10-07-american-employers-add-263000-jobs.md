Non-farm payrolls rose by 263,000 in September, according to the report the Bureau of Labor Statistics published on 7 October. The forecast had been 275,000, and the figure was below it but still strong enough to describe a tight labour market. The unemployment rate fell to 3.5 per cent from 3.7, back to its level of February 2020.

About 1 million workers had left the labour force since 2020 through retirement, and the figure had not recovered. The participation rate was 62.3 per cent, half a point below its pre-pandemic level. The prime-age participation rate was 82.7 per cent, which is close to its peak, and the shortfall was concentrated among older workers. Immigration had added 1 million workers in the year, and the shortfall would have been larger without it.

Wages rose 0.3 per cent on the month and 5 per cent over the year, a touch slower than in August. The average hourly earnings figure is watched as the best single indicator of wage pressure, and it had been above 5 per cent for six months. Economists said the number was consistent with inflation of about 4 per cent once productivity is counted. Wage growth for production workers was 5.7 per cent, and for managers 4.6.

Sectors were mixed. Leisure and hospitality added 83,000 jobs, and health care added 60,000. Manufacturing added 22,000, and construction 19,000. Government employment fell by 25,000, mostly because of a decline in local education jobs, which is a seasonal pattern that the statisticians adjust for and did not fully capture. The household survey, which is separate from the payroll survey, showed employment falling by 178,000. The two surveys can diverge for a month, and the household measure had been weaker all year. The gap was one reason the report did not change the market's view as much as the headline suggested. Retail added 11,000 jobs, and the sector had recovered all the jobs it lost in 2020.

Officials had said they wanted to see a loosening in the labour market before slowing, and this report did not show one. The Federal Reserve was watching the same numbers. It had raised rates by three quarters of a point in September and signalled another at the November meeting. Futures priced a 75 basis point move in November at 80 per cent after the release.

Payroll growth for the year was revised in February to show an average of 380,000, which is the strongest since 1994. The reaction was modest. The S&P 500 fell 2.8 per cent, and the two-year yield rose above 4.3 per cent. The dollar rose against most currencies. The report was the last payrolls figure before the midterm elections, and the White House described it as evidence that the economy was resilient. The revisions were large. The previous two months were revised up by 44,000 in total, and the three-month average was 372,000. The labour market stayed tight until the spring of 2023. Officials had said they wanted to see hiring slow to about 100,000 a month before pausing.
