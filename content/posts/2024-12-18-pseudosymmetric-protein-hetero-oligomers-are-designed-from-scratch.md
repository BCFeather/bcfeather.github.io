Many natural assemblies have subunits that differ in sequence and yet pack into a symmetric structure, and symmetry without sequence identity is the property designers have found hardest to reproduce. A paper in *Nature Communications* on 18 December 2024 builds such assemblies. Equivalent components are a constraint of homomeric design, and breaking that constraint is what makes a multi-part machine possible. Sequence-level control at three positions is a hard combinatorial problem, and the solution here is geometric rather than computational. Homomeric assemblies repeat one function, and a designer who needs three functions has no homomer to build from.

Pseudosymmetry is the term for a structure that is symmetric overall while its components are not equivalent. Nature uses it to build machines whose parts must be different, and a designed version would let those machines be programmed. Programming a machine means assigning a function to each part, and assignment requires that the parts be distinguishable. Distinguishability allows a part to be assigned, and assignment is the step that makes an assembly a machine. Assignment is what a structural characterization verifies, and verification is the difference between a design and a hope.

The strategy begins from a symmetric backbone and breaks the symmetry in a controlled way, so each position accepts only one subunit. Breaking symmetry deliberately is the inverse of the usual design problem, which is to enforce it. Intended stoichiometry is the property that a structural characterization can confirm, and confirmation is what distinguishes a design from a mixture. A platform is what a design paper leaves behind, and a platform is how a method travels to other laboratories.

Hetero-oligomers with three or more unique subunits were produced and characterized, and the characterization shows the intended stoichiometry. Stoichiometry is the property a designed machine has to guarantee. Three functions is the minimum for a machine that senses, decides and acts, and a designer wants exactly that minimum. Geometry is a cheaper control than computation when the target is a small number of positions.

The approach opens the route to assemblies whose parts do different jobs, since a homomer can only repeat one function. A machine with three parts is a machine with three functions. Backbone distortion is the parameter that trade-offs run through, and running through it is why the design space is finite.

On the modelling side, the limits are those of any backbone-based design, because the tolerated distortion sets how different the subunits can be. Comparison between model and structure is how every design paper is judged, and agreement is the currency of the field.

Experimental structures of the new assemblies are the test, and agreement between design model and structure is how the strategy is validated. A designed hetero-oligomer is a platform rather than a product, and a platform is what other laboratories build on. Validation is the step that turns a strategy into a rule, and a rule is what other designers will follow.
