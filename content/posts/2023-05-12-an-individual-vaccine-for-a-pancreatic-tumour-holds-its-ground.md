description: "A vaccine tailored to each patient's tumour mutations, given with immunotherapy and chemotherapy after surgery for pancreatic cancer, produced strong immune responses and delayed recurrence in a small trial."
---

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is the third leading cause of cancer death in the United States, and its survival rate has stayed near twelve per cent for sixty years. Surgery offers the only cure, and most patients recur within a year.

In *Nature* on 11 May 2023, a vaccine designed for each patient is tested. The authors sequence a resected tumour, identify the neoantigens its mutations create, and manufacture an mRNA vaccine that encodes them. The vaccine is given with an immune checkpoint inhibitor and with chemotherapy.

Immunology is the point of the design. A neoantigen is a peptide the tumour presents and the immune system has not tolerated, and a vaccine delivering it should expand T cells that recognize the tumour. Selection is the bottleneck, because a tumour's mutations are few and only some produce peptides the immune system can see, so an algorithm predicting which will be presented decides whether a vaccine has a target at all.

Response was measurable. In a subset of patients the vaccine expanded T cells recognizing the tumour's neoantigens, and those patients had a longer time to recurrence than those who did not respond. Biology makes the approach plausible here, since pancreatic cancer has few mutations and a dense stroma, and a vaccine targeting a small set of neoantigens can be specific without being complex.

Numbers are the caveat. The trial is a phase 1 with a few dozen participants, and a comparison within a single-arm study is a signal rather than a proof. Attribution is ambiguous as well, because vaccine, checkpoint inhibitor and chemotherapy are given together, and a benefit cannot be assigned to one component. Checkpoint blockade alone is the relevant benchmark, since immunotherapy helps a minority of pancreatic tumours, and a vaccine converting a non-responder into a responder would be a real advance rather than a marginal one.

Manufacturing is the practical obstacle. A personalized vaccine has to be designed, synthesized and released within weeks of surgery, and a process taking months misses the window. Durability is unknown, because a T cell response persisting a year may wane, and a booster schedule is a design question the trial leaves open.

Survival stays unshown, since recurrence-free survival is a surrogate and overall survival is the endpoint a phase 3 would measure. Oncology gains a platform, because a vaccine designed per patient can be applied to other tumours with resectable disease, and the pancreatic case is the hardest test of it. A biomarker question follows from the response itself, since a test of T cell expansion could in principle select patients for a booster, and the trial's immune monitoring supplies the assay such a test would need. Pancreatic surgery also leaves little tissue for restaging, so an assay that runs on blood is the practical form that test would take. The window is the constraint that a platform has to respect above all.
