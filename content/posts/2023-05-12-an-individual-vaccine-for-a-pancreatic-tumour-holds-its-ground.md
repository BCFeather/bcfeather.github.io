description: "A vaccine tailored to each patient's tumour mutations, given with immunotherapy and chemotherapy after surgery for pancreatic cancer, produced strong immune responses and delayed recurrence in a small trial reported in *Nature* on 11 May 2023."
---

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is the third leading cause of cancer death in the United States, and its survival rate has stayed near twelve per cent for sixty years. Surgery offers the only cure, and most patients recur within a year.

The trial in *Nature*, 11 May 2023 tests a vaccine designed for each patient. The authors sequence a resected tumour, identify the neoantigens that its mutations create, and manufacture an mRNA vaccine that encodes them. The vaccine is given with an immune checkpoint inhibitor and with chemotherapy.

The immunology is the point of the design. A neoantigen is a peptide that the tumour presents and that the immune system has not tolerated, and a vaccine that delivers it should expand T cells that recognize the tumour.

The response was measurable. In a subset of patients the vaccine expanded T cells that recognized the tumour's neoantigens, and those patients had a longer time to recurrence than those who did not respond.

The small numbers are the caveat. The trial is a phase 1 with a few dozen participants, and a comparison within a single-arm study is a signal rather than a proof.

The manufacturing is the practical obstacle. A personalized vaccine has to be designed, synthesized and released within weeks of surgery, and a process that takes months is a process that misses the window.

The combination's attribution is ambiguous. The vaccine, the checkpoint inhibitor and the chemotherapy are given together, and a benefit cannot be assigned to one component.

The tumour's biology is the reason the approach is plausible here. Pancreatic cancer has few mutations and a dense stroma, and a vaccine that targets a small set of neoantigens is a vaccine that can be specific without being complex.

The durability is unknown. A T cell response that persists for a year may wane, and a booster schedule is a design question that the trial does not answer.

What the paper does not show is a survival benefit. Recurrence-free survival is a surrogate, and overall survival is the endpoint that a phase 3 would measure.

For oncology, the value is a platform. A vaccine that can be designed per patient is a platform that can be applied to other tumours with resectable disease, and the pancreatic case is the hardest test.

The model's fit is its argument. A single origin fails to reproduce the diversity of ancient genomes, and a weakly structured stem reproduces it without positing a migration that the fossil record does not support.

The date matters for archaeology. A split around 120,000 years ago is consistent with fossils of that age, and a model that fits both the genes and the stones is stronger than one that fits only the genes.
The neoantigen selection is the design's bottleneck. A tumour's mutations are few, and only some produce peptides that the immune system can see. An algorithm that predicts which will be presented is the step that decides whether a vaccine has a target at all.
The comparison with checkpoint blockade alone is the relevant benchmark. Immunotherapy helps a minority of pancreatic tumours, and a vaccine that converts a non-responder into a responder would be a real advance rather than a marginal one.
