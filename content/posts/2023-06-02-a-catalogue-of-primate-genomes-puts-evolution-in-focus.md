---

A genome is a record of a species' history, and a single record is hard to read. A collection of genomes across many species lets a comparison distinguish a lineage's own past from a pattern that the whole order shares.

The set in *Science*, 2 June 2023 provides that collection. The authors assemble high-coverage genomes from 233 primate species, align them into a common framework, and release the resource together with the analyses that use it.

The coverage is the achievement. A primate genome is difficult to assemble because many species are rare and their samples are old, and the authors' sequencing and assembly pipeline gets whole-genome data from specimens that would once have yielded only fragments.

The variation is the first result. The alignment reveals structural differences between species, including inversions and duplications that a single reference genome cannot represent, and the differences cluster in regions that the order has rearranged repeatedly.

The demography is the second result. A primate's effective population size leaves a signature in its genome, and the collection shows that several lineages have passed through bottlenecks far narrower than their present numbers would suggest.

The conservation reading follows directly. A species whose genome shows a long bottleneck has less diversity than its habitat suggests, and a less diverse species is one that a single epidemic could threaten.

The comparative method is the framework's value. A trait in one species can be placed in the context of the order, and a gene that has evolved rapidly in one lineage can be compared with its counterpart elsewhere.

The limitation is the sampling. A collection of individuals is a collection of populations, and a species represented by one animal describes that animal's genome better than the species'.

The analyses that accompany the set are partial. A resource paper cannot ask every question, and the group's own speciation and hybridisation analyses are examples of what the collection makes possible rather than its uses.

For primate biology, the value is a shared standard. A field that reads each species through a different reference cannot compare them, and a common alignment is what turns a set of genomes into a dataset.
The tissue is the constraint. A genome needs DNA of a quality that only fresh or well-preserved tissue provides, and a museum skin is a degraded sample. The authors' use of cell lines and of blood samples from captive animals is how the collection avoided that limit.
The annotation is as valuable as the sequence. A gene model that is consistent across species lets a comparison of the same gene in two animals be a comparison of the gene rather than of two research groups' conventions.
The order's diversity is the reason the set is useful. Primates range from mouse lemurs to gorillas, and a resource that spans that range is one that can ask how a genome's size, its repeats and its regulation changed along the branches.
