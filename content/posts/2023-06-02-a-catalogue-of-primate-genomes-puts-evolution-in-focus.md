A genome is a record of a species' history, and a single record is hard to read. A collection of genomes across many species lets a comparison distinguish a lineage's own past from a pattern the whole order shares. In *Science* on 2 June 2023, that collection is provided. The authors assemble high-coverage genomes from 233 primate species, align them into a common framework, and release the resource together with the analyses that use it.

Coverage is the achievement. A primate genome is difficult to assemble because many species are rare and their samples old, and the sequencing and assembly pipeline gets whole-genome data from specimens that would once have yielded fragments. Tissue is the constraint, since a genome needs DNA of a quality only fresh or well-preserved material provides while a museum skin is degraded, and cell lines and blood samples from captive animals are how the collection avoided that limit.

Variation is the first result. The alignment reveals structural differences between species, including inversions and duplications a single reference cannot represent, and the differences cluster in regions the order has rearranged repeatedly. Demography follows, because a primate's effective population size leaves a signature, and several lineages have passed through bottlenecks far narrower than their present numbers suggest.

Conservation reads it directly. A species whose genome shows a long bottleneck has less diversity than its habitat suggests, and a less diverse species is one a single epidemic could threaten. Annotation matters as much as sequence, because a gene model consistent across species makes a comparison of the same gene in two animals a comparison of the gene rather than of two groups' conventions.

Comparison is the framework's value. A trait in one species can be placed in the context of the order, and a gene that evolved rapidly in one lineage can be compared with its counterpart elsewhere. Diversity is why the set is useful, since primates range from mouse lemurs to gorillas, and a resource spanning that range can ask how genome size, repeats and regulation changed along the branches.

Sampling sets the limitation, as a collection of individuals is a collection of populations, and a species represented by one animal describes that animal better than the species. Partial analyses accompany the set necessarily, because a resource paper cannot ask every question, and the group's own speciation and hybridisation work shows what the collection makes possible rather than what it concludes.

Primate biology gains a shared standard, since a field reading each species through a different reference cannot compare them, and a common alignment is what turns a set of genomes into a dataset. A reference panel that is updated as new species are sequenced would keep the resource useful, and the consortium's release schedule is the mechanism that makes it so. A shared variant set would also let clinical genetics borrow from primate diversity, since a variant common in one species and rare in another is a natural experiment in effect size.
