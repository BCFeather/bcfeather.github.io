description: "Zinc isotopes in diogenites show that asteroid 4-Vesta drew almost nothing from the outer solar system and that its volatile loss left the remaining zinc lighter than chondritic, which rules out simple evaporation."

The howardite-eucrite-diogenite meteorites are pieces of a single differentiated asteroid, and their chemistry has been read as a record of how the inner solar system's building blocks lost their volatiles. Vesta is the favoured parent body, and its depletion in volatile elements is more extreme than that of Earth, Mars or the Moon. Their spread of crustal depth makes them the best sample set available for a body that melted and differentiated early.

A paper in *Science Advances* on 16 August 2024 uses zinc to interrogate that loss. Zinc is moderately volatile, it has five stable isotopes, and its isotopic composition in a planetary body records both the source material and the process that removed it. Five isotopes fractionate differently under evaporation, condensation and mixing, which is why one element can carry three separate arguments.

Diogenites, the deep-crustal samples, carry zinc isotopic anomalies that overlap with the non-carbonaceous reservoir, the family of meteorites that formed in the inner solar system. A contribution from outer solar system material would have left a different signature, and the overlap indicates that such a contribution was negligible. That overlap means the asteroid's feedstock stayed local, and the outer solar system supplied almost none of its inventory.

A second result is the more surprising one. Vesta's zinc is lighter than chondritic zinc, whereas evaporation of a silicate melt drives the residue heavier, because the light isotopes leave the vapour more readily. An evaporative loss should therefore have produced the opposite of what the meteorites show. Laboratory heating experiments had set the expectation, and the meteorites contradict it at the scale of an entire planetesimal.

A loss that does not fractionate, or one that removes heavy as readily as light, is what the data require. The authors argue for volatile removal during a large impact, when the material was molten and the loss happened fast enough that isotopic separation could not keep up, and they note that the compositions cannot be explained by heating alone. An impact removes material quickly, and speed is exactly what suppresses the sorting that slow evaporation produces.

The finding does not settle the wider question of where the inner solar system's volatiles went, since one asteroid cannot represent the whole population. It does put a constraint on one popular mechanism, and it separates source from process in a way the isotope data can support. A second parent body measured to the same precision would show whether the impact route is general or peculiar to Vesta.

For the HED parent body the implication is that Vesta formed from inner solar system material and lost its volatiles catastrophically rather than gradually, which is a history that matches the cratered surfaces the Dawn mission photographed. That reading fits the Dawn observations, which found a basaltic crust consistent with a magma ocean rather than with slow accretion. Dawn's gravity and topography measurements add support, since they indicate a dense mantle residue left after a magma ocean stripped the crust.
