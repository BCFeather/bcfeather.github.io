description: "A model study finds that moving ocean iron fertilization to high latitudes removes carbon with a smaller ecological footprint."
---

Ocean iron fertilization is the oldest of the carbon-removal ideas and the one with the worst reputation. Iron limits phytoplankton growth across large stretches of the surface ocean, and adding it produces blooms that draw down carbon dioxide. The difficulty is that the same bloom also consumes nutrients, shifts plankton communities and can produce low-oxygen water, so the carbon accounting and the ecological accounting pull in opposite directions.

A modelling study reported in *Nature* on 30 July 2026 asks where the trade-off is least severe. The authors compared fertilization in equatorial waters, where nutrients are plentiful and blooms are fast, with high-latitude waters, where light and temperature limit growth and the ecosystem is adapted to episodic iron input. The metric was net carbon removal per unit of ecological disruption, rather than carbon per gram of iron. The model's geography also matters, since the Southern Ocean accounts for a large share of the suitable water and almost none of the demand for credits.

High latitudes came out better on that metric. Blooms there are slower and smaller, which limits the nutrient drawdown and the risk of hypoxia, while the carbon that does sink is more likely to reach depth because cold, dense water carries it down. Equatorial blooms remove more carbon per season but also perturb more of the surface ecosystem, and the model puts that cost above the carbon gain in several regions. That conclusion reverses the intuition that the fastest bloom is the best bloom, and it is the sort of result that only a model can produce before field data exist.

The result is a statement about siting rather than about viability. A model cannot demonstrate that a bloom will sink carbon for a century, and the parameter that matters most, the export ratio between what grows at the surface and what arrives at depth, is exactly the one that field experiments have struggled to measure. The study's contribution is a framework for comparing deployments, which the field previously lacked.

Governance is the harder obstacle. Large-scale fertilization would violate the London Convention's current reading unless licensed as research, and the countries with the most suitable high-latitude waters are not the countries with the most carbon to offset. A technique that works only where nobody wants to pay for it is a research programme rather than a climate policy. A licensing regime built for small research releases is not designed to supervise a deployment measured in thousands of square kilometres.

What the paper changes is the direction of field work. Instead of the tropical experiments that dominated the literature, the interesting deployments are in cold, nutrient-poor water with a long record of natural iron input. Those sites also come with a natural control, since the same water is fertilized by dust every spring, and the comparison is the experiment worth funding. A field campaign with a natural-iron control is the cheapest way to test the model before anyone argues about permits.
