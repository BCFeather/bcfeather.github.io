Peter Pellegrini won the second round of Slovakia's presidential election on the sixth of April with about 53 per cent of the vote against Ivan Korcok, a career diplomat and former foreign minister, who took 47 per cent. Turnout was about 61 per cent, higher than in the first round two weeks earlier, when Korcok had led with 42.5 per cent against 37 per cent for Pellegrini. The result gave the governing coalition the presidency for the first time since 2019.

Candidate who won is a former prime minister and the speaker of the parliament, and he leads Hlas, a party that split from the previous governing party and that has governed since October in coalition with Robert Fico's Smer and the Slovak National Party. He campaigned on stability, on the government's promises about pensions and on a message that the country needed a president who would work with the cabinet rather than against it.

Opponent who lost framed the vote as a choice about foreign policy. Korcok said the election would decide whether Slovakia remained anchored in the European Union and in NATO, and he warned that the government's hostility to Ukraine and its friendly statements about Russia would isolate the country. He won in Bratislava and in the western districts, and Pellegrini won in the east and in the south, where the coalition's support is strongest.

Office that changed hands is more than ceremonial in Slovakia. The president appoints the prime minister, can veto legislation and returns it to parliament, appoints judges and is the commander of the armed forces. The previous president, Zuzana Caputova, had used those powers to slow or block parts of the government's programme, and she announced in 2023 that she would not run again, citing personal reasons and a threat to her family.

What followed for the government is a free hand on the measures that had been held up. The cabinet has proposed changes to the criminal code that would abolish the special prosecutor's office and reduce penalties for corruption, and it has clashed with the European Commission over the rule of law. With the presidency in friendly hands, the coalition can appoint its nominees to the constitutional court and sign legislation that Caputova had resisted.

What the sixth of April means is a country that has moved in the direction of its neighbours in Hungary and in Serbia, where governments have consolidated control of the institutions that are meant to check them. Slovakia's membership of the European Union and of NATO remains settled across the political spectrum, and its voters chose a president who will work with the cabinet rather than against it on the rule of law. What the election established was that the coalition's programme has public support beyond the parliament.

What it did not establish was whether the institutions can still restrain it. Turnout in the second round was the highest for a presidential election since 2004, which officials said reflected the stakes that both camps had set.
