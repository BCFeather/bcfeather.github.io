Brucellosis is one of the oldest diseases that people get from their animals. *Brucella melitensis* infects sheep and goats, causes abortion in the herd and undulant fever in the herder, and it has been assumed since the first excavations of pastoral villages to have travelled with those animals. Genomic evidence for the assumption had not been found. Its modern burden falls on pastoral economies in the eastern Mediterranean and central Asia, where herds and households share space.

A paper in *Nature Communications* on 20 July 2024 reports it. The authors recovered a genome at 3.45-fold coverage from a sheep specimen excavated at Menteşe Höyük in north-west Türkiye, a settlement dated to roughly 8,000 years ago, early in the Neolithic. Recovery from a sheep rather than from a human skeleton is what makes the specimen direct evidence for the animal reservoir.

The genome is basal to all known *B. melitensis* strains, which places it at the root of the tree rather than on a modern branch. That position is what makes it useful for dating, since a root genome constrains when the species split from its closest relatives, the cattle-associated lineages. Branching position is a stronger constraint than sequence identity, because the root limits when the lineage could have split.

The calibrated date puts the divergence deeper than earlier estimates, and it puts a pathogen that causes abortion in small ruminants into the same period as the intensification of sheep and goat husbandry. Domestication and disease then appear as one process rather than two, with crowding and close contact supplying the route. Earlier estimates relied on modern diversity, which is shaped by recent livestock movements and by the collapse of herds in the twentieth century. A root genome also fixes the direction of travel, since the basal lineage sits in Anatolia while the derived cattle lineages radiate from elsewhere.

Ancient DNA at this depth is fragmentary, and a single specimen cannot describe the diversity of Neolithic brucellosis. The authors are careful about damage patterns and contamination, and the coverage is low enough that some genomic features are inferred rather than read. Authenticity checks rest on damage patterns at the ends of fragments, a signature that modern contamination does not reproduce.

Still, the finding changes a background assumption. Zoonoses are often treated as consequences of modern urbanization and global trade, and this genome shows one of them circulating in villages of a few hundred people, long before either. Villages of that size kept animals close to their living quarters, and the route from herd to household needs no market and no ship.

For archaeology the result is a new kind of evidence, since disease can now be reconstructed from the animals themselves rather than from lesions in human bone, which are rare and ambiguous. Lesions in bone appear only in a minority of infections, so the animal record is a far larger sample than the human one.
