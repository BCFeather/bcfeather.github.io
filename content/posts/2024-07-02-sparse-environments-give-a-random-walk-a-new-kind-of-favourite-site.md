---

A random walk in a random environment moves symmetrically most of the time, and at a sparse set of randomly chosen sites it picks up a drift. The environment is frozen once it is sampled, and the question is how the walk behaves when the average is taken over the environment as well as over the walker. Sparsity here means that the fraction of sites carrying a drift tends to zero with distance, which places the model between an independent environment and a deterministic one. The model is a solvable caricature of transport in a disordered medium.

Favourite sites are where the answer shows. A favourite site of a walk is the point it has visited most often up to a given time, and the time spent in its favourite sites measures how strongly the environment traps the walker. In a homogeneous medium that time is a small fraction of the total, and a trap raises the fraction by an amount the limit theorem has to quantify.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 1 July 2024 proves annealed limit theorems for that time. The authors work on the integers, prove convergence after rescaling in two separate cases, and identify the limiting object in each, with the drifts drawn independently at the sparse sites and the walk symmetric elsewhere. Both theorems are annealed, so they describe what a single long run would show.

Dense randomness is the familiar case. Where the distribution of the drifts controls the behaviour, the limit generalizes the known result for a walk in an independent environment, and the sparse structure only sets the rate at which the rescaled time converges to its limit. The distribution of the drift therefore acts as the parameter, and the answer is a function of it.

Sparsity produces a case of its own. In the second regime the limit does not reduce to the independent one, because the sites carrying a drift become the only ones that matter for the rescaled time, and the walker samples the defects one at a time, so their statistics enters the limit. That set is random, so the limit is a functional of the defect configuration and not only of the drift law.

Scales explain the difference. In an independent environment every site carries a drift, while here most sites carry none, so a walker spends long stretches in a symmetric medium and meets a defect only occasionally, and the rare meeting is what the limit records. The frequency of those meetings is what the sparse parameter controls, and it enters the limit as a rate.

Which randomness dominates is the summary. A dense environment lets the typical drift decide, and a sparse one lets the rarity of the defects decide, so the annealed average has two possible limits depending on which of those mechanisms wins, and the paper exhibits both. A model that interpolated between the two regimes would have a transition, and the two cases are its ends.
