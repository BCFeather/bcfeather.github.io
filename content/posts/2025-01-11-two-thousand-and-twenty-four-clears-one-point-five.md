Copernicus published its annual climate report on 10 January with a number that had been expected and still landed hard. The global average surface temperature for 2024 was 15.10 degrees Celsius, which is 0.12 degrees above the previous record set in 2023 and 1.60 degrees above the 1850-1900 baseline used to define the pre-industrial level. It is the first calendar year in the instrumental record to sit above 1.5 degrees. The report is compiled from six datasets, including surface stations, ships, buoys and reanalysis, and the agreement between them is what gives the number its weight.

The comparison that matters is with the long-term trend rather than with a single year. Global warming is measured as an average over two or three decades, because a year can be lifted by an El Nino in the Pacific and pushed down by a La Nina. The 2023-2024 pair carried a strong El Nino, and part of their anomaly came from it, which is why climate scientists describe 2024 as a preview rather than as a verdict.

Ocean heat is the part of the report that carries the most weight. Sea surface temperatures were at record levels for much of the year, and the heat content of the upper ocean rose again. Water absorbs more than ninety per cent of the excess energy trapped by greenhouse gases, and that storage is what drives marine heatwaves, coral bleaching and the intensification of tropical storms.

Concentrations continue to rise. Carbon dioxide reached about 422 parts per million, methane continued the increase that began in 2007, and the growth in both was faster than the average of the past decade. The report also noted that 2024 was the warmest year on record for every continental region except Antarctica and parts of the Southern Ocean. Water vapour is the other feedback, because warmer air holds more of it, and more vapour traps more heat.

Impacts were visible in the physical record rather than only in the temperature series. Arctic sea ice reached its second lowest summer minimum, glaciers lost mass for the third year running in most benchmark ranges, and extreme rainfall events were recorded from the Sahel to southeast Asia. Attribution studies published during the year linked several of those events to warming with a confidence that would have been unusual a decade ago. Attribution is now a routine calculation, and the models that perform it run in weeks rather than years.

The policy reading is narrower than the headline. The Paris Agreement targets a long-term average well below 2 degrees and an effort towards 1.5, so a single year above the threshold does not mean the target has failed. It does mean the margin has gone, and that the first year above the line arrived while emissions were still rising rather than falling. Emissions from land use and from fossil fuel burning are still at record levels, and the sinks that absorb them are weakening.
