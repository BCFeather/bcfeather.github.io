More than half of all animal species are insects with a pupal stage, in which the larva is dismantled and the adult rebuilt. The arrangement is extreme, and the reason it spread so widely has been a matter of speculation rather than measurement. The pupa is a sealed stage in which the larval tissues are histolysed and the adult imaginal discs expand, and the reconstruction is a wholesale replacement rather than a gradual modification.

A paper in *PNAS* on 9 September 2024 tests a specific hypothesis: that a trade-off between growth and differentiation explains it. A cell that is dividing cannot also be specializing, and an animal that must do both at once pays a price in speed. Testing a trade-off means measuring the two quantities it trades, and growth rate is the one that can be compared across species with reasonable confidence.

Comparison supplies the first measurement. Holometabolous insects, which pass through a pupa, grow faster than hemimetabolous insects, which hatch as small versions of the adult and moult through a series of nymphal stages. Growth rates were taken from published rearing data, which is uneven across orders, and the authors treat that unevenness as a limit on the strength of the comparison.

A model supplies the second argument. Given a larva that must reach a certain size and a body that must acquire adult structures, decoupling the two phases in time allows each to run at its own optimum, and a pupal stage is what that decoupling requires. The model is deliberately simple, with one growth term and one differentiation term, because a more detailed version would need parameters that no insect provides.

Conditions select for it. When growth is the limiting factor and the adult structures are complex, the pupa pays for itself; when the adult is simple, or when the environment rewards early reproduction, the hemimetabolous route stays competitive. Where the adult is a predator and the larva is a sessile filter feeder, the two stages occupy different niches, and decoupling them releases each from the other's constraint.

What the argument explains is a pattern rather than a single case. Fast growth is the correlated variable, and the pupa is the mechanism that removes the developmental constraint that would otherwise slow it, which is a cleaner account than the older suggestion that larvae and adults simply avoid competing. It also predicts that lineages with a short adult life and a long larval one should favour the pupal route, which is a testable ordering.

The model does not explain why some holometabolous lineages then slow down, and it treats differentiation as a single cost rather than a set of tissue-specific ones. Both are places where the theory could be refined. Extending the model to those cases would require differentiation costs that vary by tissue, which is exactly the kind of detail the current version leaves out. A fuller account would also have to explain why the pupal stage is sealed, since the seal is what allows the larval tissues to be dissolved without the adult being exposed.
