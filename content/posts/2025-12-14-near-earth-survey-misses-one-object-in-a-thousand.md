description: "A survey that has catalogued 34,000 near-Earth objects reports that its completeness in the 140-metre size class is 88 per cent, and the missing twelfth is the population that a deflection mission would have least time to reach."
---

Asteroid surveys count what they can see, and what they cannot see is the number that matters for planning. A survey's annual report puts its completeness at 88 per cent for objects above 140 metres, which means roughly one in eight in that class has never been observed.

The catalog holds 34,000 near-Earth objects, of which 900 are above a kilometre and 11,000 are above 140 metres. An object of 140 metres would destroy a metropolitan area, and a 10-kilometre warning window is what a survey needs to give a mission time to act.

Completeness varies with geometry. An object on an orbit that keeps it near the Sun's glare is hard to detect from the ground, and the survey's map of misses shows a cluster of orbits with a low solar elongation. That is 40 per cent of the missing population.

The survey's instrument is a wide-field camera on a 1.8-metre telescope, and it detects a 140-metre object at 0.6 AU. A larger aperture would reach further and cost 30 million dollars, which is the argument in the survey's funding request. A survey's software is part of its completeness, because an object is found when a difference image is linked across nights.

Deflection is the second half of the program. An impactor changes an object's orbital period, and the change needed depends on how much warning there is. A 10-year warning needs a 0.1 per cent period change; a 2-year warning needs ten times that, which no single mission can deliver.

A space-based survey is the expensive alternative. An infrared telescope in orbit would see objects that ground surveys miss because they approach from the Sun's direction, and its cost is 600 million. The ground program's marginal cost per object found is a tenth of that.

A survey's completeness is a model, not a count. The unseen population is estimated from the detected one by assuming an orbit distribution, and the assumption is what the missing eighth tests. A survey that finds an object on an unusual orbit revises the model rather than the count.

Warning time is set by an orbit's period, and an object on a short period returns often. A survey's blind spot is the object that spends most of its time inside the Earth's orbit, where the sky is bright and the telescope's field is empty.

A survey's cadence is the schedule it keeps. A telescope that observes every clear night for a decade covers the sky a dozen times, and an object is found when its motion is detected across two nights. A survey that loses a month to weather loses the objects that would have appeared in it.

Eighty-eight per cent is the number to keep. A survey that finds most of a population is a survey that has solved the problem's easy half, and the remaining eighth is expensive, because it is hidden where the instrument cannot look.
