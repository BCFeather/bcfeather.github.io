A co-repressor is a protein a transcription factor recruits to silence a gene, and the textbook picture is a general brake. A survey of the fly genome suggests each brake is wired to a particular set of enhancers. In *Science* on 14 July 2023, that picture is tested. The authors perturb each co-repressor in Drosophila, measure expression across a large set of genes, and identify which enhancers respond to which repressor. Specificity is the result. Each co-repressor has a distinct set of targets, and overlap between two repressors' targets is smaller than a general-brake model predicts.

Wiring supplies the mechanism. A repressor's effect depends on the DNA-binding factor recruiting it and on the enhancer's architecture, and those combinations produce the specificity. Identity of the recruiter is the first variable, since a repressor recruited by one factor acts where that factor binds, and the motif is therefore part of the answer.

Architecture is the second variable. An enhancer with a particular arrangement of sites favours one repressor over another, and the classification of enhancer classes is the test of that claim. Developmental consequence explains how one genome produces many patterns. A general brake would silence everything at once, while a specific brake lets a factor silence one enhancer without touching its neighbours.

Method is the advance. A perturbation of each repressor in a defined tissue plus an enhancer-activity readout turns a list of targets into a map of specificities, and the tissue-specific driver keeps tissue identity from being confounded with the respond. Conservation stays a question. Drosophila's co-repressors have mammalian homologues, and whether the specificity carries over is the next comparison; a homologue rescuing a fly mutant with the same specificity would argue for conservation, and a partial rescue would not.

Assay sets the limitation, because an enhancer's activity is measured in a reporter and a reporter is not the endogenous locus. Molecular detail is what the paper does not show, since specificity could come from direct interaction or from chromatin context, and the data distinguish the two only partly.

Gene regulation gains a correction to a metaphor, since the brake stands for a general mechanism and this map is a set of specific connections. A pair of repressors with overlapping targets suggests a common partner and a pair with disjoint targets suggests different ones; the distance at which a histone-modifying repressor acts remains the unresolved variable, because the assay does not control it.

A test of two repressors on the same enhancer would show whether they compete for one site or act at different distances, and competition is the mechanism a general-brake model would have predicted. A developmental time course would show whether the wiring changes as a tissue matures, since a repressor's targets may be defined by the factors present rather than by the repressor itself. A chromatin assay at the endogenous locus would close the gap the reporter leaves, since a reporter's context is not the locus's, and the assay is the experiment the paper's conclusion most needs.
