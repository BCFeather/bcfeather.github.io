Novak Djokovic arrived in Melbourne late on 5 January with a medical exemption from Australia's vaccination requirement for the tournament, and was detained by border officials in the early hours of the following morning. His visa was cancelled, he was taken to a hotel used to house asylum seekers, and his lawyers filed an application for review in the Federal Court by the end of the week. He had flown in from Marbella, where he had been training, and he described the arrival as routine until officers approached him at the gate.

The exemption was the part nobody could explain. Tennis Australia and the Victorian state government had set up two independent panels to assess applications, and Djokovic's application was supported by a recent infection, which the panels' rules allowed. The federal government had not been involved in that process, and when the border force looked at the entry requirements it found that prior infection did not meet the standard the Commonwealth had set for non-citizens.

Australia's politics made the case larger than a visa. The country had closed its borders for two years, endured long lockdowns in Melbourne, and elected a government that had built its reputation on caution. A famous and unvaccinated athlete arriving with an exemption looked, to a public that had followed the rules, like a privilege that ordinary people could not obtain, and the government's language about standing in line reflected that reaction. The minister's public statements during the week described the affair as a matter of sovereign right rather than of tennis, which framed the court's role in advance.

The legal question turned on procedure rather than medicine. Djokovic's lawyers argued that he had been given a valid visa and had been treated unfairly when it was cancelled at the airport without a chance to respond, and a judge quashed the decision on 10 January and ordered him released. Four days later the immigration minister cancelled the visa again, using a power that allows cancellation on health or public-interest grounds, and the same court upheld the exercise.

Serbia's government complained, and Serbian diplomats visited him in the hotel. Spain and France made public statements about their own entry rules, and the draw for the Australian Open took place while the defending champion's name remained in it, which tournament officials handled badly enough to draw criticism from players. His coach said afterwards that the episode had cost the player a week of practice at the age of thirty-four, and that the tournament would be remembered for the argument rather than for the tennis.

Djokovic was deported on 16 January and left for Dubai, and the tournament began the next day without him. His fate rested on a minister's discretion rather than on a court's finding, which is a familiar outcome in immigration law and an unfamiliar one for a sports star, and it left the question that mattered to him unresolved: whether the exemption was real or whether everyone involved had read a different rule.
