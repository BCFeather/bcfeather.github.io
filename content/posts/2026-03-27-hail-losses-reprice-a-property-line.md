---

Property insurance is priced on a catastrophe model, and a model that underestimates a hazard produces losses that the premium did not anticipate. An insurer reported this month a combined ratio of 112 for its property line, after two years in which hail losses exceeded the model's 1-in-50 estimate, and the reinsurance renewal repriced the whole line.

The model's problem was hail size distribution. A hail event is modelled by the probability of stones above a damaging diameter, and the model used a distribution calibrated on 20 years of radar data. Two events in consecutive years produced 3-centimetre stones over areas the model put at a 1-in-100 return period.

Losses followed the size threshold. A 3-centimetre stone breaks roof tiles and dents vehicle panels; a 2-centimetre stone rarely does. The loss ratio for the two events was 40 points above the model's expectation, and the reinsurer raised its attachment point.

Repricing is now sector-wide. Reinsurance renewals in the region cleared at 15 to 25 per cent higher rates for property-catastrophe cover, and primary insurers passed 8 to 12 per cent to policyholders. The line's profitability is being restored through price rather than through a change in exposure.

The model revision is the substantive fix. Two modelling firms have published updated hail hazard layers with an explicit convective-storm parameterisation, and both raise the 1-in-100 hail size by 1.5 centimetres in the affected corridor. Insurers are re-rating on those layers.

Policy response is limited. Building codes in the region require impact resistance for roof coverings in a zone drawn in 1990, and hail events have occurred outside it. A code update is under consideration and would apply only to new construction.

Secondary effects show up in mortgage lending. A lender's valuation includes insurance cost, and a 12 per cent rise in a property line reduces the affordable price. Two regional banks report a 3 per cent reduction in valuations in the worst-hit postcodes.

Contract terms shifted with the price. Reinsurers have shortened their notice periods and added a clause that allows a mid-term rate adjustment after a modelled event, which transfers the model risk to the primary insurer. Two insurers have declined those terms and retained more risk.

Building performance is the mitigation that pays. A hail-resistant roof covering costs 15 per cent more and reduces a claim's severity by 40 per cent, and one insurer now discounts the premium by 8 per cent for a certified roof. That is the only lever that reduces loss rather than redistributing it.

The model's own uncertainty is now disclosed. The insurer's filing states a 1-in-100-year loss estimate with a 30 per cent confidence band, and the reinsurer's price is set at the top of the band. That is a practice change as much as a price change.

For a reader, the lesson is that catastrophe risk is a modelled quantity and a model can be wrong for a decade. The repricing that follows is not a market panic; it is the correction of a price that had been set on a distribution nobody had tested recently.
