Solid electrolytes were expected to end the dendrite problem by being hard. In practice a lithium filament finds the pores and cracks in a ceramic and shorts the cell at currents near 1 milliamp per square centimetre, even in garnets that are more than 99 per cent dense. The current a ceramic can pass before a filament forms is called the critical current density, and it decides whether a solid-state cell charges in minutes or in hours.

A paper in *Nature Energy* works on a sulfide instead, the argyrodite lithium phosphorus sulfur chloride, and reports plating at 9 milliamps per square centimetre without dendrites once the ceramic is sintered to 99 per cent density. The critical current, measured by stepping up until a short appears, is 10 milliamps per square centimetre. The ceramic is a chloride argyrodite with a high ionic conductivity at room temperature, and the paper measures its critical current in symmetric cells with two lithium electrodes.

The authors do not stop at the number. They take focused ion beam tomography of the ceramic at 83 and 99 per cent density and compute how each microstructural change moves the critical current. Smaller pores and shorter cracks raise it; narrower cracks and a lower pore population lower it. The tomography resolves pores and cracks at the micrometre scale, and the model converts their dimensions into a predicted critical current that is then compared with the measurement.

The two effects pull in opposite directions, and the measurement settles which wins. In this material the gains outweigh the losses, which is why densification helps, and the argument explains why densifying other ceramics has sometimes failed to help at all. The result is a design rule for a ceramic instead of a recipe for a cell, and it says that a crack of a given volume is more dangerous than a pore of the same volume, which is not the intuition the field started with.

Density came from spark plasma sintering, a laboratory route. The paper says so plainly, and names scalable sintering as the next problem, since a process that only works in a press with a pulsed current cannot supply a cell factory. Spark plasma sintering reaches 99 per cent in minutes by passing a current through the die, and conventional sintering would need hours at a temperature that grows the grains and coarsens the pores.

Two further questions remain. Plating at 9 milliamps per square centimetre is a current density, not a charging rate for a practical cell, and the tests run on symmetric cells instead of full stacks with a cathode. The result nonetheless reframes the dendrite problem as a matter of pore geometry, which is a more tractable target than hardness. A full cell would also need a cathode that tolerates the stack pressure, and the pressure a sulfide needs to stay dense is the engineering question the paper leaves open.
