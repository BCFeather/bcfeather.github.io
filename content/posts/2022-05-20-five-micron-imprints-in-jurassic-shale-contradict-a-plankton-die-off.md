Coccolithophores are single-celled marine algae that build a shell out of calcite plates, each plate about five micrometres across, and when they die the plates sink and accumulate in sediment by the billions. Palaeontologists count those plates to estimate how productive the surface ocean was in a given epoch, and the count collapses at several points in the geological record that coincide with rapid warming. The usual reading is a biocalcification crisis: warm, acidic water made it expensive to build a shell, and the plankton stopped building them.

A paper in the 20 May issue of *Science* tests that reading by looking at what the rocks kept when they lost the plates. Sam Slater and his colleagues examined organic matter from the same beds under a scanning electron microscope and found imprints of coccoliths pressed into pollen grains, algal cysts and amorphous organic material, the shapes preserved as moulds rather than as mineral. They report these ghost fossils from the Toarcian oceanic anoxic event of about 183 million years ago, and also from two Cretaceous events, one around 120 million years ago and the other around 94 million years ago.

Mechanism explains the missing carbonate. Organic-rich sediment generates acidic pore water as it is buried, and acid dissolves calcite; the plates went into solution long after deposition, when the rock had already compacted enough to hold the impression of whatever was pressing against the organic matter. The rock therefore lost its shells in the ground rather than in the sea, and the count of body fossils measures the chemistry of the burial environment as much as the biology of the surface water.

If that holds, several well-known abundance curves are partly artefacts. The intervals where carbonate vanishes are precisely the organic-rich beds that acidify during burial, so the record is least trustworthy exactly where the climate analogy is drawn most confidently, and the blackout claimed for the Cenomanian-Turonian boundary becomes a layer where a metre of limestone lost its calcite to pore water, leaving a black shale that reads as an extinction and may be nothing of the kind.

The method has limits, and the authors are clear about them. A ghost requires a soft organic substrate and the right burial history, so the absence of imprints in another bed does not prove that the shells dissolved there, and imprint counts cannot recover absolute abundances because one grain of organic matter may hold several plates. What the work supplies is a second channel of evidence where the first channel had failed, and the two channels disagree in a way that will take years of section-by-section work to settle.

What emerges is a twist: the absence of a fossil can be evidence about rock rather than about life. Palaeontology has long treated dissolution as noise to be corrected for, and this paper treats it as the thing to be measured, which turns a familiar crisis narrative into a question about how the archive was written, and about which of its silences are the sea's and which are the rock's.
