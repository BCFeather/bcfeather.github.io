February at Chizhou No. 10 Middle School was the month in which the countdown stopped being a decoration. On the first Monday after the holiday the number on the board was a hundred and fifteen, written in red by someone who had arrived before the lights were switched on, and by the last week of the month it was ninety-five. Nobody erased it and nobody wrote a joke beside it, which was how the class had always treated numbers that mattered.

The weather was the thing everyone talked about, because the winter had been mild until it stopped being mild. There were four days in the middle of the month when the puddles in the courtyard froze at the edges and the radiators in the classroom were too hot in the morning and too cold by the fourth period. The boys who arrived on bicycles wore gloves that did not match, and the ones who walked in from the east gate arrived with their hair flattened by the wind.

Examinations began to arrive in a rhythm that nobody had prepared for. There was a test in every subject in each of the three weeks, and the papers came back with marks that were announced rather than handed out quietly. I remember the chemistry paper that took the class an hour and forty minutes, and the way the teacher read out the top five scores before the lesson began, and the silence that followed the fifth name.

The teachers had begun to speak about the school as something that would soon be in the past. One of them described the syllabus as finished in the way that a person describes a room that has been packed, and another told us that the months after June would be the longest we had ever had. Those sentences were the first indication that the year was a thing with an end, and the indication arrived in February rather than in June.

Home had its own timetable by then. My mother had started cooking the same three dishes in rotation, because they were the ones I would eat at ten at night, and my father asked about the mock examination before he asked about anything else. The flat was quiet in a way that a rented flat is quiet, with a television that was never turned on and a wall that carried the sound of the neighbours' water pipes at eleven.

What February did was make the arithmetic visible. A hundred and fifteen days sounds like a quantity, and the number on the board turned it into a sequence of Monday mornings, each of which had a test in it and each of which was shorter than the last. The month ended with the number at ninety-five and with the winter still going, and the spring that arrived in March would be the last one that belonged to that school. The corridor outside the classroom was colder than the classroom itself, which is a thing you notice only when you are standing in it.
