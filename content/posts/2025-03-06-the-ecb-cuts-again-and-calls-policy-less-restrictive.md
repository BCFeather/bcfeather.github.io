description: "The European Central Bank lowered its deposit rate to 2.5 per cent on 6 March, its sixth cut since June 2024, and said monetary policy was becoming meaningfully less restrictive while it trimmed its growth forecasts."
---

Governing council members met in Frankfurt with inflation at 2.2 per cent in the euro area and with a trade dispute gathering on the other side of the Atlantic. The decision lowered all three key rates by a quarter point, which put the deposit facility at 2.50 per cent, the main refinancing operation at 2.65 and the marginal lending facility at 2.90.

Staff projections published with the decision saw headline inflation averaging 2.3 per cent in 2025, 1.9 per cent in 2026 and 2.0 per cent in 2027. The upward revision for 2025 came from energy prices, and the path beyond it sits at or below the target, which is the condition that allows a central bank to keep cutting while inflation is still above 2 per cent.

Growth was revised down for both 2025 and 2026. Exports were the reason, with the threat of American tariffs weighing on an economy that sells a large share of its output abroad, and investment was weak because the uncertainty about trade policy made a long-lived project hard to commit to. A central bank that cuts into a downgrade is a central bank that has decided the risk to growth is the larger one.

Lagarde's language carried the signal. She said monetary policy was becoming meaningfully less restrictive, a phrase that members had used in the preceding weeks and that points to the neutral rate as the destination. A rate at 2.5 per cent with inflation near target is close to neutral by most estimates, which means the easy part of the cycle is finished and the remaining decisions are about how far below neutral to go.

Fiscal policy was the other subject. Germany had just agreed a reform of its debt brake that would allow borrowing for defence and infrastructure, and the prospect of several hundred billion euros of additional spending changed the growth outlook for the bloc more than any rate decision could. The central bank's projections do not include the plan in full, and the staff said the effect would be assessed once the legislation passed.

Markets read the combination as a reason to buy European assets. The German benchmark index reached a record, the spread between French and German debt narrowed, and the euro strengthened against the dollar. A rate cut that is accompanied by a fiscal expansion is a cut that the market treats as insurance rather than as a warning, and the reaction reflected that reading.

What the meeting did not settle is the end point. Two more cuts were priced for the rest of the year at the time of the decision, and the case for each depends on whether the tariff dispute escalates and on whether the German spending arrives on schedule. A central bank that has reached neutral has to judge each meeting on its own, and the judgments become harder as the room to move shrinks.
