description: "A small company has built a production in a language its audience does not know, with surtitles that translate the plot and leave the poetry alone. The experiment asks a question theatre has avoided: how much of a play is the words?"

The production opened in a converted warehouse with a hundred and twenty seats and a surtitle screen above the stage. The play is in a language that perhaps a dozen people in the audience speak, and the surtitles translate the plot, the jokes that survive translation and the stage directions a reader would find in a script. They do not translate the passages where the language is doing something other than conveying information, and the company says that is the point.

The idea is familiar in opera, where surtitles have been standard for decades and the audience is used to reading while listening to something it does not understand. It is unusual in spoken theatre, where the assumption has always been that the words are the play. A production in an unknown language puts that assumption under pressure: the actors are performing rhythm, gesture and tone, and the audience receives them directly, with the text arriving as a summary rather than as the thing itself.

The rehearsal method is the interesting part. The actors learned the text phonetically and then worked with a translator on the meaning of each passage, so that the performance is an interpretation of a text the actors understand rather than a recitation of sounds. What the audience hears is a play in a language it does not know, delivered by people who know exactly what they are saying. That gap between comprehension and expression is the experiment.

The audience response has been divided in a way the company finds encouraging. Some spectators reported that they stopped reading the surtitles after twenty minutes and simply watched, which is the outcome the production was designed to produce. Others found the evening exhausting, because the habit of reading is strong and the screen competes with the stage. Both reactions are data, and the company has been collecting them in a short questionnaire after each performance.

The economics are modest. A surtitle system costs less than a good lighting rig, and the translation takes one person a few weeks. What the production spends is the audience's patience, and that is a resource a hundred-and-twenty-seat house can afford to use. A larger theatre with a subscription audience would find the same experiment much harder to run.

Getting the surtitles right took longer than the staging. A translation that explains every line turns the evening into reading, and one that explains too little leaves the audience guessing at the plot. The company settled on a summary at the start of each scene plus translations of the exchanges that carry the action, and it tested the balance with audiences who had no access to the original text.

The question the production leaves open is whether this is a technique or a novelty. A technique would be a way of performing texts that resist translation, of which there are many: poetry, dialect writing, plays built on wordplay. A novelty would be something that works once. The company's next production is in a language half the cast speaks, which suggests it is testing the technique rather than the trick.
