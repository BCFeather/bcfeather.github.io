A central bank that holds a rate and raises a forecast is usually preparing to move, and the Bank of Japan did both on 31 July without changing a single instrument. The policy rate stays at 0.5 per cent, the highest in three decades, and the quarterly outlook lifted the projection for core inflation in the current fiscal year to about 2.7 per cent from a number below 2.

Rice is the reason the forecast moved. Staple rice prices roughly doubled over the past year after a poor harvest and a distribution problem, and because the item sits in the basket that households watch weekly, the effect on expectations is larger than its weight in the index. A food shock that shows up in the shopping street is a food shock that shows up in wage talks, which is the transmission that the bank has been waiting for since the rice price began to climb last autumn.

Wages are the other leg. The spring negotiations produced the largest nominal increase in more than thirty years, and the bank's view is that a tight labour market with about 1.2 vacancies per applicant will keep nominal pay rising even as the economy slows. A pay round that repeats next spring is the condition for the second hike, and the bank has said that it wants to see the effect in consumption before it acts.

Bond purchases continue to shrink. The bank is reducing its buying by a fixed amount each quarter under a plan agreed last year, and the taper is the quiet part of policy: it removes support from the long end and asks the market to hold more duration, which tests whether investors will accept a higher term premium, and which has already pushed the ten-year yield to a level that the finance ministry watches closely.

Growth is modest and the consumer is cautious. Household spending has risen less than wages because real incomes only turned positive this spring, and exports face the 15 per cent tariff agreed with Washington in July, which is lower than the rate threatened in April and higher than the rate of a year ago.

Yen is the variable that the bank watches without targeting. A currency near 147 to the dollar is weak enough to raise import prices and strong enough not to alarm exporters, and a bank that is about to raise rates in an economy with a large stock of public debt has to weigh the fiscal cost of a stronger currency against the inflation cost of a weaker one, a trade-off that no other major central bank faces in the same form.

What would settle the year is whether core inflation excluding fresh food stays above 2 per cent through the autumn. If it does, a hike to 0.75 per cent in the winter is the base case, and if it does not, the bank will be holding at a rate that the market has already decided is too low.
