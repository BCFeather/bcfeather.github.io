A sodium-ion cell is assembled in the discharged state, and that single fact explains most of its behaviour. Both electrodes start empty of the ion that will carry the current, so the sodium that the cell will use has to come from a compound that is already in one of the electrodes, and every sodium ion that is lost in the first cycle is lost from the total the cell can ever use. Lithium cells are built with a cathode that carries its lithium, so a small excess can be included and thrown away; sodium cells have no such margin, and the first-cycle efficiency is a design parameter rather than a nuisance. A cell that cannot spare sodium has to be designed around the loss, and the design begins with the ratio of anode to cathode mass, not the chemistry of either electrode. A cell that is balanced for the fresh state is out of balance after a hundred cycles, and the balance has to be designed for the state the cell will be in when it is retired.

Loss happens at the surface. The electrolyte is reduced at the anode before any current is delivered, and the products form a film of inorganic and organic salts that covers the carbon and keeps it from reacting further. That film is necessary, since without it the electrolyte would decompose continuously, and it consumes sodium and solvent in proportion to the surface area of the anode. Hard carbon has a large surface area by design, because its capacity comes from the pores and defects inside it, and the film is therefore correspondingly large. The film is also the reason a sodium cell has to be formed slowly, since a film that grows faster than it can be wetted is a film that cracks and re-forms on every cycle.

The mechanism of storage in hard carbon is still argued about. One account puts the sodium into the spaces between disordered graphene sheets, as in a classical intercalation compound, and another puts it into closed pores, where the metal is plated into a cavity and the voltage profile reflects that filling. The two accounts predict different voltage curves and different rate behaviour, and the answer seems to be both: intercalation at the higher voltages and pore filling at the low-voltage plateau. A cell that relies on the plateau is a cell whose anode swells and whose rate capability is limited by how fast sodium can find a pore. The debate matters commercially because a cell that stores sodium in pores has a different expansion behaviour from one that intercalates it, and the swelling is what limits the life of a pouch cell. A model of the pore filling also predicts a voltage plateau whose length depends on the pore volume, and the length is the number a cell designer would use.

Cathode choice sets the rest of the balance. Layered oxides of the O3 and P2 families offer the highest capacity and the least stability in air, since they exchange sodium for water and carbonate from the atmosphere. Prussian white analogues are cheap and rigid and cycle well, at a capacity below a lithium cathode. A cell designed around a Prussian white cathode has a different ratio of positive to negative mass from one built around a layered oxide, and the first-cycle loss matters more in the cell with less sodium to spare. Layered oxides also have to be handled in dry air, which adds a cost to a chemistry that is being sold on its cost.

Pre-sodiation is the direct answer and the hardest to do. Sodium can be added to the anode as a sacrificial salt that decomposes on first charge, as a metal foil that is pressed onto the electrode and dissolves, or electrochemically before assembly in a separate bath. Each method adds a process step, and each method leaves something behind: a decomposition residue, a foil that does not dissolve uniformly, or a wet step that has to be dried. The industry has settled on none of them, which is a sign that the gain is real and the cost is not yet known. A sacrificial salt is the most attractive route because it can be coated onto the electrode with the existing slurry process and needs no extra equipment. A foil also has to be handled in a dry room, and the sodium metal reacts with the moisture that a production line tries to exclude.

Efficiency has to be measured in a full cell, where the sodium is finite. A half cell has an unlimited reservoir of sodium on the counter electrode, so a first-cycle loss of twenty per cent looks like a film that forms and stops, and no sodium shortage ever appears. In a full cell the same film shows up as a capacity that cannot be recovered, and the cell has to be oversized on the cathode side to compensate. Reporting efficiency from half cells is the reason the literature numbers and the commercial numbers differ by more than the measurement error. The same discrepancy appears in the reported energy density, since a cell that loses a fifth of its sodium in the first cycle has to be built with a larger cathode than the chemistry alone would suggest.

The commercial argument for sodium is cost and temperature instead of energy density. Sodium carbonate is cheap and abundant, aluminium can be used as the current collector on both sides because sodium does not alloy with it at low voltage, and the cells can be shipped at zero volts, which removes a hazard from transport. The cells lose to lithium iron phosphate on specific energy, and they win where the price of a kilowatt hour and the ability to operate in the cold matter more than the mass. A first-cycle efficiency of ninety per cent and not eighty is worth several per cent of the cell's energy, which is exactly the margin that decides whether the chemistry is competitive. The temperature range is the other advantage, since a sodium cell keeps most of its capacity below zero where a lithium iron phosphate cell loses a large fraction of it, and the cold-weather market is where the first commercial deployments have gone. A plant that can coat a sacrificial salt is a plant that can make the cells, and the process is the part of the chemistry that decides whether the technology ships.
