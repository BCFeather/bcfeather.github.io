The Geminids are the strongest annual shower, and their parent is an active asteroid rather than a comet, which is why the physical nature of both body and stream has stayed contested. Bright fireballs give a way in, because the pressure at which a meteoroid comes apart records the strength of the material it is made of.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 31 January 2024 models those breakups. The authors take bright Geminid fireballs recorded by the European Fireball Network over several years and fit a semi-empirical fragmentation model to each light curve, with an automatic search based on parallel genetic algorithms that locates the aerodynamic pressures at which the body and its fragments fail.

Low pressures come out of the fit. Geminid meteoroids begin to crumble between 1 and 100 kilopascals, and the strongest parts hold out to between 0.4 and 1.55 megapascals before they break, a range that brackets the strength of a brittle solid, not a loose aggregate. A light curve records only when energy is released, so the model has to decide how many fragments carry that energy and at what pressure each of them fails.

The distribution is not uniform. With the fragmentation points assigned to layers of the incoming body, the fit recovers a strength profile inside each meteoroid, which is the difference between knowing that a meteoroid is weak and knowing which part of it is weak, and it is what allows the authors to speak of internal structure, not an average. Fitting a pressure profile instead of a single breaking point is what separates a body with layers from a body with one strength.

A trend appears with size. The minimum pressure that starts fragmentation rises with entry mass while the median pressure falls, so larger Geminids are harder to break open and easier to keep breaking, which is what a body with a coherent core and a friable exterior would do. Composition follows from the parent: (3200) Phaethon is a B-type asteroid of the carbonaceous complex, and a strength in the megapascal range with a coherent interior is consistent with compact carbonaceous material and not with a rubble pile.

What the fit cannot decide is how much of the strength is primordial. Thermal processing on Phaethon, repeated perihelion passages and the fragmentation history of the stream all alter the material, and the model reconstructs the strength at the moment of entry, so the number is a present-day property of centimetre-sized grains rather than a property of the parent body.

A direct measurement is what would separate the two. JAXA's DESTINY+ mission is planned to fly past Phaethon and to sample the dust around it, and a comparison of returned or remote dust properties with the strengths derived here would close the loop between a light curve and a material. Until then the fireball record is the only probe, and it now carries a strength distribution and not a single number. That comparison would also test whether the strength of a centimetre grain survives the trip from the parent body to the inner solar system.
