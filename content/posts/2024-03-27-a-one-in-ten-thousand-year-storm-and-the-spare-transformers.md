Geomagnetic storms driven by fast solar wind induce currents in power networks, and those currents have to go somewhere. They flow through windings and through the tie bars that carry magnetic flux in a large transformer, heating metal that no design was asked to cool under that load, and the heat collects at a hot spot that a thermal model can locate.

Where the heat concentrates decides the outcome. A transformer that overheats at a tie bar hot spot can fail in a way that takes months to replace rather than hours to repair, and the usual name for the result is a catastrophic electricity loss event. Estimating its probability has been a regional exercise, because the geoelectric field that drives the currents depends on the conductivity of the ground beneath a particular network.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 26 March 2024 makes the estimate global. The framework combines thermal models of high-voltage transformer tie bar hot spots, historical estimates of the geoelectric field, and a global conductivity model, and it covers the band between 70 degrees south and 80 degrees north geomagnetic latitude, which is the range in which most of the exposed transmission capacity sits.

One assumption carries the result. With a uniform 33 per cent spare capacity for high-voltage transformers, the analysis finds that a storm with a return period of 10,000 years would leave about 1 per cent of the population of Europe and North America without power for months to years, which is a rare storm producing a rare and slow failure.

Thermal modelling is what converts a storm into that number. The current follows the field, the field follows the conductivity of the ground, and the heating follows the current through the resistance of the tie bar; a hot spot that reaches a critical temperature fails locally, and the transformer leaves the fleet until a replacement is built, tested and transported.

Spare capacity converts the heating into a duration. One per cent of transformers failing is survivable if replacements exist and the network can reroute supply around them, and the 33 per cent figure is an average that says nothing about which transformer is the spare one, where it sits, or how long it would take to move it. Uniformity is therefore the model's main weakness, since fleets differ by country, age and design.

A timing model is missing as well. The paper does not model cascading failure, restoration logistics or the concurrency of failures, which is what turns a regional problem into a long one, and a transformer that fails slowly can be nursed through a storm, which nothing in the calculation allows for. Regional fleet data would sharpen the estimate, and so would age, since an older tie bar has less thermal margin. The framework is the contribution: it turns a rare storm into a probability of outage that an operator can put next to a maintenance budget.
