At twenty-three minutes past eleven on 8 September a shock struck, with an epicentre in Al Haouz province, south of Marrakech, at a depth of about eighteen kilometres. Seismologists measured it at magnitude 6.8, and an aftershock of 4.9 followed minutes later. Buildings collapsed across a region of mountain villages where houses are built of mud brick, stone and timber, and the death toll reached nearly three thousand within a week, with more than five thousand people injured and entire families killed in their sleep.

Damage was worst where the roads were worst. Villages in the High Atlas sit along valleys and tracks that become impassable when a hillside falls, and rescue teams spent the first two days digging with their hands and with the machinery of local farmers. Helicopters reached some communities on the second day, and the army set up field hospitals and a field kitchen. Foreign teams brought thermal cameras and cutting equipment, and the Spanish team was among the first to arrive. The old medina of Marrakech, a World Heritage site, lost buildings and part of a minaret, and houses that were already unsafe became more so.

The government's response was central and initially narrow. Morocco accepted offers of search teams from Spain, Britain, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, and it did not request the kind of international deployment that Turkey had welcomed after its earthquake in February. Officials said the priority was to arrange the roads and the field hospitals themselves, and some aid agencies said the coordination cost more time than it saved.

A mountain economy explains the losses. The region grows olives and nuts and raises goats, and many of its working-age men are away in the cities or in Europe, leaving older people and children in houses that fell at night when everyone was indoors. Winter is approaching, and the temporary shelters distributed in the first weeks were tents designed for summer. The government promised to rebuild with earthquake-resistant designs, and it said it would compensate families for demolished homes, at a rate that the families have described as too low to rebuild with.

Politics followed the rubble. Morocco's parliament opened a session on reconstruction, the king ordered a fund and donated blood, and the government said it would borrow and would accept foreign money. The country had been negotiating with the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, and the earthquake arrived as its fiscal position was already stretched by a fifth year of drought and by subsidies on food and fuel that cost more than the education budget.

A mountain range that had not shaken like this in a century has lost its villages and a part of its history, and the survivors are living under canvas as the cold arrives. The reconstruction will take years, and its quality will decide whether the next earthquake kills as many people. Seismologists have said the region will shake again, and that the building code is the only defence that can be built before it does.
