Walmart published results for the quarter to April on 17 May, and revenue rose 2.4 per cent to 141.6 billion dollars. Net income fell 25 per cent to 2.05 billion, and earnings per share were 74 cents against the 88 forecast. The shares fell 11 per cent on the day, which was the largest one-day fall since 1987.

Costs were the cause. Fuel costs rose 40 per cent, and the company said its trucks had driven 1 billion miles at higher prices. Wages rose 5 per cent after it raised its minimum to 12 dollars an hour, and it had hired 100,000 people in the year. Its gross margin fell 1.4 points. The company said it had absorbed 2 billion dollars of cost increases rather than pass them on.

Inventory was the other problem. Stock rose 33 per cent, and the company said a third of the increase was because of higher prices and the rest because it had ordered too much. It had 10 billion dollars of excess stock in categories such as clothing and home goods, and it said it would discount to clear it.

Grocery was the strong part. Sales of food rose 10 per cent, and the company's share of the grocery market rose 1 point. It said customers had shifted from branded goods to its own label, and that the average basket had smaller packs. Its e-commerce sales rose 17 per cent. The guidance was cut. The company said earnings per share for the year would fall 1 per cent rather than rise 3, and it said the second quarter would be worse than the first. It kept its revenue guidance, and it said it would buy back 10 billion dollars of stock. Its membership scheme added 5 million members, and the company raised the annual fee.

Target had reported a similar fall a day later, and the two companies lost 100 billion dollars of market value in two days. The results showed the pressure on discount retailers. The sector had benefited from the pandemic, and it was paying for the inventory it had built when demand was stronger. The company's chief executive said the quarter had been difficult and that the company would focus on groceries. It had 4,700 stores, and it said it would open 30 more in the year. It also said it would raise prices on some goods, and it said customers were resisting. Its rival had reported a 4 per cent rise in revenue and a 52 per cent fall in profit on the same day.

Shares recovered in the summer. The company cleared its inventory by the end of the second quarter, and its margin returned to 24 per cent. It raised its forecast in August, and the stock ended the year 1 per cent lower. Its revenue for the year was 611 billion dollars, up 6 per cent. Operating income fell 23 per cent, and free cash flow was 3 billion dollars.
