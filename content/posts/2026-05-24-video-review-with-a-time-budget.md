---

A match that pauses for four minutes while a video assistant checks an incident is not more accurate for the spectator; it is the same decision delivered late. The body that writes football's laws amended its review protocol this month to put a clock on the process, publishing maximum decision times of 90 seconds for factual corrections and 180 seconds for subjective judgements.

The distinction is the useful part. Factual questions, offside position, ball out of play, identity of the offender, are checkable against geometry and time. Subjective questions, whether a challenge was reckless, whether a handball was deliberate, require an interpretation, and interpretations take longer because they involve consensus among officials rather than a measurement.

The protocol now requires the decision clock to be displayed on the stadium board and on the broadcast overlay. Transparency is the secondary aim: a spectator who sees the clock understands that the delay has a defined limit rather than being open-ended, and the reviewers are under a constraint they cannot negotiate away.

Fallback behaviour is specified, which is the detail that turns a target into a rule. If the review exceeds its limit, the on-field decision stands, and the review cannot return a different outcome. That reverses the current default, where a review may continue until the reviewers reach a conclusion, and it deliberately makes a wrong outcome more likely in order to make a predictable one.

Umpire workload changes with it. A 90-second limit requires the assistant to have cued the relevant camera angles before the referee signals, which is a preparation discipline rather than a review skill. Training programmes have begun building pre-signal checklists, and the first pre-season seminar was held in March.

Evidence from trials is limited and favourable at the margins. A pilot in three leagues over two seasons reported median review times falling from 128 to 74 seconds for factual checks, with no measurable change in decision accuracy on a sample of incidents independently adjudicated. Subjective reviews shortened less, from a median of 214 to 189 seconds.

What the rule does not fix is the number of reviews. Longer protocols create pressure to review more incidents, since each review seems cheap once it is bounded, and the total time lost to stoppages is the product of two numbers. The pilot's total delay per match fell 17 per cent, not because reviews were fewer but because they were quicker.

Cost of the protocol falls on the broadcaster and the venue. Pre-cued camera angles require more operators and a faster switching system, and the stadium board needs a display that the referee's communication system can drive. Leagues that installed the required hardware report spending a low six-figure sum per stadium, which is minor for a top division and prohibitive for a third tier.

Player behaviour is the untested variable. A review that must conclude within three minutes may discourage players from demanding checks they know will fail, or may encourage tactical appeals late in a match when a stoppage suits the defending side. The pilot collected no data on appeals, and the law-makers have asked for it before the next revision.

Reading this as a sports fan, the interesting shift is that the law now treats accuracy as one objective among several rather than as the sole one. That is an unusual admission in a rulebook, and it will produce arguments whenever a clock-bound decision is visibly wrong. The trade is deliberate, and the spectacle is what it is trying to protect.
