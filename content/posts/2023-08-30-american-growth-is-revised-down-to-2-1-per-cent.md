American output grew at an annual rate of 2.1 per cent in the second quarter, the commerce department said on 30 August, down from the advance estimate of 2.4. The revision was in line with the forecast of 2.1. The growth in the first quarter was left at 2 per cent, and the year-on-year rate was about 2.4. The revision was the first in three quarters to cut the growth, and the average of the first half is about 2.05 per cent. The nominal output grew at about 4.5 per cent, and the deflator was about 2.0.

Inventories did the work. The change in the stock added about 0.1 percentage points instead of 0.2, and the business investment was revised down by about 1 per cent. The spending on the equipment was cut to a gain of about 5.7 per cent from 7.7. The inventories were about 20 billion dollars lower than the advance estimate, and the retailers were the part that was revised. The spending on the structures was revised up to about 8 per cent as the factories were built.

Consumption was revised up a little. The personal spending rose at about 1.7 per cent, and the services at about 2.2 as the travel and the restaurants held. The goods fell at about 0.2 as the car purchases slowed after the strong spring. The saving rate was about 3.6 per cent, which is the lowest since the autumn of 2022.

Trade subtracted a little more. The net trade took about 0.2 points instead of 0.1, and the exports were revised down as the demand from Europe weakened. The government spending was revised up to about 3.3 per cent. The imports fell at about 6 per cent, and the exports at about 11 as the global demand weakened. The government spending added about 0.6 percentage points, and the one of the state and local about 0.4.

Profits were strong. The corporate profits rose by about 2.5 per cent on the quarter, and the one of the financial sector by about 4 as the trading and the net interest income held. The unit labour costs rose by about 2.5 per cent, down from 3.6. The profits were about 3.4 trillion dollars on the annual basis, and the share of the output was about 11 per cent. The share of the profits in the output was about 11 per cent, which is about the average of the last decade.

This report is the one that the autumn forecast rests on. A growth of 2.1 per cent with the core inflation at 3.7 is the combination that argues the economy can take the higher rates, and the argument is one the committee will weigh in September. The committee meets on 19 and 20 September, and the market sees about a 20 per cent chance of another increase.
