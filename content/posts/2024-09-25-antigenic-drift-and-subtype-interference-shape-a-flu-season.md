Influenza seasons differ from year to year in ways that surveillance records but does not explain, and two candidate explanations have competed: the virus changes its surface proteins enough to reinfect people who were infected before, and the subtypes that circulate together interfere with one another. Surveillance records the size of each season and the dominant variant, and the two are not always related in the way that theory would predict.

A paper in *eLife* on 25 September 2024 puts numbers on both. The authors analyse A(H3N2) epidemics in the United States and ask how much of the variation in a season's size can be assigned to antigenic drift in hemagglutinin and how much to subtype interference. The analysis is statistical rather than mechanistic, and its value lies in the size of the record it can bring to a question of that kind.

Antigenic drift is the familiar mechanism. Mutations in the epitopes of hemagglutinin and neuraminidase allow the virus to escape antibodies that a previous infection or vaccination had produced, and a drift variant therefore finds a larger pool of susceptible people. Drift is measured by antigenic cartography, which places variants in a map whose distances are defined by serological cross-reaction.

Subtype interference is the less familiar one. When A(H1N1) and A(H3N2) circulate in the same population, infection with one can alter the susceptibility to the other, through cross-reactive immunity or through competition for hosts, and the effect shows up as a season that is smaller than the drift alone would predict. Interference has been documented between subtypes for decades, and its magnitude has been hard to estimate from observational data alone.

The analysis treats the two as separable terms in a model of epidemic size, and the result is that together they account for most of the observed variability, which is a stronger claim than either mechanism alone could support. Separating the terms requires an assumption about how they combine, and the paper tests that assumption rather than adopting it silently.

Practical consequence concerns forecasting. A model that tracks drift but ignores interference will over-predict a season in which the other subtype has just swept through the population, and the reverse error is possible as well. A forecast calibrated on past seasons inherits the mixture of mechanisms those seasons contained, which is a reason to model them separately.

Limits are those of an analysis built on surveillance data. Reporting intensity varies, the serological history of a population is only partly known, and the model treats immunity as a single quantity when it is stratified by age and by infection order. The paper's contribution is to make both mechanisms explicit and to compare them on the same timescale, which is what a forecasts needs before it can be improved. None of those problems is fatal, but each narrows the range of seasons over which the conclusion can be trusted.
