Insect taste has long sat outside the mammalian template. Mammals read sweet, bitter and umami through G-protein-coupled receptors that hand the signal to second messengers inside the cell. Flies read them through gustatory receptors, a family of 68 proteins in *Drosophila*, nine of which carry the sweet sense and the rest of which cover bitter, pheromonal and contact chemistry. Until this month nobody had a structure of any of them, so the family was classified by sequence and by behaviour, which is a thin basis for a receptor.

Guo Jiangtao at Zhejiang University, working with Xu Haoxin of the Liangzhu Laboratory and groups at the Chinese Academy of Sciences, published cryo-electron microscopy structures of GR43a and GR64a on 2 February 2024 in *Science*. The answer to the classification question is a channel, and the mechanism is direct.

GR43a and GR64a split the sugar world between them along chemical lines. GR43a responds to fructose and refuses glucose and the disaccharides. GR64a responds to sucrose and maltose and refuses the monosaccharides. Electrophysiology and calcium imaging in HEK293 cells confirmed that sugar binding opens a cation conductance, so the receptor is sensor and switch at once, with no second messenger in between.

Cryo-EM maps at 2.5 to 2.6 ångström resolution show a tetramer arranged like a four-bladed pinwheel. Each subunit carries seven transmembrane helices, of which S1 to S6 build the ligand-binding domain and the four S7b helices meet at the centre to form the pore. In the sugar-free state the pore is shut. Sugar occupies a pocket on the outer face of each subunit, and the four pockets work together to open the gate, which is why the tetramer and not the monomer is the functional unit.

A second route into the pore appeared in the maps. On the intracellular side, between neighbouring subunits, a lateral cavity opens toward the membrane, and the authors argue that ions may enter through it as well as through the central channel. Whether both routes carry current in a living sensillum is a question the structures raise without settling.

Two things the structures cannot show are worth stating. The receptors were solved in detergent, outside the membrane and the sensillum that shape their behaviour, and the lateral cavity has no confirmed current path. Both questions need electrophysiology on the intact organ.

Practically, the work hands insect-control research a molecular target. Flies find hosts, mates and oviposition sites by chemical cues, and a structure of the receptors that read sugars and bitter compounds gives designers a handle for lures and repellents acting on machinery that mammals never share, which is a safety argument as much as a specificity one.

The family has 68 members in Drosophila alone, most of them still uncharacterised, and the two structures reported here give the rest a template to be modelled against. The paper also closes a taxonomic gap in a tidy way. Taste appears to have been solved twice, by two unrelated molecular machines, and the insect version is the older and simpler of the two.
