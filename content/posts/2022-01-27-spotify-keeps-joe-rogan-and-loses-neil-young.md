description: "A veteran songwriter told the streaming service to choose between his catalogue and its most popular podcast, and the company chose the podcast, which is the deal it signed when it paid for exclusivity."
---

Neil Young asked Spotify to remove his music on 24 January unless the company also removed The Joe Rogan Experience, the podcast it licenses exclusively, over what he described as the spread of misinformation about vaccines. Spotify took the catalogue down on 26 January, and Joni Mitchell asked for the same treatment the following day, and the company complied with that request as well. The company's shares had fallen through the winter on subscriber worries, and the dispute arrived when it could least afford a cultural fight. Two other musicians withdrew catalogues in the same week, and the company said it had received more requests than it expected.

The dispute is about a contract rather than about a song. Spotify had paid a reported hundred million dollars or more in 2020 for exclusive rights to Rogan's show, which brings millions of listeners and advertising revenue, and the deal had made him the most valuable single property on the platform. Removing him would cost the company money and would also concede that a licensing decision can be reversed by a musician's objection. The episode also revealed how little the platform's rules said about licensed speech, which is a category its public guidelines had never addressed.

Young's letter was blunt and it made the terms explicit. He wrote that Spotify could have Rogan or could have him, and he described the podcast as spreading false claims about vaccination, a reference to episodes in which Rogan interviewed guests who questioned the shots and in which he made claims about treatments that regulators had not approved. Rogan's contract gave him editorial control, which meant that the company could remove an episode only by breaching the deal or by invoking its own rules, and it had done neither. Advertising revenue from podcasts had become a meaningful line in the company's accounts, and the show was the largest single source of it.

Company's answer was procedural. It said it had removed the music at the artist's request, that it does not editorialise the content it licenses, and that its rules on misinformation apply to podcasts as they apply elsewhere. Those rules had been written after a year of argument about medical claims on the platform, and their enforcement has been uneven in ways that the dispute made visible.

Wider argument is about what a streaming service is. A record label is a licensor of music with a catalogue and a taste; a platform that pays for exclusive speech is a publisher in all but name, and the two businesses sit inside the same company. Young's demand forced the company to say out loud that it had chosen the second one.

Musicians watched the episode closely. Several said publicly that they would not follow him, and several said privately that they had no leverage against a platform that pays for catalogues in bulk. The catalogue that came down is small; the precedent that it set, that a service will keep the podcast and lose the song, is the part that will be remembered.
