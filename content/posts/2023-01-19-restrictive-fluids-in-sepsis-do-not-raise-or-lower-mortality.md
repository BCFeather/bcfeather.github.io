title: "Restrictive fluids in sepsis do not raise or lower mortality"
date: "2023-01-19T09:30:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "Restrictive fluids with earlier vasopressors give the same 90-day mortality as a liberal strategy in sepsis-induced hypotension, the CLOVERS trial finds in *The New England Journal of Medicine*."
---

Sepsis lowers blood pressure by dilating vessels and leaking fluid into tissue, and the first response is fluid. How much fluid, and when to stop giving it and start a vasopressor instead, has been argued for two decades without a definitive trial. The trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 21 January 2023 enrolled patients with sepsis-induced hypotension that persisted after one to three litres of fluid. They were randomized to a restrictive strategy, with limited fluid and earlier vasopressors, or to a liberal strategy with more fluid and vasopressors only when needed.

The result is a null. Mortality before discharge home by day 90 did not differ significantly between the groups, in either direction. The trial was designed to detect a difference, and the absence of one is reported with a confidence interval that excludes large effects.

Secondary outcomes describe the two paths. The restrictive group received less fluid and more vasopressor, and the liberal group the reverse. Organ support, length of stay and the rate of renal replacement therapy were similar, which suggests that the two strategies trade one intervention for another without changing the trajectory.

The clinical reading is that both strategies are acceptable. A clinician who worries about fluid overload can restrict and press early, and one who worries about hypoperfusion can load and wait, and the trial does not punish either. That is a permissive result, and permissive results are what guidelines need when the evidence is balanced.

One limitation is the population. Patients who had already received large volumes before enrolment were included, and the comparison is between strategies after initial resuscitation rather than between strategies from the first hour. The first hour is where the most influential protocols sit.

A second limitation is the outcome.The trial was not powered to detect a difference in days alive outside intensive care. Mortality at 90 days is a blunt measure, and a strategy that shortens intensive care without changing survival is a strategy with a real benefit that the primary end point cannot see. The paper's secondary analyses hint at that, and they are underpowered to prove it. What the field needs next is a trial in the patients who are most fluid-sensitive. A patient with heart failure or with acute kidney injury may respond differently, and a subgroup analysis in a trial of 1,500 patients cannot settle it.

Practically, the emphasis shifts. The question is no longer whether to restrict or to load, but how to tell, at the bedside, which patient is which. Dynamic measures of fluid responsiveness are the candidate answer, and they have never been tested in a trial of this size. The cost of the two strategies differs, and the trial did not collect it. Vasopressors require central access and a monitored bed, and fluid requires neither, so a strategy that shifts from fluid to drug moves expenditure from the pharmacy to the intensive care unit without necessarily buying survival.
