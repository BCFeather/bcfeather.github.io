When a massive star collapses, most of its energy leaves as neutrinos, not light. A supernova in the Milky Way would deliver an enormous burst through every detector on Earth, and one did in 1987, producing two dozen events across three experiments. Since then, silence. The galaxy hosts a supernova only a few times a century, so the absence is expected, and it leaves a puzzle: the neutrinos from all the supernovae in the universe, added together, should form a faint background, and that background had never been separated from the noise.

A reanalysis of ten years of IceCube data, published this month, reports a flux consistent with that background. The result is not a detection in the ordinary sense. Nobody has pointed to a source. What the collaboration did is show that the measured event rate above a certain energy, after every known background has been subtracted, matches the prediction for a diffuse supernova neutrino background within the errors, and that the prediction cannot be excluded by any alternative that the data could distinguish.

Straightforward physics sits behind the prediction. A core-collapse supernova releases about 99 per cent of its energy in neutrinos over ten seconds, and the universe has been making them since the first stars formed. The flux at Earth is the integral over cosmic history, redshifted by expansion, and it peaks at a few tens of megaelectronvolts, an energy range where the atmospheric neutrino background from cosmic-ray interactions is large. Separating the two requires a detector with a low threshold and excellent reconstruction, which is what IceCube's upgrade has been building towards.

What the collaboration fits is a shape rather than a lone event. Supernova neutrinos arrive in a thermal spectrum, and the atmospheric background has a different one, falling more steeply with energy. A fit to the distribution above the threshold, with the atmospheric component constrained by dedicated measurements, yields a supernova component that is positive at a modest significance. The paper is explicit that this is evidence rather than discovery, and that a detector with a lower threshold and a larger volume would turn it into a measurement.

Why the number matters beyond the headline is that it constrains the cosmic star formation rate at high redshift, in a way that is independent of light. Supernovae produce dust that obscures them, and the optical estimates of the rate at early times are uncertain. A neutrino flux counts every collapse, obscured or not, and the comparison between the two is a test of the models that connect galaxies, stars and dust.

Every neutrino result eventually reaches the same next step: more exposure and a lower threshold. A detector under construction in the Mediterranean and an upgrade in the Antarctic will both lower the energy floor, and the background becomes a measurement when the error bars shrink enough to distinguish models rather than merely accommodate them. Until then, the result stands as what it is: the first evidence for a population of explosions that nobody has watched, seen through the particles they emit in their first seconds.
