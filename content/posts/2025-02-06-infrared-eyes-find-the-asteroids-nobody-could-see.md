Asteroid surveys work by reflected light. A telescope on the ground sees a rock in the main belt as a faint point whose brightness depends on its size and on how reflective its surface is, and both are unknown until something else measures them. A dark asteroid and a bright one of half the diameter can return the same flux, and the bias that follows is the reason the catalogues are incomplete at the small end.

Infrared observations remove part of that ambiguity. An asteroid at a few astronomical units is warmed by the Sun to a temperature of a couple of hundred kelvin, and it re-emits that heat at wavelengths near ten to twenty micrometers. The emitted flux depends on the diameter and on the temperature, and the temperature depends on the albedo, so a measurement at thermal wavelengths and one in the visible together give both quantities without an assumption.

A report in *Nature* on 6 February used the mid-infrared instrument on the James Webb Space Telescope to survey a field in the main belt, and the telescope's sensitivity at those wavelengths reached objects a few tens of metres across at distances where a ground-based survey detects nothing. Ground telescopes cannot observe in the mid-infrared from below the atmosphere, because water vapour absorbs at those wavelengths and because the thermal background of a warm telescope swamps a cold target.

Population that appears in such a survey is the one that matters for planetary defence. A body of fifty metres does not produce a global catastrophe, and it does produce damage over a region the size of a city if it arrives with the right geometry. The surveys that find the global-scale threats, which are the kilometre-sized objects, have found nearly all of them; the smaller sizes are where the catalogue is thin, and a thin catalogue is a catalogue that cannot give a warning with years of notice.

Characterization is the second use. A thermal measurement yields a diameter, and a diameter with a mass, which radar or a spacecraft can supply, yields a density, and a density separates a rubble pile from a monolithic rock. That distinction decides whether a deflection by impact would work, which is the technique that the DART mission tested in 2022 on a moonlet of Didymos.

Cost is observing time. Webb is oversubscribed by a factor of several, and a survey of faint asteroids competes with the programmes that study exoplanet atmospheres and the early universe. A dedicated survey would require a facility built for the purpose, and the proposals for a space-based infrared telescope for planetary defence have been made and not funded at the scale that a complete catalogue of fifty-metre objects would need.

What the paper establishes is the feasibility. The instrument can find these objects, the thermal flux is measurable, and the orbit can be determined well enough that a future close approach is predictable. The step from a demonstration to a survey is a budget decision, and the decision is easier when the demonstration exists.
