The length of a day is not constant, and the deviations are tiny: a millisecond here, a fraction of a millisecond there, measured by comparing the rotation of the solid Earth against the ticking of atomic clocks and against the positions of distant quasars. The variations have been attributed to tides, to the redistribution of mass as ice sheets melt, and to exchanges of angular momentum between the solid mantle and the fluid core. Explaining the residual signal has been a problem for decades.

A modelling study published in *Nature* on 23 September proposes a mechanism that operates inside the core itself. The liquid iron alloy of the outer core flows, generates the magnetic field, and feels both the pull of gravity from the mantle above and the Lorentz force from the field it produces. Zhang and Dumberry argue that a tug-of-war between these two forces can produce fluctuations in the rotation of the core and hence in the length of the day.

Attractive because it links two observables that have been difficult to reconcile. The magnetic field recorded in rocks shows reversals and intensity changes on timescales of thousands of years, and the length of day shows variations on timescales of years to decades, and a single mechanism that couples them would explain both. The gravitational interaction comes from density heterogeneity in the lowermost mantle, which is known from seismic imaging to be uneven.

Modelling is not observation, and the paper's claim rests on parameters that cannot be measured directly. The viscosity of the outer core, the strength of the magnetic field at the core-mantle boundary and the amplitude of density anomalies in the deep mantle are all estimated within ranges, and a model that reproduces the observed wobble within those ranges is a candidate explanation. Competing explanations involving tidal dissipation have not been excluded.

The deep Earth also matters for navigation and for timekeeping. Satellite orbits and financial systems rely on clocks that stay synchronized with the planet's rotation, and a leap second exists because the two drift apart. Any improvement in predicting that drift has value beyond geophysics, and the prediction horizon is limited by how well the core's behaviour is understood.

Structures that drive the mechanism are the ones seismologists have been mapping for thirty years. Large low-shear-velocity provinces beneath Africa and the Pacific are the density anomalies in question, and their stability over hundreds of millions of years would provide a steady gravitational forcing. If the model is right, the shape of the deep mantle is written into the length of a day.

A confirmation would require a better record of both variables. Length-of-day measurements exist for a century and for a few intervals in the geological past, and magnetic field records come from lavas and sediments with dating that is coarse by comparison. The paper gives the field a mechanism worth testing, which is what a good model is for.
