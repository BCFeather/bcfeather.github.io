description: "The House passed a stopgap spending bill on 14 November 2023 and the Senate followed on 15 November, with two separate deadlines in January and February, ending a standoff that had pitted the new speaker against the right wing of his own party."
---

The Senate passed a stopgap spending bill on the evening of 15 November, a day after the House approved the same measure, and President Biden signed it before the deadline at midnight on 17 November. The bill funds part of the government through 19 January and the rest through 2 February, an unusual structure that the new speaker, Mike Johnson, designed to avoid a single cliff and to buy time for the twelve annual appropriations bills.

In the House the vote was 336 to 95, with 209 Democrats and 127 Republicans in favour, and it was passed under a procedure that required a two-thirds majority because Johnson could not rely on his own conference. Ninety-three Republicans opposed it, most of them from the Freedom Caucus, which had spent the year demanding deep cuts and a single deadline that would force them. The speaker had been in the job for less than three weeks when he brought the bill to the floor.

Johnson's calculation was straightforward. The alternative was a shutdown that would have furloughed federal workers and closed national parks over Thanksgiving, and the Senate had already signalled that it would not accept the deeper cuts. By splitting the deadlines, he separated the defence and veterans funding, which he expected to pass with bipartisan support, from the domestic programmes that the right wanted to cut. The approach won Democratic votes and lost him support on the right.

Spending stayed at the levels agreed in the spring budget deal and included no new money for Ukraine or Israel, both of which the White House had requested in a supplemental package of more than a hundred billion dollars. That package became the next fight, and it split the Republican conference more sharply than the spending bill had, with a faction arguing that money for Ukraine should be tied to border enforcement.

What the bill left untouched was the deeper structural problem. Congress has not passed all twelve appropriations bills on time since 1996, and it has funded the government through continuing resolutions for most of the years since. Each stopgap carries the same cost: agencies operate at last year's levels, new programmes cannot start, and the threat of a shutdown becomes a routine instrument of negotiation rather than an emergency.

For Johnson, the vote was a first test of whether a speaker with a four-seat majority can govern. He passed the bill with Democratic votes, which is what his predecessor did in the spring and what ultimately cost Kevin McCarthy his job. The difference is that Johnson has the goodwill of the right for now, and that the deadlines he set fall early in a presidential election year, when a shutdown would be blamed on whoever is seen to have caused it. The Senate's vote was 87 to 11, with the opponents drawn from both parties, and the White House said the president would sign as soon as the measure reached his desk, which he did the following morning.
