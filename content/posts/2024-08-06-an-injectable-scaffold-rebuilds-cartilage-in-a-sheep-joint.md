Hyaline cartilage does not heal. It has no vessels, few cells and little capacity to rebuild the matrix that gives a joint its smooth bearing surface, so a defect left after injury tends to enlarge until the bone beneath it is exposed. A defect that reaches the bone surface is usually treated by drilling, by grafting or by an implant, and each of those has a failure mode.

A paper in *PNAS* on 6 August 2024 reports a material designed for that gap. The scaffold combines two components: a peptide amphiphile supramolecular polymer that binds transforming growth factor β-1, and crosslinked hyaluronic acid microgels that drive the peptides into filament bundles. Two components with different jobs is the design principle, since one supplies the signal and the other supplies the mechanics.

The architecture mimics a motif. Bundles of filaments are common in musculoskeletal tissue, where collagen packs into fibres that carry load, and reproducing that hierarchy is what allows a soft injectable material to behave like a stiff one once it is in place. Bundles form because the microgels template the peptides, and the resulting material stiffens without needing a chemical crosslinker.

Delivery is the practical advantage. The material is a slurry that can be injected into a defect, and exposure to calcium ions triggers the formation of a porous rubbery solid, so the surgeon fills the space rather than cutting a preformed implant to fit it. Calcium is already present in the joint, so the setting trigger is the tissue itself rather than an external light or temperature source.

Bioactivity comes from the bound cytokine. TGFβ-1 drives chondrogenic differentiation, and holding it in the scaffold rather than dosing it into the joint keeps the signal where the cells are, which is the difference between a material that instructs and one that merely occupies. Retention also limits the dose, since a cytokine that stays put can act at a concentration far below one that diffuses away.

The sheep model is the test that matters. A joint of that size carries loads closer to a human knee than a mouse joint does, and the paper reports cartilage formation in the defect over the study period. Sheep are used for cartilage work because their stifle joint approximates the human knee in size and load history.

Limits remain. A sheep trial over months cannot show whether the repair survives a decade of walking, and the mechanical behaviour of the repaired surface under shear is reported only indirectly. Integration with the surrounding cartilage, the seam that typically fails first, is the place to look next.
