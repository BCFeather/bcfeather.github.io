Millisecond pulsars spin hundreds of times a second and sweep a beam past the Earth with a regularity that rivals atomic clocks, which is why radio astronomers have spent two decades using them as a timing array to listen for gravitational waves. The waves expected at these frequencies belong to a different class from the brief chirps of merging stellar-mass black holes. They come from pairs of supermassive black holes at the centres of merged galaxies, orbiting each other for millions of years and filling the universe with a low, continuous strain.

The standard method measures times of arrival at radio telescopes, and it carries a complication that took years to appreciate. Radio pulses slow down as they cross the ionized gas of the interstellar medium, the delay varies with frequency and with the line of sight, and the correction is imperfect in ways that can look like the very signal being sought. A background of gravitational waves and a badly modelled plasma can produce spectra that resemble each other, and the field has had to argue carefully about which is which.

A new measurement sidesteps that problem entirely. Gamma rays from the Fermi Large Area Telescope arrive at frequencies a hundred billion times higher than radio, so the plasma barely affects them, and the spacecraft's clock timestamps individual photons to within 300 nanoseconds. The trade-off is photon count: the telescope collects only a few hundred photons from a faint pulsar in a year, which is why the analysis had to run for more than a decade. Using 12.5 years of survey data, the collaboration timed 35 of the brightest millisecond pulsars and searched for a common red signal in their residuals. Two independent pipelines, one working photon by photon and one folding data into arrival times, agreed on every pulsar where they overlapped.

No such signal appeared. The team reported a 95 per cent credible upper limit on the characteristic strain of one part in a hundred trillion at a frequency of one cycle per year, a figure that sits three to five times above the amplitude that radio arrays had already reported as a possible detection. That gap is the useful part: the two instruments have different noise, and an independent method that finds nothing in the same band forces the radio community to defend its noise model.

The technique also improves with time in a predictable way. Sensitivity scales as the observing span raised to the power of minus thirteen sixths, so another five years of data would lower the ceiling by a factor of about three, and the constant instrument calibration makes the low-frequency end of the band easier to reach than it is with radio arrays.

Whether a background is finally heard will depend on how heavy the black hole pairs are and how often galaxies merge, and on whether the radio signal survives scrutiny. What this paper supplies amounts to a referee rather than a detection, and fields that claim a discovery usually need one more than they need another confirmation. The arrays that will matter in the next decade are those that can rule out noise in more than one way, and this is the first that does so from orbit.
