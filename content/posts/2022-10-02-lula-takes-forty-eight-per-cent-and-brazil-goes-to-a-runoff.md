Voting closed at five in the afternoon in Brasília, and the count was finished before midnight. Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva took forty-eight per cent and Jair Bolsonaro took forty-three, and neither crossed the line that ends a Brazilian election in one round. The runoff was fixed for 30 October. For the first time since the return of civilian rule, a president and a former president would meet in a second round. In the streets around the counting centres, crowds in red and in green waited without incident.

The opinion polls had put Lula ahead by double digits through September, and the gap closed in the final week. The polling firms were wrong about the size of the president's vote, and their error became the story of the night. Bolsonaro's campaign had spent the month arguing that the institutes were measuring the wrong country, and the result gave him a claim he intends to use. Simone Tebet and Ciro Gomes, who finished far behind, were courted within hours for their endorsements. The Senate and the Chamber returned a large bloc of the president's allies, which means the next government will face an opposition with weight.

Lula, who is seventy-six, governed from 2003 to 2010, when commodity prices were high and his programme was chiefly one of cash transfers to poor households. He left office with the highest approval rating of any president since the return of democracy, and with an economy growing at seven per cent a year. He was later convicted on corruption charges and spent five hundred and eighty days in prison before the Supreme Court annulled the convictions on procedural grounds in 2021. His running mate this time is Geraldo Alckmin, a conservative former governor who ran against him in 2006. The ticket is an alliance of convenience that both men describe as a government of reconstruction.

Bolsonaro, who is sixty-seven, campaigned on the record of a government that cut taxes on fuel, expanded the payment to the poorest and appointed a majority of the Supreme Court. His party did better in the Chamber than the analysts had forecast, and it won governorships in the south-east. His attacks on the electronic voting system had led diplomats to say publicly that they would watch the count. He said in the last debate that he would accept the result, and he said it in a conditional clause that his opponents quoted all evening.

Turnout was about seventy-nine per cent, and the count ran fast because it runs on a single national system. In São Paulo, the largest state, the governor's race went to a runoff as well. The electoral court reported no serious complaint, and the result was accepted by both camps within four hours. The result removes the certainty that the polls had offered, and it hands the campaign four weeks in which both candidates will fight over the same pool of centrist voters.

Both campaigns said the second round would be a choice about the past. Lula promised to restore the social programmes that the current government had trimmed and to reopen the Amazon fund with foreign donors. Bolsonaro promised a second term that would finish the privatisations he began and would keep the appointments he has made to the courts. Neither of them mentioned the other's criminal record, which the advertisements will do for them. The campaign resumes on Monday with a broadcast in which each side gets the same number of minutes.
