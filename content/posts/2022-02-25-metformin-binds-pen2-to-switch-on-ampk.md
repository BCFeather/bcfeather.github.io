Metformin lowers glucose, reduces hepatic fat and extends lifespan in some animals, and it has done so for decades without a known molecular target. The drug activates AMP-activated protein kinase, and the assumption has been that the kinase is switched on indirectly, through a change in cellular energy charge. The kinase sits at the centre of a signalling pathway that responds to glucose starvation, and a drug that activates it without starving the cell is pharmacologically unusual.

A chemical biology approach has now identified the missing link. The authors synthesized a photoactive derivative of metformin, used it to label proteins that bind the drug in living cells, and found PEN2, a subunit of the gamma-secretase complex, as the binding partner. The work appeared in *Nature*, 23 February 2022.

Metformin binds PEN2 at micromolar concentrations, which is the range reached in the portal vein at clinical doses, and the bound protein associates with ATP6AP1, a subunit of the lysosomal proton pump. That association inhibits v-ATPase and activates AMPK without changing cellular AMP levels, which is the mechanism that the energy-charge model had assumed. ATP6AP1 is the accessory protein that presents the pump to the pathway, and metformin's effect depends on that presentation, which is why the mutant that cannot bind ATP6AP1 loses the response.

Genetic tests support each step. Removing PEN2 or replacing it with a mutant that cannot bind ATP6AP1 blunted the activation of AMPK, and in mice a liver-specific deletion of the gene abolished the reduction in hepatic fat while an intestinal deletion impaired the glucose-lowering effect. The liver and intestine experiments were done separately, and each deletion removed only the local effect, which is a clean demonstration of tissue autonomy.

The tissue specificity is informative. The liver and the intestine use the same molecular route for different outcomes, which explains why metformin reduces fat in one organ and glucose absorption in another, and why the two effects can be separated by where the gene is removed. Lifespan extension in nematodes requires the same gene, since knocking down its worm homologue abolished the effect of metformin, and that conservation is the strongest evidence that the route is ancient.

The concentrations used in vitro are higher than the peak plasma levels achieved in patients, and the authors argue that the portal circulation and the lysosomal compartment concentrate the drug. That argument is reasonable and is not a direct measurement, so the gap between the probe's affinity and the clinical dose remains a question.

An old drug with a new target invites repurposing. If the effect runs through PEN2, then other inhibitors of the lysosomal pump might reproduce the metabolic benefit, and compounds that bind PEN2 more tightly might extend the lifespan effect without the gastrointestinal side effects that limit adherence. The gastrointestinal side effects that limit adherence are likely to be a consequence of the same local action in the intestine, and a drug that reached the liver without touching the gut might be better tolerated.
