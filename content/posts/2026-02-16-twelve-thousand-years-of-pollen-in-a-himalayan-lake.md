Lake sediments accumulate layer by layer, and each layer carries pollen from the surrounding vegetation. A core drilled from a lake at 4,800 metres in the Himalaya has produced a 12,000-year record, and the treeline's movement in it is the finding.

Dating rests on two independent methods. The core has annual laminations, counted and cross-checked against radiocarbon dates on terrestrial plant fragments, and the two agree within 40 years across the sequence. That is tight enough to compare intervals of a century.

Pollen analysis identifies taxa to family and often to genus, and the proportion of arboreal pollen tracks the treeline's elevation. The record shows the treeline at 4,200 metres 9,000 years ago, at 4,050 during a cooler interval 4,000 years ago, and at 4,350 today.

Rate of change is what distinguishes the present. Earlier movements took 400 to 800 years to cover 150 metres; the movement since 1950 has been 300 metres in 70 years. The paper attributes the difference to the rate of warming rather than to its magnitude.

Mechanism includes a lag that the record reveals. The treeline trails the temperature optimum by 60 to 120 years, because a tree must establish, survive and reproduce before the line moves. That lag is why a warming trend produces a delayed response and why the current movement is still accelerating.

The core also records fire. Charcoal layers appear at nine horizons, and the two largest coincide with the periods of fastest treeline retreat, which supports the argument that grazing and burning, not only temperature, shaped the boundary in the past.

Limits are the usual ones for a single site. A treeline is a local phenomenon, and a lake in one valley records its own slope's history. The team compares its record with two others from the same range and finds the timing consistent within a century.

Human influence complicates the recent record. The last 2,000 years show a rise in pollen from plants that grazing favours, and the paper separates the climate signal from the land-use signal with a paired lake 40 kilometres away that has no grazing in its catchment.

The core is archived and available. The team published the raw counts and the age model, which lets other groups test the treeline reconstruction with their own transfer function. That openness is increasingly the norm in palaeoclimate.

Sediment focusing is the dating problem. A lake's sediment can slump and mix, and the team checked for it with three independent age markers. Two cores agreed within 200 years across 8,000 years of record, which is a tight chronology for a terrestrial archive.

For a reader, the importance is in the baseline. A 12,000-year record tells you what a natural rate of change looks like, and the current rate is three to five times faster. That comparison is the substance of the paper, and it depends on the dating precision that the laminations provide.
