---

The population stood at 1,408.28 million at the end of 2024, a fall of 1.39 million from a year earlier, and the decline was smaller than the 2.08 million recorded in 2023. Births rose to 9.54 million from 9.02 million, the first increase since 2016, and deaths fell slightly to 10.93 million. The natural growth rate was -0.99 per thousand, and the urban share of the population reached 67 per cent.

A single year of rising births is a weak signal, and its composition matters more than its size. Births fell for seven consecutive years after 2016, and the 2024 rebound coincided with a Year of the Dragon, which Chinese families have long treated as auspicious. Demographers expect a fallback in 2025, and the question is whether the level settles above or below the 2023 trough rather than whether the rebound continues. A dragon year shifts a decision by months rather than by years, and the births that arrive in 2025 were conceived in the second half of 2024.

Ageing is the trend that the annual numbers describe. The population over sixty reached 310 million, about 22 per cent of the total, and the cohort now entering retirement is larger than the cohort entering the labour market by a wide margin. That gap is what makes pension financing and health spending a structural budget problem rather than a cyclical one. Life expectancy reached 79 years, which is a success that raises the cost of the transfers the same government has to make.

Migration inside the country is the other variable. Urban population rose by more than 150 million since 2010 even as the total stagnated, and the counties that lose residents are the ones with the oldest age profiles and the thinnest health services. A village with a school that closes is a village that will not attract young families back. Hukou reform has made it easier to move, and the schools and hospitals in the receiving cities have not grown as fast.

Policy has moved from exhortation to payment. Several provinces now offer birth bonuses, some cities pay a housing subsidy tied to a second or third child, and the national government announced a childcare payment in the following summer. None of those transfers is large enough to cover the cost of raising a child, and each of them is aimed at the timing of a decision rather than at its feasibility. Cash transfers of a few thousand yuan change the timing of a birth and not the number a family plans.

The arithmetic that demographers return to is the cohort size. A country that records about nine and a half million births a year, with a life expectancy near seventy-nine years, converges on a population of roughly 750 million within a century if nothing changes. Nothing in the 2024 release changes that trajectory, and the rise in births is a fluctuation on a slope. A population that shrinks slowly and ages quickly is a country that has to raise productivity faster than it did in the previous two decades.
