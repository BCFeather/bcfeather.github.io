description: "Russia said a blaze set off its own ammunition and that the cruiser went down while being towed, Ukraine said two cruise missiles had hit it, and either account leaves the Black Sea Fleet without its command ship."
---

The Russian cruiser Moskva, flagship of the Black Sea Fleet, sank on 14 April after a fire and explosions in its ammunition storage, according to the Russian defence ministry, which said the ship lost stability while being towed towards Sevastopol in rough weather and that the crew had been evacuated. The ministry first reported the fire as contained, then acknowledged the loss within a few hours.

Ukraine's southern command gave a different account. Two Neptune anti-ship cruise missiles, a domestic design, struck the vessel while it was off the coast, according to officials in Odesa, and the damage was severe enough that the ship could not be saved. Western officials, speaking on condition of anonymity, said the missile account was credible, and open-source tracking of the ship's last transmitted position supported it.

Either explanation carries the same result for the fleet. The Moskva was built in 1979 as the Slava, refitted in the 1990s, and carried the air defences that were meant to shield Russian operations along the western coast. Its loss removed the command platform for the amphibious force that had been threatening Odesa, and it pushed Russian surface ships further from the Ukrainian coast, which is a change in the balance of the war at sea. Naval analysts put the replacement cost in the hundreds of millions, and no hull of that class remained in the fleet.

The ship had already become a symbol before it sank. It was the vessel that answered a Ukrainian demand for surrender at Snake Island with a message that the defenders should go and get themselves killed, a recording that circulated widely and was turned into a postage stamp. The same ship had seized Ukrainian vessels in 2018, and its reputation travelled further than its guns. The phrase became a slogan on posters and stamps, and the garrison's defiance became the most quoted line of the war.

Militarily, the sinking changed what the fleet could do rather than what it had done. Amphibious landings near Odesa, which had been the main reason for the ships to sit off the coast, became too risky to attempt, and the grain corridor negotiations that ran through the summer depended on Russian ships staying out of the way. Naval analysts also noted that a cruiser designed to survive a saturation attack had failed against two missiles, which says something about the maintenance of a fleet that had not fought a war since 1945. Damage control on a ship of that age is a question of crew training as much as of armour, and the fire spread through a magazine that should have been flooded in time.

A flagship is a political object as much as a warship, and this one went down in a war that Russia had described as a limited operation. The ministry's version, in which an accident sank its own ship, was the best available answer to a question the Kremlin could not afford to discuss in public. Within a week the ministry had stopped publishing the positions of its ships in the western Black Sea.
