When one of Jupiter's moons passes in front of another, the timing of the dimming carries their positions. Brazilian observers recorded nine such events during 2021, eight among the Galilean satellites and one involving the inner moon Thebe, and the reduction of those light curves is reported in a preprint posted on 1 October 2023. The campaign was timed for a season in which the orbits line up as seen from Earth, a geometry that returns only every few years and then lasts a few months.

Astrometry of this kind is how ephemerides improve. Mutual phenomena offer an unusual geometry, since occulting and occulted bodies are both followed against a dark sky, and the mid-time of an event constrains their relative position far more tightly than a single image can. The gain shows up in the orbit fit, where a small positional error becomes a large one after many revolutions. Each event also yields two coordinates instead of the one a single image provides, and the reduction runs independently of the international campaign, which gives a cross-check on both.

Average uncertainty came to 5 milliarcseconds, about 15 kilometres at Jupiter's distance, for the Galilean satellites, and 32 milliarcseconds, or 96 kilometres, for Thebe. The observations, nine of them independent, add 17 per cent to the event count of the international PHEMU21 campaign and 10 per cent to its light curves. The Thebe eclipse is only the second such measurement published, which makes it a new data point for a moon whose orbit is poorly constrained.

Precision of that order comes from fitting a geometric model rather than a photometric one. Each event is reproduced with the DE440 and JUP365 ephemerides, the light curve is simulated, and the match is found by Monte Carlo with a chi-squared criterion. The full Oren-Nayar reflectance model is used throughout, so the scattering properties of the moons are parameters to be fitted and not assumptions built into the geometry. A wrong reflectance law would leave a systematic residual in the light curve.

Nine events from one campaign, reduced against one ephemeris, cannot settle orbital dynamics by themselves; the value lies in accumulation. The fit also leans on the reflectance model, and the Thebe result rests on a single eclipse, which makes its 96-kilometre figure a first estimate, not a settled constant. Campaigns at other nodes of the orbit are what would reduce the model dependence, and a single season improves the orbit without certifying it.

Feeding these positions into orbit solutions is the immediate step, and the campaign leaves a template behind: a modest telescope, a good clock, and a model that treats the light curve as data and not as a picture. Repeating the exercise in later oppositions is what converts a catalogue of timings into a better ephemeris, and the published light curves are what make that repetition comparable. The next season will test whether a small campaign can hold the standard.
