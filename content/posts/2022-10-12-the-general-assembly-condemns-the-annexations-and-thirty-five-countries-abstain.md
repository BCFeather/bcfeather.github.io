The General Assembly adopted a resolution on Wednesday that condemns the Russian annexations in Ukraine and demands that Moscow reverse them immediately and without conditions. The vote was a hundred and forty-three in favour, five against and thirty-five abstentions. The five were Belarus, North Korea, Nicaragua, Russia and Syria. The result was read in the chamber as a measure of how isolated the government in Moscow has become, and as a measure of how many countries would rather not choose.

A veto in the Security Council had blocked a similar text the previous week, and the vote was then moved to the chamber where no veto exists. The text affirms the territorial integrity of Ukraine within its internationally recognized borders and calls the referendums held in the occupied areas invalid. It also asks all states to refuse to accept any change in the status of the four regions. The document runs to four pages, and its operative paragraphs ask for the withdrawal of Russian troops from the whole of the territory the assembly has defined as Ukrainian.

Abstentions are the interesting part of the count. India, Pakistan, South Africa, China, Turkey and most of the states of Central Asia declined to take sides, and their diplomats said afterwards that they wanted to keep channels open. Several of them also buy Russian weapons and Russian energy, and a vote against Moscow would have been expensive at home. The abstention list is longer than it was for the resolution in March, which suggests the war has become harder to condemn as it has gone on, and that the non-aligned position has become more valuable to the countries that hold it.

Thirty-five is a small number in a chamber of a hundred and ninety-three, and the countries that abstained represent a large share of the world's population. The Brazilian delegation said it had abstained because the text said nothing about a negotiated settlement. The Indian delegation said the resolution would improve nothing on the ground.

Speakers from the countries that voted yes argued that the annexation was a test of the charter rather than a quarrel between two neighbours. The American ambassador said the vote showed that a majority would defend the rule that borders are changed by agreement and not by force. The Ukrainian ambassador said the count mattered less than what the capitals do with it, and he asked for air defences rather than statements.

What the resolution can do is set a legal baseline that other institutions can use. Sanctions, reparation claims and the refusal of visas all rest on the argument that the annexations were void, and the vote gives that argument a number. Lawyers in several capitals had asked for exactly this text, and they will cite it for years in cases that have nothing to do with this war. What it cannot do is move a single Russian brigade, which is the reason the Ukrainian government describes the count as important and insufficient at the same time.
