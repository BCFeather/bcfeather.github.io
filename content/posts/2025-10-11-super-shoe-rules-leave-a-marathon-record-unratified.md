A marathon record is a claim about a person and a claim about a shoe, and the governing body's ratification process has to separate the two. A record set in April has been pending for eleven months because the winner's shoe used a foam that the manufacturer had not submitted for approval, and the rule that governs it was written before the foam existed.

Three numbers make up the regulation. The sole's stack height may not exceed 40 millimetres, it may contain at most one plate, and the shoe must be available for sale to any athlete within four months of the record's date. The third condition is the one in dispute. According to the manufacturer, the shoe was a production model and the foam a variation of an approved material. The governing body's technical committee says a variation is a new material and needs its own approval, which takes 90 days and requires a sample from a retail pair.

Athletes' interests cut both ways. A record that stands is a career's peak and a marketing asset for a sponsor; a record that is annulled after eleven months is a public humiliation. Four current record holders have written to the committee asking for a faster procedure. Swimming's rulebook offers the closest comparison. Swimming's governing body publishes an approved list of suits and tests samples at events, and a suit not on the list is disqualified on the day rather than a year later. That approach front-loads the decision and removes the retrospective risk.

Cost is the reason athletics resists it. A testing program for shoes at every major marathon would cost 300,000 dollars a year, and the sport's governing body has a budget of 90 million for everything. A pre-approval list with spot checks is the cheaper model, and it is what the committee has proposed.

Enforcement is retrospective because nobody inspects a shoe before a race. A record is submitted with the model, the batch code and the stack height, and a technical delegate compares the sample against the approved list. A shoe that was approved in one colourway and modified in another is the case that produced the dispute.

Amateur runners sit outside the dispute and inside its consequences. A race that certifies a course for records must also apply the shoe rule to its elite field, and a mass race's rules for the rest of the field are a local decision that no federation enforces. A record's value is mostly commercial and its certification is mostly administrative, and the two have drifted apart. A shoe company's launch calendar rewards a record within a season, and a federation's technical process takes a season to complete. A record set in October is adjudicated in March.

Innovation outpaces rule-making, and the gap is where records become contested rather than celebrated. A manufacturer can produce a new foam in 18 months, a regulator needs two years to write a rule and another to test for it, and the gap is where records become contested rather than celebrated.
