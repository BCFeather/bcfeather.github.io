Bitcoin had spent three months below its January record, and it cleared the level in two days. The price passed 110,000 dollars for the first time on 21 May and reached an intraday record of about 111,878 on 22 May before falling back below 110,000, in a week when American stocks were falling. That divergence is the part of the move worth explaining.

The immediate cause was legislative. The Senate had voted on 19 May to advance the Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act, known as the GENIUS Act, which would require payment stablecoins to hold reserves one for one in short-dated Treasuries or insured deposits, bar issuers from paying yield, and put large issuers under a federal charter. A bill about stablecoins is a bill about the plumbing that connects bank money to blockchains.

Institutional demand supplied the second push. Jamie Dimon, who had called bitcoin a fraud in 2017, told investors that JPMorgan would allow clients to buy it, and the largest corporate holder of the asset, Strategy, disclosed holdings worth more than 50 billion dollars. An asset that a systemic bank will custody for clients is an asset that has moved from the periphery to the product shelf.

Macro supplied the third. The Moody's downgrade a week earlier had put the fiscal position of the issuer of the reserve currency in the same conversation as the fiscal position of a country with a much weaker currency, and gold had set records in the same month. Bitcoin is not gold, and the correlation is a narrative and not a mechanism; the narrative is what moves a price when the buyers are funds.

Leverage did the rest. Open interest in perpetual futures had risen sharply through the week, funding rates turned positive, and the move from 105,000 to 111,878 happened in a session when spot volumes were ordinary. A record set on derivatives is a record that can be reversed by a liquidation cascade, and the intraday fall back below 110,000 showed the mechanism working in both directions. A market that can move six thousand dollars in a day on derivatives can move as far in the other direction when the funding flips.

The regulatory picture is not settled by one procedural vote. A stablecoin law defines what a token backed by a Treasury bill must do, and it says nothing about whether bitcoin is a commodity, a security or a currency for tax purposes. The bill's Senate passage came in June and the House moved it in July, so the price in May was a price on expectations of clarity rather than on clarity itself.

What would settle the level is whether the new buyers are sticky. A treasury department that holds bitcoin, a bank that custodies it and a payments firm that settles in stablecoins are three different kinds of demand, and only the third is a use case. The record of 22 May is a statement about the first two, and the third is the one that would survive a bear market.
