description: "Transcriptomes from more than 25 tissues across four species carry ageing signals conserved enough to estimate chronological age and forecast mortality, a *Nature* paper reports."
---

Ageing clocks began with methylation marks on DNA and moved through protein levels and clinical variables. A paper in *Nature* on 28 May adds the transcriptome, and does it across species rather than within one. That matters because most clocks are calibrated on a single organism and fail when moved, and a comparative clock has to find the part of ageing that is conserved rather than the part that is peculiar to a mouse.

Alexander Tyshkovskiy, Vadim Gladyshev and colleagues analysed more than 11,000 transcriptomic signatures drawn from more than 25 tissue types in mice, rats, macaques and humans. The question was whether ageing leaves a pattern in gene expression that is conserved across mammals, or whether each species and each tissue drifts in its own direction.

Conservation turned out to be substantial. Genes tied to cellular senescence, inflammation and apoptosis were up-regulated with age, while genes for wound healing, cell differentiation and extracellular matrix synthesis were down-regulated. Those are broad programmes rather than single markers, and their consistent direction across species is what allows a single model to read several tissues at once.

The authors built multi-tissue, multi-species clocks from that structure. The clocks estimate chronological age, and they also predict expected mortality, which is the harder test. Validation used statistical checks and existing animal and cellular models of ageing, and the reported accuracy was comparable to second-generation epigenetic clocks. Comparable is the operative word: the transcriptome clock is not better, it is a different instrument with a faster response time.

The practical advantage over epigenetic clocks is that transcriptomes are dynamic. Methylation records an accumulated history; RNA reports what a cell is doing now, which means an intervention can move the readout within weeks rather than years. That makes the clocks plausible instruments for testing whether a candidate anti-ageing treatment is working, and it makes them cheaper to apply in species where no methylation clock has been built.

Limits remain. A clock trained on cross-sectional samples still needs longitudinal data to prove it tracks an individual rather than a population average, and the mortality prediction is a group-level forecast that says little about any one animal. Tissue composition is a further complication, since a biopsy of liver and a biopsy of skin do not carry the same signal, and pooling them into one score hides that. A clock that is accurate on average can still be wrong for the tissue a clinician sampled, and the paper reports tissue-level performance for exactly that reason. The claim worth testing next is whether the same signature responds to caloric restriction and to the drugs already in trials, because a clock that moves with a known intervention is a clock with a validated mechanism. A readout that shifts without the animal living longer is a biomarker of the treatment, not of ageing, and the difference is what separates a clock from a proxy.
