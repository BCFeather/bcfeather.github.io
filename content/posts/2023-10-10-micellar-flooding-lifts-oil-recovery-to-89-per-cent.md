Oil recovery is a competition between the pressure that drives water through a rock and the capillary forces that hold oil in place. Surfactants and nanoparticles are meant to tip that balance, and a model pore network is one of the few settings where the tipping can be measured rather than inferred. Water alone leaves behind the films and pockets that capillary trapping creates, and every enhanced recovery scheme is an argument about how to remove them.

A preprint posted on 3 October 2023 reports a microfluidic porous network, built as a channel with constrictions, flooded with aqueous solutions of Triton X-100 micelles at volume fractions from 0 to 30 per cent. Deionized water supplied the baseline, and the displaced oil was measured directly. The network gives the experiment a geometry that can be described and repeated, and a single channel makes the displaced volume easy to read.

Baseline recovery came to 39 per cent. At a micelle volume fraction of 30 per cent the network gave up 89 per cent of its oil, and the gain between those endpoints ran nearly linearly with concentration. A straight line over that range is a strong claim, since most flooding curves flatten well before the end of the range.

Structural disjoining pressure is the mechanism the paper names. Where micelles crowd at the three-phase contact line, they form a structured film whose osmotic pressure pushes the oil away from the wall, and the effect grows with the volume fraction. The film is thin, local and hard to observe, which is why a transparent network earns its place in the argument. Its thickness is set by the balance between osmotic pressure and the capillary pressure that holds the oil.

Scatter is the second result. At a fixed volume fraction, repeated experiments differed by more than the trend line allows, which points to surface energy heterogeneity: the same fluid meets walls of different wettability, and displacement responds to the local energy rather than to the average. That reading turns an obstacle into evidence, since a uniform surface should have produced a reproducible number, and the variance is the measurement of how far from uniform the walls are.

Rock is what the network cannot supply. A microfluidic channel has smooth walls, a known geometry and no clay, while a reservoir has all three, and the linear trend says nothing about where recovery would plateau. Cores under brine, at reservoir temperature, are the next test, since temperature is a variable a reservoir fixes and a bench experiment does not.

A sharper test of the mechanism would keep the volume fraction fixed and vary the micelle size. Structural disjoining pressure depends on the packing of the particles near the contact line, so a fluid with the same concentration but different particles should displace a different fraction. Nothing in the preprint separates those two variables, and the next experiment could.
