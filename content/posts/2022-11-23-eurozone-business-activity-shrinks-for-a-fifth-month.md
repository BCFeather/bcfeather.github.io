S&P Global published the survey on 23 November, and the composite index was 47.1 for November. The reading was up from 47.1 in October and below the 50 that separates growth from contraction. It was the fifth consecutive month below 50, and the survey has a good record of tracking the official figures. The survey covers 5,000 companies, and it is published three weeks before the official figures.

Manufacturing was 47.3, and services were 48.6. The new orders component was 43.9, the weakest since 2012 apart from the pandemic, and the backlog of work fell for the fifth month. Companies reported that customers were delaying orders because of uncertainty about energy costs. Germany was the weakest of the large economies, at 46.3. Its manufacturing index was 36.7 for the energy-intensive sectors, and the survey said chemical and metal producers had cut output. France was 48.3, and Italy 48.4. The periphery was stronger, and Ireland was above 50. The manufacturing index had fallen for six months, and the services index for four.

Prices were still rising. The output price index was 62, down from 65 in the previous month and the slowest since March 2021. Input prices fell for the first time in two years as energy and shipping costs came down. That combination, of falling input prices and weak demand, is what the central bank wants to see. Its chemical industry used 20 per cent less gas than in 2021, and output fell 12 per cent.

Employment was growing slowly. The index was 51, and firms said they were reluctant to hire because of the outlook. The survey said some companies had used short-time work schemes to keep staff. Unemployment was 6.5 per cent, the lowest on record for the currency area. The survey was published a day before the central bank's December meeting. Economists said the numbers supported a slower pace of increases, and the bank raised by 50 basis points rather than 75. The bank's president said the survey was consistent with a contraction in the fourth quarter. The input price index was 66, and it had been 80 in the summer.

Published in November, the commission's forecast said the economy would shrink 0.5 per cent in 2023. The bank's own forecast was similar, and both were revised upward in the spring as the energy price fell. The winter was warmer than usual, and gas consumption fell 20 per cent. The bank raised by 50 basis points in December, and by 50 again in February.

January brought an upward turn as the gas price fell and as China reopened. It passed 50 in April, and it stayed there through 2023. The recession the survey had predicted was a shallow one, and output fell only in the fourth quarter of 2022 and the first of 2023.
