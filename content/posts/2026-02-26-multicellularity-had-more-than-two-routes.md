The transition to multicellularity has happened at least twenty-five times, which makes it one of the most repeated innovations in the history of life and one of the most studied. Two routes were known. In the first, a single cell divides repeatedly and stays together, producing a body of genetically identical cells. In the second, independent cells aggregate, producing a body of genetically diverse ones. The two were thought to be alternatives that rarely mix.

Brunet and colleagues tested that assumption with a set of small aquatic organisms that sit close to the boundary between single-celled and multicellular life. The experiments follow populations through the transition and characterise the bodies that form, and the paper reports three distinct routes, two of which correspond to the known pathways and one of which combines them. The work appeared in *Nature* on 26 February as an open-access article.

The third route is the interesting one. A body can begin as a clone and then recruit unrelated cells, or begin as an aggregate and then impose a division of labour that makes the parts dependent on one another, and either sequence produces a multicellular organism that is neither purely clonal nor purely aggregative. The categories that biology has used to classify the transition are therefore descriptions of endpoints and not of mechanisms.

Why this matters is that the two pathways imply different evolutionary puzzles. A clonal body has no conflict of interest between its cells and can afford to specialize, and an aggregative body has a standing conflict that selection must suppress. A route that passes through both has to solve the conflict and then build the specialization, and it can do so in stages and not in one step.

An experimental system allows the transition to be watched rather than inferred. Populations of a single-celled organism can be subjected to selection for a multicellular habit, and the resulting bodies can be examined at each stage, which is the kind of evidence that the fossil record cannot supply for events this old. The limitation is that a laboratory selection experiment reproduces the first steps and not the last ones.

Generalising from a few organisms to the whole tree of life is a stretch that the paper does not make. What it shows is that the space of possible transitions is larger than the two-pathway model allows, and that some organisms occupy positions between the categories. That is a claim about biology as it exists and not about what happened two billion years ago. Selection for a multicellular habit is easy to impose in a flask and hard to reproduce for organisms that took a billion years to get there.

Practical consequences concern how multicellularity is taught and modelled. Models that assume a single origin pathway make assumptions about the costs of cheating and the benefits of division of labour, and a third route changes those assumptions. Theoreticians will need to redo some arithmetic, and it is the useful kind of arithmetic to redo.
