---

Muscle-invasive bladder cancer has been treated the same way for a generation: chemotherapy, then surgery, then surveillance, with the cure rate stuck where it was. A trial published in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 24 October adds an immunotherapy-antibody combination to that sequence, and the survival curves separate early. Event-free survival at 24 months, the trial's primary measure, was 63.5 per cent with durvalumab and enfortumab vedotin against 45.1 per cent in the control arm.

The hazard ratio is the number that matters, because it measures separation over the whole follow-up rather than at one landmark. For the two-drug regimen it was 0.51, with a confidence interval from 0.38 to 0.67, and the p value was below 0.001. Overall survival at 24 months was 78.4 per cent against 69.4, a hazard ratio of 0.58 and a p value of 0.003. A disease that had no new standard now has one with an overall survival benefit, which is a different kind of result from a progression-free gain.

Enfortumab vedotin is an antibody-drug conjugate aimed at nectin-4, a protein that bladder cancer cells display in quantity, and durvalumab is a checkpoint inhibitor aimed at PD-L1. Pairing a drug that delivers a cytotoxic payload with one that releases the immune system's brakes is the logic, and the trial was built to see whether the pair beats the older standard as well as whether adding a third agent, tremelimumab, improves on the pair.

It did not. The triplet, durvalumab plus tremelimumab plus enfortumab vedotin, reached 60.1 per cent event-free survival at 24 months, a hazard ratio of 0.61, which is worse than the pair and comes with more toxicity. Grade 3 or higher adverse events occurred in 79.5 per cent of patients on the triplet, against 71.1 per cent on the pair, of which 30.7 per cent were judged treatment-related. More drug bought less benefit, which is the result a well-designed three-arm trial exists to find.

The toxicity figures are the part a patient will weigh. Seventy-one per cent grade 3 or higher events on the winning arm is a lot, even if most are attributable to the underlying treatment sequence rather than to the new agents. Peripheral neuropathy and skin reactions are the known costs of enfortumab vedotin, and a regimen that adds two survival benefits has to be tolerable enough that people finish it, since a survival curve is built from patients who stayed on treatment.

Open questions remain about who benefits most, since the trial reports an intention-to-treat effect and the subgroup analyses will take time to settle. The control arm will also age: if the older standard changes, the comparison has to be repeated. What the paper establishes is narrower and firmer than a new era: adding these two drugs to the treatment of muscle-invasive disease improves survival, and adding a third does not.
