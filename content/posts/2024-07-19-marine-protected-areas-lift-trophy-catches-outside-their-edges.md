Fisheries science has argued for decades about spillover, the idea that a protected area produces fish that can be caught outside its boundary. Small studies have supported it, others have found nothing, and the disagreement has been hard to settle because each study measured a different species, a different gear and a different distance. The argument has been political as well as scientific, since spillover is one of the benefits that justify closures.

A paper in *Science Advances* on 19 July 2024 replaces the case studies with a global test. The authors use recreational angling records, which log trophy-size fish, as a proxy for spillover outside fully protected areas, and compare accumulation of those records near boundaries against matched reference areas. Pooling records across jurisdictions is what gives the analysis its reach, and it is also what limits its precision.

The proxy is well chosen. Recreational anglers report large fish preferentially, the records span many countries and many years, and trophy size is what a spillover effect should change first, since protected populations export the older and larger individuals that anglers value. A metric that responds to the largest individuals is the right one for a question about export of mature fish.

Accumulation accelerates close to the boundary. Records near protected areas pile up faster than in reference waters, and the gradient is steepest within the first few kilometres, which is the distance a fish is likely to move in a season rather than the distance it might travel in a lifetime. Distance decay of that kind fits movement behaviour better than it fits larval dispersal. Anglers also concentrate effort near accessible boundaries, which the design controls for by matching reference sites on the same coast.

The effect grows stronger over time, which distinguishes spillover from a placement artefact. If protection had simply been sited where fish were already abundant, the advantage would be static, and instead it deepens as the protected population builds up. Placement bias would not produce a gradient that steepens over decades.

Limits are clear. Recreational records are presence data rather than abundance estimates, angler effort varies, and reporting rates differ between countries, so the analysis measures relative change rather than absolute biomass. Commercial fleets, which fish differently and report differently, are not covered. Standardized effort across fleets would be needed before the numbers could be read as biomass.

Policy implications run both ways. A protected area can be justified partly by what it exports, which strengthens the case for networks of them rather than isolated reserves. The same result, however, means that a boundary concentrates fishing pressure, and management that ignores the edge may simply move effort to the place where the spillover arrives. Designing boundaries with the expected flow in mind keeps the benefit from becoming a concentration of pressure. Networks that place closures upstream of fishing grounds would spread the benefit instead of concentrating it.
