A galaxy's dust grains align with its magnetic field, and the alignment polarizes the dust's thermal emission. The polarization is a probe of a field that is otherwise invisible.

The detection in *Nature*, 21 September 2023 is at redshift 2.6, when the universe was a quarter of its present age. The authors measure the polarization of a galaxy's far-infrared emission and infer the field's geometry.

The distance is the achievement. A polarized signal from a nearby galaxy is faint, and one from a distant galaxy is fainter still, and the measurement requires a sensitive far-infrared polarimeter.

The geometry is the result. The polarization's angle traces the field's direction on the sky, and the pattern is consistent with a field ordered on the galaxy's scale.

The dust's alignment is the mechanism. A grain that is elongated and exposed to radiation aligns with its axis along the field, and the emission is polarized perpendicular to the grain's long axis.

The implication is that fields existed early. A galaxy at that redshift with an ordered field is one whose field grew quickly, and the growth's origin is a question for the models.

The limitation is the signal's weakness. A single detection is not a survey, and the systematic errors in polarimetry are large.

What the paper does not show is the field's strength. A polarization angle gives a direction, and the strength requires an assumption about the grain's alignment efficiency.

The next step is a sample. A set of galaxies across redshift would trace the field's growth, and the observing time is the constraint.

For astronomy, the value is a new observable. The cosmic magnetic field's history has been inferred from Faraday rotation in nearby objects, and a dust-polarization probe extends the reach.

The far-infrared band is the window. A dust grain at tens of kelvin emits at hundreds of micrometres, and the atmosphere is opaque there, which is why the measurement needs a space telescope.

The grain's alignment is an active field of study. Radiative torques align a grain on a timescale that depends on the radiation field, and the efficiency is the unknown in the strength's inference.

The instrument's sensitivity is the enabler. A polarimeter measures a difference of two intensities, and the difference is a small fraction of the total.

The foreground is the contaminant. A galactic dust cloud along the line of sight polarizes light, and the authors' modelling separates the two contributions.

The field's origin is the theory. A seed field amplified by turbulence is one scenario, and a field generated by a battery is another, and a high-redshift detection constrains both.

The sample's future is a survey. A telescope with a wide field could measure many galaxies, and the map of polarization would be a map of fields.
The galaxy's star formation is the context. A starburst produces dust and a radiation field, and the two together are what align the grains and polarize the emission.
The calibration is the assumption. A polarization fraction of a few per cent is typical, and the authors' comparison with nearby galaxies is what anchors the interpretation.
