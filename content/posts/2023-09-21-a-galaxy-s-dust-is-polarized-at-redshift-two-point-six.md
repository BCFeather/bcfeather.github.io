A galaxy's dust grains align with its magnetic field, and that alignment polarizes the dust's thermal emission, so the polarization becomes a probe of a field otherwise invisible. Measured in *Nature* on 21 September 2023 at redshift 2.6, when the universe was a quarter of its present age, the polarization of a galaxy's far-infrared emission is used to infer the field's geometry.

Distance carries the achievement. A polarized signal from a nearby galaxy is faint, one from a distant galaxy is fainter, and detecting either needs a sensitive far-infrared polarimeter; the band is the window, because dust at tens of kelvins emits at hundreds of micrometres where the atmosphere is opaque. Geometry is the result. The polarization angle traces the field's direction on the sky, and the pattern is consistent with an ordered field on the galaxy's scale.

Grain alignment supplies the mechanism. An elongated grain exposed to radiation aligns its axis with the field, and the emission is polarized perpendicular to the grain's long axis; radiative torques drive that alignment on a timescale set by the radiation field, and the efficiency is the unknown in any estimate of strength.

Early fields are the implication, since a galaxy at that redshift with an ordered field had one growing quickly, and the growth's origin is a question for models. A field's history has been inferred from Faraday rotation in nearby objects, and dust polarization extends the reach. Weakness of the signal sets the limitation, because a single detection is not a survey and systematic errors in polarimetry are large. Strength stays unmeasured, since an angle gives a direction and the amplitude needs an assumption about alignment efficiency.

Sampling is the next step, with galaxies across redshift tracing the field's growth, and observing time is the binding constraint. Astronomy gains a new observable, and the instrument's sensitivity is the enabler, because a polarimeter measures a difference of two intensities that is a small fraction of the total.

A dozen galaxies at similar redshift would show whether the ordered field is typical or exceptional, and a null result in most of them would change the interpretation of this one. A measurement of the same galaxy's polarization at a second wavelength would also test whether the signal comes from dust rather than from a synchrotron background, because the two emit with different spectra and different polarization fractions.

Statistics from such a sample would let the field's growth be traced against the galaxy's star formation, and that comparison is where the physics of dynamo amplification would become measurable. A theoretical model of the dynamo that could reach this coherence by redshift 2.6 would be the interpretive target, and the measured angle is the constraint such a model would have to satisfy. A second instrument with an independent calibration would guard against a systematic that mimics a polarization angle, and that cross-check is what a survey would need before its numbers are trusted.
