An election this month returned a parliament in which the largest party holds 118 of 300 seats, and no pairing reaches 151 without one of the two smallest groups. Formation talks have begun with four plausible coalitions, and arithmetic rather than personality will decide which one forms.

The electoral system shapes the options. Proportional representation with a 4 per cent threshold fragments a parliament when the vote is evenly spread, and this election had five parties above 8 per cent for the first time. A majoritarian system would have produced a single-party majority on the same vote shares.

Coalition theory offers predictions that arithmetic constrains. The minimal winning coalition is a three-party combination with 158 seats; the option with fewest parties is a four-party combination at 172. The second is more durable and more expensive in policy concessions.

Policy distance is what the seat count hides. Two plausible partners disagree on the budget deficit by 1.4 per cent of GDP, the largest single gap in the manifestos. A coalition bridging that gap needs a fiscal framework with an escape clause, which is what the previous government wrote into its own agreement.

Formation rules matter more than they appear. The constitution gives the head of state 30 days to name a formateur and sets no formal deadline, and the practice in the last three elections has been to let the largest party try first. That is a convention rather than a rule, and a smaller party has already argued that the mandate should go to the combination holding the most seats.

Minority government is the fallback the arithmetic permits. A party with 118 seats can govern with confidence-and-supply agreements from two others, avoiding a formal coalition and its cabinet divisions. Two of the last four governments in this system were of that kind, and both lasted under two years.

For a reader outside the country, the case isolates institutions from personalities. The same votes under a different electoral rule would have produced a majority; under this rule they produced four options, and the choice among them turns on procedural conventions that are themselves contested.

The cabinet allocation is the day-to-day constraint. A four-party coalition of 172 seats would hold 14 ministries, and the arithmetic of proportional allocation gives each partner a share that does not match its weight in the agreement. Two of the smaller parties have said they would accept a policy concession in exchange for one fewer ministry.

The opposition's leverage is procedural. A budget that requires only a plurality can pass without the coalition's full majority, and a constitutional amendment requires two thirds. Knowing which decisions need which threshold is how a minority government survives.

The next milestone is the budget, due in eight weeks. Any government will need to pass it through the same fragmented chamber, and the parties outside the coalition will have their first chance to price their support. That vote, rather than the formation talks, is where durability will be tested.
