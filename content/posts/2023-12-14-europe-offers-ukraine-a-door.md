The European Council decided on the evening of 14 December to open accession negotiations with Ukraine and Moldova, and to grant Georgia the status of candidate country, in a summit that produced one of the fastest enlargements of the process in its history. The decision was taken without a vote against it because the Hungarian prime minister left the room, a manoeuvre that the presidency described as a pre-agreed absence and that Budapest described as a decision taken in its absence. Bosnia was offered negotiations once it meets the conditions, which the commission will assess in March.

Formally the step is small. Accession negotiations are a technical process of 35 chapters, each requiring legislation, institutions and money, and the commission's own assessments put Ukraine at the beginning of that road. Informally the step is large, because it converts a war into a European question and gives the government in Kyiv a political horizon that the military situation has not provided since the counter-offensive stalled in the autumn. The commission's report in November had already recommended the step, attaching conditions on corruption, on minority languages and on the independence of the judiciary.

Money was the part that failed. Hungary vetoed the revision of the seven-year budget that contained 50 billion euros for Ukraine over four years, and the summit ended with a promise to try again in January. Without the money, Ukraine's finance ministry has said it will have to delay salaries and pensions in the first quarter, and the American Congress had by then passed no new aid either, which means that the two largest donors were both stuck in domestic politics on the same day.

Russia's response was to describe the decision as a threat and to repeat that the objectives of the war remain unchanged. The Kremlin has spent a decade arguing that the association agreement of 2014 and the later trade provisions were the origin of the conflict, and the opening of accession talks gives that argument a formal target. In the occupied territories the passportization programme continued through the winter, and the Ukrainian government's own estimate of the children moved to Russia passed 19,000.

For the European Union the decision is about credibility as much as about Ukraine. The union has promised membership to six western Balkan states for twenty years and delivered to none, and its refusal to move on enlargement has been the standing evidence in every argument about whether the promise means anything. Opening talks with a country at war, and with a country of 2.5 million people whose GDP per head is a third of the union's average, is a statement that the promise carries weight beyond the communique.

The timetable remains unknowable. Negotiations with the fastest previous entrants took five years and with the slowest took fifteen, and Ukraine will be negotiating while fighting, with a land border that Russia does not accept and with a reconstruction bill the World Bank put at 411 billion dollars in March. What the summit delivered was a beginning with no schedule, which is what the process has always delivered, and for a government that needed a signal in December it was enough.
