Intel announced on the second of December that Pat Gelsinger had retired from the company and stepped down from the board, effective from the first of December. Announcement came three years into a turnaround that had been presented as the company's return to manufacturing leadership and had instead produced record losses. Board named David Zinsner, the finance chief, and Michelle Johnston Holthaus, who runs the client computing group, as interim co-chief executives while a search for a permanent successor begins.

Career had been entirely Intel's until 2022. Gelsinger joined the company in 1979, became its first chief technology officer, and left in 2009 for a decade at software companies before returning as chief executive in February 2021. Return was welcomed by investors who believed the company had lost its engineering culture, and his early plan was built on that idea: five manufacturing nodes in four years, a foundry business that would build chips for other companies, and a promise that Intel would be making the most advanced processors in the world by 2025.

Money for the plan was never the problem. American government awarded the company up to eight billion dollars in subsidies under the chips act, and the company committed tens of billions to new plants in Arizona, Ohio, Germany and Israel. Problem was execution. Manufacturing yields on the most advanced process were lower than planned, customers were slow to commit to the foundry, and the data centre business lost share to competitors whose processors were made elsewhere. Losses in the foundry division reached billions of dollars a year, and the company suspended its dividend in August.

Autumn brought the crisis forward. Shares fell by more than a quarter in a single day in August after results that missed expectations, and the company announced a plan to cut fifteen per cent of its workforce, about fifteen thousand jobs, and to postpone some of the German and Polish plants. Reports in the weeks before the announcement said the board had hired advisers to consider options, including a separation of the manufacturing arm, and that the search for a replacement had begun before the retirement was announced.

Reaction was mixed and the market's first move was to mark the shares up. Analysts who had argued that the turnaround needed a different leader took the change as overdue, and analysts who had backed the strategy argued that it had been interrupted before it could work. Michael Dell and other investors had publicly urged a separation of the foundry, and the board's statement said the company would continue to pursue the strategy, which leaves that question open.

Succession now becomes the central issue for the company and for the policy that has supported it. American government has treated Intel as the only domestic manufacturer capable of producing leading-edge logic, and the subsidies were written with that assumption. Interim leadership by two executives who were inside the failed plan is a holding measure, and the search that follows will have to decide whether the company is run as a chipmaker that also manufactures or as a manufacturer that also designs. Answer will determine what happens to the plants, the subsidies and the jobs.
