title: "Three billion francs and a wiped out class of bonds"
date: "2023-03-19T23:50:00+08:00"
categories: [business]
description: "UBS agreed on 19 March 2023 to buy Credit Suisse for three billion Swiss francs, with the regulator ordering that sixteen billion francs of the bank's additional tier one bonds be written down to zero."
---

The takeover was arranged by the government, the central bank and the regulator over four days, and it was announced on a Sunday evening. UBS would pay three billion francs in its own shares, at a ratio of one share for every 22.48 of the target, and the government would guarantee losses above a first slice that UBS agreed to absorb. The price was about a tenth of what the bank had been worth a year earlier, and it was negotiated by the state rather than by the two boards. The merger was drafted in a hotel near Bern over a weekend, and the two boards were told on Sunday morning that the terms had been settled by the authorities.

For weeks the bank had been losing deposits. Its clients had withdrawn billions after the collapse of an American technology lender, and its own annual report had disclosed weaknesses in its financial reporting that the regulator had raised. The central bank had lent it fifty billion francs in the week before the takeover, and the loan had slowed the outflow without stopping it.

Treatment of the additional tier one bonds was the clause that caused the argument. Those instruments are designed to absorb losses when a bank's capital falls below a threshold, and they rank above equity and below senior debt in the normal hierarchy. The regulator, acting under an emergency decree, ordered sixteen billion francs of them written to zero while the shareholders received three billion, which inverted the order that investors had been promised.

Reaction in the bond market was immediate and European-wide. The value of similar instruments issued by other banks fell, and the cost of insuring bank debt rose, and the regulators in other countries issued statements insisting that their own rules would respect the hierarchy. The European Banking Authority clarified that equity would be written down first in a normal resolution, and the clarification was necessary because the Swiss case had made the rule look optional. Insurance against bank defaults rose across the continent, and supervisors in Frankfurt spent the following week explaining that their own rules preserved the hierarchy.

Exposure taken by the government was large and was explained as necessary. It guaranteed up to nine billion francs of losses beyond the five billion that UBS would absorb, and it provided a liquidity backstop of up to a hundred billion. The finance minister said the alternative was a disorderly failure of a bank that was too large and too entangled to be wound down in a weekend, and the parliament had no chance to be consulted before the announcement. Two of the bank's largest shareholders had opposed the deal in public and had no vote at the meeting that approved it.

The deal completed on 12 June, after the competition authorities in several countries approved it and after the government passed the guarantees through parliament in a special session. The combined bank held a domestic market share that the competition commission described as a problem, and the commission accepted it because the alternative had been a failure. The country's two largest banks became one, and the concentration was the price of the rescue.
