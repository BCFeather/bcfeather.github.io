Osaka opened a world exposition on 13 April on Yumeshima, an island built from reclaimed land in the bay. More than a hundred and fifty countries are participating, the theme is a future society designed around technology, and the site is dominated by a timber ring designed by a Japanese architect. The event runs for six months, and the budget has roughly doubled since the bid. A site that the city built for the event is a site that the city has to maintain for whatever comes next.

World fairs are a nineteenth-century format in a twenty-first-century economy. The Crystal Palace was a showcase for industrial products that most visitors had never seen, and the format worked because the world was large and information travelled slowly. A visitor in 2025 has seen the products on a screen, and a pavilion has to offer an experience rather than a display. A screen has replaced the catalogue, and a pavilion has to compete with everything else on the same screen.

The architecture is the part that still works. A national pavilion is a statement about a country's self-image, and the pavilions that get attention are the ones with a strong idea. A ring of timber that frames the site is a structure that people will remember, and the national exhibits inside are the part that critics find uneven. A pavilion that is a walk-through installation is one that people queue for, and a pavilion of vitrines is one they skip.

The cost overrun is a familiar story. A large event with a fixed opening date has no ability to slip, and a contractor that knows the date can price accordingly. The organisers have published a cost that is roughly twice the estimate made when the bid was submitted, and the local government is carrying a share. A date that cannot move is a contract that a contractor prices with a risk premium, and the premium is the overrun.

Legacy is the question that every host answers badly. A site on a reclaimed island with a hundred pavilions has to be either dismantled or repurposed, and the repurposing plan is written before the event and rarely followed. The ring is designed to remain, and the halls around it are designed to be removed. A plan to remove the halls assumes a tenant will want the land, and the island has no tenant yet.

What a world fair can still do is convene. A hundred and fifty countries sending delegations to one site is a diplomatic event as much as a cultural one, and the bilateral meetings that happen in the margins are the reason governments pay. The exhibits are the public face, and the meetings are the product. A delegation that meets twenty counterparts in a week justifies the trip on the meetings rather than on the exhibits. A fair that is remembered for its architecture has done the job that the format can still do.
