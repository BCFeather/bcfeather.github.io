Factor XI inhibitors were the most anticipated class in antithrombotic medicine, on the theory that blocking the amplification pathway of coagulation would prevent clots without the bleeding that factor Xa inhibition causes. The theory was supported by observational data from people with factor XI deficiency, who bleed rarely and have fewer strokes, and by phase 2 trials in which the drug looked safe at doses that were pharmacologically active. The interim look was pre-specified, which is what makes stopping early a result rather than an accident.

LIBREXIA ACS tested the theory where the unmet need is largest, in patients shortly after an acute coronary syndrome who are already on dual antiplatelet therapy. The trial randomised patients within seven days of the event to oral milvexian at 25 milligrams twice daily or placebo, and enrolled 14,194 people, 7,094 assigned to the drug and 7,100 to placebo, before a planned interim analysis. The setting is the hardest one for any anticoagulant, because these patients are already bleeding-prone on two antiplatelet drugs.

Futility was declared at an analysis based on 556 adjudicated efficacy endpoints, found no benefit and the trial was terminated for futility. Over a median follow-up of 12.2 months, the composite of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke occurred in 384 patients on milvexian, 5.4 per cent, and 365 on placebo, 5.1 per cent, a hazard ratio of 1.05 with an interval from 0.91 to 1.21 and *P*=0.50.

Safety behaved as the hypothesis predicted. Bleeding Academic Research Consortium type 3c or 5 events, the intracranial and intraocular bleeds that compromise vision or kill, occurred in 23 patients on the drug and 22 on placebo, with *P*=0.88. A class that does not cause serious bleeding has fulfilled half of its promise, and the half it failed is the one that pays for the drug. Rates that close differ by a single event in a trial of this size, so the safety conclusion rests on an absence that the confidence intervals support.

Dose selection is is the question the trial cannot answer. Twenty-five milligrams twice daily was selected from phase 2 data on the basis of safety, and a higher dose might have produced efficacy at the cost of more bleeding. Trials that stop for futility rarely have the information to distinguish a wrong dose from a wrong hypothesis, and this one does not. A negative trial in the hardest setting is expensive information, and it is cheaper than a decade of phase 2 optimism.

Surrogate enthusiasm is the wider lesson is about surrogate enthusiasm. A mechanism that is elegant in coagulation cascade diagrams and safe in a 1,000-patient phase 2 trial can still fail in 14,000 patients with hard endpoints, and the factor XI class now has one large negative result in the setting where it was most needed. Other trials in stroke prevention and atrial fibrillation are still running, and they will decide whether the class has a future or a cautionary tale.
