---

A tissue is a community of cell types, and a dissociation destroys the community. Sequencing a dissociated tissue tells an experimenter what cells are present and not where they sit or whom they touch.

The atlas in *Nature*, 13 July 2023 keeps the tissue intact. The authors combine CODEX, an imaging method that labels many proteins at once, with single-cell sequencing, and register the two datasets into one map of the human intestine.

The registration is the technical feat. A section's image has coordinates and a cell's sequencing has an identity, and matching them requires a common frame that both modalities can be projected onto.

The result is a census with addresses. Each cell type has a position, and the positions reveal gradients that a dissociated sample would average away.

The tissue's organization is the biological finding. The intestine's epithelium, its immune population and its vasculature occupy distinct zones, and the boundaries between them are sharper than a bulk measurement suggests.

The variation across donors is the caveat. A map from a few individuals describes those individuals, and the inter-individual differences are where disease phenotypes live.

The scale is the advance. A method that labels dozens of proteins on a section can distinguish cell states that a single marker cannot, and the distinction is what makes the map useful.

The application is pathology. A biopsy compared with a reference map becomes a set of deviations, and a deviation with a position is a lesion rather than a mixture.

The limitation is the antibody panel. The proteins that the authors can label are the ones with good antibodies, and a cell type defined by an unlabelled protein is invisible.

What the paper does not show is a dynamics. A map is a snapshot, and a tissue that is turning over needs a time series to show the turnover.

For histology, the value is a fusion of two traditions. Microscopy has the position and sequencing has the identity, and an atlas that has both is what a modern anatomy reference looks like.

The reference frame is the practical asset. A coordinate system that others can register their sections into turns a single study into a shared resource, and the authors' release includes the registration parameters.

The donor's age is a variable the map carries. A child's intestine and an adult's differ in the composition of the immune population, and an atlas that spans ages is one that can ask when a cell type appears.

The comparison with the kidney map is the natural one. Both organs are tubes and both have an epithelium that renews, and a comparison of their zonation is a comparison of two solutions to the same problem.

The computational burden is the barrier to entry. A CODEX dataset is large, and a laboratory that wants to use the map needs the pipeline as well as the data.

The clinical translation is the long route. A map is not a diagnostic, and a test that compares a biopsy to a reference needs a threshold that the atlas does not define.
