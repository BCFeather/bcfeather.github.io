A tissue is a community of cell types, and dissociation destroys the community. Sequencing a dissociated tissue tells an experimenter what cells are present, not where they sit or whom they touch. In *Nature* on 13 July 2023, the tissue stays intact. The authors combine CODEX, an imaging method labelling many proteins at once, with single-cell sequencing, and register the two datasets into one map of the human intestine.

Registration is the technical feat. A section's image has coordinates and a cell's sequencing has an identity, and matching them needs a common frame both modalities can be projected onto. A coordinate system others can register their sections into turns a single study into a shared resource, and the release includes the registration parameters.

Census with addresses is the result. Each cell type has a position, and those positions reveal gradients a dissociated sample would average away. Organisation is the biological finding, since epithelium, immune population and vasculature occupy distinct zones, and the boundaries between them are sharper than a bulk measurement suggests.

Scale is the advance. Labelling dozens of proteins on a section distinguishes cell states a single marker cannot, and that distinction is what makes the map useful. Antibody panel sets the limitation, because only proteins with good antibodies can be labelled, and a cell type defined by an unlabelled one is invisible.

Donor variation is the caveat. A map from a few individuals describes those individuals, and inter-individual differences are where disease phenotypes live. Age is a variable the map carries, since a child's intestine differs from an adult's in immune composition, and an atlas spanning ages can ask when a cell type appears.

Dynamics is what the paper does not show, because a map is a snapshot and a turning-over tissue needs a time series. Pathology gains the application, since a biopsy compared with a reference becomes a set of deviations, and a deviation with a position is a lesion rather than a mixture.

Histology gains a fusion of two traditions, because microscopy has position and sequencing has identity, and an atlas with both is what a modern anatomy reference looks like. Translation remains a long route, since a map is not a diagnostic, and a test comparing a biopsy to a reference needs a threshold this atlas does not define. Computational burden is the barrier to entry, because a CODEX dataset is large, and a laboratory using the map needs the pipeline as well as the data. A longitudinal atlas would be the next resource, because a tissue renews and a static map cannot say which cell becomes which, and the lineage questions that follow belong to a different kind of experiment. A shared coordinate framework also lets two laboratories compare sections they never exchanged, which is the practical difference between an atlas and a figure in a paper, and the field's adoption will be the test of whether the registration generalizes.
