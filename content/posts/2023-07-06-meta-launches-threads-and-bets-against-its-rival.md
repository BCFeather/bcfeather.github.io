Zuckerberg had not posted on Twitter in eleven years, and his first message there in more than a decade was a link to something else. Meta released Threads on 5 July in a hundred countries, a text-first app with five hundred characters per post, photographs, videos and links, built on the same account system as Instagram and available in Apple's and Google's stores. Within a day it had thirty million users. Within five, by the company's own count, it had a hundred million, which makes it the fastest-growing consumer app anyone has measured.

The design is deliberately familiar. Threads borrowed the layout of its rival along with the vocabulary of reposts and replies, and it imported the follower graph that Instagram users had spent a decade building. That single decision is the commercial logic of the product: asking people to start a new social network is hard, and asking them to bring the one they already have is easier. Meta also built the app without direct messages, without a chronological feed option and without the community features that made Twitter useful to journalists and organizers, and it has said all three may come later.

Twitter's response was a mixture of derision and alarm. Musk called the app an imitation, noted that he had once offered to buy Instagram, and threatened legal action over trade secrets and the movement of former employees. His own platform was already in trouble: advertising revenue had fallen sharply since his takeover, and in early July he imposed limits on how many posts users could read per day, a measure he presented as a response to data scraping and which users experienced as an outage. The comparison between an app restricting reading and an app inviting sign-ups wrote itself.

Europe is the gap in the plan. Threads did not launch in the European Union, where Meta faces the Digital Markets Act and a set of competition and privacy rules that the company has decided to study before shipping a new network into the single market. Regulators in Ireland and Brussels have asked whether the app's data combination with Instagram and Facebook complies with the rules, and the answers will shape whether Threads is a global product or an American and British one for now.

Sceptics point to a long list of apps that grew quickly and then stopped growing. Sign-ups are cheap, retention is expensive, and the early numbers measure curiosity more than habit. Meta's moderation machinery, which has spent two decades being criticized from both directions, now has to run a text network in dozens of languages, and the app already carries the kinds of fights that drove people away from Twitter.

Threads began with a promise of a friendly public square and ended its first week as the fastest-growing app in the world. Whether it becomes a place where news is discussed, or a photo-sharing network with a comment box attached, depends on decisions Meta has not yet made, and on whether the people who arrived in the first week find a reason to return in the fifth.
