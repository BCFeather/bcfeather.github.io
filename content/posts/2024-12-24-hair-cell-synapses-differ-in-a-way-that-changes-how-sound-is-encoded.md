A hair cell releases transmitter at several synapses, and the afferents that receive it differ in threshold and in adaptation. A paper in *eLife* on 24 December 2024 connects those differences to the presynaptic machinery. A release site that fires at low levels is a site that converts a quiet signal into a spike, and conversion is what a sensitive channel does. An arrangement that is a design would be expected to appear during development, and a developmental study is the natural follow-up. A synapse that releases readily is a synapse with a short latency, and latency is part of what a sensitive fibre delivers. A short latency is a small advantage that compounds, and compounding is why the ear resolves transients that other senses do not.

Neural diversity expands the encoding capacity of a circuit, and the auditory nerve is an example in which diversity appears as distinct fibre classes. Distinct classes have been described for decades without a presynaptic explanation. Threshold diversity in the auditory nerve is a property the ear needs, since a single fibre cannot cover the range a listener hears. A developmental study would ask whether the matching is built or learned, and the answer would decide how the arrangement is described in a textbook.

The paper measures presynaptic function at individual release sites and compares it with the response properties of the afferent. A site that releases more readily serves a fibre that fires at low sound levels. Parallel channels are an old idea in audition, and the paper supplies the presynaptic half of the explanation. Damage to the ear removes high-frequency sensitivity first, and an account in terms of release sites would predict which channels fail earliest.

Matching is the result, since the variation in release generates the variation in threshold. The diversity is produced at the synapse rather than imposed by the postsynaptic cell. Plasticity and damage both act on the same synapses, so stability is an empirical question rather than a theoretical one.

The arrangement gives the ear parallel channels with different sensitivities, which is what allows a wide dynamic range to be encoded by a narrow set of fibres. Whether the matching is stable over a lifetime is open, because synapses are plastic and the ear is exposed to damage. A bridge between literatures is a service, and a service of this kind is what lets mechanism papers cite physiology.

Stability would decide whether the arrangement is a design or a steady state. Two descriptions that never met are what a field accumulates when its methods differ, and the method here is the meeting point.

On the modelling side, the paper bridges two literatures, and a bridge is what a field needs when its physiology and its anatomy have been described separately. Adaptation matters as much as threshold, since a fibre that adapts slowly serves a different role from one that adapts quickly. A prediction about which channels fail first is a prediction an audiogram can test, and testing is what a model owes a clinic.
