Redox flow batteries store energy in liquids that circulate past electrodes, which makes them easy to scale and hard to diagnose. The concentrations that matter sit inside a porous electrode where neither light nor a camera can reach, and the models that describe them have been fitted rather than measured. A cell's performance depends on how evenly the electrolyte wets the electrode, and on how the concentration of the active species changes from inlet to outlet as the cell discharges.

A paper in *Nature Communications* on 5 September 2024 opens the cell to neutrons. The authors image an operating flow cell radiographically and separate the signals of the active species from those of the supporting electrolyte by subtraction, using the fact that the two absorb neutrons differently. Imaging a working device rather than a model cell is the part that matters, because the geometry that a beamline can accommodate is not the geometry that a commercial stack uses.

Chemical contrast is what the method exploits. Redox-active organic molecules carry a high hydrogen content, and hydrogen attenuates neutrons strongly, while the supporting electrolyte contains boron, which attenuates them in its own way. The geometry is in-plane, which means the beam passes along the cell rather than through its thickness, so that the concentration profile across the electrode is resolved as a function of position rather than averaged into one number. An in-plane view also avoids the path integral that a through-plane measurement produces, where a single pixel integrates over the whole thickness of the electrode.

Profiles were then related to performance. Polarization curves measured on the same cell can be matched to the concentration distribution that produced them, so that a voltage loss can be assigned to a depletion of the active species in a particular region rather than to a generic resistance. That correlation is the step from a picture to a diagnosis, and it is where the method earns its place beside electrochemical impedance and flow visualization.

None of this comes free. Neutron sources are large facilities, the cell has to be built to fit a beamline, and the subtraction requires a reference state in which one species is absent, which limits the chemistries that can be studied.

For flow battery development the value is diagnostic. A design that keeps the active species evenly distributed across the electrode can be tested rather than inferred, and a cell that fails at high current can be examined where the failure happens. A stack designer can also compare two electrode architectures directly, since the same beamline and the same subtraction protocol apply to both. A stack that passes the beamline test would be one that keeps its concentration flat, and flatness is a property a designer can aim at from the beginning.
