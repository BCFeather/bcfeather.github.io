Eleven exchange-traded funds that hold bitcoin directly were approved by the Securities and Exchange Commission on 10 January, ending a decade of refusals. Trading began the next morning, and the funds recorded about 4.6 billion dollars of volume in the first session. The vote was split, and the chair wrote that the approval was the least bad option after a court found that the agency had treated the applications inconsistently.

What the approval changed is the wrapper rather than the coin. A fund that holds the coin must buy it in the market and store it with a custodian, and the shares can be bought in any brokerage account, which is the access that the previous products did not give. A wrapper that fits an ordinary account is a wrapper that reaches ordinary savers. The funds hold the coins with custodians that are audited, and the auditors have said the holdings are verifiable, which answers the oldest objection.

Custody is the risk the regulator kept naming. The order describes the coins as held by institutions that are regulated as fiduciaries, and it warns that the market for the asset remains susceptible to fraud and to manipulation. A product can be approved and its market still distrusted. The exchanges that trade the asset remain outside the supervision that applies to the markets for shares, and the order says so in a footnote rather than in the text.

An existing trust with about 28 billion dollars of assets was converted by the largest applicant, whose fee is four times that of the cheapest new fund. A conversion that keeps the assets is a business that has to decide whether to compete on price. The fee war began before the first trade, and the cheapest funds waived their charges for the first six months to attract the assets. The trust had traded at a discount to the value of its coins for two years, and the conversion removed that gap in a single session.

Demand showed in the flows. The funds took in money faster in their first week than any previous launch, and the price of the coin rose to about 46,000 dollars before giving back part of the gain. A launch that attracts flows is a launch that the asset's holders use to sell. The advisers who allocate for pension funds have said they will not buy the funds, because the mandate does not permit an asset without a cash flow. The funds that took in the most money were the ones with the lowest fees, and the largest applicant lost assets on the first day.

What the decision shows is that the regulator has accepted that the asset exists and has refused to endorse it, and the difference between those two positions is where the next ten years of argument will happen. A decade of refusals that ends in a split vote is a decision taken under duress, and the duress is the part the industry will remember.
