title: "Six point four and a number that stopped falling"
date: "2023-02-14T23:30:00+08:00"

description: "Consumer prices in the United States rose 6.4 per cent in the year to January 2023, and core inflation stood at 5.6 per cent, slower than in December but firmer than the market had expected."
---

Consumer prices in January, published on 14 February, were 6.4 per cent higher than a year earlier, down from 6.5 per cent in December. Core prices, which exclude food and energy, rose 5.6 per cent, down from 5.7 per cent, and the monthly increase was 0.5 per cent on the headline measure and 0.4 per cent on the core. Economists had expected 6.2 per cent and 0.4 per cent respectively, and the difference was enough to move the market.

The composition explained why the fall had slowed. Energy prices were lower than a year earlier, and used car prices had fallen for a seventh month, which was the disinflation that the Fed had pointed to. Shelter, which carries a weight of about a third in the index, rose 0.7 per cent in the month and 7.9 per cent over the year, and it was the largest single contributor, because rents had been climbing since 2021 and the index measures them with a lag. The lag between a signed lease and the rent that the index records is about nine months, which is why the shelter component kept rising after the rental market had cooled.

Services were the other half of the story. Prices for transportation services, medical care and household furnishings all rose faster than the overall index, and the categories that depend on labour were the ones that refused to cool. Wage growth of 4.4 per cent, recorded in the jobs report ten days earlier, was still running above the productivity growth that would make it consistent with two per cent inflation.

Markets adjusted rather than panicked. The two-year yield rose above 4.6 per cent, its highest since November, and futures moved to price a peak near 5.3 per cent. The S&P 500 fell less than a per cent, and the dollar rose, which was a modest reaction by the standards of the previous year, when a tenth of a point had been enough to move equities by two.

Politics around the report followed the usual lines. The White House noted that inflation had fallen for six consecutive months and that real wages had begun to rise, and Republicans noted that prices were still 6.4 per cent higher than a year earlier and that the administration had called the inflation transitory. Both statements were accurate, and neither described what the report actually said, which was that the disinflation was real, slow and concentrated in goods.

The report set the tone for the six weeks that followed. It was the last clean inflation reading before the banking crisis, and it was read at the time as evidence that the Fed would have to raise rates above 5 per cent and hold them there. Two months later the same number was being cited as evidence that the inflation problem had been overstated, which is how a statistic becomes a weapon in an argument it was never designed to settle.
