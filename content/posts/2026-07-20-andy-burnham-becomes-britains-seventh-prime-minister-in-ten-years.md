Andy Burnham became Britain's prime minister on 20 July after receiving the king's commission at Buckingham Palace, hours after his predecessor, Keir Starmer, tendered his resignation. Burnham had been elected leader of the Labour Party at a special conference on 17 July, and he formed a cabinet the same evening. He is the seventh person to hold the office in ten years, a turnover that no other major democracy has matched in the same period. He spoke outside Downing Street that evening and promised to move power out of London.

Career before Downing Street explains the pitch he brought with him. Burnham was born in 1970 in a suburb of Liverpool, read at Cambridge, entered the Commons in 2001 and served as culture secretary and then as health secretary. He left the Commons in 2017 to run for mayor of Greater Manchester, won, and spent nine years arguing that power should sit with English city regions rather than with Whitehall. His first campaign for the leadership failed in 2010 and his second, in 2015, also fell short.

Political situation he inherits is a compound of the last six years. Growth has been weak since the pandemic, public services have not recovered their waiting times, and the fiscal position leaves little room for the spending that the party promised in opposition. His immediate problem is that the promises were written for a different economy. The party's poll ratings had been falling since the spring, and its MPs had begun to talk openly about a change.

Devolution is the idea he has been selling for a decade. As mayor he negotiated a transport budget, a housing fund and a police settlement, and he argued that a city region knows better than a ministry how to spend money on skills. Whether that model works at national scale is the question his government will be judged on, and it will be tested by whether the money is devolved or only the responsibility. He has said he wants a council of mayors to sit alongside the cabinet, with a budget of its own.

Constraints are harder than the rhetoric. Borrowing costs rise when a government loses credibility, and Britain's debt service is already larger than its defence budget. The health service needs capital as well as staff, the armed forces need equipment that factories cannot supply quickly, and the tax rises that would pay for both have been ruled out by his own party. The fiscal watchdog will publish a forecast in the autumn, and it will set the terms of the argument.

Ten years and seven prime ministers is a fact about institutions rather than about individuals. Each change was a response to a political crisis, and each new occupant inherited the same set of unresolved questions about growth, about the union and about Britain's place in Europe. Burnham has begun with the argument that the answers lie in devolution and in cross-party agreement, which is what every predecessor also said in the first week. His first hundred days will be spent on a budget rather than on a bill.
