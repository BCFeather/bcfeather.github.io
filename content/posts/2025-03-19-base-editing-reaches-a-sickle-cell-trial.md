Sickle cell disease is caused by a single substitution in the beta-globin gene, and a therapy that reverses the effect of that substitution without cutting the genome has now been given to patients in a clinical trial. Base editing converts one nucleotide into another chemically, using a modified Cas protein that binds a target sequence and an enzyme that deaminates a base. The result is a permanent change to the genome that does not create a double-strand break. A single change at position six of the beta chain is enough to make a red cell deform into the shape that gives the disease its name.

An older approach cut the DNA and relied on the cell's repair machinery to stitch it back together. That works, and it produces a heterogeneous mixture of repair outcomes, some of which are unwanted deletions. A base editor avoids the break entirely, which removes the class of errors that comes from rejoining a cut, and it is the reason the technique is being tested in diseases where a precise substitution is enough.

The target is a regulatory element rather than the mutated gene. Fetal haemoglobin is produced before birth and switched off after it, and a base editor that disrupts a repressor binding site in the promoter of the gamma-globin genes raises fetal haemoglobin in adult red cells. Fetal haemoglobin does not sickle, so the cells that carry enough of it survive, and the disease becomes mild rather than cured. Disrupting a repressor site leaves the gene intact and changes only how much of it is read, which is a smaller intervention than a deletion.

Delivery is the limitation. Editing a cell in the laboratory and returning it, which is what the sickle cell trial does, requires a bone marrow transplant procedure with chemotherapy conditioning. Editing inside the body, which several programmes are attempting for liver targets, needs a delivery vehicle that reaches the right organ and does not provoke an immune response. A conditioning regimen that ablates the marrow is the part of the procedure that carries most of the risk, and it is the same for every ex vivo therapy.

Off-target editing is the safety question that regulators will ask about. A base editor that acts on a sequence similar to its target will change other sites, and the assays that detect those changes are whole-genome sequencing of treated cells and of their descendants. A change in a stem cell is inherited by every cell that descends from it, which is why the bar for off-target rates is high. A liver is easier to reach with a lipid nanoparticle than a bone marrow is, and the immune response to the particle is the limiting factor.

Economics of a one-time therapy are the other constraint. A treatment that cures a disease that costs a health system a lifetime of transfusions and pain crises has to be priced against that lifetime cost, and the first approved gene therapy for sickle cell was priced at more than two million dollars. A base edit that is simpler to manufacture could come in below that, and it will still be a price that most of the patients who need it cannot pay without a government programme. A therapy that is given once and works is a therapy that a payer has to finance up front and amortize over decades.
