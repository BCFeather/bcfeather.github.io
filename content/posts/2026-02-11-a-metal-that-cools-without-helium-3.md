Cooling to a tenth of a kelvin is routine in a laboratory and expensive in a supply chain. Standard dilution refrigeration, which uses helium-3, and helium-3 is produced mainly as a byproduct of tritium decay in nuclear weapons programmes, which makes it scarce, expensive and geopolitically awkward. Quantum computing, precision measurement and low-temperature physics all depend on the temperature that the isotope supplies.

Magnetocaloric cooling is the alternative. A magnetic material heats when a field is applied and cools when the field is removed, because the spins that were aligned by the field disorder and absorb entropy. Adiabatic demagnetization uses that effect to reach low temperatures without any cryogen, and the obstacle has been the material, since a good refrigerant needs a large entropy change at the working temperature and a way to conduct heat away.

The material reported in *Nature* on 11 February is a three-dimensional metallic dipolar magnet with a frustrated lattice, and it shows a giant magnetocaloric effect together with high thermal conductivity. The combination is unusual because magnetic refrigerants are usually insulators, and an insulator cannot carry heat out of the sample fast enough for a practical device. A metal can, and the same conduction that makes it useful also makes it harder to keep the entropy change contained.

A spin supersolid is the state that gives the material its behaviour. In a supersolid, a crystal order coexists with a superfluid-like response, and the spin version of that state produces a large low-temperature entropy that a magnetic field can release. The team reports evidence for the state in the metallic alloy, along with a mechanism in which indirect exchange between electrons cooperates with magnetic dipolar interactions to produce it.

Practical claim is cooling to about 100 millikelvins by adiabatic demagnetization, which is the range where quantum bits and superconducting circuits operate and where helium-3 dilution units are currently installed. A device built from a metal that can be machined and thermally anchored is a different engineering problem from one built around a paramagnetic salt, and the paper presents the material as a candidate for a working refrigerator.

Open questions remain. Cycling a magnetocaloric material thousands of times is what a refrigerator does, and hysteresis, cracking and degradation under thermal cycling are the failure modes that a single measurement cannot assess. The thermal conductivity that helps in one direction also lets heat leak back in the other, and the efficiency of a real device will be lower than the entropy change suggests. It also removes a component that a laboratory cannot easily stockpile, which is the practical definition of a supply risk.

Strategy is the more immediate argument here. A cooling technology that does not depend on helium-3 removes a supply constraint from quantum research, and the constraint is tightening as the isotope becomes scarcer. Whether this alloy becomes a product or a demonstration, the direction of the work is towards a component that a laboratory can own.
