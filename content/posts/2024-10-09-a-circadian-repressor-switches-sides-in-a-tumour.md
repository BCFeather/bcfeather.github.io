---

Disrupted circadian rhythm is associated with cancer, and the association has been hard to convert into a mechanism. A paper in *PNAS* on 9 October 2024 supplies one for a specific regulator, by showing that REV-ERBα changes both where it binds and what it does when it gets there. Circadian disruption in shift workers has been documented for decades, and the association with cancer has never had a molecular explanation of this kind.

In normal tissue the receptor represses genes that control circadian and metabolic programmes. It does so by recruiting the NCoR and HDAC3 corepressor complex, which removes acetylation marks and closes chromatin at the sites it occupies. A receptor that recruits different cofactors in different cells is a general mechanism, and the paper shows the mechanism operating in a disease.

In tumours the same protein becomes a strong activator. Its association shifts from the corepressor complex to BRD4 and p300, the coactivators that acetylate histones and keep chromatin open, and the consequence is that thousands of genes are switched on rather than off. Binding-site changes and cofactor changes are both required for the inversion, since a receptor that moved without changing partners would still repress.

The change is in genome binding as well as in function. Sites that the receptor occupies in a tumour differ from the sites it occupies in healthy tissue, so the inversion is not a matter of the same binding event producing a different output. Therapeutic interest follows from the coactivator, since BRD4 is already a drug target and an agent that displaced the receptor might restore repression.

Tumorigenic programmes are among the activated genes, which is why the inversion matters clinically rather than only mechanistically. A repressor that becomes an activator does more than lose a function, since it gains one that the tumour can use. The causality question is the one a mouse model could answer, by asking whether the inversion precedes tumour formation or accompanies it.

The finding explains why circadian disruption in cancer has resisted a simple account. If the regulator's sign depends on the cofactors available in a cell, then the same molecule can be protective in one context and permissive in another. Metabolic genes are among the activated set, which connects the finding to the observation that tumour metabolism is reorganized rather than merely accelerated.

On the modelling side, the paper does not establish whether the inversion causes the tumour or follows from it, and the answer would decide whether the receptor is a target or a marker. Therapeutically the implication is that a drug aimed at the receptor would have to be context-specific, since an agent that strengthened repression in healthy tissue might do nothing in a tumour where the cofactor has already changed. The wider lesson is that a transcription factor's sign is a property of its context rather than of its sequence, which is a lesson the field keeps relearning.
