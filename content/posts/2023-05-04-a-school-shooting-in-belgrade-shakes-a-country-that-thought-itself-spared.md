---

A thirteen-year-old boy walked into the yard of a primary school in the Vracar district of Belgrade on the morning of 3 May carrying two pistols that belonged to his father and opened fire. Eight pupils and a security guard were killed, and six other children and a teacher were wounded. Police arrested the boy in the schoolyard. The interior ministry described weeks of planning, a list of names, and a sketch of the classrooms that the boy had drawn before the attack. Serbia had no modern memory of a shooting of this kind in a school.

Its belatedness is part of the shock. The wars of the 1990s left the region with a deep reservoir of firearms and a culture in which guns were ordinary objects in ordinary homes, yet the violence that the country knew was organized, political, or criminal, and it belonged to adults. That a child could take a parent's licensed weapon into a classroom and kill classmates was a possibility that the institutions had simply never planned for, and the security procedures at Serbian schools reflect that absence.

Within a day the argument had moved to the guns. Serbia has one of the highest rates of civilian firearm ownership in Europe, a legacy of compulsory military service and of the 1990s, and the law allows registered owners to keep pistols at home for self-defence. The government responded with a promise to tighten the rules, including stricter storage requirements and more frequent inspections of owners, and a review of the minimum age and the psychological tests that accompany a licence. Those measures are sensible and slow, and the debate about how many weapons a household should hold had begun before the funerals.

The second argument is about the culture that surrounded the boy. Serbian media and social networks spent the week circulating claims about bullying, about the influence of a particular music genre, about violent content online, and about a school system that leaves psychological support to a single pedagogue per building. The interior ministry has said that the boy had been planning the attack for a month, which raises the question of what a school notices when a pupil stops eating and starts counting. In a country where counselling is scarce, the answer tends to be found after the fact.

The political reaction has been unusually quiet. A country still governed by a coalition built around the president, and still digesting the political crises of the past two years, has found no easy partisan line to take on a thirteen-year-old with his father's gun. The president called the shooting an attack on the whole country and promised a national dialogue on violence, and the opposition, which usually attacks the government on security, has limited itself to demanding that the promises be kept.

What the country is left with is a set of dates. The school will reopen, the injured will be counted in a hospital, and the boy will be dealt with under a juvenile code that was written for a different kind of offender. The parents of the dead will receive the condolences that every government offers and will be told that nothing could have been done. Serbia will discover, as other countries have, that the sentence is true only until the next time it is tested.
