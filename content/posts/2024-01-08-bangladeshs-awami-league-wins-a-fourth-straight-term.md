Polling in Bangladesh's twelfth parliamentary election closed on the afternoon of the seventh of January, and by the following morning the count had produced the answer everyone expected. Sheikh Hasina's Awami League won 223 of the 300 directly elected seats, a two-thirds majority that gives her a fourth consecutive term and a fifth overall, and the Election Commission reported a turnout of 41.8 per cent. In 2018, the last election the party contested with the opposition present, turnout was above eighty per cent. What changed between the two votes was not the enthusiasm of the electorate but the field. The Bangladesh Nationalist Party, the largest opposition force, boycotted the poll, as it had demanded a caretaker government to oversee the vote, and its leaders spent the campaign either in prison or in hiding.

The campaign itself was thin. Ruling party candidates ran largely unopposed in dozens of constituencies, and where they did face rivals, several of those rivals were drawn from smaller parties that had agreed to participate. Independent candidates connected to the Awami League ran against official nominees in some seats, which gave the count a little colour without changing its direction. Turnout of 41.8 per cent is respectable by the standards of a country where elections have been fought over whether they would happen at all, but it is low for Bangladesh, and the gap between the official figure and what observers saw in the cities became a subject of argument within hours.

Foreign reaction was divided along lines that have become familiar. The United States and the European Union said the election had not been free and fair, and the American government imposed visa restrictions on individuals it said had undermined the democratic process before the vote. India, which shares a long border and a long history with the ruling party, congratulated Hasina and said the election had been conducted peacefully. China sent congratulations as well. The governments that mattered most to Dhaka were the ones that did not make the result conditional on the count, and the governments that did said so in language that leaves them room to keep the relationship going anyway.

For Bangladesh the arithmetic of the next five years is harder than the arithmetic of the vote. The economy that Hasina's government managed through the pandemic and the energy shock is now under pressure from foreign-exchange reserves that have fallen, inflation that has stayed high, and an IMF programme that imposes conditions the government would rather not discuss. The garment industry, which employs millions of women and earns most of the country's foreign currency, is facing a European Union review of its labour and safety standards. The Rohingya camps in Cox's Bazar hold about a million people whose return to Myanmar is no closer than it was in 2017. Each of those problems needs a government with a mandate, and each of them will be harder to solve for a government whose mandate is disputed.

The opposition's problem is the mirror image of the government's. A party that boycotts an election loses the platform it would have had, and the BNP now has neither seats nor a strategy that its own supporters can see. Its leaders have said they will continue to press for new elections, but the streets that would have to carry that demand have been quiet since 2015, and the security forces have spent a decade learning how to keep them quiet. Hasina, who is seventy-six, has not named a successor, and the question of what the Awami League becomes after her is one that nobody inside the party wants to ask out loud. A fourth term was won on the seventh of January. What it will be used for is the part that the count did not decide.
