Donald Trump was arraigned in a Manhattan courtroom on 4 April on thirty-four felony counts of falsifying business records, becoming the first former president in American history to be charged with a crime. He pleaded not guilty to all of them, spent about an hour in the building, and was fingerprinted but not photographed. The indictment was unsealed in the afternoon and ran to sixteen pages.

Charges concern payments made in the final weeks of the 2016 campaign. Prosecutors say that a payment of 130,000 dollars to a former adult film actress was recorded in the company's books as legal fees, that the reimbursement to the lawyer who made it was disguised as a retainer, and that nineteen separate documents were falsified between February and December 2017. Each count carries a maximum of four years, and the district attorney said the falsification was done to conceal a second crime, which is what elevates misdemeanors to felonies.

Legal theory is the part that will be argued. New York law makes it a felony to falsify records with the intent to conceal another crime, and the district attorney named two: violations of campaign finance law and of tax law. Defense lawyers have said the campaign finance argument rests on a federal statute that state prosecutors cannot enforce, and that the tax argument was never charged. The judge set the next hearing for December and mentioned a trial in the spring of 2024.

Political choreography was as carefully arranged as the legal one. The former president travelled from Florida on his own aircraft, was escorted into the building by officers of his own detail, and returned to a rally at his club in the evening where he described the case as an attempt to interfere with an election. His campaign said it had raised more than ten million dollars in the four days after the indictment was announced. The crowd outside was a fraction of the estimate, and the barricades outnumbered the demonstrators.

Outside the courthouse, the crowd was smaller than the city had prepared for. A handful of demonstrators on each side shouted at each other behind barricades, and the Republican congressman who had promised to protest was told by the district attorney's office to keep the peace. The police had closed several streets and expected thousands, which is a measure of how much of American politics now happens in the gap between what is announced and what arrives.

Other cases were already in line. A federal investigation into the handling of classified documents, a state inquiry into attempts to overturn the 2020 election in Georgia, and a federal inquiry into the events of 6 January were all proceeding, and each of them produces its own calendar. What the arraignment established is that a former president can be indicted, processed and released, and that the country's institutions will now be asked to run a presidential campaign and a series of trials at the same time, in the same country, before the same voters.
