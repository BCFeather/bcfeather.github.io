The House passed a spending bill of about one point seven trillion dollars on Friday, a day after the Senate approved it, and the measure averts a shutdown that would have begun at the end of the week. The bill funds the government for the whole of the next fiscal year and includes about forty-five billion dollars in military, economic and humanitarian aid for Ukraine. It also carries a ban on the Chinese-owned video app on government devices, a revision of the electoral count law and a raise of about seven per cent in defence spending. The president was expected to sign it before the deadline.

Ukraine money is the largest single package since the war began, and it includes weapons, training, budget support and money for the European allies to replace the equipment they have sent. The administration had asked for more, and the number in the bill was the result of a negotiation with the Republican leadership of the Senate. The defence provisions also fund the production of ammunition and of the missiles that had been drawn down from American stocks, which the Pentagon says are running low. Supporters described the package as a signal to Moscow, and opponents described it as a blank cheque.

Several unrelated measures had to ride along, because the alternative was a set of full-year appropriations that the chambers could not agree. The electoral count reform changes the law that the former president's lawyers had tried to use in 2020, raises the threshold for objections and makes the vice president's role ministerial. The TikTok provision applies to the phones of federal employees rather than to the app itself, and it was added after a series of hearings. There was also money for the police in the capital and for the Smithsonian.

Negotiations went to the last week because the two parties could not agree on the domestic numbers. Republicans had asked for a freeze in non-defence spending and for the repeal of some of the tax credits for clean energy, and the bill keeps the credits and raises domestic spending by about five per cent. The Senate's vote was sixty-eight to twenty-nine, which means that eighteen Republicans joined the Democrats. The House's vote was closer, and the leadership had to hold the bill for a day while it counted the votes.

A shutdown was avoided with hours to spare, and the agencies that had been preparing notices began to withdraw them. The last-minute procedure has become the normal one, and the previous year had needed three continuing resolutions before the omnibus passed. Budget analysts noted that the measure is larger than the one it replaces in both defence and domestic accounts, which is what inflation does to a fixed nominal number. The deficit effect was put at about a hundred and twenty billion dollars over the year.

What the vote shows is that the two parties can still pass a bill when the alternative is a shutdown they would both be blamed for. The fight over the tax credits will continue in the next Congress, when the House changes hands and the committees that wrote the energy provisions will be chaired by the opposition. What the Ukraine money shows is that the support has held through a year of argument, and that the size of it has grown. The next request will come in the spring, and the answer will be decided by a chamber that will look different.
