A Long March 4C lifted off from the Xichang satellite launch centre in Sichuan at 16:55 on 17 August and placed the 28B-02 test satellite into its planned orbit. The mission was declared a success and counted as the 589th flight of the Long March family. The series has now flown 589 times since 1970, and the pace has risen sharply: the previous hundred flights took about four years, and the last hundred took a little over two.

The satellite is described as a platform for space environment detection and related technical experiments. That phrase covers a range of instruments: particle detectors that count charged particles and measure their energy, magnetometers, and sensors that record the flux of radiation a spacecraft meets on its way through the Van Allen belts and the edges of the atmosphere. The Van Allen belts are the region where trapped particles are densest, and a satellite that spends time there accumulates dose in a way that a low-inclination orbit does not.

Data of this kind matters for two reasons. The first is engineering: a satellite's electronics are damaged by radiation in ways that depend on energy and flux, so the design of shielding and the choice of components depend on measurements rather than on models. The second is forecasting: the state of the near-Earth environment changes with solar activity, and a satellite that measures the change is part of the warning system for the satellites that cannot measure it themselves.

Long March 4C is the workhorse for sun-synchronous and low-inclination orbits from the inland sites. It is a three-stage vehicle burning storable propellants, which lets it sit fuelled on the pad while the weather is dealt with, and it has flown often enough that its schedule is limited by the satellite rather than by the rocket. Its record is long enough that the vehicle is no longer the subject of the story; the payload is. The vehicle's four stages and its storable propellants are a compromise: lower performance than a cryogenic rocket, but a launch that can wait out a thunderstorm without defuelling.

Xichang sits in a valley about 1,800 metres above sea level, which gives a small advantage in launch energy and a large disadvantage in access: the stages arrive by rail and by road, and the final approach is a mountain pass. The site has been in service since 1984 and remains the busiest for launches into low and medium orbits.

The 28B series appears to be a small programme rather than a constellation, which is worth noting. Not every launch in a busy month belongs to a mega-project. Some flights exist to keep an instrument in the sky and a measurement series unbroken, and those are the ones that produce the data other programmes rely on. A test satellite is also a way of qualifying components: a detector that works for two years in this orbit can be flown on something more expensive with less risk.
