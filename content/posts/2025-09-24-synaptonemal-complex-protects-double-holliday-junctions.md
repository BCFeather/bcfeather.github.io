Meiosis halves a cell's chromosomes, and it does so by building physical links between homologous chromosomes through crossover recombination. The DNA intermediate that becomes a crossover is the double Holliday junction, and its resolution has to be biased: a junction resolved one way gives a crossover, the other way gives a non-crossover that leaves no link and can leave a pair of chromosomes to segregate wrongly. Crossovers also hold the homologues together physically, so a failure of the bias produces missegregation instead of merely fewer exchanges.

That resolution happens inside the synaptonemal complex, the meiosis-specific structure that holds homologues end to end during pachytene. What the complex contributes to the bias has been unclear, since the recombination machinery works in its absence in vitro and the complex is a scaffold rather than an enzyme. The complex assembles between the homologues from the lateral elements inward, and its central region is where the recombination complexes sit. Without the complex the junctions still form, which is why the ablation is needed to see the protection at all.

A paper in *Nature* answers the question by removing components and watching what happens to the junctions. Conditional ablation shows that cohesin, the core of the complex's lateral elements, is required to maintain both synapsis and the crossover recombination complexes associated with the junctions during pachytene. Loss of cohesin dissolves synapsis as well, so the experiment separates junction maintenance from the pairing it depends on. The timing of the ablation matters, since the junctions form before pachytene ends.

The central region contributes too. The transverse-filament protein of the central region is required to maintain those recombination complexes, and the relationship runs both ways: the stability of the central region needs the continuous presence of the complexes. Synapsis is therefore coupled to junction formation, and desynapsis to resolution. Reciprocity is the surprising half, since it makes the central region dependent on the intermediates it is meant to protect. That dependence runs against the assumption that the structure is built once and then left in place.

The ablation experiments separate protection from synapsis. Double Holliday junction protection and maintenance of the recombination complexes can occur without end-to-end synapsis mediated by the central element, which means a local ensemble of complex components is enough to keep a junction on the crossover path. That local ensemble is what the authors mean by protection: a patch of complex around the junction, not a continuous filament. The experiment tests this by ablating the central element after synapsis is complete.

What follows is that the complex protects intermediates from aberrant dissolution, and the protection is local. What the paper does not establish is whether every component of the structure contributes, or whether the same division of labour holds in organisms with different complex architecture. The experiments are in mouse meiosis, where the complex is well defined and the genetics are tractable. The mouse is the organism in which the complex can be ablated in a controlled way, and its architecture is the one the field has characterized in most detail.
