Japan's economy grew at an annual rate of 1.6 per cent in the first quarter, above the forecast of about 0.7 and the fastest in three quarters. The quarter-on-quarter change was about 0.4 per cent. The figure is the first after the lifting of the virus restrictions in the autumn of 2022. The figure compared with a growth of about 0.1 per cent in the previous quarter, and the year-on-year measure was about 1.0 per cent. The nominal output rose by about 2 per cent, and the deflator was about 1.5, which is high for an economy that has been fighting the deflation for decades.

Consumption did the work. The private spending rose by about 0.6 per cent as the households returned to the restaurants and the travel, and the spending on the services rose the most. The reopening of the borders in October has brought about 2 million visitors a quarter, which is still below the level of 2019. The spending on the hotels and the restaurants rose by about 3 per cent, and the one on the transport by about 2 as the domestic travel recovered. The households have been running down the savings they built during the pandemic, and the saving rate has fallen to about 28 per cent from 34.

Capital spending held. The business investment rose by about 0.9 per cent, and the machinery orders have been rising since the winter. The manufacturers have been spending on the automation as the labour force shrinks, and the government has been subsidizing the investment in the chips. The investment in the software rose by about 6 per cent, and the one in the buildings by about 1.

Trade was a drag. The exports fell by about 1.5 per cent as the demand for the cars and the machinery from China and Europe weakened, and the imports rose by about 1. The net trade subtracted about 0.3 percentage points from the growth. The exports to China fell by about 7 per cent, and the ones to the United States rose by about 3 as the car shipments held.

Inventories added. The change in the stock contributed about 0.4 percentage points, which is the part that the economists discount. The domestic demand was the stronger half, and the revision in the next month will show whether it held. The government has revised the forecast for the year to about 1.3 per cent, and the bank's estimate is about 1.0.

This report is the one the bank has been waiting for. A growth of 1.6 per cent with the consumption leading is the mix the bank wants to see before it changes the policy, and the inflation report of the next week is the other half. The core inflation was about 3.4 per cent in April, and the bank has said the wage growth is the part it needs to see before it lifts the cap.
