Treasury notified Congress on 19 January that the federal government had reached its statutory borrowing limit of 31.4 trillion dollars, and that it would begin what it calls extraordinary measures to keep paying its bills. The measures are accounting manoeuvres, authorized by law, that free room under the ceiling by suspending investments in two retirement funds and in a savings plan for federal employees. The cap covers the total debt the government may carry, and it has been raised, extended or suspended more than seventy times since the Second World War.

The letter from the treasury secretary, Janet Yellen, set the start of a debt issuance suspension period at 19 January and said it could run until 5 June. The date is an estimate rather than a deadline, because it depends on the timing of tax receipts in April, and the estimate had moved by weeks in previous episodes. The two funds affected are the civil service retirement fund and the postal retirement fund, and the manoeuvre does not reduce the obligations, it postpones the investment.

The political arithmetic was set by the new House majority. The Speaker had promised the faction that had nearly blocked his election that he would not raise the ceiling without spending cuts, and the faction had promised its donors that the cuts would be large. The White House said it would not negotiate over the full faith and credit of the United States, which is the position every administration has taken in public and abandoned in private. The White House's position was that the ceiling had been raised three times under the previous president, twice with large bipartisan majorities, and that the debt was the product of decisions both parties had voted for.

Both sides cited the precedent of 2011, when a similar standoff took the government to the edge of default, the rating agency Standard and Poor's removed the country's top credit rating, and the stock market fell by about seventeen per cent over a summer. The agreement that ended it cut spending over a decade and damaged the economy in the year it took effect.

Outside the negotiation there were two ideas that circulated in the press. One was that the fourteenth amendment obliges the president to pay the debts whatever Congress does, an argument that had been made in 2011 and never tested. The other was a platinum coin of a trillion dollars, which the Treasury could mint and deposit, and which the administration had already ruled out. Neither idea had a constituency in the Senate, and both were discussed mainly as evidence that the executive had options it was choosing not to use.

A limit that is raised every time it binds is a limit in name only. The interesting question in January was how much damage the two parties would do to the instrument before they raised it again. By the end of the month the Treasury had begun to publish the dates in its daily statements, and the market had begun to price them.
