title: "Turning moderately ill COVID patients onto their front does not help"
date: "2022-03-23T10:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A pragmatic trial in 15 North American hospitals stopped for futility after 248 patients, finding no reduction in death or respiratory failure from instructing patients who needed up to 50 per cent oxygen to lie prone."
---

Proning became a standard of care in intensive care during the first wave, when patients with severe acute respiratory distress syndrome improved their oxygenation within minutes of being turned onto their front. The physiology is straightforward: dorsal lung regions are better perfused and less well ventilated when a patient lies on their back, and gravity reverses that. The manoeuvre recruits the dorsal lung, improves ventilation-perfusion matching and buys time, and in a ventilated patient it was shown to reduce mortality in a large trial.

Whether the same manoeuvre helps patients who are not yet ventilated was an open question, and one with a large potential benefit because proning costs nothing and needs only a nurse and a few pillows. A pragmatic trial in 15 hospitals in Canada and the United States tested it in patients admitted with COVID who needed supplemental oxygen but not mechanical ventilation. The results appeared in *The BMJ*, 23 March 2022.

Eligible patients needed up to 50 per cent inspired oxygen and had to be able to lie prone with verbal instruction, and 257 of 570 assessed patients were randomized, with 248 in the analysis. The primary outcome was a composite of in-hospital death, mechanical ventilation or worsening respiratory failure defined as needing at least 60 per cent oxygen for 24 hours. A composite outcome mixes death with intubation, and the two are not equally important to a patient, though the composite is the standard design for a respiratory trial in this population.

The trial stopped early for futility. The median time from admission to randomization was one day, the median age was 56 years, 36 per cent were women and 90 per cent were receiving oxygen by nasal prongs, which describes a population that was only moderately ill and not one at the edge of intubation.

Time spent prone was the variable that connected the trial to the intensive care evidence. Patients in the intervention arm were asked to proning for a target number of hours per day, and adherence in a ward without the staffing of an intensive care unit is inevitably lower than in a trial of sedated and ventilated patients. The trial could not mask the intervention, since a patient knows whether they are lying on their stomach, and that limits what can be inferred about subjective components of the outcome.

A negative result in a pragmatic setting is not the same as a negative physiological result. Oxygenation may have improved during the sessions without translating into fewer intubations, because the outcome depends on the trajectory of the disease rather than on a single measurement of saturation. A trial powered for a composite of death and ventilation needs several hundred events, and this one had far fewer, so the futility analysis is a statement about the observed effect rather than a proof of absence.

The practical message is that proning should remain a rescue manoeuvre in critical care and not a routine instruction on a respiratory ward. It is cheap, it is safe, and in this population it did not change the course of the illness.
