The prefrontal cortex holds a rule in working memory and, when the rule changes, replaces it. Maintenance and replacement are different operations, and the circuit that performs either of them has been hard to isolate: lesions cannot separate the two, and recording alone cannot show which neurons cause the switch, only which ones are busy while it happens.

Kathleen Cho, Jingcheng Shi and colleagues, writing in *Nature* in April 2023, isolate the circuit with rule-shift behaviour in mice. Long-range inhibition, carried by parvalbumin-expressing neurons whose axons cross the corpus callosum, turns out to switch the prefrontal circuit between maintenance and learning.

What the switch amounts to is a gate. The callosal projections suppress inputs that would keep the old rule's representation active, and that suppression opens the circuit to the inputs encoding the new rule. The cell type carries the argument: parvalbumin neurons are fast-spiking interneurons, and their projection across the midline is unusual, since most interneurons act locally, so a long-range inhibitory wire links the two hemispheres' prefrontal circuits.

Because silencing the callosal population changes the transition, the manipulation is causal, and the direction of the change says whether the population promotes or restrains the switch. Recording supplies the timing that separates cause from correlate, since the authors measure the population's activity during the transition and compare it with the behavioural change.

Behaviourally, the switch is a two-phase transition. A mouse that has learned a rule responds by it, and a mouse facing a new rule must first stop responding and then learn; the measurements resolve detection from learning, which matters because a circuit that learned new rules without noticing the old one's failure would be useless. One hemisphere may lead during such a shift, and a callosal projection is the route by which the leading side informs the other, an asymmetry the lateralized analysis addresses.

Maintaining and updating are antagonistic, so a circuit that does both needs an arbiter, and inhibition is that arbiter. A mouse's task is a simplification, however, and the human prefrontal cortex carries connections a mouse's lacks, so the finding remains a hypothesis about human flexibility until imaging tests it. Whether the projection persists into adulthood decides more: a population that remains suggests a capacity a mature brain keeps, while one that disappears suggests a critical period. A population with a defined projection and a defined behavioural role is the unit a mechanistic account needs, and this is one.

Beyond one rule, generalization is the open test, because a circuit that learns a reversal in a laboratory task and one that learns a rule in a real environment are related claims, and the second needs a task whose structure the animal has not seen. The hemisphere asymmetry needs more than one paradigm as well, since a lead that appears in a rule shift may be absent in a spatial task, and a lateralized finding is stronger when it repeats.
