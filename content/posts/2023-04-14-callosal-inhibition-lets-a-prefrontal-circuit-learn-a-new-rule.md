---

The prefrontal cortex holds a rule in working memory and, when the rule changes, replaces it. The two operations are different, and the circuit that performs them has been hard to isolate.

The experiments in *Nature*, April 2023 isolate it. Kathleen Cho, Jingcheng Shi and colleagues use rule-shift behaviour in mice and show that long-range inhibition, carried by parvalbumin-expressing neurons whose axons cross the corpus callosum, switches the circuit between maintenance and learning.

The switch is gating. The callosal projections suppress inputs that would keep the old rule's representation active, and the suppression opens the circuit to the inputs that encode the new rule.

The cell type is the key. Parvalbumin neurons are fast-spiking interneurons, and their projection across the midline is unusual: most interneurons act locally. A long-range inhibitory projection is a wire between two hemispheres' prefrontal circuits.

The behavioural correlate is a transition. A mouse that has learned a rule responds according to it, and a mouse whose rule has changed must first stop responding and then learn. The paper's measurements resolve the two phases.

The manipulation is causal. Silencing the callosal population changes the transition, and the change's direction tells you whether the population promotes or restrains the switch.

The network consequence is a division of labour. Maintaining a representation and updating one are antagonistic operations, and a circuit that does both needs a mechanism that arbitrates. Inhibition is that mechanism.

The limitation is the species. A mouse's rule-learning task is a simplified version of a cognitive operation, and the human prefrontal cortex has connections that a mouse's lacks.

What the paper does not show is the human relevance. A finding about callosal inhibition in a mouse is a hypothesis about human flexibility, and the imaging that would test it is a separate study.

For systems neuroscience, the value is a circuit with a function. A population of neurons with a defined projection and a defined behavioural role is the unit that a mechanistic account needs.

The anatomy is unusual enough to be the paper's starting point. A parvalbumin neuron that projects across the midline is a rarity, and its existence suggests a role that local inhibition cannot perform.

The recording is the evidence. The authors measure the population's activity during the transition, and the timing relative to the behavioural change is what distinguishes a cause from a correlate.
The two hemispheres' roles differ. In a rule-shift task one hemisphere may lead, and a callosal projection is a route by which the leading side informs the other. The paper's lateralized analysis addresses that asymmetry.
The developmental question is whether the projection persists. A callosal interneuron population that exists in adulthood suggests a capacity that a mature brain retains, and a population that disappears suggests a critical period.
The task's structure matters. A rule shift requires the animal to notice that the old rule has failed, and a circuit that only learns new rules without detecting the failure would be useless. The paper separates detection from learning.
