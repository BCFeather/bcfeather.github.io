A patient who does not move or speak may still be aware, and functional magnetic resonance imaging has shown that some of them follow commands inside the scanner. The method is expensive, it needs a cooperative subject in a machine, and it is unavailable in most intensive care units. The gap between behavioural and actual awareness is the problem neuroimaging has been trying to close for twenty years.

A paper in *PNAS* on 26 August 2024 tests a portable alternative. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy shines light through the skull and measures changes in blood oxygenation, and it can be brought to a bedside and run while a patient is lying still. Bringing the measurement to the bedside removes the transport, the sedation and the staffing that a scanner suite demands.

The authors first established what the technique can see in healthy volunteers. Resting-state networks were detectable at the level of an individual subject, along with sensorimotor processing, auditory processing specific to speech, and volitional command-following. Establishing the battery in volunteers is what allows a null result in a patient to mean something.

Specificity matters here. A signal that rises whenever anything happens is useless for detecting awareness, so the battery separates responses to speech from responses to other sounds, and separates passive listening from a task the subject has to perform. A mismatch between the two listening conditions is the signal that a patient is processing language rather than merely hearing sound. Command-following is the decisive condition, because a patient who can modulate a signal on request is a patient who is aware.

The same battery was then applied to behaviourally nonresponsive patients. The paper reports neural correlates of consciousness in some of them, which is the result that the field has been waiting for from a device that could be deployed outside a scanner suite. Patients in intensive care are often sedated, so recordings have to be taken in the intervals when sedation is light.

Physical limits apply. Light penetrates a few centimetres, so the signal comes mostly from superficial cortex, and deep structures such as the thalamus are invisible to it. Movement artefact and scalp blood flow can also contaminate a recording. Depth matters because awareness is not a cortical phenomenon alone, and the structures that gate it sit below the reach of the light.

Clinical adoption would need a threshold. A test that sometimes reports awareness in a patient who has none would raise expectations that cannot be met, and the paper's sample is too small to set one. Even so, the direction is clear. A cheap portable device that can ask a paralysed patient to move a hand, and register the attempt, changes what intensive care can know about the people in its beds. A device that raises expectations needs a protocol for what happens when the answer is yes. Bedside neuroimaging would change the ethics of intensive care as much as its diagnostics, and that deserves an explicit debate.
