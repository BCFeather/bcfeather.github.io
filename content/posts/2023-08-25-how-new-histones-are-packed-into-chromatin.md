DNA replication doubles the genome, and the new duplex must be wrapped into nucleosomes. The deposition is done by a chaperone that hands histones to the DNA, and the hand-off has been a black box.

The structure in *Science*, August 2023 opens it. The authors determine the structure of the chromatin assembly factor CAF-1 with its histone cargo, and the arrangement shows how a tetramer of histones is held for delivery.

The holding is the finding. The chaperone grips the histone pair in a conformation that prevents premature association with DNA, and the grip is released at the right moment.

The coupling to replication is the design. CAF-1 travels with the replication machinery through an interaction with a processivity factor, and the structure shows where that interaction sits.

The deposition is ordered. A new nucleosome needs a tetramer first and two dimers after, and the chaperone's cargo is the tetramer that the first step requires.

The consequence is epigenetic inheritance. A new nucleosome must carry the marks of the old one, and a chaperone that delivers histones is a node where the marks can be re-established.

The comparison with other chaperones is the frame. A histone chaperone in the nucleus and one in the cytoplasm bind the same proteins for different purposes, and the structures show the difference.

The limitation is the complex's dynamics. A hand-off is a motion, and a structure is a snapshot of one state.

What the paper does not establish is the mark's transfer. The chaperone delivers histones, and the enzymes that modify them are a separate system.

For molecular biology, the value is a step in a cycle described at atomic resolution. Chromatin's inheritance has been inferred from genetics, and the structure makes the inference a mechanism.

The tetramer's grip is the protection. A histone pair that meets DNA prematurely forms an aberrant particle, and a chaperone that holds it prevents the mistake.

The processivity factor is the address. A chaperone that binds the replication clamp is one that is present where the fork is, and the interaction is the delivery's address label.

The nucleosome's assembly is the outcome. A tetramer and two dimers wrap about 147 base pairs, and the chaperone's role ends when the particle is complete.

The mark's re-establishment is the epigenetic question. A new histone arrives unmodified, and the enzymes that copy the old marks are the machinery that completes the inheritance.

The structure's resolution is the advance. A complex of a chaperone with a histone tetramer is flexible, and the authors' cryo-electron microscopy is what resolves the interfaces.

The cancer connection is plausible. A chaperone that is overexpressed supports proliferation, and an inhibitor would be a candidate drug.
The histone's supply is the cell cycle's constraint. A dividing cell must double its histone content, and a chaperone that delivers at the fork is one that matches supply to demand.
The nucleosome's position is the second question. A deposited particle must be placed at a defined site, and the sequence's preference for a histone octamer sets the lattice that the chaperone fills.
