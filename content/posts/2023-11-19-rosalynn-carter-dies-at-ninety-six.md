Rosalynn Carter died on 19 November at her home in Plains, Georgia, two days after the family announced that she had entered hospice care alongside her husband, the former president, who is ninety-nine. She was ninety-six. The Carter Center said she had been diagnosed with dementia in the spring and had been living with the condition at home, where she and her husband had lived since leaving the White House in 1981.

Her tenure as first lady was unusually consequential. She sat in on cabinet meetings, travelled abroad as an emissary of the president, and took on the mental health portfolio as a policy area rather than a cause. She had worked on the issue during her husband's time as governor of Georgia, and in 1977 she chaired a presidential commission that recommended changes in federal funding for community programmes, and she pushed for the deinstitutionalization that followed.

After the White House, the couple founded the Carter Center in Atlanta, and she became its most visible advocate for caregiving and for the treatment of neglected diseases. She travelled to villages in Africa and Asia to see guinea worm programmes, and she campaigned for the recognition of the unpaid work that families do for relatives who are ill. In 1999 she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom, and in 2001 she was inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame.

Her marriage was the longest of any American presidential couple, at seventy-seven years. They met when she was three days old, in the small town where both families lived, and she later said that she had known him since she could remember. He wrote about her in his memoirs and in public statements as the person who had made his career possible, and she managed the business and the household during the years when he was away at the navy and then in the state senate.

The Carters' later life became a public example of endurance. They taught Sunday school in Plains, they built houses with Habitat for Humanity, and they spoke openly about the illnesses of old age. In February, the family announced that the former president had entered hospice care at home, and the couple's decision to stay in the house they had lived in for decades rather than accept institutional care was widely reported and admired.

The tributes came from across the political spectrum. Presidents and former presidents from both parties issued statements, and mental health organizations noted that she had put the subject on the national agenda before it was fashionable. For a woman whose husband's presidency lasted a single term, her influence on policy outlasted it by decades, and the institutions she built in Atlanta will carry the work further than any single career could. Her funeral was held at the Maranatha Baptist Church in Plains, where the couple had taught Sunday school for decades, and the guest list included every living former president and first lady, a gathering the family described as the last reunion of a generation that had shaped the office.
