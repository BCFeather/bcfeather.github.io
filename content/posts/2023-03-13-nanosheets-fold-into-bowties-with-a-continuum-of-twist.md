Chirality in inorganic materials is usually fixed by the crystal. A particle that is left-handed because its lattice is left-handed cannot be made right-handed without growing a different crystal, and the optical response follows the structure.

The assemblies in *Nature*, 15 March 2023 decouple the two. Prashant Kumar, Thi Vo, Nicholas Kotov and colleagues fold semiconductor nanosheets into bowtie-shaped particles, and the twist of the fold can be varied continuously, so that the same building block produces a range of chiral geometries.

The fold is the mechanism. A nanosheet under stress buckles into a saddle or a helix, and the amount of twist depends on the sheet's dimensions and on the strain applied. The bowtie is a shape that concentrates the twist at its waist.

The optical consequence is circular dichroism. A chiral object absorbs left- and right-circularly polarized light differently, and the magnitude depends on the geometry's scale relative to the wavelength. A tunable twist is a tunable response.

The continuum is the result that matters. Most chiral nanomaterials come in two enantiomers, and a material with a continuous parameter is a material whose optical response can be dialled rather than chosen.

The assembly is solution-based. Sheets are grown, functionalized and assembled under conditions that the authors control, and the process is the kind that a colloidal chemist can scale. A lithographic structure with the same geometry would be a single particle at a time.

The application is polarization optics. A filter that rotates a polarization by a controlled amount is a component in displays and in sensors, and a colloidal version would be cheap to make and easy to coat.

The stability question is open. A folded sheet is a metastable shape, and a particle that unfolds with time or with temperature is a particle whose optical response drifts. The paper's durability tests are the first indication.

What the assemblies cannot yet do is act individually. A film of bowties averages their orientations, and a single particle's response requires a measurement technique that the paper develops and that most laboratories lack.

For the field, the value is a design space. Twisted nanosheets with a continuous parameter are a family, and a family is what a materials chemist needs to optimize a response rather than to demonstrate one.

The theoretical model accompanies the synthesis. A continuum of twist implies a continuum of chiral geometries, and the paper's model relates the sheet's elastic properties to the fold's angle, which is how the parameter is predicted rather than found.
