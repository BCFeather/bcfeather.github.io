Chirality in inorganic materials is usually fixed by the crystal. A particle that is left-handed because its lattice is left-handed cannot be made right-handed without growing a different crystal, and the optical response follows the structure.

In *Nature* on 15 March 2023, the two are decoupled. Prashant Kumar, Thi Vo, Nicholas Kotov and colleagues fold semiconductor nanosheets into bowtie-shaped particles, and the twist of the fold can be varied continuously, so that one building block produces a range of chiral geometries.

Folding is the mechanism. A nanosheet under stress buckles into a saddle or a helix, and the amount of twist depends on the sheet's dimensions and on the strain applied, with the bowtie concentrating the twist at its waist. Substrate drives the fold, since a sheet deposited on a surface of defined curvature adopts the shape that minimizes its elastic energy, and the method varies the curvature to vary the twist.

Because a chiral object absorbs left- and right-circularly polarized light differently and the magnitude depends on the geometry's scale relative to the wavelength, circular dichroism is the optical consequence, and a tunable twist is a tunable response. Continuity is the result that matters, since most chiral nanomaterials come in two enantiomers while a material with a continuous parameter has a response that can be dialled rather than chosen.

Assembly is solution-based. Sheets are grown, functionalized and assembled under conditions the authors control, and the process is the kind a colloidal chemist can scale, whereas a lithographic structure with the same geometry would be made one particle at a time. Theory accompanies the synthesis, because a continuum of twist implies a continuum of chiral geometries, and the model relates the sheet's elastic properties to the fold's angle, which is how the parameter is predicted rather than found.

Polarization optics is the application. A filter that rotates polarization by a controlled amount is a component in displays and in sensors, and a colloidal version would be cheap to make and easy to coat.

Stability stays open, since a folded sheet is a metastable shape and a particle that unfolds with time or with temperature has an optical response that drifts, for which the durability tests are a first indication. Individuality stays out of reach, because a film of bowties averages their orientations and a single particle's response needs a technique the paper develops and most laboratories lack. Molecular chirality offers the comparison, since a molecule's handedness is fixed by its bonds while a nanostructure's is set by its shape, and shape can be changed after synthesis, which is the freedom exploited here. A design space is what the field gains, since a family of twisted nanosheets is what a materials chemist needs to optimize a response rather than to demonstrate one. A coating is the product form the chemistry suggests, since a colloidal dispersion can be spun into a film whose average twist is set by the field applied while it dries.
