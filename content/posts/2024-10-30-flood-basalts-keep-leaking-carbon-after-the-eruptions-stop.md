Large igneous provinces flood continents with basalt, and the standard account has the eruptions warming the climate and the weathering of the fresh rock then drawing carbon dioxide down again. A paper in *Nature Geoscience* on 30 October 2024 explains why the cooling often failed to arrive on schedule. Weathering draws carbon down only when fresh silicate is exposed, which is why a hidden eruption removes the sink as well as the source.

Geodynamic modelling of mantle melting and thermomechanical modelling of magma transport are the two tools, and together they describe how magma moves through a crust that is being loaded by earlier magma. Rheology is the part of the system that a simple eruption-rate model omits, and the feedback is what makes the timing long.

Rheological feedback throttles the eruption rate. A crust that has been heated and weakened by intrusions cannot transmit magma as fast, so melt continues to be generated and carbon dioxide continues to be supplied while the surface flux falls. Cryptic degassing is invisible at the surface and visible in the isotopic composition of gases that reach the atmosphere, which is how it can be detected.

Cryptic degassing is the consequence. Gas escapes through the crust without a surface eruption, which means the climatic forcing continues after the visible volcanism has stopped, and the weathering response has nothing fresh to work on. A delayed recovery is not a prediction that every province should satisfy, and the claim is that it explains the cases where it occurs.

The mismatch explains the record. Several Phanerozoic provinces are followed by warming and delayed biotic recovery lasting millions of years beyond the main extrusive phase, and a throttled eruption rate is a mechanism that produces exactly that. Calibration against a dated province would convert a plausible mechanism into a tested one, and the dating exists for several provinces.

The model is not calibrated against a specific province, and the rheological parameters are uncertain by a factor that matters for the timing. A test against a well-dated province would be the obvious next step. Correlation and causation are what the timing argument addresses, since a delay is evidence that the two processes were decoupled.

What the paper changes is the reading of a correlation. A province that erupted a certain volume and left a certain climate signal need not have done both at the same time, and the delay is a prediction rather than an anomaly. For the end-Permian and other extinction boundaries, the implication is that the interval of stress was longer than the interval of visible eruption, which lengthens the window in which a biological crisis could develop. Lengthening the stress window matters for extinction because a crisis needs time to develop, and a longer interval gives it more.
