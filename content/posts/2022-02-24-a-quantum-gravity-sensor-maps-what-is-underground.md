Gravity surveying has been practical for decades and has been limited by vibration. A gravimeter measures acceleration to extraordinary precision, but the ground moves, the instrument tilts, and separating the signal of a buried void from the noise of a passing lorry has required averaging over hours. Surveying has therefore been a slow business, and a measurement that takes hours cannot be repeated along a line long enough to map a site.

Atom interferometry offers a different instrument. Cold atoms fall through laser pulses, and the interference pattern depends on the local acceleration, so the apparatus measures gravity without a mechanical test mass. The technique had been demonstrated in laboratories and had not been shown to work on a roadside. The atoms are laser-cooled, launched and interrogated in free fall, and the whole apparatus fits in a van that can be parked beside the survey line.

A sensor described in *Nature*, 23 February 2022, measures the gradient of gravity rather than gravity itself, which cancels much of the common-mode noise. It suppresses microseismic vibration, laser phase noise, thermal and magnetic variation and tilt, reaching a statistical uncertainty of 20 E, where one E is 10^-9 per second squared. The gradient falls off faster with distance than the field itself, which sharpens the response to a shallow feature and suppresses a distant one.

Along an 8.5-metre line, the survey resolved features at half-metre spacing and detected a two-metre tunnel at a signal-to-noise ratio of 8. Bayesian inference placed the tunnel's centre within 0.19 metres horizontally and its depth at 1.89 metres, with an interval from 0.59 metres above to 2.3 metres below. The quoted depth interval is asymmetric because gravity data constrain the product of density and volume, and a tunnel of unknown width trades width against depth.

The engineering that made this possible is mostly noise rejection. Vibration enters as a common mode when the two atom clouds that form the gradient measurement are separated vertically, so subtracting one from the other removes it, and the remaining errors come from the laser and the magnetic field. Field surveys require the instrument to be levelled and re-levelled as it moves, and the tilt rejection described here is what makes a moving survey possible at all.

What the survey cannot do is see through everything. The method responds to mass density, so it finds voids, tunnels, pipes and aquifers and is confused by anything else of similar contrast, and the instrument still needs a stable platform even if it no longer needs a quiet one. Small objects near the surface produce a sharp anomaly that the spacing can resolve, while a deep and diffuse target produces a broad and faint one that requires longer averaging.

The applications the authors list are the ones where knowing the subsurface cheaply matters: mapping aquifers, locating archaeological structures, checking ground before construction, and monitoring the water table. A sensor that works beside a road makes all of those routine rather than specialist.
