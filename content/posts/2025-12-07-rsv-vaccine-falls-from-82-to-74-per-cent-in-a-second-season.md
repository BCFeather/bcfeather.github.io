Respiratory syncytial virus is a childhood disease that returns in old age with a higher mortality, and a vaccine for older adults completed its second season this month. The efficacy against lower respiratory tract disease was 74 per cent, against 82 in the first season.

The decline is the result that programs have to plan around. A 8-point fall in one year suggests waning immunity, and a third season's data will determine whether an annual or a biennial booster is the right schedule. The endpoint is specific. The trial counts cases with three or more symptoms and a confirmed test, which excludes the milder upper respiratory infections that most people experience. That definition is why the efficacy number is high and the population it applies to is narrow.

Severity protection held better than infection protection. Against cases requiring hospitalization the efficacy was 81 per cent in the second season, against 94 in the first, which is the pattern a hospital's budget cares about. Hospital admission is the endpoint a health system budgets for, and the second season's figure against that endpoint was 74.

Safety data accumulated without a new signal. Over 24 months and 25,000 participants, the rate of serious adverse events was equal between arms, and the earlier concern about a neurological condition has not reappeared. A program that revaccinates every winter spends twice and prevents more, and the arithmetic depends on the price a purchaser negotiates.

Cost-effectiveness depends on the price. At 300 dollars a dose and a hospitalization cost of 15,000, a program breaks even at a number-needed-to-vaccinate of 100, which the trial's incidence supports in the highest-risk age band. Two programs compete for one budget line. A maternal dose protects an infant for six months, and an older adult's dose protects the adult. A health system with a fixed budget has to choose, and the two programs' cost per case averted differ by a factor of three.

A second season's number is a different measurement from a first's. The first season's trial enrolled adults who had not recently been infected, and the second season's population includes those whose immunity from the first winter has waned. The 8-point fall combines waning with a change in the population. Program design absorbs the decline. A vaccine that falls to 74 per cent is still protective for the group at highest risk, and a program that revaccinates the oldest every second winter spends its budget where the decline costs the most. A vaccine's second season is the number a budget is built on. A program that plans a winter's hospital capacity uses the lower figure, and a program that uses the first season's number buys beds it will not need and misses beds it will.

Seventy-four and 81 are the numbers that matter, and the gap between them is a program's problem. A vaccine that prevents mild disease less well than severe disease is a vaccine that a hospital can justify and a public program may not.
