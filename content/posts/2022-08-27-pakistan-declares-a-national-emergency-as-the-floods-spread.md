title: "Pakistan declares a national emergency as the floods reach a third of the country"
date: "2022-08-27T23:50:00+08:00"

description: "Monsoon rain that is three times the average has displaced millions, and the climate minister calls it a disaster of epic proportions."
---

The government declared a national emergency on Friday as the floods that began with the monsoon in June spread across the provinces of Sindh and Balochistan and into the Punjab. The climate minister said the country was facing a humanitarian disaster of epic proportions, and she put the number of people affected at thirty-three million. The death toll was above a thousand and rising as the water moved south, and the army had been deployed to build embankments and to evacuate villages. The prime minister cancelled his foreign travel and set up a relief fund.

The rainfall is the part that the meteorologists emphasize. Sindh received about three times its thirty-year average for August, and Balochistan received more than five times. The rivers that run from the mountains in the north were already full when the rain arrived, and the dams that regulate them are old. The water broke the embankments in several places, and the districts that are usually dry were flooded first. The villages on the flood plain have been built there for generations, and the land is fertile because the river has flooded it before.

At the same time, the damage to the harvest will decide how long the crisis lasts. About half of the cotton crop in Sindh has been lost, and the rice and the dates that the province exports were under water at the wrong time of year. The farmers who lost the crop also lost the seed for the next one, and the livestock that survived has nowhere to graze. The food programme has warned that the price of wheat will rise, and the country was already importing it.

Meanwhile, the relief effort is the one the state has done before, and it is doing it again with less. The helicopters that the army uses are old, and the boats that the local authorities use are the ones that were bought after the floods of 2010. The international appeal has raised a fraction of what the United Nations asked for, and the donors who promised money in 2010 did not pay all of it. The roads that carry the aid are the ones that the water has cut, and the bridges that remain are carrying more traffic than they were built for.

In the meantime, the political argument has already begun. The government says the floods are a consequence of the climate change that the country has done least to cause, and it has asked for the losses to be covered by the fund that was agreed at the climate summit in Glasgow. The fund has not been set up, and the pledges that were made for it are small. The province that has been hit hardest is governed by a party that is in opposition to the government, and the two have argued about the distribution of the relief.

What the flood shows is a country that is paying for a climate it did not create. The emissions of Pakistan are less than one per cent of the world total, and the monsoon that has broken the records is the kind that the models said would become more frequent. The water will take weeks to fall and the reconstruction will take years, and the money for it will be argued about in rooms from which the people who live on the flood plain are absent.
