Sex differences in cancer are usually read as hormone effects on the tumour itself. Glioblastoma is commoner in men, and androgens have long been assumed to feed it, which is why androgen deprivation looks like a sensible adjunct. A study in *Nature* on 6 May reports that inside the skull the assumption reverses: removing androgens made tumours grow faster, not slower. The finding does not overturn hormone therapy for prostate cancer, where the tumour sits outside the cranium, and it does not make testosterone a treatment for glioblastoma, which would need a randomized trial before anyone could claim that. What it does is force the question of which organ a hormone is acting on.

The experiments used mouse models of glioblastoma and compared tumours growing in the brain with tumours growing under the skin. Castration accelerated the intracranial tumours and delayed the extracranial ones, which is the sort of result that cannot be explained by a single growth-promoting pathway. The organ decides the direction.

Human data pointed the same way. Among male patients with glioblastoma, testosterone treatment was associated with a significantly lower risk of death. That is observational evidence gathered from patients who were treated for low testosterone rather than randomized, and it cannot settle causation, but it is consistent with the mouse result and it makes the question worth a trial. The patients in that comparison were not selected for brain tumour biology, so the group is heterogeneous in ways a trial would have to control: steroid use, seizure medication, and the extent of resection all move survival independently of testosterone.

The mechanism runs through the immune system rather than the tumour cell. Castration raised serum glucocorticoids, and those steroids acted on myeloid cells to create an immunosuppressive environment in which T cells stopped working properly. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis was hyperactive, and the driver was neuroinflammation, with interleukin-1 beta and tumour necrosis factor signalling upward from the brain.

Spatial transcriptomics filled in the local detail. Loss of androgens increased inflammasome activation in microglia, and the inflamed microglia in turn pushed the neuroinflammatory state that kept the axis switched on. A loop of this kind explains why the effect is specific to the brain: the same hormonal change reaches extracranial tumours without the microglial amplifier in between.

Clinical practice will read this cautiously. Androgen deprivation is used in prostate cancer and in some salvage settings, and nothing here says it should be withheld from a patient with a brain metastasis or a primary brain tumour, since the extracranial result ran the other way. It does say that the brain is an immunologically distinct compartment, and that trials of hormonal manipulation in glioblastoma should stratify by sex, measure glucocorticoid exposure, and treat the adrenal axis as part of the intervention rather than as background noise. Dexamethasone is already given to most of these patients for oedema, and the paper implies that the dose is an immunological variable and not only a symptomatic one.
