Apple's December quarter is the quarter in which the company either justifies its valuation or explains why it cannot, and this year it justified it. Revenue for the fiscal first quarter, which ended on 27 December, was 143.8 billion dollars, an increase of 16 per cent on the same quarter a year earlier. Diluted earnings per share were 2.84 dollars, up 19 per cent, and net income rose to 42.1 billion dollars from 36.3 billion.

The iPhone supplied the growth. Sales were 85.27 billion dollars, a rise of 23 per cent, and the company described it as the best quarter the product has had, with records in every geographic segment. That framing matters because Apple had spent two years being described as a company whose hardware had plateaued, and a quarter of that size requires demand rather than pricing to explain it.

Services reached an all-time record of 30 billion dollars, up 14 per cent, and the installed base passed 2.5 billion active devices. Services is the part of the business with the margins that investors pay for, and the installed base is the number that determines how much room remains. A base of that size growing at all is a better signal than a single quarter of app store revenue.

Two categories declined. Mac revenue fell to 8.39 billion dollars from 8.99 billion, and wearables, home and accessories fell to 11.49 billion from 11.75 billion. Neither decline is dramatic, and both were attributed to the timing of product refreshes and not to weak demand, and that is the explanation companies offer when the comparison base was strong and the calendar did not help.

Cash generation remained the defining feature. Operating cash flow was close to 54 billion dollars, and the company returned almost 32 billion to shareholders during the quarter. The board declared a dividend of 0.26 dollars per share, payable on 12 February to shareholders of record on 9 February, a figure that has become a formality and that still costs the company several billion dollars a year.

The quarter has to be read against a difficult comparison and against currency. Revenue growth of 16 per cent in dollars includes a translation effect that the company did not break out prominently, and the geographic detail shows that the growth came from markets where Apple had been losing share. Whether that is a durable recovery or a single strong cycle is the question the next two quarters will answer.

For the wider technology industry the signal is about premium hardware. If the most expensive phone on the market can grow units at 23 per cent in a quarter when the global smartphone market is flat, then the constraint on the market is not demand for devices but demand for cheap ones, and the substitution toward the high end continues.
