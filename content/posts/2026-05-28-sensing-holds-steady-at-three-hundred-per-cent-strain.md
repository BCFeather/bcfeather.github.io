Wearable sensors fail at the joint. A patch that reads glucose or lactate through the skin has to survive bending, stretching and sweating, and the electrode that works on a forearm at rest drifts the moment the wearer lifts something. A paper in *Science* on 28 May reports a device that holds its electrochemical readout under strain up to 300 per cent, which is more than a joint asks for. The 300 per cent figure is also a design margin rather than a use case, since skin on a knee stretches by less than half of that in ordinary movement, and the margin is what allows a device to survive a fall.

Xu and colleagues combined three materials. Liquid metal provides a conductor that deforms without cracking, carbon nanotube-polyurethane composites give a stretchable electrode with a large surface area, and soft functional coatings carry the chemistry that recognizes the target molecule. Each component solves one failure mode, and the stack is what makes the combination work. Strain is not the only mechanical load a body applies: compression, torsion and repeated impact all arrive at once, and a patch tested only in tension has been tested on the easiest axis.

Strain tolerance is the number that matters: 300 per cent with a stable signal. Most stretchable electrodes lose contact or change resistance as they elongate, which corrupts a current measurement. Keeping the readout stable means the sensing chemistry, not just the wiring, tolerates deformation.

The device was built in two configurations, wearable and implantable, and tested in animals and in humans. That progression matters because an implant faces a different problem from a patch: biofouling, immune reaction and the absence of a sweat layer to condition the electrode.

Applications are the obvious ones. Continuous monitoring of metabolites in people who are moving rather than lying still, and implants that report on a healing wound or a graft without a wire through the skin. The chemistry can be swapped for different targets, which makes the platform claim plausible rather than specific to one analyte. That is also the route by which a laboratory demonstration becomes a product, because the chemistry has to survive sterilization and shelf storage before a clinic will stock it.

Lifetime and calibration are the open questions, and neither is answered by a laboratory stretch test. A stretchable composite that is cycled thousands of times will fatigue somewhere, and an implantable sensor that cannot be recalibrated in place has to hold its calibration for months. The paper establishes that the mechanical problem is solvable. Whether the chemical one is depends on how the coating behaves after a year in tissue, and that is the experiment nobody has reported yet. Regulatory pathways for an implantable sensor are slower than for a patch, and the trial that would license one has to show that the reading tracks a laboratory measurement over months rather than weeks.
