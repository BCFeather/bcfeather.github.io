Cancer care is organized around the tumour, and the distress that accompanies the diagnosis is managed, when it is managed at all, by a liaison service that sees a fraction of patients. The scale of that gap has been estimated in single cancers and rarely across the whole range of adult disease. Distress is common enough that guidelines recommend screening, and screening without a service to refer to is a formality that changes nothing.

A cohort of 459,542 people with cancer in Hong Kong linked psychiatric diagnoses, self-harm episodes and causes of death, and estimated the cumulative burden of mental illness by cancer type and by treatment exposure. The paper appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 28 March 2022. Depression was defined by a recorded diagnosis, and the true prevalence in this population is certainly higher than the coded rate.

Depression was the most common psychiatric diagnosis across the cohort. Patients who received chemotherapy, radiotherapy and surgery together carried the highest cumulative burden, and among drug classes the alkylating agents were associated with the most psychiatric diagnoses while kinase inhibitors were associated with the least. The burden accumulates with each modality because each brings its own symptoms, its own disruption of work and its own uncertainty about the future.

The treatment differences are not necessarily causal. Alkylating agents are given for cancers with poorer prognosis and greater symptom burden, and kinase inhibitors are given for cancers that are often managed as chronic disease, so the drug is a marker for the clinical situation as much as a cause of it. A patient receiving all three modalities has had surgery, weeks of radiotherapy and cycles of chemotherapy, each of which imposes its own disruption on sleep, appetite and family life.

Self-harm carried the clearest signal. Every category of mental illness raised the risk of subsequent self-harm, the risk was highest within twelve months of the diagnosis of the mental illness, and people who harmed themselves were 6.8 times more likely to die of unnatural causes within a year, an interval from 4.3 to 10.7. The design cannot distinguish a psychiatric illness caused by cancer from one that would have occurred anyway, and the comparison with a matched non-cancer cohort goes some way towards that.

That hazard falls to 2.0 after the first year, which suggests that the period around a psychiatric diagnosis is the window in which intervention would do the most good. It also suggests that the current model, in which a patient is referred after a crisis, is timed badly.

The study uses recorded diagnoses, so patients who were depressed but never seen by a psychiatrist are counted as unaffected, and the burden is therefore an underestimate. It is an underestimate of a problem that is already large enough to justify a routine screening programme. A collaborative care model, in which a psychiatrist sits in the oncology clinic, has been tested in other settings and is the model that this evidence supports.
