Japanese core consumer prices rose 2.9 per cent in the year to October, the statistics bureau said on 17 November, down from 3.3 per cent in September and below the forecast of 3. The figure was the lowest since the spring of 2022, and the measure has been above the target for nineteen months. The figure was the first below 3 per cent since the summer of 2022, and the drop was about 0.4 points larger than the forecast.

A fuel subsidy did the work. The government has been paying part of the household fuel bill since the start of the year, and the effect on the index has grown as the programme expanded. A subsidy that is paid is an index that is cut. The subsidy costs about 1 trillion yen a year, and the government has said it will extend it into the spring.

Underlying pressure stayed high. The measure that excludes the fresh food and the energy rose by about 4 per cent, the fastest in decades, and the services were the reason as the wages rose. A service that is priced up is a wage that has arrived. The core-core has been above 4 per cent for four months, and the reading is the one the bank watches for the domestic pressure. The prices of the services rose by about 2.7 per cent, and the restaurants were the strongest as the labour shortage pushed the wages up.

Food is the household's problem. The rice prices rose by about 30 per cent after the poor harvest, and the vegetables have been volatile through the year. A bowl of rice that costs more is an inflation that the shopper cannot avoid. The price of the imported wheat has fallen from the peak, and the government has raised the price it charges the mills by about 10 per cent. The price of the bread rose by about 9 per cent, and the one of the eggs by about 15, which the households see every week.

Its governor waits for wage data. He has said the spring talks will be the test, and the unions have been asking for about 5 per cent after the settlements this year. A claim that is high is a policy that must change. The bank has said the exit will be gradual, and the economists expect the first move in the spring at the earliest.

What the month shows is that the headline has fallen below 3 per cent for the first time in more than a year and the underlying is still rising, and the gap is the argument inside the bank. A core at 2.9 per cent with a core-core at 4 is the puzzle the bank must explain, and the explanation is the wage data. The bank's next meeting is in December, and the economists expect no change before the spring talks.
