A large station runs all night in the travel season, and the departure board in the small hours is the part of the timetable that the daytime passenger never sees. The overnight trains are the ones that carry the passengers who could not get a day ticket, and the hall at two in the morning holds the people who arrived early for a train at five and the people whose train was delayed.

The work that keeps the hall open is done by several groups who rarely appear in the same conversation. The cleaners run the floor in shifts, the porters handle the luggage that a passenger cannot lift, the dispatchers in the control room decide which platform a train will use, and the police walk the concourse in pairs. The four groups are employed by four different organizations, and the coordination between them is a set of procedures that the station rehearses.

The cleaning is the part that the passenger notices only when it fails. A hall that handles a hundred thousand people in a day produces an amount of rubbish that is measured in tonnes, and the floor has to be washed between the arrivals and the departures rather than after the last train. The night shift is when the deep cleaning happens, and the machines that polish the floor work in the hours when the crowd is thin.

The overnight train itself has a staff that the daylight passenger does not think about. A sleeper carries a conductor for each carriage, a dining car with a cook and a server, and a driver and an engineer in the front. The train that arrives at six in the morning has been staffed since eight the previous evening, and the crew's shift is the reason the timetable has the shape it has.

What the night does to the people who work it is the part that the labour statistics record. The body's clock is set by light, and a shift that begins at ten in the evening and ends at six in the morning is a shift that the body treats as a permanent jet lag. The workers who do it for years accumulate a set of health risks that the occupational medicine literature has documented, and the rotating roster is the mitigation that the employers use.

The station also has a social function at night that the daytime does not. The hall is warm, it is lit, and it is open, and in a January night it holds the people who have nowhere better to be. The police who walk the concourse know the regulars, the cleaners know which bench is occupied, and the station becomes a shelter for a few hours without ever describing itself as one.

What the night shift shows is that a transport system is a labour system. The train that a passenger takes at dawn is the product of a hundred people who worked while the passenger slept, and the timetable is a promise that is kept by people whose names are not on it. The hall is clean at six because somebody cleaned it at three.
