A bookshop in a town of 6,000 people cannot sell enough books to pay a rent, and the ones that survive sell something else as well. A shop that has traded for 40 years published its accounts this month, and the numbers describe a business that became three businesses.

Book sales are 46 per cent of revenue and 12 per cent of profit. The cafe is 30 per cent of revenue and 40 of profit, and the printing service, which produces wedding invitations and parish newsletters, is 24 per cent of revenue and 48 of profit.

The printing service's margin is the highest because it sells a service rather than a product. A customer who needs 200 invitations pays for design, paper and time, and the shop's only competitor is an online service with a two-week turnaround. Mail order is the fourth arm and the smallest. The shop's online sales are 4 per cent of revenue, and the shop's owner describes them as a service to former residents rather than as a growth channel. The employment effect is the argument the town makes. The shop employs nine people, four of them part-time, and it is the only retail employer within 12 kilometres. A closure would remove those jobs and the town's only place to sit indoors.

A chain's economics are the contrast that matters. A chain bookshop's model is volume and central purchasing, and a small shop cannot buy at the chain's price. The small shop's advantage is that it sells a service that a chain does not offer. Succession is the risk. The owner is 68 and no family member wants the business, and the shop's value without the building is 40,000 dollars of stock and goodwill. A community trust has begun raising money to buy it. A small town's bookshop earns from services rather than from books. Printing, ordering and events have margins of 30 to 50 per cent, and a book's margin is 12 after a wholesaler's discount. The shop's survival depends on the mix and not on the shelf.

Rent is the town's hidden subsidy. A shop paying a market rent in a town of 6,000 would need twice the turnover, and the shop's building is owned by a trust that charges a nominal rent. That is the transfer that keeps the shop open and is invisible in its accounts.

A town's bookshop is a service as much as a shop. A shop that orders a title overnight for a customer is performing a function that a website performs worse, and a town without one loses a place where a request is answered by a person who knows the town.

A shop that is a town's infrastructure has a different job from a shop that is a business. The books are the reason it exists and not the reason it survives, and the survival is what keeps the books on the shelf.
