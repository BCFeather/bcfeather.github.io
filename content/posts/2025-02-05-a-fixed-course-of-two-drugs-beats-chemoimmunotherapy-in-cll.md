Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia was treated with chemotherapy and an antibody for twenty years, and the regimen worked well enough that patients could expect years of remission. The arrival of targeted drugs changed the arithmetic: a Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor taken indefinitely controls the disease in most patients, and a BCL-2 inhibitor given for a fixed year can produce remissions deep enough that treatment can stop.

Combining the two classes is the obvious next step, and the question has been how long to treat and whether a third drug adds anything. AMPLIFY, reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 5 February, randomized previously untreated patients to acalabrutinib plus venetoclax, to the same pair with obinutuzumab added, or to chemoimmunotherapy, and it measured progression-free survival as the primary endpoint in a population large enough to detect a difference of the size the investigators expected.

At a median follow-up of 40.8 months, the estimated 36-month progression-free survival was 76.5 per cent with the doublet, 83.1 per cent with the triplet and 66.5 per cent with chemoimmunotherapy. The hazard ratio for progression or death with the doublet against chemoimmunotherapy was 0.65, and the triplet did better still, which means the antibody earns its place in the regimen.

The design is the part that matters to patients. A fixed course has a beginning and an end, and a patient who finishes it can stop attending for infusions and can stop paying for a drug every month. A treatment that continues until progression is a treatment that a health system pays for indefinitely, and the difference in cost between the two models is larger than the difference in efficacy, which is why payers have pushed for fixed courses in every disease where a stop is possible.

Toxicity followed the class. Acalabrutinib causes headache, bruising and atrial fibrillation, venetoclax causes neutropenia and requires a ramp-up to prevent tumour lysis, and obinutuzumab adds infusion reactions. The rates in the trial were the ones the labels describe, and the discontinuation rate was low enough that most patients completed the planned course, which is the test that a regimen of this length has to pass.

What the trial does not answer is which patients should receive the triplet. The absolute gain over the doublet was a few percentage points at three years, and a few points is a lot in a disease where the median patient is over seventy and where the third drug adds visits and cost. A biomarker that identified the patients who need the antibody would be worth more than the trial's overall result.

The field has moved since the trial began. Second-generation BTK inhibitors with less atrial fibrillation, and non-covalent inhibitors for patients who relapse on the first, are in use, and the combination tested here will be one option among several. The trial's contribution is the demonstration that a fixed course can beat chemoimmunotherapy by a margin that a guideline cannot ignore.
