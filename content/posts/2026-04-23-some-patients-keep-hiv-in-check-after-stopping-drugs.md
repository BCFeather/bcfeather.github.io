Antiretroviral therapy suppresses HIV but does not remove it, and the reason is a reservoir of cells carrying integrated provirus that the drugs do not touch. Stop the drugs and the virus returns within weeks in almost everyone, which is why therapy is lifelong and why a cure has been so hard to demonstrate.

A small group of people do not relapse. Posttreatment controllers stop therapy and keep the virus suppressed, sometimes for years, and they differ from elite controllers, who suppress the virus without ever starting treatment. What distinguishes the two groups is a question that a study in *Science Translational Medicine*, reported in *Science* on 23 April, sets out to answer.

Working with cohorts of both groups, Crain and colleagues compared immune responses and reservoir composition. In the posttreatment controllers, HIV-specific CD8 positive T cells expanded robustly and acquired a stemlike phenotype, which is the state that allows a T cell population to renew itself and to keep producing effectors over a long period, and which is the property that vaccines and cell therapies have tried and mostly failed to induce.

Reservoir composition differed as well. Posttreatment controllers harboured a less diverse viral reservoir than noncontrollers, which means that fewer distinct proviral sequences were present and that the surviving virus was a narrower population. A narrow reservoir is easier for an immune response to hold down than a broad one.

Epitope targeting was the third difference. The CD8 cells in posttreatment controllers recognized a different set of viral peptides from those recognized by elite controllers, which suggests that the two routes to control are not the same route taken at different speeds but two distinct immunological strategies.

What the comparison implies for cure research is that CD8 immunity is the lever. Strategies that expand and preserve stemlike HIV-specific CD8 populations, and that reduce reservoir diversity, are the strategies that a functional cure would combine, and the study gives the field two measurements to optimize. Limitations are those of any small observational cohort. Posttreatment controllers are rare, the groups are small, and the timing of sampling relative to the stop of therapy varies between participants. A study of this kind identifies correlates and cannot establish causation, and the correlates it identifies are plausible and unproven.

Practical value lies in trial design. A cure trial needs an endpoint, and viral load after interruption is a crude one that exposes participants to risk. If stemlike CD8 expansion and reservoir diversity predict who will control the virus, they become intermediate endpoints that can be measured on therapy. What the paper describes is a state that the immune system can reach. It does not describe how to induce that state in someone who has never reached it, and the distance between describing a state and inducing one is the distance that the next decade of cure research will be spent crossing, one correlate at a time.
