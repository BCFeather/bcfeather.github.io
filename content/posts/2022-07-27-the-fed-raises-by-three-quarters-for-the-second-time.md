The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate by three quarters of a percentage point on Wednesday, and the range is now two and a quarter to two and a half per cent. It is the second increase of that size in a row and the fourth of the year. The statement said that the committee is strongly committed to returning inflation to its target, and the chairman said in his press conference that the next meeting could produce another unusually large increase. The markets had expected the size of the move and reacted to the language.

Every member of the committee voted for the increase. The committee said that the indicators of spending and production had softened, and that the labour market remained tight. The officials have been raising at a pace that has not been seen since the 1990s, and the rate is now above the level that they consider neutral. The balance sheet is also being reduced, which adds to the tightening, and the sales of the securities will accelerate in September.

Asked whether the economy is in recession, the chairman said it does not meet the definition while the labour market stays this strong. He acknowledged that the path to a soft landing has narrowed, and he said that the committee would rather risk a slowdown than let inflation become entrenched. He also said that the decisions will be taken meeting by meeting and that the guidance will be less specific than it was in the spring.

Markets took the statement as a sign that the pace will slow. The yields on the two-year Treasury fell and the shares rose, and the dollar weakened. The traders now expect an increase of half a point in September rather than three quarters, and they expect the rate to peak at about three and a half per cent next year. The officials have said that they will keep raising until the inflation figures come down, and the next report is due in two weeks, with the annual meeting at Jackson Hole in between.

Outside the United States the effects are already visible. The dollar has risen against most currencies and the countries that borrow in it are paying more for their debt. The central banks in Europe and in Asia have been raising rates as well, and the ones that have not are seeing their currencies fall. The emerging markets that import food and fuel have had to spend their reserves, and several of them have asked the fund for support.

An era of near-zero rates has closed. The rate that was near zero for two years is now above two per cent, and the mortgages and the loans that were priced off it will reprice. The committee has said that it will not pivot until the inflation comes down, and it has also said that it cannot promise a soft landing. The autumn will show whether the tightening has been enough, and the answer will come from the data rather than from the statements.
