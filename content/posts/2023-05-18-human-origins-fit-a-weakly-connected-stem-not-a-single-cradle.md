title: "Human origins fit a weakly connected stem, not a single cradle"
date: "2023-05-18T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "Genomes from living African populations are better explained by a network of loosely connected ancestral groups than by one founding population, according to a model published in *Nature* on 17 May 2023."
---

The textbook account gives Homo sapiens a single origin in one region of Africa, followed by a migration that filled the continent and then the world. The account's simplicity is its weakness: the genomes of living Africans do not look like the product of one founding population.

The model in *Nature*, 17 May 2023 replaces the cradle with a network. The authors fit a structure in which several ancestral populations exchanged migrants over tens of thousands of years, and the fit reproduces the genetic diversity of African populations better than a single-origin model.

The method is a demographic inference. The authors simulate genealogies under different structures, compute the genetic patterns each would produce, and compare them with data from living populations. The structure that matches is a weakly connected stem.

The weakness of the connections is the point. A strongly connected network would homogenize the populations, and a disconnected one would produce divergent lineages. A weak connection reproduces both the shared ancestry and the differences.

The date is part of the result. The earliest split that the model supports falls between roughly 120,000 and 135,000 years ago, which places the divergence within the range of the fossil record.

The fossil evidence is the other constraint. Fossils of early Homo sapiens appear across the continent rather than in one corner, and a model with several populations is consistent with that spread.

The migration's pattern changes. A single origin implies one exit from Africa, and a stem implies that the populations that left carried a mixture. The difference matters for the interpretation of ancient genomes outside Africa.

The criticism is the model's abstraction. A simulation is a simplification, and a structure that fits the data is not a proof of the past. The authors' comparison of competing structures is what makes the exercise informative.

What the paper cannot resolve is the populations that left no descendants. A structure fitted to living people cannot see a lineage that vanished, and the African fossil record's gaps are where such a lineage would hide.

For palaeoanthropology, the value is a shift in the question. The question has been where humanity began, and the paper's answer is that the beginning was a process distributed across a continent.

The model's fit is its argument. A single origin fails to reproduce the diversity of ancient genomes, and a weakly structured stem reproduces it without positing a migration that the fossil record does not support.

The date matters for archaeology. A split around 120,000 years ago is consistent with fossils of that age, and a model that fits both the genes and the stones is stronger than one that fits only the genes.
The computational cost is a practical limitation. A structured stem has many parameters, and a fit with many parameters can accommodate several histories. The authors' comparison of structures with matched flexibility is what keeps the exercise honest.
The medical use is unexpected. The authors developed the inference method to study how genetic disease risk varies between populations, and the method's application to origins is a by-product that turned out to be the paper's headline.
