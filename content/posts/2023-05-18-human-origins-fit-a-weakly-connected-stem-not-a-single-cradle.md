title: "Human origins fit a weakly connected stem, not a single cradle"
date: "2023-05-18T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "Genomes from living African populations are better explained by a network of loosely connected ancestral groups than by one founding population."
---

The textbook account gives Homo sapiens a single origin in one region of Africa, followed by a migration that filled the continent and then the world. The account's simplicity is its weakness, since the genomes of living Africans do not look like the product of one founding population.

In *Nature* on 17 May 2023, the cradle gives way to a network. The authors fit a structure in which several ancestral populations exchanged migrants over tens of thousands of years, and the fit reproduces the genetic diversity of African populations better than a single-origin model.

Demographic inference is the method. The authors simulate genealogies under different structures, compute the genetic patterns each would produce, and compare them with data from living populations. The structure that matches is a weakly connected stem.

Weakness of the connections is the point. A strongly connected network would homogenise the populations and a disconnected one would produce divergent lineages, so a weak connection reproduces both the shared ancestry and the differences. Dates form part of the result, because the earliest split the model supports falls between roughly 120,000 and 135,000 years ago, which places the divergence inside the range of the fossil record.

Fossils supply the other constraint. Early Homo sapiens fossils appear across the continent rather than in one corner, and a model with several populations is consistent with that spread. Migration's pattern changes with the model, since a single origin implies one exit from Africa while a stem implies that the populations leaving carried a mixture, and that difference matters for interpreting ancient genomes outside Africa.

Abstraction invites the criticism. A simulation is a simplification, and a structure fitting the data is not a proof of the past, so the comparison of competing structures is what makes the exercise informative. Computational flexibility is the practical worry, since a structured stem has many parameters and a fit with many parameters can accommodate several histories, which the matched comparison is designed to police. Descendant populations constrain nothing here, as a structure fitted to living people cannot see a lineage that vanished, and the gaps in the African fossil record are where such a lineage would hide. Medical genetics supplies an unexpected by-product, because the inference method was developed to study how disease risk varies between populations, and its application to origins became the headline.

Palaeoanthropology gains a shift in the question: the question has been where humanity began, and the answer here is that the beginning was a process distributed across a continent. A split around 120,000 years ago is consistent with fossils of that age, and a model fitting both genes and stones is stronger than one fitting only genes. A structured stem also predicts how much of a genome comes from each ancestral group, and that prediction is the kind of number a fossil genome from outside Africa can test directly. Whether such a stem produced the humans who left Africa is the next test, and it needs genomes from the departure window rather than from the present.
