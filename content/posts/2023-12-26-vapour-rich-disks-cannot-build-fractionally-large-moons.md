title: "Vapour-rich disks cannot build fractionally large moons"
date: "2023-12-26T09:20:00+08:00"

description: "Impact simulations show that planets larger than about 1.6 Earth radii produce fully vapour disks, in which growing moonlets are dragged inward, so large exomoons should be rare around big rocky worlds."
---

Earth's Moon is unusually large for its planet, and the standard account of its origin is a giant impact that left a disk of debris from which the Moon accreted. Impacts are common while planets are forming, so moons built in the same way should be common too, yet no exomoon has been confirmed around a transiting planet.

A preprint posted on 22 December 2023 proposes a reason. A disk that is initially rich in vapour cannot make a large moon, because the growing moonlets, the building blocks of a moon, experience strong gas drag inside the vapour and lose orbital energy fast enough to fall back towards the planet before they can accumulate into a body.

The argument rests on impact simulations. Terrestrial and icy planets larger than roughly 1.3 to 1.6 Earth radii produce disks that are entirely vapour, because the impact energy is enough to vaporize the material that stays bound, and a fully vapour disk is the case in which drag dominates every other process that acts on a small body.

A threshold in planetary radius is the practical consequence of the argument. Below it, a disk keeps enough condensed material for moonlets to grow without being braked, and above it the disk is vapour and the moonlets are lost, since the braking force scales with the vapour fraction and not with the mass of solids. Earth sits below the threshold, which is why the Moon exists, and a super-Earth sits above it, which is why its moons would not.

The result supports the family of Moon-formation models that start from vapour-poor disks. Those models had been favoured for other reasons, chiefly that a vapour-rich disk is hard to reconcile with the Moon's depletion in volatiles, and the drag argument supplies a physical mechanism that selects them, leaving less of the choice to a fit of the Moon's composition.

It also supplies a target for observers. Rocky and icy exoplanets with radii below about 1.6 Earth radii, which are the most common planets known, are better candidates for hosting fractionally large exomoons, and the argument predicts that the largest moons should orbit the smallest rocky planets. A survey that concentrates on bigger worlds looks where the formation barrier is strongest, not where a moon would be easiest to see.

Limits are those of the simulation. The impact parameters are sampled rather than exhaustive, the pre-impact spin of the planet is not varied, the disk evolution is treated in a simplified way, and the radius threshold depends on composition and on the impact angle, so the numbers are order-of-magnitude boundaries and not sharp cutoffs. For the field, the paper converts a null result into a prediction. The absence of confirmed exomoons around large planets becomes consistent with a formation barrier rather than with a shortage of suitable host stars, and the test is a search around small planets, where the barrier is absent and the moons, if they form, should be detectable as transit timing variations.
