Age is the parameter that demography needs and that most organisms cannot supply, and a clonal species is one where the individual can be dated another way. A paper in Nature Ecology & Evolution on 10 June 2024 proposes a somatic genetic clock. Clock is the metaphor, and the metaphor describes a measurement rather than a mechanism. A mutation rate measured in a laboratory is not the rate in the sea, and the sea is where the clock is meant to run. A clock calibrated in one species is a clock with one calibration, and calibration is what a new user has to repeat. A species-specific calibration is a cost that early adopters absorb, and absorbing it is how a method becomes a standard.

Somatic mutations accumulate with each division, and accumulation is a record of how many divisions an individual has been through. Divisions are what a clock counts, and counting them is what an age is. The record is in the genome, and the genome is what makes the clock somatic. Running in a natural environment introduces repair and selection, and selection removes mutations that a clock counts. Repeating it is the price of the method, and price is what determines how widely it is adopted.

Clonal species grow by division without sexual reproduction, and an individual in such a species can be enormous and ancient. Ancient individuals are where age matters most, since a clone's age determines its role in a community. Community role is what a demographer would want to reconstruct. Removal is a bias that a clock has to correct, and correction is the calibration the paper discusses.

Rates of mutation set the resolution, and resolution is what a clock can resolve. A clock that resolves decades is useful for a clone that lives centuries, and useful is the standard. Calibration is the step that turns a rate into an age, and calibration requires a known-age sample. Discussing it is what a method paper owes a user who will apply the clock to a new species.

Requiring a sample is the practical limitation, and limitation is what a method paper should state. State the limitation and the method becomes a tool, since a tool with a stated scope is one a user can apply. Scope is what the paper defines, and defining it is the work of a careful author.

Careful authors are what a field needs when a method crosses disciplines, and crossing is what this paper does. Discipline boundaries are where jargon becomes a barrier, and a barrier is what a clear scope statement removes. Removing barriers is a form of contribution that citations rarely reward.

Reward aside, the clock is a demographically useful instrument, and an instrument is what the field has lacked for clonal organisms. Organisms of that kind include corals and seagrasses, and both are conservation priorities. Priorities shape funding, and funding shapes what methods get developed.
