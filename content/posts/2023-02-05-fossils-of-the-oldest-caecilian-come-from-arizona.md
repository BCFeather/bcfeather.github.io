Caecilians are amphibians that look like earthworms, live underground in the tropics, and have almost no fossil record. Their origin has been a gap in the tetrapod tree, and the gap has been filled by inference rather than by specimens.

The fossils in *Nature*, 23 February 2023 are the inference made concrete. Ben Kligman and colleagues identified 76 specimens of a new species, Funcusvermis gilmorei, in the Upper Triassic Chinle Formation of Petrified Forest National Park in Arizona, and the age is about 220 million years.

The material is fragmentary, which is what caecilian fossils tend to be. The identification rests on jaw elements and vertebrae with features that are diagnostic of the group, and the assemblage of many individuals at one site is itself informative: a burrowing animal that died in numbers suggests a floodplain with soil.

The species is a mosaic. It carries features of the living caecilians and features of the dissorophoid temnospondyls, an extinct group of four-legged amphibians, and it lacks some features that modern caecilians have. That combination places it near the branch point rather than at the end of it.

The implication is about the origin of limblessness. A caecilian-like animal in the Late Triassic means that the body plan evolved early, and the later history of the group is a history of specialization within a shape that was already established.

The geographic surprise is the location. Arizona is not a tropical rainforest today, and the Triassic Chinle was a river system in a monsoonal climate. The fossil shows that the group's early range included what is now North America, which is consistent with a Pangaean distribution and not with an origin in Gondwana alone.

The reconstruction of the animal's life is the speculative part. A burrowing amphibian with a solid skull and reduced eyes is an animal that lived in soil, and the Chinle's palaeosols are the environment the fossils are found in, which supports the interpretation without proving it.

What the paper does not provide is a complete skeleton. Without one, the posture, the limb remnants and the feeding apparatus are reconstructed from fragments, and a second locality with better preservation would change the picture.

For the amphibian tree, the find shortens the ghost lineage. A group with a 220-million-year-old member needs less time unrecorded, and the molecular estimates that placed the origin earlier can now be tested against a fossil that exists.

The specimens were recovered by sieving sediment rather than by quarrying skeletons. Microfossils of this kind accumulate in the concentrates that wash through a screen, and the assemblage of dozens of individuals came from a single horizon, which suggests a mass mortality or a accumulation in a pond.
The jaw's structure is the diagnostic feature. Caecilian jaws have a unique arrangement of bones and a reduced dentition, and the Chinle specimens show the arrangement in a form that is simpler than the living one, which is what a stem-group member should look like.
