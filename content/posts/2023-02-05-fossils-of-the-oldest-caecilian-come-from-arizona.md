title: "Fossils of the oldest caecilian come from Arizona"

description: "Seventy-six fossils from the Triassic Chinle Formation belong to the oldest known caecilian, which pushes the origin of the limbless amphibians back by tens of millions of years."
---

Caecilians are amphibians that look like earthworms, live underground in the tropics, and have almost no fossil record. Their origin has been a gap in the tetrapod tree, filled by inference rather than by specimens.

In *Nature* on 23 February 2023, the inference becomes concrete. Ben Kligman and colleagues identify 76 specimens of a new species, *Funcusvermis gilmorei*, in the Upper Triassic Chinle Formation of Petrified Forest National Park in Arizona, and the age is about 220 million years.

Fragmentary is what caecilian fossils tend to be, and the identification rests on jaw elements and vertebrae with features diagnostic of the group; caecilian jaws carry a unique arrangement of bones and a reduced dentition, and these specimens show a simpler version of it, which is what a stem-group member should look like. The assemblage carries information in its own right, since dozens of individuals in one horizon suggest a floodplain with soil, and perhaps a mass mortality or an accumulation in a pond.

Because the species carries features of the living caecilians and features of the dissorophoid temnospondyls while lacking others that modern caecilians have, the combination places it near the branch point rather than at the end of it. What follows for limblessness is that a caecilian-like animal in the Late Triassic means the body plan evolved early, and the group's later history is specialization inside a shape that was already established.

Geography supplies the surprise. Arizona is not a tropical rainforest today, and the Chinle was a river system in a monsoonal climate, so the animal's early range included what is now North America, which fits a Pangaean distribution rather than an origin in Gondwana alone. A reconstruction of its life stays speculative: a burrowing amphibian with a solid skull and reduced eyes lived in soil, and the palaeosols the fossils come from support that reading without proving it.

A complete skeleton is what the paper cannot provide, so posture, limb remnants and feeding apparatus are rebuilt from fragments, and a second locality with better preservation would change the picture. For the amphibian tree the find shortens a ghost lineage, because a group with a 220-million-year-old member needs less time unrecorded, and molecular estimates that place the origin earlier can now be tested against a fossil that exists. Recovery method explains the assemblage as well, since these specimens came from sieving sediment rather than quarrying skeletons, and microfossils of this kind accumulate in what washes through a screen. The name marks the occasion, combining a digging worm with the Chinle's fossiliferous beds and honouring the palaeontologist who worked them a century ago.
