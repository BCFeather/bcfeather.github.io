Munich opened the European championship this evening. Twenty-four teams qualified, drawn into six groups of four. Fifty-one matches will be played in ten German cities over a month. That match finished five-one. The format has grown since the tournament was sixteen teams. A group stage of three matches per team, then a round of sixteen. Expansion was designed to bring more countries into the competition, and it has also brought more matches of low consequence. A team can lose two games and still advance.

Host cities are spread across the country. Munich, Berlin, Dortmund, Gelsenkirchen, Frankfurt, Hamburg, Stuttgart, Düsseldorf, Cologne, Leipzig. Each has a stadium of at least forty thousand seats. Travel between them is by train, and the railway has promised extra services that it will struggle to provide. The tickets were sold in phases through a portal. Cheapest group-stage seat cost about thirty euros, the final several hundred. A fan who bought in the first phase paid less than a fan who bought in the last one, and the resale market did the rest.

Fan zones are the part that costs the cities money. A square closed for a month, a screen, a stage, a set of toilets, a security contract. Munich expects a million visitors in the zone over the tournament. Cities earn it back in beer and hotel tax, or they do not. The squads are young this year. Several of the favourites brought players who are twenty-one. A tournament played at the end of a long club season rewards a squad with fresh legs. Coaches have noticed, which is why several squads carry six substitutes who can run.

Football itself has become more controlled. A team that wins the ball high up the pitch scores within eight seconds or gives it back. The statistics that the broadcasters show have changed to match. The number of passes in the final third is now printed beside the score. The broadcast is the largest part of the tournament's economy. Rights were sold to a handful of networks for a sum that dwarfs the ticket revenue. A match kicks off at nine in the evening so that it lands in prime time across the continent. A viewer in Asia watches at three in the morning, and pays for the privilege through a subscription.

Security bills are carried by the cities. A match day brings eighty thousand people to a stadium and twice that to the surrounding streets. Police draft officers from other regions and close the approach roads four hours before kick-off. Bags are searched at the gates by a contractor. Cost is counted afterwards, in a report that nobody reads. A city that hosts four matches spends more on policing than it earns on the tickets it was allotted.

A tone was set by the first match that the tournament will struggle to repeat. The host scored in the first ten minutes, the crowd sang for an hour, and the opposition was beaten by four goals. Tomorrow afternoon brings the second match.
