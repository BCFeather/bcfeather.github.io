A journal issued a retraction notice on 24 April for a 2023 paper that reported improved editing efficiency in a human cell line. The retraction is unremarkable in itself: an image had been duplicated, and the authors agreed that the figure could not be corrected. What is remarkable is the trail the claim left behind.

Bibliometric analysis published alongside the notice found 341 citations of the original paper between its publication and March this year. Of those, 12 cited the retraction or noted a concern. The rest cited the finding as established. Several reviews, including two in high-impact journals, used it to support a general statement about editing efficiency.

The mechanism of this failure is known and boring. Citations are copied from other papers' reference lists, and the paper that is cited is the one that was read three years ago, not the one whose status changed last week. Reference managers do not query retraction databases; publishers do not push updates to citing authors; and the citation is a social act performed under time pressure.

Detection tools exist and are weakly adopted. CrossCheck and similar services flag text overlap at submission; retraction watch databases are maintained by volunteers and queryable by API; and at least three publishers now run automated checks against retraction registries at proof stage. Coverage is partial, and no major citation index re-labels references in previously published articles.

Consequences are asymmetric. A retracted claim that supports a method spreads into protocols and is expensive to remove; a retracted claim that supports a background sentence costs a correction paragraph. The gene-editing result fell into the first category, and two labs have reportedly built optimization pipelines on the reported parameters.

What would help, according to the authors of the analysis, is a status flag carried by the digital object identifier rather than by the journal page. A DOI that resolves to a retraction notice, and a reference list that displays it, would catch most of the problem at the point of reading. The infrastructure is technically simple and institutionally difficult, because it requires citation databases to display information that publishers would rather not surface.

Publisher-side tooling has improved unevenly. Three large publishers now run automated checks against retraction registries at proof stage, which catches new citations of old retractions in their own journals. Smaller publishers and most society journals have no such step, and preprint servers, where much of the citation traffic now begins, do not attach status to references at all.

The economics explain the gap. A retraction check is cheap per article and expensive to build, and the benefit accrues to the literature rather than to the publisher that pays for it. Journals that have built the check describe it as reputation management rather than as a service to readers, which is an honest description of the incentive.

For an author, the practical habit is a search of the paper's title against a retraction database before citing a specific figure. That takes a minute and is the only intervention that reaches the point where the error propagates, which is the reference list of the next paper.

The narrow lesson for a reader of the literature is procedural. Check the status of any paper you rely on for a specific number, especially one older than two years and cited second-hand. The broader lesson is that correction is a distribution problem: the correction exists, and the claim travels faster than the correction can follow.
