The shipping alliances have published their winter schedules, and the routings tell a story that the press releases do not. Instead of returning to the Suez Canal for the Asia-Europe services, the two largest alliances have kept a substantial part of their capacity on the Cape of Good Hope route, which adds roughly a week to the voyage and a corresponding amount of fuel. A diversion that began as a temporary response to Red Sea risk has now been written into the timetable.

The arithmetic of diversion is simple and unforgiving. A ship that spends an extra week at sea earns nothing for that week, which means the effective capacity of the fleet falls by roughly the proportion the voyage lengthens. To move the same volume, a carrier needs more ships or faster ships, and neither is available quickly. The result is a market where capacity is structurally tighter than the order book suggests, and where freight rates on the affected routes have stayed higher than the pre-diversion trend.

The fuel bill is the second-order effect that matters more than it looks. Sailing round the Cape at a speed that keeps the schedule means burning more per tonne carried, and the carriers have been passing the cost on through bunker adjustment factors that shippers now treat as a permanent line item. The environmental consequence is awkward: an industry that has committed to cutting its emissions per container is currently emitting more per container on its main east-west route, and the emissions accounting rules do not have a good way to represent a security diversion.

Shippers have adapted in the way they always do, by routing around the problem. Some have shifted volume to the Mediterranean, where the diversion penalty is smaller, and some have moved to rail across Eurasia for time-sensitive cargo, which is expensive and limited in capacity. The bulk of the volume has simply absorbed the longer transit and adjusted its inventory planning, which shows up in higher working capital and in the buffer stocks that retailers have rebuilt since the pandemic.

The strategic question is how long the carriers expect this to last. Building a schedule is a commitment measured in months, and the alliances have now committed twice. If the Red Sea route becomes usable again, the capacity that has been absorbed by the longer voyage will be released, and rates will fall faster than the alliances would like. If it does not, the industry will have to order ships for a route it did not plan for, which is a multi-year decision about a temporary-looking problem.

For the shipper, the practical lesson is that the cost of the detour is now embedded in contract terms rather than floating in a risk premium, and that negotiating a rate means arguing about a baseline that includes a war. That is an uncomfortable position, and it is the ordinary one for a trade route that runs through water somebody else controls.
