Governments of Ireland, Norway and Spain announced on the twenty-second of May that they would recognize Palestine as a state, with the formal step taking effect on the twenty-eighth. Irish prime minister said the recognition was a signal of support for a two-state solution rather than a reward for any party to the conflict, and his Norwegian counterpart said the move was consistent with Norway's position since the Oslo accords, which his country helped to negotiate in 1993.

Spanish position was presented as a decision taken in coordination with the other two. Prime minister said Spain had waited for a moment when recognition could contribute to peace, and he argued that the war in Gaza had made the step necessary. All three governments said they would recognize the state within the 1967 borders with East Jerusalem as its capital, and that recognition did not imply endorsement of Hamas.

Israeli reaction was immediate. Foreign ministry ordered its ambassadors in the three capitals to return for consultations and said it would summon their ambassadors in Tel Aviv for a reprimand. Minister of foreign affairs said the step would encourage extremism and reduce the chance of a negotiated settlement, and the government released footage of the hostages held in Gaza to foreign correspondents in an attempt to shift the terms of the debate.

Practical consequence for Palestinians is diplomatic rather than material. Recognition gives their representatives in those countries a status closer to that of diplomats, and it opens the possibility of bilateral agreements on trade and cultural exchange. It does not change the situation in Gaza and does not produce a state on the ground, and it does not alter the position of the United States, which has said that recognition should come through negotiation.

European politics behind the decision is a widening split. France has signalled that it might follow, and Slovenia recognized Palestine in June, while Germany, the Netherlands and the Czech Republic have said they will not act before a settlement is reached. The European Union has no common position on recognition, and the divergence has become more visible as the war has continued, which is a problem for a union that presents itself as a single actor in foreign policy.

Recognition also obliges the three governments to treat Palestinian representatives as diplomatic counterparts, which creates practical questions about representation in capitals where no embassy exists.

Meaning of the twenty-second of May lies in a number that has been rising slowly for decades. More than 140 of the 193 members of the United Nations now recognize Palestine, including most of the countries of the global south, and the addition of three European states is a change in the balance within Europe as much as in the total. Recognition does not create a state, and the argument that it produces one has always relied on accumulation rather than on any single act. Three more recognitions move that argument forward by a small amount and signal that the war has cost Israel support among governments that had been reluctant to act.
