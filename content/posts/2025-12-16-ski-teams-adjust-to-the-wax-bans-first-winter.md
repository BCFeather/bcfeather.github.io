---

Waxing a ski is a small craft with a large effect, and this winter is the first in which the fluorocarbon compounds that dominated it are banned. Teams have published their preparation notes, and the notes describe a sport whose differences have moved from the material to the technician.

Fluorocarbon wax reduces friction by forming a low-surface-energy layer, and its advantage over a hydrocarbon wax was 1.5 per cent of a race time on wet snow. The replacement's best formulations reach within 0.3 per cent, and the gap depends on the snow's water content.

Structure is the substitute. A ski's base is ground with a stone pattern whose depth and spacing match the snow's grain, and a service team now carries 14 patterns where it carried 5. The right structure is worth more than the old wax's advantage.

Servicemen's variance widened for the same reason. A structure choice is a judgment about snow that a technician makes from a handful of measurements, and the teams' data show a 0.8 per cent spread between two servicemen preparing identical skis. A structure's depth is measured in microns, and a grind that is 5 microns too deep is a ski that runs slow in cold snow and fast in wet.

Glide testing has become the substitute for a formulation. Teams run a test pair down a measured slope with a timing system and choose a structure from the result, which is a 40-minute process on race morning.

Two formats distribute the same variance differently. A mass-start race shares the snow and a time trial does not, and the variance in preparation matters more in the second. The rule applies to both and its effect is uneven.

Cost moved down and up at once. A hydrocarbon wax costs 20 dollars and a stone grind 60, so the material's cost fell; the technicians' time rose by a third, and a national team's service budget rose 12 per cent.

A ski's base is a structure and a wax is a film, and the film's job was to present a low-energy surface to water. A structure's job is to manage the water's flow, and a pattern that channels water out of the contact zone replaces the film's effect.

Cold snow is the harder case. A dry, cold base has little free water and a structure that manages water does nothing, and the hydrocarbon wax that replaced the fluorocarbon is 0.4 per cent slower in those conditions. The gap is where the rule's cost falls.

A ban's enforcement is a laboratory test rather than a race-morning check. A ski's base is scraped and analysed for fluorine, and a positive result is a disqualification that arrives after the race. A team's risk is a base that was prepared with a banned product in a previous season and cleaned imperfectly.

A rule changes where skill lives, and this winter shows the move. A banned material removes one variable and exposes another, and the sport's hierarchy is now set by the person who reads the snow.
