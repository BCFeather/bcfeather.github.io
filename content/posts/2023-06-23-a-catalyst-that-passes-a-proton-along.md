description: "Moving an electron and a proton in one step avoids an intermediate at high energy, and a catalyst that has a proton acceptor next to its active site uses the route to split water at a lower voltage."
---

Splitting water takes four electrons and four protons, and the order in which they are removed decides the energy the reaction costs. An electron taken alone leaves a charged intermediate that is expensive to make, and a proton taken alone leaves a basic site that is also expensive. A reaction that moves both at once avoids both intermediates, and the mechanism has a name that says so: proton-coupled electron transfer.

The coupling is a kinetic effect rather than a thermodynamic one. The overall energy of the reaction is fixed by the potentials and the pH, and the route to it is what the coupling changes. A catalyst that follows the coupled path climbs a gentler slope, and the current it delivers at a given voltage is higher than the one that follows the sequential path. The difference is visible in a voltammogram as a shift of the onset potential. A catalyst that follows the coupled path is one whose rate is set by the proton and the electron arriving together, and the engineering of that coincidence is the whole design problem.

The design that exploits the effect places a proton acceptor next to the metal site. The acceptor is a basic group, often a carboxylate or an imidazole on the ligand, and it takes the proton as the metal takes the electron. The two movements are concerted at the transition state, and the ligand is the part of the catalyst that a chemist can modify, since the metal is chosen for its redox potential and the ligand for its basicity.

Evidence for the mechanism comes from the isotope effect. Replacing hydrogen with deuterium slows the reaction if a proton is moving in the rate-determining step, and the size of the effect distinguishes a concerted transfer from a sequential one. The effect is also visible in the pH dependence of the rate, which follows a slope of one for a concerted transfer and a different slope for the sequential route.

Enzymes use the same principle, and the comparison is useful. A hydrogenase or a photosystem moves electrons and protons together through a chain of residues, and the residues are placed by evolution to make the transfer concerted. A synthetic catalyst that copies the placement is doing what the enzyme does with a much smaller structure, and the copy is the test of whether the principle has been understood.

Limits are about stability and about scale. The ligands that make the proton transfer easy are also the ligands that are oxidised under the operating conditions, and a catalyst that works for an hour in a beaker is a catalyst that has to be tested for a thousand hours in a cell. The voltage that a laboratory cell reaches is also smaller than the one a stack delivers, because the stack adds the resistance of the membrane and the overpotential of the counter electrode. The mechanism is established and the device is not. A stack that runs at a hundred milliamperes per square centimetre for a year is the target, and no ligand has yet survived it.
