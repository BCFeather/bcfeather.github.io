Australia won the men's Cricket World Cup for a sixth time on 19 November, beating India by six wickets in the final at the Narendra Modi Stadium in Ahmedabad. India, unbeaten in ten matches and playing at home in front of 92,453 people, were bowled out for 240 in fifty overs. Australia reached the target with 42 balls remaining, and the stadium emptied in near silence as the last run was taken.

It turned in the space of an hour. India's innings had been built around Rohit Sharma and Virat Kohli, and both were gone inside the first twenty overs, Rohit to a catch at mid-off and Kohli to a slower ball that he chipped to cover. The middle order rebuilt through KL Rahul, who made 66, and the total looked competitive until the last ten overs produced only sixty runs against a bowling attack that used cutters and slower balls on a surface that gripped.

Australia's chase began badly, with three wickets down inside seven overs and the runs required climbing. Travis Head, who had been dropped early, made 137 from 120 balls, and Marnus Labuschagne stayed with him for a partnership of 192 that took the game away. Head was named player of the match, and he finished the tournament with more runs than any other batter in the knockout stages.

India had been the best side of the competition by a wide margin, and the defeat produced the kind of recrimination that follows a home final. Questions were asked about the pitch, which was used for the first time in the tournament and offered more turn than the team had expected, and about the decision to bat first after winning the toss. The captain, Rohit Sharma, said the total was twenty runs short and that the team had been outplayed in the second innings.

Australia's win extends a record that has no equivalent in the sport. The country has now won the trophy in 1987, 1999, 2003, 2007, 2015 and 2023, and it has won the last two finals it has played in Asia. The team arrived in India with a squad that had lost five of its previous eight one-day internationals, and it left with a trophy that most observers had assigned to the hosts before the tournament began.

It also closed a tournament that the sport's governing body had organized on an unusual scale, with forty-five matches across ten venues and a schedule that kept the biggest teams in the largest cities. Attendance records were set, and the broadcast audience was estimated in the hundreds of millions. For the hosts, the commercial success made the result harder to accept, and for Australia the silence in Ahmedabad was the sound of a plan that had worked. Australia had lost the toss and been sent into the field on a surface prepared for the hosts, and the bowlers used the slower ball so relentlessly that the Indian batters never settled into a rhythm.
