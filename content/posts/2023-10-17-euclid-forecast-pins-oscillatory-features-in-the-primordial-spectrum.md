description: "A Fisher-matrix forecast finds that the Euclid mission alone would measure the amplitude of a primordial oscillation to about 20 per cent, and that adding a CMB experiment would reach one per cent of the fiducial value."
---

The standard account of the early universe gives the density perturbations a smooth, nearly scale-invariant spectrum. Anything oscillatory superimposed on that shape would point to new physics at energy scales no collider can reach, and the question is whether a survey can measure such a signal. A feature in the spectrum would carry the imprint of whatever process generated it.

A preprint posted on 29 September 2023 takes the question seriously with a forecast for the Euclid mission. Two models of undamped oscillations are considered, one spaced evenly in wavenumber and the other spaced logarithmically, and the expected uncertainties are computed with a Fisher matrix. The two differ in the spacing they assume, so a forecast that constrains one spacing does not automatically constrain the other. Forecasting is the only way to know whether a survey is worth building, and the Fisher method is standard in cosmology.

The probes are numerous, and each one costs a separate analysis pipeline. Spectroscopic galaxy clustering, weak lensing, photometric galaxy clustering, the cross-correlation between photometric probes, the spectroscopic bispectrum, and several CMB channels including lensing are combined, which is what makes a forecast of this kind more than an estimate for one observable. Each probe contributes over its own range of scales, and the combination reaches where no single channel does.

Euclid alone would measure the feature amplitude to 21 per cent for the linear model and 22 per cent for the logarithmic one in pessimistic settings, improving to 18 per cent for both in optimistic settings, at 68.3 per cent confidence. Those numbers come from the primary probes combined, and they are one-sigma errors on the amplitude, so the confidence level belongs to the reading.

Adding a CMB experiment of the sort proposed for the next generation tightens the constraint sharply. With that combination the forecast amplitude reaches \(A_{\mathrm{lin}}\simeq 0.010\pm 0.001\), and the logarithmic case matches it, over a frequency range from 1 to about 126 in the relevant units. Equal uncertainties for the two models is itself informative, since it means the forecast does not favour one spacing.

Nonlinearity is the obstacle the paper addresses next. Oscillations imprinted at early times are processed by gravitational collapse, and a density reconstruction method is studied as a way to recover them from the late-time field. Reconstruction is an inverse problem: the smooth part is modelled and removed, and what remains is tested for oscillation. The bispectrum enters because it carries coupling between scales that the power spectrum averages away.

Forecasts are not measurements, and the assumed model of nonlinear evolution is where the uncertainty would bite if the real universe differs. A detection at the one per cent level would need the reconstruction to work as advertised, and the survey data will show whether it does. The forecast also sets the standard that any claimed detection would have to meet.
