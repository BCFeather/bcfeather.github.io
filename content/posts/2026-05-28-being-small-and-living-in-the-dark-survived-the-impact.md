Mass extinctions are usually explained by what killed things. The Cretaceous-Paleogene event, 66 million years ago, killed most of the marine plankton alongside the dinosaurs, yet some groups came through, and why has never been settled because the event bundled several stresses together.

Rui Ying and colleagues at the University of Bristol built a numerical model of the base of the food web, published in *Nature* on 28 May, to separate the stresses. Ocean acidification, darkness from impact debris and the loss of photosynthesis arrived within a short interval, and a field observation cannot isolate which one did the work. Sediments record who disappeared, not why, and the proxies for darkness and for pH are indirect at the best of sites. A model is the instrument that lets those proxies be tested against each other instead of read in sequence.

The result is a rule about size and habitat. Tiny organisms in polar oceans survived, and the reasons are energetic and optical: they need less energy per cell, and they were already adapted to long periods of low light, so the darkness that starved tropical plankton was closer to their normal winter. Size enters twice: a small cell needs less light to stay alive and less carbon to divide, so the same darkness is survivable at one end of the size range and fatal at the other.

That reverses a common intuition. Polar species are often treated as the vulnerable ones, specialized for a stable cold regime and easily pushed out of it, and modern conservation planning assumes exactly that. The K-Pg case suggests the assumption is about the direction of the stress, not about specialization as such. In this event the specialization was the asset, because the post-impact ocean briefly resembled a polar winter everywhere.

The model also puts numbers on the selectivity. Groups with larger cells and higher metabolic demands disappeared at higher rates, and the pattern held across the base of the food chain rather than only among the apex predators, which is where the fossil record is easiest to read and most misleading. Apex predators are large, sparse and well studied; the base of the food web is small, abundant and usually counted by proxy.

Resolution is the caveat. Century-scale environmental change is exactly the timescale that fossil and proxy records handle worst, and a model's answer depends on the parameters chosen for darkness and acidification. The value of the work is that it makes the causal question answerable at all, and it suggests that extinction risk is set by a species' energy budget and its tolerance for darkness, two traits that are measurable in living organisms, which are traits that can be compared across groups. Modern analogues, from polar diatoms to deep-water foraminifera, could test that rule against the fossil pattern, and no one has run the comparison yet. That comparison is the sort of work a model can propose and a sediment core can answer.
