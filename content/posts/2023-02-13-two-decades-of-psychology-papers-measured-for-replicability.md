The replication crisis in psychology produced a set of manual projects, each re-running a sample of studies and reporting how many held up. Manual replication is expensive, and a discipline publishes far more papers than any project can re-run.

In *PNAS* on 7 February 2023, the other route is taken. Wu Youyou, Yang Yang and Brian Uzzi build a model that predicts whether a paper will replicate, train it on the results of existing replication projects, and apply it to two decades of published psychology.

Text and statistics supply the predictors. Sample size, the strength of the reported effect, the presence of certain phrases, the structure of the abstract and the pattern of citations all carry information, and a model that combines them predicts outcomes better than any single feature. The features are interpretable, which is a virtue, because a prediction resting on sample size and effect size is one a reader can check and recompute.

Because the pattern runs by subfield and by period, with cognitive estimates higher than social ones and estimates improving over time in fields that adopted reforms, the trend is consistent with those reforms having an effect, and replicability looks like a property of practices rather than of people. Preregistration, larger samples and open data move the estimates, and a discipline that adopts them moves its own distribution.

Training data bound the claim. A model trained on a few hundred replicated studies learns the regularities of those studies, and its predictions for a different literature are extrapolations, so the accuracy reported on held-out projects is the limit of what the estimates can mean. Use is triage rather than judgement, since a flag on a paper that is less likely to replicate tells a reader where to look more carefully, and the authors are explicit that a flag is not a verdict.

Unpublished work stays invisible, because a model trained on published papers predicts the fate of published papers and the file drawer is closed to it. Policy gains a metric that can be computed continuously, since a discipline estimating its own replicability from its published record can track reforms without waiting for the next manual project, and a field that publishes those estimates can see whether its distribution moved. Teaching is the second use, because a course that shows students the predicted replicability of what it assigns teaches how to read a result, and a number makes the lesson concrete rather than rhetorical.

Reform is the part of the argument that generalizes, since preregistration and larger samples are decisions a field makes rather than properties a paper carries, and a model predicting from those decisions is predicting practice. A department that used the estimates to choose what to teach would be using them for a purpose the authors did not design them for. Estimates of this kind will be revised as projects accumulate, and the revision is the point, since the quantity is a running measurement of a discipline rather than a fixed verdict.
