The replication crisis in psychology produced a set of manual replication projects, each of which re-ran a sample of studies and reported how many held up. Manual replication is expensive, and a discipline publishes far more papers than any project can re-run.

The study in *PNAS*, 7 February 2023 takes the other route. Wu Youyou, Yang Yang and Brian Uzzi build a model that predicts whether a paper will replicate, train it on the results of existing replication projects, and apply it to two decades of published psychology.

The predictors are textual and statistical. Sample size, the strength of the reported effect, the presence of certain phrases, the structure of the abstract and the pattern of citations all carry information, and a model that combines them predicts replication outcomes better than any single feature.

The finding is a trend by subfield and by period. Cognitive psychology's estimates are higher than social psychology's, and the estimates improve over time in fields where reforms were adopted. The pattern is consistent with the reforms having an effect.

The caveat is the training set. A model trained on a few hundred replicated studies learns the regularities of those studies, and its predictions for a different literature are extrapolations. The paper reports the model's accuracy on held-out replication projects, and that accuracy is the limit of what the estimates can mean.

The use is triage, not judgement. A model that flags a paper as less likely to replicate is a model that tells a reader where to look more carefully, and a flag is not a verdict. The paper's authors are explicit about that.

The field-level reading is that replicability is a property of practices rather than of people. Preregistration, larger samples and open data move the estimates, and a discipline that adopts them moves its distribution.

What the study cannot see is the unpublished literature. A model trained on published papers predicts the fate of published papers, and the file drawer of studies that were never submitted is invisible to it.

For science policy, the value is a metric that can be computed continuously. A discipline that can estimate its own replicability from its published record can track the effect of reforms without waiting for the next manual project.

The model's features are interpretable, which is a virtue. A prediction that rests on sample size and on effect size is a prediction that a reader can check, and a reader who disagrees can recompute the estimate.
The policy use is a dashboard. A field that publishes its replication estimates can see whether a reform changed the distribution, and a change in the distribution is the evidence that a reform worked.

A second use is teaching. A course that shows students the predicted replicability of the papers it assigns is a course that teaches how to read a result, and the estimate makes the lesson concrete rather than rhetorical.
