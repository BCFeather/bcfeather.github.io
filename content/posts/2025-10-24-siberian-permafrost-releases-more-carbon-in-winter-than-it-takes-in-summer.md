description: "A tower in Siberia has measured the carbon exchange of thawing permafrost for eleven years, and its data show that the winter release of old carbon is larger than the summer uptake, which reverses the sign of the budget."
---

A permafrost landscape in summer absorbs carbon dioxide as plants grow and in winter releases it as microbes decompose soil that no longer stays frozen. A tower that has measured both seasons for eleven years reported this month that the winter release exceeds the summer uptake, which makes the landscape a net source.

An eddy covariance tower measures vertical wind velocity and carbon dioxide concentration 20 times a second and integrates the product. That method captures turbulent exchange and misses the slow diffusion through the soil, which the team estimated with chambers. The finding sits in the seasonal numbers. Summer uptake averaged 210 grams of carbon per square metre, and winter release 260, and the gap widened over the eleven years as the thaw season lengthened by 9 days. Released carbon is old carbon. Radiocarbon dating of the respired carbon dioxide puts its age at 2,000 to 8,000 years, which means the microbes are decomposing soil that was frozen since the Holocene rather than last year's litter. Instrument drift is an annual check, and a tower's calibration gas is run through the analyser every few weeks to keep a decade's record comparable.

Deepening comes first in the mechanism, and the active layer is what deepens. The layer that thaws each summer has deepened from 0.8 metres in 2014 to 1.1 in 2025, and the additional soil is old, carbon-rich and unprotected. A one-metre deepening of a landscape this size is a release on the scale of a small country's emissions.

Heterogeneity across the landscape limits the extrapolation. The site is a peat plateau, which holds more carbon than a mineral soil, and three other towers in the region show a smaller net release. The paper reports its result as a site measurement and models the regional total separately.

Winter flux was assumed to be negligible because cold slows decomposition. The tower's data show the opposite, and the mechanism is not biological: as the active layer freezes from the surface down, gas is expelled from the shrinking pore space, and a fraction of it reaches the atmosphere rather than staying in the soil.

Snow cover controls the winter's total. A thin snowpack insulates less, the soil freezes deeper and faster, and the expulsion is stronger. A winter with little snow can release more carbon than one with a thick cover, which is a relationship the seasonal record shows clearly. A tower's footprint is a hectare and a region is a million square kilometres, and the gap is the paper's central caveat. A single site's annual balance can be anomalous for reasons of drainage, snow and soil type, and a network of 20 towers is what a regional estimate would need.

Measurement can see a feedback that models missed, and permafrost carbon is the example. Models of permafrost carbon assumed a near-neutral budget, and a decade of tower data was what it took to show that the winter half of the year is doing the work.
