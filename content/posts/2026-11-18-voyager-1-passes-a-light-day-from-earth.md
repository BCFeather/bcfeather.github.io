On the morning of 18 November, at 10:16 UTC, Voyager 1 passed a distance of 173.14 astronomical units from Earth. That number has a simple reading: light, which crosses the Earth-sun gap in about eight minutes, would now need a full day to reach the spacecraft. No other object built by people has gone that far.

A light-day is a comfortable unit until it is applied to a real machine. Neptune orbits at about 30 astronomical units, so the probe is nearly six times farther out than the outermost planet. The nearest star system, Alpha Centauri, sits more than four light-years away, which makes one light-day about one part in fifteen hundred of the journey to a destination the spacecraft is not heading for.

Distance at this scale is also a schedule. A command radioed to Voyager 1 arrives about twenty-four hours later, and the answer takes another day to come back, so every exchange is a two-day conversation. Engineers who send a correction to a computer or a thruster wait through both halves of it before they know whether the fix worked, and the recent repairs have been done exactly that way.

The flight began on 5 September 1977. Jupiter came in March 1979, Saturn in November 1980, and the encounter with Titan bent the trajectory out of the plane of the planets and towards interstellar space. In August 2012 the spacecraft crossed the heliopause, where the sun's wind of charged particles gives way to the medium between the stars, and it has been measuring that medium ever since.

Power is what sets the end of the mission. Three radioisotope thermoelectric generators turn the heat of decaying plutonium into electricity, and their output falls a little every year. NASA has been switching off instruments and heaters one at a time to keep the transmitter running; the Low-Energy Charged Particle instrument went dark in April 2026, and most of the original science payload has already stopped.

Voyager 2, launched sixteen days earlier, took the longer tour past Uranus and Neptune and is now about two billion miles closer to Earth. Its slower speed puts its own light-day in November 2035, nine years from now. The twin spacecraft also sampled a different region of the heliosphere, which is why the two sets of measurements are usually read together.

Leaving the solar system is a phrase worth keeping in proportion. Beyond the heliopause the spacecraft is in interstellar space, but the sun's gravity still holds it, and the Oort cloud of comets is thought to begin hundreds of years farther out and to extend for tens of thousands. At roughly 17 kilometres per second, a single light-year would take about eighteen thousand years.

NASA marked the day with a worldwide event on the evening of 17 November, asking people to flash the letters V-G-R in Morse code with a torch or a phone. Both probes carry a golden record of images and sounds, and the greeting was always addressed to a reader who would receive it long after the launch.

What comes next is a power budget rather than a discovery. Each year removes a little more capability, and the agency has not named a date for the last instrument. For the moment the spacecraft keeps moving outward, and every message it sends spends a full day in transit before anyone on Earth can read it.
