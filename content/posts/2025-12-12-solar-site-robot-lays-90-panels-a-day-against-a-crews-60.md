A utility-scale solar farm is a construction project with a repetitive task at its center, and the task is placing a panel on a rack and torquing four bolts. A robot has installed 12,000 panels at a site this year, and the operator's comparison with a crew is the report's most useful number.

The robot is a tracked platform with an arm, a vision system and a bolt driver. It picks a panel from a stack, locates the rack's rails with a camera, places the panel and torques the bolts, and it moves along the row on rails it lays itself.

Ninety panels a day against a crew's 60 is the rate the trial measured, and the robot works a 10-hour shift with one technician watching four machines. That is a 50 per cent productivity gain before the technician's cost.

Quality shows in the rework rate. A panel placed out of tolerance must be removed and reseated, and the robot's rework rate was 1.2 per cent against 2.5 for the crew, because the vision system checks the position before torquing. Torque logs are the audit trail, and a site that can produce one per panel resolves a warranty question that a crew's word cannot.

Capital cost is 350,000 dollars per machine, and the site's labor cost is 9 dollars per panel. The payback is 3,000 panels, which is 34 days of operation at the trial's rate.

The constraint is site geometry. The robot needs rows of at least 40 panels and a graded surface within 5 centimetres, and a site with irregular terrain or small arrays cannot use it.

A fixed line and a travelling machine solve different problems. A factory line builds a panel assembly and a site needs a machine that travels. The robot's value is that it moves, and its cost is that it must carry its own positioning.

A panel's installation is four operations: pick, place, align, torque. A crew does them with two people and a lift, and the robot does them with a vacuum gripper and a torque driver that reads the bolt's resistance. The resistance reading is what removes a rework step.

Site logistics set the ceiling. A robot needs a stack of panels within reach, and a site that delivers pallets to the row's end needs a shuttle. A site with a 200-metre row and no shuttle spends a third of the robot's day walking.

A robot's case rests on a labor market rather than on a machine. A site that cannot hire 60 installers for a season pays a premium for the ones it finds, and a robot that needs four technicians is a robot that scales with a scarce skill rather than a plentiful one.

Construction has become manufacturing in this case, and the cost of a solar farm now sits there. The panel is the same and the labor is different, and the difference is where the cost of a solar farm now sits.
