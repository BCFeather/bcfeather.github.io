A seller who listed a flat last summer has cut the price four times. First cut was two per cent, the last eight. Fifteen years old, the block has a lift and a car park. It is now priced below what the same unit sold for in the spring. The viewings have stopped. An agent who showed the flat to eleven families in September showed it to two in March and none in June. The phone still rings with people who want to know whether the price is negotiable, which is the question that a seller answers last.

Stock has grown. A district that had four hundred listings three years ago has a thousand. Flats priced correctly sell in six weeks. Others stay on the page and become part of the statistics that a buyer reads before making an offer. The buyers are waiting for something. A household that sold its old flat and is renting has no reason to buy while the price falls by a per cent a month. Expectation of a further fall produces the fall.

Agents have started to call the neighbours. A block with one flat for sale often has three within a year. An agent who knows that a family is thinking of moving can list the unit before it is advertised. Cheapest marketing in the trade is a telephone call. The developer is the other seller. A project that was launched in 2021 has unsold units in 2024. A company cuts the price by more than a household can. A buyer who compares a new unit with a resale one finds the new one cheaper, which is why the resale market is slow.

Rent has held. A flat that cannot be sold is rented instead. Rent in the district has risen by a few per cent, because families that would have bought are renting. By default the seller becomes a landlord. The land sale is the part that the city notices. A plot that would have sold for a large sum two years ago receives no bid. A budget written on the assumption of the sale has to be rewritten. Flat and plot are the same market.

A school district holds the price. A flat in the catchment of a good primary school sells at a premium that survives every other fall in the market. A family that buys it for the school sells it to another family that wants the same place, and the chain repeats. The mortgage is the constraint on the buyer. A loan is approved against an income and a valuation, and a bank that values the flat at last year's price lends less than the seller expects. Gap is paid in cash or the deal does not close. A listing page keeps its own history, and a buyer who scrolls back can see every cut the seller has made.

Eventually the flat will sell. A price that is low enough finds a buyer in any market, and the question is only how much the seller will accept before the family that needs the money accepts it for them.
