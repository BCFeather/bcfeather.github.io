An animal that weighs a tonne eats a great deal, and the largest herbivores have appeared in several lineages at different times. Whether gigantism is one adaptation or many is a question about the shape of a lineage's history.

In *Science* on 12 May 2023, a phylogeny answers it. The authors trace body size across herbivorous mammals and relate the transitions to the spread of grasses, and find that gigantism arose repeatedly along a common pathway rather than as a single innovation.

Threshold describes that pathway. A herbivore crossing a certain size gains access to abundant, low-quality forage, and the digestive architecture a large body permits is what makes the diet viable. The digestive constraint is the core of the mechanism, since a large body can hold a fermentation chamber big enough to extract energy from grass and a small one cannot, which makes the threshold a physiological number rather than a stylistic one.

Grasses supplied the opportunity. Grasslands expanded through the Cenozoic, and each expansion opened a niche a lineage could enter by growing larger. Repetition is the pattern, because several unrelated lineages crossed the threshold, which suggests the pathway belongs to the niche rather than to any group's genetics. Latitude adds support, since gigantism is more common at low latitudes where forage is available year round, and the pattern indicates that productivity sets the ceiling.

Decline is the modern puzzle. Large herbivores are now few, and the analysis of extinction shows that the same size conferring access to grass also conferred vulnerability to hunting and to habitat fragmentation. Africa is the best case, because elephants, rhinoceroses and giraffes occupy the niche on one continent, and their coexistence shows it can hold several species at once, which a single-lineage account would not predict.

Method is a comparative model. A phylogeny with body masses and diets allows transitions to be counted, and counting is what distinguishes a repeated pathway from a coincidence. Body-size estimates are the soft spot, since a fossil's mass is inferred from teeth and limb bones and the error grows large for the largest animals, and the sensitivity analysis is what keeps the threshold from moving with each assumption.

Record bias sets the limitation, because a large animal's bones preserve better than a small one's and that bias inflates the apparent frequency of gigantism. Mechanism for each transition stays unestablished, since a threshold in a model describes a pattern, and the developmental and ecological steps behind each case are separate studies. Macroecology gains a template: a trait that evolves repeatedly along a common route has constraints that can be studied, and the megaherbivores offer a case with a clear environmental trigger. A lineage that shrinks again can lose the chamber and with it the diet, and the reversibility of the step is what makes the pathway a threshold rather than a ratchet. Whether the same threshold applies to extinct giants is the test the fossil record still has to pass.
