An animal that weighs a tonne eats a great deal, and the largest herbivores have appeared in several lineages at different times. Whether gigantism is one adaptation or many is a question about the shape of a lineage's history.

The analysis in *Science*, 12 May 2023 answers it with a phylogeny. The authors trace body size across herbivorous mammals and relate the transitions to the spread of grasses, and find that gigantism arose repeatedly along a common pathway rather than as a single innovation.

The pathway is a threshold. A herbivore that crosses a certain size gains access to a diet of abundant, low-quality forage, and the digestive architecture that a large body permits is what makes the diet viable.

The grasses are the opportunity. Grasslands expanded through the Cenozoic, and each expansion opened a niche that a lineage could enter by growing larger.

The repeated appearance is the pattern. Several unrelated lineages crossed the threshold, which suggests that the pathway is a property of the niche rather than of a particular group's genetics.

The decline is the modern puzzle. Large herbivores are now few, and the paper's analysis of extinction shows that the same size that conferred access to grass also conferred vulnerability to hunting and to habitat fragmentation.

The method is a comparative model. A phylogeny with body masses and diets allows transitions to be counted, and the counting is what distinguishes a repeated pathway from a coincidence.

The limitation is the fossil record's bias. A large animal's bones preserve better than a small one's, and the record's bias inflates the apparent frequency of gigantism.

What the paper cannot establish is the mechanism of each transition. A threshold in a model is a description of a pattern, and the developmental and ecological steps that produced each case are separate studies.

For macroecology, the value is a template. A trait that evolves repeatedly along a common route is a trait whose constraints can be studied, and the megaherbivores are a case with a clear environmental trigger.

The digestive constraint is the mechanism's core. A large body can hold a fermentation chamber large enough to extract energy from grass, and a small one cannot. The threshold is therefore a physiological number rather than a stylistic one.

The latitudinal pattern is suggestive. Gigantism is more common at low latitudes, where forage is available year-round, and the pattern supports the idea that the niche's productivity sets the ceiling.
The body-size estimate is the analysis's soft spot. A fossil's mass is inferred from teeth and from limb bones, and the inference's error is large for the largest animals. The authors' sensitivity analysis is what keeps the threshold from moving with each assumption.
The African record is the best case. Elephants, rhinoceroses and giraffes occupy the large-herbivore niche on one continent, and their coexistence shows that the niche can hold several species at once, which a single-lineage account would not predict.
