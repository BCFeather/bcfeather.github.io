The agreement had survived a year, several threats and one previous suspension, and on 17 July it ran out. Russia told the United Nations and Turkey that it was withdrawing from the Black Sea Grain Initiative, hours before the deal expired, and the Kremlin's spokesman said the parts of the arrangement that concerned Russian exports had never been implemented. The corridor that had carried almost thirty-three million tonnes of Ukrainian maize, wheat and sunflower oil out of three ports closed the same day.

Put together in July 2022 by the United Nations and Turkey, the arrangement had an architecture that no lawyer would have designed. Ships were inspected in Istanbul by a joint coordination centre staffed by Russian, Ukrainian, Turkish and UN officials, and they sailed along a corridor whose neutrality was guaranteed by the signature of the country attacking the ports it served. It was a humanitarian arrangement built on the assumption that both sides needed the money, and for a year that assumption held, through a winter of attacks on the power grid and a spring of mine clearance along the route.

Moscow's stated reasons concerned the parallel memorandum on Russian food and fertilizer exports, which Western sanctions had made difficult to service despite formal exemptions. Russian officials complained about payments, insurance, shipping and the ammonia pipeline from Tolyatti to Odesa that had been damaged in the fighting. They also argued that the corridor had become a supply route for military cargo, a charge Ukraine and its partners rejected. On the day the deal ended, the bridge linking Russia to Crimea was attacked, and Moscow cited that as further justification.

Markets answered within hours. Wheat prices on the Chicago exchange rose by about eight per cent in a day, having fallen for months, and the countries that buy Ukrainian grain in North Africa and Asia began looking for alternatives. Ukraine's farmers, who had borrowed against a harvest that could now sit in silos, face the loss of a route that had moved more than thirty million tonnes of produce and paid for a war economy.

Regional politics weigh as heavily as global ones here. Turkey's president had spent weeks saying he would extend the deal and would discuss it with Putin, and the announcement undercut him. Kenya, Somalia and Ethiopia, which depend on imported grain, had warned about prices, and the World Food Programme, which had shipped a large share of its wheat through the corridor, lost its cheapest supply route.

Ships already loaded were allowed to sail, and the corridor's insurance was withdrawn. Ukraine has said it will establish a temporary route of its own and that it will not be deterred by Russian threats, which will be tested in the coming weeks by whether cargoes move and what happens to them. Brokers in London have quoted war-risk premiums that make an unescorted voyage uneconomic, and no coalition has offered a warship to escort one. Whether that changes depends on insurers and on navies rather than on communiques.
