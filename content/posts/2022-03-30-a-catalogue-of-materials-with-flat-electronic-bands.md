Flat bands are the ingredient that turns a metal into a correlated insulator or a superconductor, and until recently they were engineered rather than found. Twisted bilayers produced them by accident of geometry, and the search for materials that have them naturally has been a matter of trial and experiment. The search has been limited by the cost of calculating band structures for tens of thousands of compounds, and a geometric criterion avoids that cost for the first pass.

A high-throughput survey screened 55,206 entries in the Inorganic Crystal Structure Database, using the Topological Quantum Chemistry catalogue for structural and band information, and applied several direct signatures of band flatness to every structure. The results appeared in *Nature*, 30 March 2022. A database that already carries structural parameters, space groups and band structures for every entry makes that screen possible without recomputing anything.

Its signatures combine lattice geometry with band structure. Materials whose atoms form line-graph or bipartite sublattices tend to produce flat bands through destructive interference of hopping paths, and that geometric criterion can be evaluated for every crystal without calculating the full band structure. Interference requires that the hopping amplitudes cancel around a loop, which happens when the lattice contains a triangle or a bipartite pattern, and the catalogue searches for those motifs.

The screen produced 2,379 high-quality candidates and a shorter list of 345 that may host flat bands whose charge is not strongly localized on atomic sites. That second filter matters because a flat band made of atomic states is chemically inert, while one made of extended states can carry current and respond to a gate.

Five representative materials were analysed with an S-matrix method that explains the origin of their flat bands near the Fermi energy. The method identifies the real-space motif that produces the interference, which is the kind of explanation that lets a chemist design a new member of the family. An S-matrix analysis decomposes the Hamiltonian into scattering channels, and a flat band appears as a channel with vanishing dispersion, which is an explanation rather than a fit. A band that is flat over a wide region of momentum space is more robust to disorder than one that is flat at a single point, and the catalogue records the width.

The catalogue is published as a searchable website, which is the part of the work most likely to be used. A theorist looking for a material to calculate and an experimentalist looking for a crystal to grow can both start from the same list and not from the literature on twisted graphene. A chemist can use the list to choose a synthesis target, and an experimentalist can use it to choose a crystal that is likely to show a correlated state.

A prediction is not a measurement, and the flat bands in these materials have not been observed. The value of the catalogue is that it converts a search over an unbounded space into a list of candidates that can be tested.
