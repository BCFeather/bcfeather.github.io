Every autumn, in the days after the harvest moon, the tide comes into Hangzhou Bay as a wall rather than as a rise. The bore appears first as a white line across the water, perhaps half a metre high, and it reaches the bank in a few minutes with a sound that people on the dike describe as a train passing. The height varies with the moon and the weather, from a ripple that barely breaks to a front of two metres or more, and the difference between a good year and a bad one is decided by the tide, the river discharge and the wind. A bore needs three things at once: a strong flood tide, a funnel that squeezes it, and a river pushing back against it. The spring tide that follows the new moon and the full is the one that builds the wall, which is why the bore keeps a calendar as much as a season.

The bay is a funnel, and the funnel explains the rest. At its mouth the bay is tens of kilometres wide and opens into the East China Sea; upstream, towards Hangzhou, it narrows to a few kilometres and shoals, so the flood tide that enters has to fit into a channel that shrinks under it. The water piles up, the front steepens, and the tide that would elsewhere rise over an hour arrives in minutes. Sediment bars inside the bay slow the flood further, which is why the bore in some reaches degenerates into a chaotic line of broken water rather than a clean wall. Where the bars are shallowest the front can break into two, and the second arrives after the first has already passed.

People have watched from the same places for centuries. The viewing banks at Yanguan and Laoyancang sit on a sea wall that was rebuilt many times, most famously in the Qing dynasty as a facing of interlocking stone blocks, laid like fish scales, that has survived storms the earth dikes did not. The wall is what makes the bore safe to watch at all: the water arrives at the foot of the masonry rather than across the flats, and the spectators stand a few metres above it. The blocks are cut to a wedge and laid in rows, the stone taking the shock of the water and the earth core behind it holding the volume, and the two materials have lasted together where either alone would have gone. The wall runs for kilometres, and the road along its top is the only level ground in the neighbourhood for the crowds that come in the eighth month.

In the towns along the dike the bore is an industry as much as a phenomenon. Platforms with railings, car parks, a festival with a date set by the lunar calendar, and forecasts issued by the tide station for the days when the bore is expected to be at its height. The forecasts matter for more than tourism, because the flats are deceptive at low water and people walk out on them; the same bore that is a spectacle from the bank is a wall of water across a surface with no cover. The tide station publishes the height and the time for the coming month, and the schools along the bay give the bore a place in the term.

The bore also carries salt water inland, and that is the part the city thinks about. A flood tide that runs upstream as a front pushes the brackish limit several kilometres further than an ordinary tide, and the intake of a waterworks that sits too low in the estuary takes in chloride instead of freshwater. The reservoir upstream of the bore's reach exists partly for that reason, and the operation of the gates is timed against the tide table. The bore moves sediment as well: the front lifts the bed as it passes, and the channel behind it is briefly deeper than the one in front.

Evening comes with the front already past, and the bay returns to a flat sheet of water with the marks of the bore still running along it in lines of foam. The dike is crowded at the hour of the arrival and empty an hour later, the stalls closing, the last of the light going off the stone facing. What remains is the shape of the bay doing what it has always done, twice a day, with the extra violence of the spring tide twice a month and the particular violence of the autumn, when the moon, the river and the wind happen to agree. The next bore comes tomorrow, and the one after that, on a timetable the moon sets and a bay that has not changed shape in the time anyone has been counting.
