title: "Bempedoic acid and fenofibrate together lower HDL"
date: "2025-03-26T20:40:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A research letter in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 26 March reports that combining bempedoic acid with fenofibrate produced a marked and reversible fall in HDL cholesterol, an interaction that neither drug's label warns about."
---

Bempedoic acid is an oral drug that inhibits an enzyme upstream of cholesterol synthesis in the liver, and it lowers LDL by about 18 per cent on its own, which is enough to be useful in a patient who cannot take a statin, and fenofibrate is an older drug that lowers triglycerides and raises HDL. Both are used in patients who cannot tolerate a statin or who need more lowering than a statin provides, and the two are prescribed together in exactly that population, where the aim is to lower triglycerides and to raise HDL at the same time as the LDL falls.

Observation came from a trial of the combination instead of from a spontaneous report. Investigators measured lipids in participants who took both drugs and compared them with participants who took one, and the HDL in the combination arm fell by a margin that the study had not expected. The fall was largest in patients whose HDL was highest at baseline, and the authors report the numbers per group in the supplementary material rather than in the letter itself.

Withdrawal reversed it. When the combination was withdrawn, HDL returned to the previous value over a few weeks, which is the behaviour of a pharmacological effect and not of a disease. The authors report the time course and the magnitude, and they recommend that clinicians check HDL when the combination is started.

Mechanism is a guess and a plausible one. Fenofibrate raises HDL by increasing the production of apolipoprotein A-I, and bempedoic acid activates a pathway that can suppress it, and the two effects may cancel in a way that leaves less HDL than either drug alone. The letter does not prove the mechanism, and it does establish the phenomenon, which is what a research letter is for.

Clinical significance depends on whether HDL matters. Trials that raised HDL with drugs did not reduce cardiovascular events, and a drug that lowers HDL is therefore not obviously harmful. A large fall in a surrogate marker that has failed as a target is a finding that a clinician should note and should not panic about, and the letter takes that line.

What the report shows is the value of measuring in combination. Two drugs that are each well studied can interact in a way that neither study sees, because neither study enrolls patients who take the other. The regulatory system approves drugs one at a time, and the combinations that doctors assemble are tested in the clinic and not in a trial that a regulator designed.

Follow-up study with a mechanistic arm is the next step. The authors call for one, and the population is available, because the combination is already prescribed for patients with high triglycerides and a low HDL. A trial that measures apoA-I production and clearance would settle the mechanism, and the question of whether the fall matters would need an outcome trial that nobody is likely to fund.
