Alphabet reported first-quarter revenue of 80.5 billion dollars on 25 April, up 15 per cent from a year earlier, and net income of 23.7 billion. It also announced its first dividend, of twenty cents a share, and authorized buybacks of up to seventy billion dollars. The shares rose after the release, and the gain was attributed less to the quarter than to the announcement that the company would stop accumulating cash without a plan.

Dividend is a signal rather than a payment. A company that begins paying one is telling the market that it no longer expects to need all of its cash for growth, and the announcement removed a long-standing criticism that the balance sheet was idle. A payout that is small is a promise about the direction of the policy. The board tied the dividend to the free cash flow rather than to the earnings, which is a commitment that the company can meet even in a year when it spends heavily on computing.

Advertising carried the quarter. Search revenue grew faster than the market expected, and the video platform inside the company continued to add users, which is the part of the business that competes most directly with the social networks. A search business that grows is a search business that has not yet been displaced by a chatbot. Paid clicks grew while the price per click fell, which is a mix that the company has explained as a shift of advertising into the video format where the units are cheaper.

Cloud was the swing factor. The division that sells computing to other companies returned to profit and grew faster than in the previous quarter, after a year in which growth had slowed to single digits. A cloud business that accelerates is evidence that the demand for artificial intelligence is reaching the companies that do not build their own models. The division has been hiring and building capacity for two years, and the customers it has won are the companies that want to run models without building the infrastructure themselves.

Capital spending is the offset. The company said it would spend more on computing capacity this year than it had planned, and the depreciation of that equipment will weigh on margins for several years. A cost that is spread over the life of a machine is a cost that outlasts the enthusiasm that justified it. The company has said that the spending will peak this year and that the returns will take longer than a single quarter to appear, which is a claim the market has heard from every builder of capacity.

What the quarter shows is that a company with a mature business can choose to be valued as a utility rather than as a growth stock, and the dividend is the instrument it uses to make that choice legible. A dividend is a promise about the future that a company makes with money, and the promise is what changed the way the shares are valued.
