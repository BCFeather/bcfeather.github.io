A fossil site tells you who was there and a lake core tells you what the weather was, and the two records have rarely been aligned at the same resolution. A basin in southern Ethiopia contains both, and the alignment is the paper. The two archives sit within a few tens of kilometres of each other, which is what makes a joint reading possible.

A 620,000-year environmental record from Chew Bahir, proximal to key fossil sites in the region, and compares the climatic episodes with the biological and cultural transitions in the hominin record. The paper appeared in *Nature Geoscience*, 26 September 2022. A basin that contains both the fossils and the sediments is the setting that makes the alignment possible, and the proximity is the design.

High anatomical diversity in hominin groups coincides with long-lasting and relatively stable humid conditions from about 620,000 to 275,000 years ago, interrupted by several abrupt and extreme hydroclimate perturbations. A stable interval is the kind of setting in which populations can persist without being repeatedly fragmented. Whether the diversity reflects one interbreeding population or several is a question the fossils alone cannot settle.

A pattern of pronounced climatic cyclicity transformed habitats during episodes 7 to 9, from about 275,000 to 60,000 years ago, which is the phase that encompasses the gradual transition from Acheulean to Middle Stone Age technologies and the emergence of *Homo sapiens* in eastern Africa. A transition of both technology and species within one climatic regime is the coincidence that the record documents. A coincidence of a technological and a biological transition within one climatic regime is what the record documents, and the documentation is the claim.

Cumulative innovations and the alignment of humid pulses between northeastern Africa and the eastern Mediterranean during the high-frequency oscillations of episodes 10 to 12, from about 60,000 to 10,000 years ago, could have facilitated the global dispersal of the species. A corridor that opens during a wet phase is a corridor that a population can use. Whether people actually moved along that corridor is an inference from dates and not from footprints.

A sediment core is a proxy, and the proxies for aridity are indirect, so the climatic reconstruction carries the uncertainty that any continental record carries. A record this long is valuable precisely because it is continuous, and the continuity is what the correlation with fossils requires. A continental proxy carries the uncertainty of any indirect reconstruction, and the continuity is what makes this record valuable. Correlation between climate and behaviour is not a causation, and the authors present the alignment as a possible influence. A plausible influence is the kind of claim that a record can support and that a mechanism would have to confirm. The record can date the climate and cannot date the behaviour, and the mismatch is the central limitation.

The record's value is that it puts the fossil sites into a climate that can be dated, and the dating is what allows the biological and the environmental to be discussed together. A stratigraphy that both disciplines share is the contribution. A shared chronology is what lets an archaeologist and a climatologist argue about the same interval.
