title: "Protostellar disks lose their outer radii as the source ages"

description: "A 1.3 millimetre survey of 184 protostellar disks in seven nearby clouds finds that large disks thin out from Class 0 to Class I and that gas and dust radii decouple during the early Class I stage."
---

A protostellar disk is the reservoir from which a star and its planets are assembled, and the radius of that reservoir is set by the balance between infall onto the disk and the transport of material inward. Measuring the radius at the earliest stages is therefore a test of formation models, and the measurement became routine once interferometers could resolve a few tens of astronomical units in nearby clouds. The clouds sit at distances of 130 to 250 parsecs, so a resolution of 0.1 arcseconds corresponds to physical scales of 15 to 30 astronomical units.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 3 April 2024 reports a survey built for that purpose. The authors observed nearly all the embedded protostars in seven nearby clouds, Corona Australis, Aquila, Chamaeleon I and II, Ophiuchus North, Ophiuchus and Serpens, with the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array at 1.3 millimetres, at a resolution of 0.1 arcseconds. Millimetre continuum traces dust in the cold outer disk, so the radii measured are dust radii, and the gas radius has to be inferred from the width of line emission.

The sample is large enough to split. The survey detected 184 protostellar disks, and 90 of them were resolved at 14 to 18 astronomical units, one of the most comprehensive high-resolution samples across the protostellar stages. Ninety disks at that resolution is what allows a comparison across classes instead of a description of individual objects.

Substructure appears where it was not expected. The images show annular rings or gaps in two Class I sources and in two flat-spectrum sources, and 21 of the embedded protostars show asymmetries or substructures, which is a reminder that a disk can be structured long before a class II disk is. Rings in a Class I disk suggest that the substructure familiar from more evolved systems begins early, and the paper reports the position and contrast of each feature.

Radii vary widely, and the variation has a direction. The fraction of disks larger than 60 astronomical units falls as a source evolves from Class 0 to Class I, so the largest disks are an early phenomenon that later stages do not retain. The trend is stated as a fraction rather than a mean, because the distribution of radii is broad and a mean would hide the change in the tail.

Gas and dust separate at the same time. Compiling literature measurements, the authors find that the ratio of the gas radius, measured from Keplerian rotation, to the dust radius, measured from millimetre continuum, rises with bolometric temperature, which puts the decoupling in the early Class I stage; in Class 0 the two radii agree.

That agreement is what makes the earliest stage tractable. Because dust and gas trace the same extent in Class 0, a dust radius can be compared with a model directly, and the distribution of radii for the 52 Class 0 disks in the sample is in high tension with current formation models, which is the result the survey leaves behind. That tension is the point: the earliest disks are the ones that models predict with least confidence, and the observed radii are narrower than the models allow.
