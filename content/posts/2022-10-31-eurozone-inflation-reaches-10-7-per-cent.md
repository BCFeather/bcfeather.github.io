Eurostat published the flash estimate on 31 October, and consumer prices rose 10.7 per cent in the year to October. The reading was up from 10 per cent in September and above the 10.3 that analysts had expected. It was the highest since the series began in 1997, and the fifth record in seven months. The figure was 0.4 points above the average of the forecasts in a survey of economists.

Energy was still the largest contributor, at 41.9 per cent over the year. The contribution had stopped rising in September, and the October figure was driven by the lagged repricing of household contracts. Food rose 13.1 per cent, and it had overtaken energy as the fastest rising component in four countries. Core inflation was 5 per cent, up from 4.8. Services rose 5.6 per cent, and that was the fastest since the currency began. The core number mattered because it is the one that responds to interest rates, and it had been above 4 per cent for six months. Wholesale gas prices had fallen 30 per cent since August, and the retail effect was not yet visible.

Germany reported 11.6 per cent, and France 7.1, and Spain 7.3. The differences between countries remained large. The Baltic states were above 20, and Malta was the lowest at 7.4. The gap reflected the design of national support schemes, and the commission said it would assess them for compatibility with state aid rules. The bank's deposit rate was 1.5 per cent, and the real rate was minus 9 per cent.

Rates went up the next day by 75 basis points, taking the deposit rate to 1.5 per cent. It said it expected further increases, and it said the risks to growth were on the downside. The president said the bank would not stop until inflation was falling towards 2 per cent, and she declined to say where the peak would be.

Growth was slowing. The economy had grown 0.2 per cent in the third quarter, and the commission forecast a contraction in the fourth. Industrial production fell 0.8 per cent in September, and the survey of purchasing managers showed activity shrinking for the fourth month. The bank's staff projections in December put the recession at 0.5 per cent for 2023.

Wages were catching up. Negotiated pay rose 4.5 per cent in the third quarter, and the bank's tracker showed that settlements were running at 5 per cent. The bank said unit labour costs were rising at 5 per cent, which is inconsistent with a 2 per cent inflation target, and that was the argument for continuing to raise rates. Inflation peaked at 10.7 and fell to 10.1 in November and 9.2 in December. The fall was driven by energy, and the core rate kept rising until March 2023. The bank raised rates four more times and paused in September at 4 per cent, which it held until June 2024.
