The coronal heating problem is old and stubborn. The visible surface of the Sun is about six thousand degrees, and the corona above it reaches millions, which means energy is being deposited in the outer atmosphere after it has left the surface. Radiation cannot do it, because the corona is optically thin and the energy has to arrive as waves or as particles, and the question is which mechanism, at which scale, deposits it, which is a question about turbulence, about waves and about the smallest structures the current generation of telescopes can resolve.

Small jets, called spicules when seen from the side and jets in other projections, are ubiquitous. They rise from the surface in narrow columns, last minutes, and carry hot plasma upward. A team at the Indian Institute of Astrophysics in Bengaluru tracked their formation and propagation through several layers of the solar atmosphere, and reported the results in a research highlight in *Nature India* on 27 March, describing work published in *Solar Physics* in 2025.

Reconnection is the trigger. The Sun's field near the surface is tangled, and when oppositely directed field lines are forced together they snap and reconnect, converting magnetic energy into kinetic energy and heat. The jets the team observed form at reconnection sites, and the material is accelerated along the newly connected field, like a stretched rubber band released.

Motion is more complicated than a straight shot. The jets twist and swirl as they rise, which means the reconnection is releasing magnetic helicity along with energy, and the twist allows the jet to carry energy into the corona in a form that can be dissipated there. A straight jet would deposit its energy low, within a few hundred kilometres of the surface, and a twisted one travels a distance before the twist unwinds and the energy is dissipated as heat.

Scale is the reason the proposal carries weight. A single jet is small and carries little energy, and the corona loses energy at a rate that requires an enormous flux. If jets are as common as the observations suggest, and if each carries the energy inferred, the sum across the whole surface is enough to balance the budget, and the mechanism stops being a curiosity and becomes a candidate that models can be asked to reproduce.

Observations have limits. What has been measured are of a subset of jets, seen in particular wavelength bands, and the energy carried is inferred from line-of-sight velocities and densities and not measured in three dimensions. A jet observed side-on is not the same object as a jet observed from above, and reconciling the two projections is where the uncertainties live.

Practical value is in space weather. The same reconnection that heats the corona also accelerates particles and perturbs the solar wind, and a mechanism that explains the heating is a mechanism that predicts when the disturbances will occur. Forecasting is done today by extrapolating magnetic fields, and a better understanding of where energy is released would improve that extrapolation.
