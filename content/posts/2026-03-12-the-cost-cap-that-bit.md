---

A spending cap is unusual in sport because the resource it limits is invisible on the broadcast. Formula One adopted one in 2021 at 145 million dollars per team per season, indexed to inflation, with a list of exclusions that runs to aerodynamics for the first season and includes driver salaries and marketing. The effect this season is the first that spectators can see.

Upgrade cadence is the visible variable. Two midfield teams have brought three aerodynamic packages to the first five races, against five or six in the previous two seasons, and one has said publicly that a wind-tunnel programme would exceed the cap's allowance for development. The cars are closer: the gap from pole to the slowest car in qualifying has narrowed from 1.6 seconds to 0.9.

Enforcement is the part that determines whether the cap means anything. The regulator requires annual accounts in a standardized format, audits a sample of teams each year, and has an investigative panel with the power to impose penalties including exclusion from the championship. The first penalty was a financial settlement and a reduction in wind-tunnel time, which is a sanction that costs future performance.

Circumvention risk is structural. A manufacturer that also builds road cars can move work between the racing budget and the road-car budget, and a team owned by a larger group can buy services at internal transfer prices. The regulations address this with a requirement to declare related-party transactions at market value, and the audit's ability to test that is the weakest link.

The cap has redistributed rather than equalized. Teams with a large commercial income still spend up to the cap without difficulty; teams near the cap have to choose between development and operations, and the smallest teams remain below it because they cannot raise the money. Three teams spent under the cap last season.

Competitive effect is measurable in the constructors' table. The standard deviation of points across the season has fallen in each year since the cap, and the number of different race winners rose from four in 2022 to six last season. That is the outcome the cap was designed for, and it has taken four years to appear because aerodynamic development has a long lead time.

Driver market effects follow. A cap limits the salary a team can offer without counting it, and the top drivers' pay has shifted into commercial arrangements outside the cap, which is exactly the arbitrage the rules anticipated and partly permitted.

Audit capacity is the enforcement question. The regulator employs a small team of accountants and engineers, and a full audit of a team's accounts takes months. The first case took eleven months from submission to penalty, which is long enough that a team can spend in a season what it will be penalized for two years later.

For a reader, the interesting design question is what the cap is indexed to. A fixed nominal cap falls in real terms, and a cap indexed to revenue rises with the sport's success; the regulation uses a hybrid, with an inflation adjustment and a review every three years. That decision, not the number, determines how tight the constraint becomes.
