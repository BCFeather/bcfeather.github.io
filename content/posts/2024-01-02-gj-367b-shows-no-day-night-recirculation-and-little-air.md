GJ 367b orbits a red dwarf every eight hours, sits about 1,370 kelvin on its dayside, and packs ten grams per cubic centimetre into a body smaller than the Earth. A density that high is hard to explain with any mixture of rock and iron, and the planet's proximity to its star means that tides and irradiation have had time to reshape it. What the surface looks like, and whether any atmosphere survived, is what a phase curve can answer. Small and dense at the same time is the signature of a body that lost its outer layers, and the orbital period makes that loss plausible.

A phase curve measures the flux of the planet as it moves through its orbit, so the dayside, the nightside and the terminator each contribute at a different phase. Work posted to arXiv on 2 January 2024 reports such a curve in the mid-infrared, taken with the MIRI instrument on the James Webb Space Telescope. Mid-infrared is the band where a bare rock's own heat appears, so the measurement is of the surface rather than of any gas above it.

Numbers here are blunt. The eclipse depth is 79 parts per million, the nightside flux ratio is 4 parts per million, and the relative phase amplitude is 0.97, all with uncertainties of a few parts per million or a few per cent. A zero-albedo planet with no heat transport would produce exactly this combination. An uncertainty of a few parts per million is what makes a null result meaningful, since the signal that an atmosphere would add is of that order.

Heat recirculation is what the phase amplitude measures. An atmosphere that moves heat from the day side to the night side would raise the nightside flux and shrink the amplitude, and the measured amplitude is consistent with no transport at all. The phase offset, 11 degrees east, is also within the range a bare planet would give. What a phase curve separates is the day side from the night side, and the separation here is as clean as the measurement allows.

Composition gets a separate constraint from the spectrum. It matches a blackbody with a low albedo of about 0.1, apart from one bin that the authors attribute to systematics, and it rules out a carbon dioxide atmosphere above about 1 bar and an outgassed atmosphere above a few millibar or a few hundredths of a millibar depending on the redox state. An outgassed atmosphere is what a hot rock would produce if it retained anything, and the limits are stated per redox condition because the chemistry differs.

What the observations do not do is identify the surface. A blackbody at these wavelengths is nearly featureless, and the spectrum therefore constrains the atmosphere far better than it constrains the mineralogy. The authors are explicit that the composition is left open. Mineralogy would need absorption bands in the spectrum, and a bare rock at this temperature emits a continuum instead.

The inference the paper does draw is that the planet lost most of its volatiles, and with a dayside that should be largely molten, the picture is one of a stripped core rather than a cooled rock. Stripped core is a description rather than a model, and the next step is to ask how much mass the star has removed.
