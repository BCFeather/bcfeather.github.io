description: "Sending clinicians an alert when a deep-learning model read liver fibrosis risk from a routine electrocardiogram roughly doubled new diagnoses of advanced chronic liver disease within 180 days across 98 primary care teams."

Cirrhosis announces itself late. By the time ascites or a variceal bleed brings a patient to a hospital, the disease has usually run for a decade, and the window in which fibrosis can still be slowed has narrowed. The tests that would find it early, elastography or biopsy, cost money and require a reason to order them, and that reason is usually an already-recognized risk: heavy alcohol use, known hepatitis, diagnosed fatty liver. Most advanced disease sits outside those categories until it does not, which is why case-finding strategies that wait for a risk label reach so few people.

In 45 clinics across Minnesota and Wisconsin, the investigators attached a model trained on electrocardiograms, already shown to carry a signal for advanced fibrosis, to ordinary machines and randomized not patients but whole care teams, 98 of them, to receiving a silent alert or nothing at all. The trigger needed no new equipment: an electrocardiogram is taken, paid for and filed in enormous numbers of primary care visits. *Nature Medicine* carried the result on 17 December 2025, from a team led by Douglas A. Simonetto at the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota.

The primary count was new diagnoses of chronic liver disease with advanced fibrosis within 180 days among 15,596 patients, 8,034 in the alert arm and 7,562 in usual care. The alert arm recorded new advanced disease in 1.0 per cent against 0.5 per cent, an odds ratio of 2.09 with a 95 per cent confidence interval from 1.22 to 3.55 and a *P* of 0.007. Among patients the model itself flagged as positive, the rate reached 4.4 per cent against 1.1 per cent, an odds ratio of 4.37, interval 1.94 to 9.88. Any fibrosis, not only advanced, was newly diagnosed in 1.7 per cent against 0.5 per cent, an odds ratio of 3.17, interval 1.86 to 5.40.

Design carries as much of the interest as the numbers. Clustering the randomization by care team, not by patient, matches how an intervention of this kind would actually deploy, since the unit being changed is a workflow. Patients were entered through record linkage without individual consent, which makes the trial a study of a health system noticing something it had in its files. The mechanism is the least explained part: the electrocardiogram is not a liver test, and what the model reads from electrical tracings of the heart, whether humoral signals alter cardiac electrical patterns in fibrosis or something plainer, remains unspecified in the paper.

Worth naming are the limits the authors set out. The modelling population carried a higher prevalence of disease than the community, which is precisely why a positive alert converts to a confirmed case only sometimes; the trial measured diagnoses, not biopsies, and relied mostly on non-invasive criteria without histological confirmation. A prompt to a clinician may itself change follow-up behaviour in ways the design cannot separate. The cohort lacked racial diversity, and the study had no power for decompensation, liver cancer, transplant or death, the outcomes a diagnosis is supposed to improve.

To settle that, a replication in a more diverse population with biopsy-confirmed cases and a hard-outcome follow-up is the obvious next design. The cheaper question, and the one health systems will ask first, is how many false alarms per true case an alert workflow can absorb before clinicians stop acting on it; the ratio of 4.4 per cent confirmed among model-positive patients is the beginning of that arithmetic, not its end.
