Yen fell past 152 per dollar in the second week of April, its weakest level since 1990, and the finance ministry said it was watching the market and would respond to excessive moves. Officials have used that phrase before instructing the central bank to buy the currency, and the market treated it as a warning rather than a promise. The last intervention of comparable size took place in 2022, when the currency approached the same level, and it bought a few weeks rather than a turn.

Interest rate gap is the cause. American short-term rates sit above five per cent while Japanese rates remain near zero, so an investor who borrows in yen and lends in dollars earns the difference, and that trade is what pushes the currency down. A spread that wide attracts capital until something forces it to close. Speculative positioning in the futures market shows the trade is crowded, and a crowded trade is one that unwinds violently when the spread narrows.

Intervention has limits. Japan holds foreign reserves that are large but finite, and past purchases of yen have not changed the direction of the market for more than a few weeks at a time. A reserve is a stock, and the flow that moves the rate every day is far larger than any single sale. The reserves are mostly securities rather than cash, and selling them to buy yen means selling the bonds of an ally, which is a diplomatic cost as well as a financial one. The ministry has not confirmed any operation, and the market's estimate of the amount is based on the change in the central bank's deposits rather than on any disclosure.

Import bill is the cost that reaches households. Japan buys most of its energy and food abroad, and a weaker currency raises the price of both, which means the inflation the central bank spent years trying to create is now arriving faster than it wanted. A cheaper currency is a tax collected at the shop. The wage round in the spring produced increases that the central bank had been waiting for, which is the argument for raising rates even at the cost of a stronger currency.

Exporters benefit, and that is the political economy of the problem. Manufacturers that earn abroad report their profits in yen, and their shares rose as the currency fell, which is why the policy that produces a weak currency has survived so many governments. A lobby that gains from the exchange rate resists every attempt to normalize it. The exporters' shares are held by households through their pension funds, which gives the weak currency a constituency that no finance ministry can ignore.

What the month shows is that a central bank can hold rates at zero only while the rest of the world does not move far above it, and the cost of leaving that policy appears in the exchange rate before it appears in any other statistic. Normalization is now a question of timing rather than of direction, and the exchange rate is the clock that the market is watching.
