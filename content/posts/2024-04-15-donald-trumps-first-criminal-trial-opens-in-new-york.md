---Jury selection began in a Manhattan courtroom on the fifteenth of April in the first criminal trial of a former president of the United States. Donald Trump faces thirty-four felony counts of falsifying business records in the first degree, which prosecutors say he used to conceal a payment of a hundred and thirty thousand dollars to the actress Stormy Daniels before the election of 2016. He has pleaded not guilty and denied the encounter.

Legal theory of the case is unusual and depends on a second offence. Falsifying records is a misdemeanour in New York unless it is done to conceal another crime, and the prosecution says the payment was made to influence the election and that the reimbursements to Michael Cohen, the lawyer who made it, were recorded as legal fees. Judge Juan Merchan ruled before the trial that the second offence could be described as a conspiracy to promote a candidate, which is a state offence, or as a violation of federal election law.

Selection of the jury took four days and was slowed by the defendant's presence and by the publicity around the case. Hundreds of prospective jurors were dismissed after saying they could not be impartial, and the final panel of twelve, with six alternates, was seated on the nineteenth of April. Judge imposed a gag order that barred Trump from attacking witnesses, jurors and court staff, and he fined him for violations of it during the trial.

Witnesses who testified over the following weeks included David Pecker, the former publisher of the National Enquirer, who described a scheme to suppress stories that could damage candidates, and Hope Hicks, a former White House communications director. Cohen testified that Trump had approved the payments and that the records had been falsified, and Daniels described the encounter in detail, which the defence said was irrelevant and prejudicial.

Political setting of the trial is a campaign in which the defendant is the presumptive nominee. Trump attended court four days a week and campaigned on the weekends, and he used the trial to raise money and to argue that the prosecution was an instrument of his opponents. The judge declined to postpone the trial and refused to relax the gag order, and the Secret Service and the court increased security around the building.

The Consequence of the fifteenth of April is a precedent that no other American court has set. The trial put a former president in a criminal courtroom as a defendant, with a jury of ordinary citizens deciding his fate, and it did so while he was running for the office he had lost. A conviction would not prevent him from being elected, and the constitution offers no mechanism for a president to pardon himself in a state case.

What the trial established was that the legal process could proceed against a candidate. Whether it should have, and whether it was brought for political reasons, was the question that the verdict in May answered for the jury. The trial was expected to last about six weeks, with the verdict due in late May.
