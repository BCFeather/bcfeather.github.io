Porphyry copper deposits supply most of the world's copper, and their standard explanation requires a mantle wedge above a subducting slab to supply the metal-bearing magma. A paper in *Nature Geoscience* on 4 November 2024 shows that a major district formed when that wedge was absent. A flat slab is a geometry that removes the source the standard model requires, which is why this district was a problem for the model rather than a confirmation of it.

The district is the Laramide porphyry province of Arizona, which was built during Late Cretaceous and Palaeocene flat-slab subduction. A flat slab lies beneath the continent instead of descending steeply, so the asthenospheric wedge that would normally melt is missing. Thermobarometry on mineral inclusions is the standard route to a source region that has been eroded, and its reliability depends on the calibration chosen for the assemblage.

Geochronology and thermobarometry were used to reconstruct the deep crustal conditions, with ages constraining when melting happened and pressures and temperatures constraining where. Together the two measurements locate a source region that has since been eroded away. Copper inherited from the crust tells a different story from copper delivered by a mantle melt, and the isotopic evidence is what separates the two histories.

Water-fluxed melting of the lower crust is the mechanism the data support. Water released from the slab hydrates the base of the crust, lowers its melting point and produces magmas that carry copper inherited from the crust rather than delivered from the mantle. Water released by a flat slab is a budget that depends on the sediment load and the thermal structure of the slab, both of which are inputs to the model rather than measurements.

The reinterpretation matters for exploration. A district that formed without a wedge is one that a prospectivity model keyed to arc magmatism would rank as unfavourable, and the Laramide belt is large enough for the error to be costly. A prospectivity model is built on the mechanism a district is thought to require, so a revised mechanism changes the ground where a company would stake a claim.

Uncertainty remains in the water budget, since the amount that a flat slab releases depends on its sediment load and thermal structure, and the crustal reservoir is heterogeneous. A second district in the same tectonic setting is the test that the mechanism needs. A second district with the same setting would turn a plausible reinterpretation into a tested one, and the literature contains candidates a comparable study could examine.

What the paper changes is where the metal is thought to come from, and an exploration programme that follows the revised source will look in a different place from one that follows the old one. The practical consequence for a geologist is that the question changes from where the wedge was to where the water went, and that question has a different map.
