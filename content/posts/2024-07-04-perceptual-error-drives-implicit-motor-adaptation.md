Reaching for a cup, a hand corrects itself before the person decides to correct it. The phenomenon is implicit adaptation, and its odd features have resisted explanation for two decades. Small perturbations are over-learned, so the hand overshoots the target it was shifted away from. Large perturbations saturate quickly, and the correction persists after the disturbance is removed. A simple account in which the system minimizes the difference between seen and felt hand position handles none of these well.

A paper in *eLife* on 4 July 2024 proposes that the error being learned is perceptual rather than sensory. Vision and proprioception each give an estimate of where the hand is, the brain combines them in proportion to their reliabilities, and what reaches the adaptation mechanism is that combined estimate. The error the system acts on is therefore a property of the observer, not of the display.

The first experiment tests the premise directly. As a visual perturbation grows, the uncertainty attached to the visual cue grows with it, and that curve is measured through psychophysics rather than assumed. It rises faster than a linear account would predict, which matters because the shape of the curve is the input the rest of the model runs on. 

The second experiment then predicts trial-by-trial learning across a range of perturbation sizes, and the predictions hold. Over-learning at small offsets falls out of the model because visual reliability is high there, so a small offset is perceived almost as given. Saturation at large offsets falls out for the opposite reason, since reliability collapses and the perceptual error stops growing even as the imposed perturbation continues to grow.

A third experiment looks at proprioception. The model predicts how the sensed position of the hand shifts, and how that shift scales with the size of the visual disturbance, which is a signature no sensory-error model produces. The prediction is quantitative, and the measured drift follows it. Because the model ties drift to the reliability of vision, the size of the drift should track the size of the perturbation, and it does so across the tested range.

Experiment four modulates visual uncertainty directly, degrading the visual cue rather than the perturbation, and produces adaptation curves that move in the direction the model specifies. Manipulating the proposed cause is the strongest test in the set, since the earlier experiments observe the cause's correlates.

Generality is the open question. The framework is fitted to a reaching task in healthy adults, and whether it covers force-field learning, gait adaptation or rehabilitation after stroke is not shown. The authors present it as a replacement for the sensory-error account rather than an addition to it, which raises the bar it has to clear. If the account holds, a clinician who wants to drive adaptation should attend to how uncertain the patient's visual feedback is, since that is what sets the size of the error the system will act on. The account also makes a prediction for patients, since a manipulation that sharpens visual feedback should shrink the error the system learns from, and that is a testable clinical claim rather than an interpretive gloss.
