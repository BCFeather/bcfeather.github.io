Near-Earth asteroid (153,201) 2000 WO107 has a spectrum and an albedo that place it in the M class, the taxonomic group associated with metallic bodies. Metal implies a density well above rock, and density is the quantity that links a shape to a bulk composition. Radar had already shown a bilobed echo, which put the object among the contact binaries, two bodies resting against each other rather than one body with a waist. A bilobed shape is evidence about history as well, since contact binaries form either by slow spin-up or by a gentle collision.

*Astronomy & Astrophysics* published the analysis in October 2025. Photometry from the 2020 apparition, taken between 28 November and 8 December at phase angles from 5 to 68 degrees, produced light curves with a maximum amplitude of 1.24 magnitudes. The authors fitted a shape model by Markov chain Monte Carlo and constrained it with Goldstone radar data, so the optical and the radar views had to agree. Markov chain Monte Carlo matters here because a light curve constrains shape weakly on its own, while radar delay data fix the scale and the orientation.

The fitted shape is two ellipsoids with axes of 0.68, 0.38 and 0.36 km, and 0.44, 0.42 and 0.16 km. The sidereal rotation period is 5.017 hours with a range of 0.002 hours. Density comes out at 4.80 grams per cubic centimetre, with an interval from 4.17 to 5.14, which is consistent with a metallic composition and well above any rubble pile. Expressed as a bulk property, that density is close to what a nickel-iron body would give and roughly twice what a stony asteroid would.

Spin and shape do the work. A contact binary's lobes rotate as one body, so the light curve carries the joint period and the ratio of lobe sizes, while the radar echo fixes the absolute scale that photometry alone cannot supply. Density then follows from the volume the shape encloses and the mass implied by the mutual motion. The pole admits two solutions, one at ecliptic latitude near minus 78 degrees and another near minus 76, differing in longitude by 190 degrees. Both solutions fit the light curve, and only radar taken at a different geometry separates them.

What the study does not settle is composition. M-class objects include metallic cores and also bodies whose surfaces are dark and featureless for other reasons, and density separates metal from rock without identifying the metal. One apparition supplies the light curves, so seasonal effects and any tumbling go unconstrained, and the second pole solution remains admissible. The M class is a spectral label that has been revised repeatedly, and membership rests on albedo and radar reflectivity, both indirect.

An orbital calculation puts the probability of a collision with Earth in the next 10,000 years at 7 in 100,000, small but non-zero, which is why the object is classified as potentially hazardous. A radar campaign at the next apparition would shrink the shape error and could decide between the two poles, and thermal infrared data would add the albedo that density cannot provide. Its orbit and its size make it a plausible target for a rendezvous mission, and a landed measurement would settle the composition question that remote sensing cannot.
