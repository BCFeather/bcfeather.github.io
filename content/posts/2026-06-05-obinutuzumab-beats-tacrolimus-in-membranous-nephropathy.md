Primary membranous nephropathy is an autoimmune disease in which antibodies against the phospholipase A2 receptor deposit in the glomerulus and damage the filtration barrier. Proteinuria follows, and the risk of kidney failure rises with it, so the treatment is aimed at the antibody-producing B cells. The antibody titre against the receptor is the marker that predicts response, and it falls when B-cell depletion works. Proteinuria is the variable that clinicians follow, and a partial remission that lowers it substantially also lowers the risk of progression.

Rituximab has been the biologic of choice, given as one or two courses, and calcineurin inhibitors such as tacrolimus are the alternative when a patient needs proteinuria to fall quickly. The trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 5 June compared a second-generation anti-CD20 antibody against tacrolimus.

Obinutuzumab is glycoengineered for stronger B-cell depletion than rituximab, and the trial randomized 142 patients. At week 104, complete remission was observed in 26 of 71 patients on obinutuzumab and 4 of 70 on tacrolimus, 37 against 6 per cent with multiple imputation, an adjusted difference of 31 percentage points. Multiple imputation was used because some patients withdrew, and the adjusted difference accounts for the baseline proteinuria that predicts remission. Completing a two-year course is a commitment, and the patients who withdrew are counted in the imputation.

Comparison against tacrolimus rather than rituximab was deliberate. Tacrolimus controls proteinuria while it is given and relapse follows when it stops, which is the pattern the trial was designed to beat, and the two-year time point is long enough for that pattern to appear. Tacrolimus also has a narrow therapeutic window and requires drug-level monitoring, which is a burden that a monthly infusion does not impose. A two-year endpoint also captures the relapses that occur when tacrolimus is withdrawn, which is the weakness the comparison was designed to expose.

Safety is the other half of the story. Anti-CD20 therapy carries infusion reactions and infection risk, and steroid exposure differs between arms. A drug that produces remission in a third of patients at two years is worth its risk in a disease where the alternative is dialysis. A third of patients in complete remission still leaves two-thirds who need something else, and the trial does not say what.

Sequencing is the practical question. If obinutuzumab achieves a third of patients in complete remission at two years, it becomes the first-line biologic, and rituximab becomes the option for patients who do not respond or who relapse. The trial does not settle duration of follow-up, and membranous nephropathy can relapse years after remission, which is why registries matter as much as trials here. Cost is another sequencing input, since obinutuzumab is substantially more expensive per course than rituximab and the health service will ask for the difference in outcomes. The antibody titre is also the marker that trials are starting to use as an endpoint, which would make future comparisons faster and cheaper.
