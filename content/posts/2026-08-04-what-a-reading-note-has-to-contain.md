Most posts here are reading notes on papers, and the form has a reputation it does not deserve. A note is often taken to be a summary with a citation attached, which is what a press release already provides. The version worth writing records what was measured, on whom or on what, with what comparison, and where the inference stops, because those four things are what a reader needs a year later when the paper is being cited in an argument. The distinction is easy to state and hard to keep, because a summariser is rewarded for fluency and a note-taker is rewarded for precision, and the two rewards rarely coincide.

A note begins with the state of the question rather than the state of the world. The useful opening answers why the paper had to exist: which measurement was too imprecise, which trial had failed, which model could not be tested with the data available. That framing is not decoration, since it decides which of the paper's numbers matter and which are description.

The number comes early and with its interval. An effect size without a confidence interval is a claim about direction, and a hazard ratio without its comparator is unreadable, so the note has to name the group, the duration and the endpoint in the same sentence. Where a trial reports a composite, the components deserve a line, because a composite that moves for one component flatters the others. Where the authors report a per-protocol analysis next to an intention-to-treat one, the gap between them is often the most informative number in the paper.

Mechanism is the part that separates a note from an abstract. A result that cannot be explained is still a result, but a reader needs to know whether the explanation is a measured pathway or a plausible story, and the difference is usually visible in the methods. Where the authors infer a mechanism from a knockout, the note should say so plainly, since the inference and the assay are not the same thing.

What the paper does not show belongs in the note as a paragraph, not a caveat sentence. Sample size against prevalence, follow-up against the natural history of the disease, the population against the one the clinic actually sees: each of these limits the conclusion in a specific way, and a note that omits them reads as publicity for the authors' interpretation. A note that names the missing subgroup is doing the reader a service that the abstract cannot, since abstracts are written to survive a skim.

The test of a good note is whether it can be used without the paper. A reader should be able to take the number, the design and the limit and argue with someone else, and if the note cannot support that, it has recorded the press release rather than the result. That is the standard this site tries to hold, imperfectly and in public.
