Store brands began as a price play: a can of tomatoes with a plain label, made by the same plant as the branded one, sold for 30 per cent less. The chain reporting 38 per cent own-brand penetration this month has built something different, a three-tier range in which the premium line is priced above the national brands it imitates and the standard line is priced below.

Margin arithmetic explains the shift. A national brand's price includes the manufacturer's marketing budget and the retailer's margin on a wholesale price; a store brand removes the first term and captures the second as its own gross margin. Retailers report own-brand gross margins 8 to 12 points above branded equivalents at similar shelf prices.

Consumer acceptance is the variable that had to change. Surveys of the chain's shoppers put taste parity with national brands at 71 per cent for the premium tier, and the chain has invested in blind trials and in reformulating the standard tier, which is where the older stigma persists.

Supplier capacity is the bottleneck. A retailer that wants a premium own-brand line needs a manufacturer willing to run a small batch to a specification, and the plants capable of that are the same plants that make the national brands. Those manufacturers often decline, because the contract would cannibalize a brand they also produce.

Category differences are sharp. Own-brand penetration runs above 60 per cent in milk, flour and frozen vegetables, where branding adds little, and below 15 per cent in carbonated soft drinks and infant formula, where brand trust is the product. The premium tier has grown fastest in coffee and prepared foods, which are categories where a specification can be written.

The branded manufacturers' response has been to consolidate and to invest in varieties the retailer cannot easily copy, and to negotiate shelf-position agreements that limit own-brand placement. Both are marketing instruments, and neither changes the underlying cost gap.

Risk sits in quality control. A retailer that moves from 26 to 38 per cent own-brand volume is now a food manufacturer by procurement, and a recall of its own label hits the store's reputation directly rather than the brand's. The chain has added a supplier audit programme and a testing laboratory, which are costs the margin calculation usually omits.

Contract structure decides who carries the risk. A retailer that specifies a recipe and a price usually retains the recall liability, and a supplier that runs the line carries the manufacturing risk. The chain's audit programme costs about 0.4 per cent of own-brand sales, which is a real number against a margin advantage of eight points.

For a reader, the number to watch is the premium tier's share, not the total. A store brand that wins on price alone is a cyclical response to inflation; one that wins at a higher price point is a permanent change in how the category is structured, and the branded manufacturers' margins are the place it shows.
