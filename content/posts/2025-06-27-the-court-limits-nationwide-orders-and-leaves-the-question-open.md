description: "The Supreme Court ruled 6-3 on 27 June that federal judges may not issue universal injunctions that bind the government everywhere, sending the birthright citizenship case back to lower courts."
---

A case about who is a citizen became a case about what a judge may order, and the court decided the second question on 27 June without deciding the first. In a 6-3 decision, the justices held that a federal district court may not issue a universal injunction that grants relief to parties who are not before it, and returned the dispute over the president's executive order on birthright citizenship to the lower courts for narrower proceedings.

Order at issue had sought to deny citizenship to children born in the United States to parents who were in the country unlawfully, which is a reading of the Fourteenth Amendment that most constitutional scholars reject and that several district courts had blocked. The court did not reach the merits, which means the order's legality is still unsettled and the practical effect for the families involved depends on the next round.

Remedy is the part that will outlast the case. Universal injunctions had become the standard tool of litigation against the executive, used dozens of times in the first months of the administration, and the majority's objection was institutional: a single district judge should not be able to set national policy while an appeal is pending. A rule that limits the tool limits every case that follows it, including cases brought by plaintiffs the majority might sympathise with.

Dissent framed the stakes differently. The three liberal justices argued that a nationwide order is sometimes the only way to prevent irreparable harm to people who cannot sue in every district, and that the majority had substituted a procedural preference for a substantive right. A child born in a hospital in Texas does not have the resources to litigate in four circuits.

Practical consequence is a shift to class actions and to appeals. Plaintiffs will now seek certifying classes, which binds relief to identifiable people, and cases will move to the circuit courts faster, which is slower for the individual and more orderly for the government. A system that produces fewer sweeping orders produces more precedents, and precedents are what the next administration inherits.

Politics are already written. The president had campaigned on the order and on the judges who blocked it, and the decision gives both sides a sentence: the government can say the courts overreached, and the plaintiffs can say the right is still unresolved. A decision that decides procedure is a decision that both coalitions can claim.

What would settle the substance is the appeal that follows, which will reach the same court within a year on the merits of the citizenship clause. The country has been here before, more than a century ago, when the court held that a child born here is a citizen, and that precedent has not been overruled. A government that loses a procedural case can still win the policy, and a government that wins a procedural case can still lose the right, which is why both sides spent the week claiming the same ruling.
