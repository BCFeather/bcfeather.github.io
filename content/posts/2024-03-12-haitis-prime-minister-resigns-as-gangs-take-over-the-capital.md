---Ariel Henry agreed on the eleventh of March to resign as prime minister of Haiti, ending a tenure that had lasted since the assassination of President Jovenel Moïse in July 2021 and that had become untenable as armed groups took control of most of the capital. Announcement was made from Jamaica, where he had been stranded after gangs closed the airport in Port-au-Prince, and it came at a meeting of Caribbean leaders who had been pressing him to step aside.

Violence that forced the decision had been building for weeks. Gangs led by Jimmy Chérizier, a former police officer known as Barbecue, launched a coordinated offensive at the end of February, attacking the airport, the national palace, police stations and the main prison, from which several thousand inmates escaped. Ports closed, food prices rose, and hospitals stopped admitting patients. More than fifteen thousand people were displaced from their homes in the capital within a fortnight.

Political vacuum that allowed the offensive predates the resignation. Haiti has not held an election since 2016, and the parliament expired in 2023, which left Henry governing by decree with a mandate that he had never been given by voters. He had promised to hold elections in 2023 and then postponed them repeatedly, citing insecurity and the cost of organizing a vote, and the opposition had accused him of using the crisis to remain in office.

Plan that replaced him is a transitional presidential council of seven members, appointed by political parties and civil society groups, with a prime minister chosen by the council and a mandate to organize elections. Council was created at the meeting in Jamaica with the support of the United States, the regional body CARICOM and the United Nations, and it took weeks to name its members because the parties could not agree on who would be represented.

International response the crisis had been waiting for was slow. A Kenyan-led police mission, authorized by the Security Council in October 2023, was delayed by Kenyan courts and by a lack of funding, and it began to deploy only in the summer. American and Canadian officials said they would not send troops, and the administration in Washington said it would provide money and equipment rather than personnel. Mission's mandate is to support the Haitian police, which has fewer than ten thousand officers for a country of eleven million people.

The Consequence of the twelfth of March is a government that has to be built while the state is absent. The transitional council must appoint a cabinet, negotiate with the gangs or fight them, and prepare an election in a country where half the population is food insecure and where the main port and airport cannot operate reliably. Haiti has been through transitions of this kind before, and each one has left the institutions weaker. Whether this one is different depends on whether the police mission arrives in enough strength to matter, and on whether the council can act faster than the armed groups that made it necessary.
