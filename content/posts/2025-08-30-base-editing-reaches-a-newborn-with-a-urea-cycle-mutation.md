A newborn with a mutation in the gene CPS1 was treated with a base editor delivered by lipid nanoparticles, according to a case reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* and discussed in *Nature* on 28 August. The child had a severe form of a urea-cycle disorder, in which ammonia accumulates because the enzyme that clears it is missing or defective, and high ammonia causes brain damage within days.

The sequence of events was unusually fast. Rapid genomic sequencing of the newborn found two mutations, one inherited from each parent, and the paternal one was of the type that an adenine base editor can correct: a single adenine that should be a guanine. The team, led by Kiran Musunuru and colleagues, designed a guide RNA and an editor protein for that specific change, tested them in cell lines and in mice, and produced a clinical-grade formulation in a little over six months.

Delivery was the hard part. A base editor is a large molecule and will not cross a cell membrane on its own, so it was packaged in lipid nanoparticles and infused. The particles accumulate in the liver, which is where CPS1 is expressed and where the ammonia is produced, so the targeting problem was solved by anatomy rather than by a ligand. A lipid nanoparticle is a delivery vehicle with a preference, and in this case the preference matched the organ where the defective enzyme is made.

The infant's ammonia levels fell and the episodes of hyperammonemia became less frequent, which is the clinical endpoint that matters in this disease. The report is a single case, and the follow-up is short, so the durability of the edit and the long-term safety of the editor are open questions. Base editors can make off-target changes, and a liver that has been edited once cannot be edited again if the first attempt was incomplete. Editing a population of liver cells is not an all-or-nothing event, and the fraction corrected determines how much ammonia the child can clear.

The regulatory pathway is as interesting as the biology. A therapy designed for one patient is not a product in the ordinary sense: it has no fixed dose for a population, no batch consistency, and no way to run a randomized trial. Regulators in the United States and Europe have begun to create platforms for these N-of-1 treatments, and this case is the clearest demonstration so far that the platform can deliver.

Cost is the unresolved part. An individualized therapy requires a manufacturing run for one person, with release testing and documentation that would normally be amortized across thousands of doses. The groups involved have argued that a standardized platform, in which only the guide RNA changes, can bring the price down to something a health system can pay. That argument is now testable, and the first data point suggests that the timeline, at least, can be compressed to months rather than years.
