The president signed the Inflation Reduction Act into law on Tuesday, and the ceremony took place in the state dining room rather than in the open air. The bill had passed the Senate on the previous Sunday by a single vote and the House on Friday by two hundred and twenty to two hundred and seven. It spends about four hundred and thirty-seven billion dollars over ten years and raises about seven hundred and thirty billion, and the difference is supposed to reduce the deficit. The package is smaller than the one that the president proposed in his first year and larger than the one that the Senate had been expected to accept.

The climate provisions are the largest part of the spending. They are written as tax credits rather than as a programme, and they cover wind and solar plants, batteries, heat pumps, electric vehicles and the factories that make them. The credits are uncapped, which means the cost will depend on how many projects are built, and the estimates run from three hundred and seventy billion to more than four hundred. The bill also offers a rebate for the purchase of a used electric car for the first time, and it requires the minerals and the components to come from countries with which the United States has a trade agreement.

At the same time, the health provisions are the ones that the voters will notice. The bill lets the government negotiate the price of a small number of drugs for the elderly programme, beginning with ten medicines and rising to twenty, and it caps the cost of insulin at thirty-five dollars a month. It also extends the subsidy for health insurance for three years, which prevents a rise in premiums that was due in January. The drug industry has said it will challenge the negotiation in court, and the administration has said it is confident that the law is written to survive.

Meanwhile, the taxes fall on companies and on the very rich. The bill sets a minimum tax of fifteen per cent on the profits that large corporations report to their shareholders, and it adds a one per cent tax on the buyback of shares. It also spends eighty billion dollars on the tax service, which the administration says will recover several times that amount from people who do not pay what they owe. The Republicans said the tax service would use the money to audit ordinary families, and the administration published a plan that it said would prevent that.

Over ten years, the deficit effect is the argument that the bill's supporters use and the one that its opponents dispute. The congressional scorekeeper said the package would reduce the deficit by about three hundred billion dollars over ten years, and the analysts who model the economy said the effect would be smaller because the credits would push up the cost of the projects. The bill is named for inflation and its effect on prices in the next year is expected to be small. The name was chosen by its authors for a reason that has more to do with the campaign than with the arithmetic.

What the bill does is put a climate policy into law after a decade of failure. The last attempt, in 2009, was a cap and trade scheme that died in the Senate, and the one before that was a tax that never reached a vote. The credits are a different instrument and they are harder to repeal, because the industries that benefit will defend them. The money will begin to flow next year, and the projects that it pays for will be built in the districts of the senators who voted against it.
