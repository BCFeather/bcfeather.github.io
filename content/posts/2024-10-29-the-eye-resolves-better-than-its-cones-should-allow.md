The fovea is where human vision is sharpest, and its acuity has usually been explained by cone density and by the optics that focus light onto it. A paper in *eLife* on 29 October 2024 adds a third term, the motion of the eye during fixation, and finds that the motion improves resolution rather than blurring it. Cone density sets an upper bound only if the sampling grid is fixed, and the eye is not a fixed grid.

Cone-resolved imaging was combined with microscopic photo stimulation in both eyes of sixteen participants, and the stimulus path across the retina was recorded as the measurement ran. Recording the stimulus path is what makes the drift measurable, since a movement that is not tracked cannot be related to the pattern that was seen.

Acuity tracked sampling capacity, which is the expected result, and it exceeded the limit that static sampling would predict by 18 per cent on average. That excess is the anomaly the paper explains. Eighteen per cent is a large excess for a system already at its optical limit, and the number is the reason the result is interesting.

Fixational drift does the work. The small movements that the eye makes while it is apparently still were thought to be random, and their length and direction turn out to be structured in a way that lets a stimulus be sampled at more positions than a fixed array of cones would allow. Structured drift is a hypothesis about statistics rather than about anatomy, and the statistics can be estimated from the recordings.

Adaptive sampling is the description, and it is the same idea that appears in imaging, where a detector is moved to fill in the gaps that a fixed one leaves. Adaptive sampling is a familiar idea in engineering, and finding it in the eye is a case of biology reaching a solution that instrument designers found too.

The measurement is psychophysical as well as anatomical, which is what makes the claim strong. A result that combined acuity thresholds with cone maps could only be explained by a mechanism that used both. Psychophysics and anatomy together are what make the claim testable, since either alone would leave the excess unexplained.

Generalisation beyond the foveola is unclear, since the periphery has lower cone density and different drift statistics, and the benefit may be confined to the few degrees where resolution is already extreme. The clinical reading concerns patients whose fixation is unstable, since a mechanism that depends on structured drift would be degraded by drift that is unstructured, and that is a hypothesis worth testing in nystagmus. Testing the prediction in nystagmus would require patients whose drift is large and irregular, and that comparison is a natural experiment.
