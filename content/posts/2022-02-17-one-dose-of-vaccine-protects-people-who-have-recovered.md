---

Recovery from infection leaves immunity, and policy nevertheless recommended vaccination for people who had recovered. The recommendation rested on the observation that natural immunity varies between individuals and on the finding that hybrid immunity is broader, not on a measured estimate of how much a dose adds. The policy also had a practical motive, since a documented prior infection is not always available and a dose is easy to record.

An Israeli retrospective cohort supplied that estimate. Electronic records from a large health organization identified 149,032 people who had recovered from SARS-CoV-2 before any vaccination, and compared reinfection among those who subsequently received BNT162b2 with those who did not, between 1 March and 26 November 2021. A Cox model with time-dependent covariates adjusted for demographic factors and coexisting illness. The paper appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 16 February 2022.

During 270 days, 83,356 of the recovered patients, or 56 per cent, were vaccinated. Reinfection occurred in 354 vaccinated patients, a rate of 2.46 cases per 100,000 persons per day, and in 2,168 of 65,676 unvaccinated patients, a rate of 10.21 per 100,000 per day. Vaccine effectiveness was 82 per cent in those aged 16 to 64, with an interval from 80 to 84, and 60 per cent in those aged 65 or older, with an interval from 36 to 76. The rates are given per person-day, which normalizes for the different follow-up times that arise in a cohort assembled from records.

One dose performed as well as two in this population, which is the result that makes the finding useful for policy. A recovered immune system already holds memory B and T cells, and a single exposure is enough to broaden and raise that response, so a second dose adds little in the short term.

The design has the weaknesses of observational data. People who chose to be vaccinated may differ in behaviour and in health-seeking from those who did not, and the adjustment covers recorded illnesses rather than everything that affects infection risk. Reinfection was counted when a test was positive, so asymptomatic cases in people who did not test are invisible. The cohort is large enough that the confidence interval is narrow in the younger group and wide in the oldest.

The follow-up ends before Omicron, which changes the calculus because the variant escapes much of the antibody response. Waning is also not addressed, and the 270-day window is short for a question about duration. Omicron escapes much of the neutralizing antibody response, so the protection measured here applies to a variant that no longer circulates.

For a country with limited supply, the result supports one dose for the recovered and the rest for the susceptible. For an individual, it is evidence that the recommendation was not merely precautionary. Countries that adopted a single dose for the recovered saved supply and spared people who had been ill the reactogenicity of a second dose.
