Order on a curved surface cannot be uniform. A field of molecular orientation laid on a sphere or a vesicle must contain points where the direction is undefined, and those points are topological defects whose charge is fixed by the topology of the surface. What has been less clear is how the defects act back on the shape that hosts them.

A preprint posted on 27 December 2023 treats the feedback in equilibrium. A fluid membrane carries a nematic field with an integer defect, and the elastic energy of the field competes with the bending energy of the surface. When the Frank constants, which set the cost of distorting the orientation field, dominate, the surface deforms spontaneously into a cone with an aster at the apex.

An aster is a defect in which the director points radially outward from the core, so a cone with an aster at its tip is the shape in which the orientation field is least distorted on a pointed surface. At vanishing tension the conical solution satisfies the condition of normal force balance, which means that the membrane is in mechanical equilibrium and not merely at an energy minimum of the field.

Stability is not automatic. It depends on the ratio of elastic parameters and on the phase of the defect, so a cone that forms can also flatten again if the bending stiffness rises, and the transition between the two is continuous in the parameters, not a switch.

Adding boundary constraints produces three distinct modes of deformation. Each mode exploits the fact that splay, twist and bend can be exchanged on a curved surface: a distortion that costs a lot in one type can be converted into another type by bending the membrane, which is why the shape responds to a constraint that the field alone could not satisfy.

Fusion of two half-integer defects lowers the total energy of a deformable surface, so defects can drive coarsening as well as shaping. Two plus-one-half defects that merge into a charge-one defect relieve distortion, and if the membrane is free to bend, the relief shows up as a change of shape rather than a change of field alone.

Relevance to biology is argued and not demonstrated. Nematic order appears in actin and in lipid systems, and cells and vesicles do change shape, so a mechanism that couples a defect to curvature is a candidate for morphogenesis. Continuum equilibrium theory, however, cannot say whether a living system uses it, and an active system with molecular motors would add terms that the paper does not include. Limits follow from the idealization. The membrane is fluid, the field is nematic, the defect is prescribed and the theory is static, so dynamics, activity and the creation of defects are outside the treatment. The paper supplies a catalogue of equilibrium shapes and the conditions under which each is stable, which is the input a more detailed model would need.
