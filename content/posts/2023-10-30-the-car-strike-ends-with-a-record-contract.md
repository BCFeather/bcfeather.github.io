The United Auto Workers reached a tentative agreement with General Motors on 30 October, the third in six days, and the strike that had run for forty-six days against the three Detroit carmakers came to an end. Ford settled on 25 October after forty-one days, Stellantis on 28 October, and each deal follows the pattern of the first: a 25 per cent increase in base wages through April 2028, a top rate above forty dollars an hour, the restoration of cost-of-living adjustments, and a faster path to permanent status for temporary workers. The agreements cover about 146,000 workers and end a strike that had spread to plants in five states.

Its strategy was to strike selectively rather than everywhere. Instead of walking out all 146,000 members at once, it called strikes at a handful of plants whose output fed the rest, which forced the companies to furlough workers elsewhere and multiplied the cost without exhausting the union's strike fund. The companies said the approach cost them billions in lost production, and the union said it kept the fund solvent long enough to outlast them.

Wages matter less to the industry than one clause about battery plants. At General Motors the agreement brings the battery plants owned through the Ultium joint venture into the master contract, which means workers assembling electric vehicle cells will be paid on the same scale as those building engines. That has been the central question of the transition: whether the electric car industry will be a union industry with middle-class wages or a non-union one with lower pay, and the answer in Detroit is now partly settled.

Ratification votes are the next test. Ford's members approved their contract with about two thirds in favour, and the votes at the other two companies are running through November. Rejection would send negotiators back, and the possibility has been taken seriously because the agreement on wages arrived alongside concessions on temporary workers that some locals consider insufficient.

For the companies the cost is measurable and the benefit is uncertain. Labour costs per vehicle will rise by several hundred dollars, which they have said they will recover through prices, cost cuts and automation. The carmakers are simultaneously spending tens of billions on electric platforms that are selling more slowly than planned, and the strike interrupted production of some of their most profitable trucks at the moment when dealers were short of inventory. Parts suppliers, many of them non-union, had furloughed workers as the assembly lines stopped.

For the union the settlement is the largest gain in decades and a demonstration that a different kind of strike can work. Its president has said the next targets are non-union plants owned by foreign carmakers in the South, and the contracts expire in April 2028. The wage pattern that was set this week will be the reference in every other manufacturing negotiation in between. Its next organizing drive will test whether the pattern can be repeated outside Detroit.
