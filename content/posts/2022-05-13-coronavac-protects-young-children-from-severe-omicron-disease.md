description: "In 490,694 Chilean children aged three to five during the Omicron wave, two doses of CoronaVac were 38.2 per cent effective against symptomatic COVID, 64.6 per cent against hospitalization and 69.0 per cent against intensive care admission."
---

Chile vaccinated children aged three to five with CoronaVac while Omicron was circulating, which gave the country a population large enough to estimate effectiveness in an age group that most trials had excluded. The result is the first national estimate for an inactivated vaccine in this age band. A country that vaccinated young children early in the Omicron wave produced the natural experiment that a randomized trial would never have delivered, and the cohort is large enough to estimate rare outcomes.

A national cohort included 490,694 children, followed from 6 December 2021 to 26 February 2022, and the analysis used inverse probability weighted survival models with time-varying vaccination exposure. The paper appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 23 May 2022.

Effectiveness against symptomatic COVID was 38.2 per cent, with an interval from 36.5 to 39.9, against hospitalization 64.6 per cent, and against admission to intensive care 69.0 per cent. The interval around the last estimate is wide, from 18.6 to 88.2, because intensive care admissions in this age group are rare. A modest effectiveness against infection is what the inactivated platform has consistently shown against Omicron, and the reason is the narrow repertoire that a whole-virus immunogen elicits against a heavily mutated spike.

A familiar gradient appears here. Protection against infection is modest for an inactivated vaccine against Omicron, and protection against the outcomes that matter is substantial. A parent who is told that the vaccine is 38 per cent effective will hear a weak product, and the number that should be quoted is the one for hospitalization.

Time-varying exposure is the methodological detail that makes the estimate credible, because a child who is vaccinated during the follow-up contributes person-time to both groups and the model has to account for that. A naive comparison would attribute the healthy-vaccinee effect to the vaccine. A model that lets exposure change over time is a model that can handle a child who was unvaccinated in December and vaccinated in January without misclassifying that child's person-time.

One country, one vaccine and one variant define the cohort and one variant, and the follow-up is three months. A waning analysis would need longer, and the question of a third dose for this age group is not addressed. An estimate that rests on a small number of intensive care admissions is an estimate that a single additional case would move, and the width of the interval is the honest description of that fragility. A three-month follow-up cannot separate a vaccine that never worked from one that worked and then waned, and the second possibility is the one that the next paper will address.

What the authors conclude is that vaccination of children aged three to five is justified by the protection against severe illness, and that layered measures remain necessary because the protection against infection is partial. A decision about vaccinating a healthy child is made by a parent who weighs a small risk of myocarditis against a small risk of severe COVID, and the paper provides the denominator for that comparison.
