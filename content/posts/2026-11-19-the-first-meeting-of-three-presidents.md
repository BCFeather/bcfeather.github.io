Three presidents sat in one room in Shenzhen on the afternoon of 19 November, the closing day of the APEC leaders' meeting: the host's president, Russia's president and the president of the United States. The host's readout described the session as the first gathering of the three heads of state in history, the same description the foreign ministry used when it announced the meeting on 16 November. The two days before it had gone to bilaterals.

The arrangement took shape over two weeks. On 4 November the foreign ministry said it was in communication with the relevant parties about a possible meeting. On 16 November it named the date and the city. The session followed the leaders' meeting that the host had chaired that morning, and it drew the three into one room after a week in which the host had met each of the other two separately.

In his remarks the host set the common ground in institutional terms: the three are founding members of the United Nations and permanent members of the Security Council, and carry a responsibility the other economies at the forum do not. The two relationships already in place were named as the tracks, a comprehensive strategic partnership with Russia and a constructive strategic stability relationship with the United States.

The bilaterals carried the paper. On 18 November the host met the Russian president for the fourth time this year, and the readout listed record bilateral trade, visa-free travel and an education exchange year. On the 19th he met the American president, and the two agreed to draw up an action plan for the constructive strategic stability relationship, a document with a name and a date still to be set.

A table of three has a lighter frame than a forum of twenty-one. There is no trilateral secretariat, no agenda document circulated in advance, no draft for the three to initial, and no panel to hear a complaint. What such a table can produce is a shared reading of the moment and an instruction to officials, which is why the sentence worth watching in any readout is the one that names a follow-up.

Next door sat the week's written output. The leaders' meeting was built around the Shenzhen Declaration, the end of a chain that ran through about three hundred events, ten ministerial meetings and twenty-five trade outcomes, among them an electronic port pilot that cut declaration costs by 66 per cent. The business summit that preceded it, on 17 and 18 November, issued its own Shenzhen Initiative, the first time in thirty years that the gathering published its conclusions in that form. That record is reviewable: dates, indicators, reviews. The afternoon's record is spoken.

What happens next turns on three things: whether a second trilateral is scheduled and who calls it, whether the action plan is published as a text, and whether a sentence from that room enters a document a ministry can cite next year. Until one of those happens, the meeting stands as a first, and the point of a first meeting is that it makes a second possible.
