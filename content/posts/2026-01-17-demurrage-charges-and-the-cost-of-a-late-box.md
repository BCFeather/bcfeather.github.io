Demurrage is the charge a container accrues when it stays at a terminal beyond the free period, and detention is the charge when it stays out of the terminal beyond the return date. Both have risen faster than freight rates, and a survey published this month by a freight audit firm puts the average bill at 780 dollars per late import container.

The mechanics are contractual. A carrier's bill of lading specifies free days, typically five to seven at the destination port, and thereafter a daily rate that steps up. A container held for fifteen days can accrue 2,000 dollars, which is more than the ocean freight on many routes.

Why the bills have grown is a capacity story on the land side. Terminal yards have less space, appointment systems have replaced first-come access, and a chassis shortage means a box sits until equipment arrives. The survey found that 62 per cent of demurrage days were caused by a factor other than the importer's own delay.

Disputes follow from that. Importers argue that the charge is a penalty for something outside their control, and regulators in two jurisdictions have opened inquiries into whether the practice constitutes an unfair term in a contract of adhesion. Carriers argue that free time is a commercial concession and that the charge is the mechanism that keeps boxes moving.

The avoidable portion is the finding importers care about. The audit firm classifies each day of demurrage by cause, and finds that 31 per cent of days are attributable to documentation errors, incorrect commodity codes and missed appointments, which are fixable with process rather than money.

Mitigation is increasingly software. Importers that run a demurrage dashboard, tracking free days and terminal dwell per container, report bills 45 per cent lower than comparable importers without one. The tool is mundane and the effect is large, which is a common pattern in logistics.

For carriers, the revenue is meaningful and the optics are poor. Demurrage and detention accounted for about 6 per cent of one large carrier's revenue last year, and the charges are counter-cyclical: they rise when congestion is worst and fall when terminals are quiet.

Terminal incentives are the piece importers rarely control. A terminal's contract with a carrier sets a dwell-time target and a penalty for exceeding it, and the terminal responds by moving boxes to an off-dock yard, where the clock on free time keeps running. Importers report that the charge begins while the box is in a yard they did not choose.

Documentation is a cheap fix with a measurable effect. A pilot at one forwarder moved customs pre-clearance from arrival to the pre-arrival window and cut average free-time overrun from 4.2 days to 1.1. That is a process change worth more than any dispute over the rate.

For a reader, the economic point is that a late fee is a price for a scarce resource, terminal space, and prices of that kind are set by whoever controls the queue. When the queue is long the fee becomes a profit centre, and the regulatory question is whether the party charging it can also control the cause.
