Steelmaking produces hydrogen as a by-product and consumes it as a reductant, which makes a steel plant an unusual host for an electrolyzer. A 12-megawatt solid-oxide stack began operating inside a working plant this month, producing 4,800 cubic metres of hydrogen an hour from steam and the plant's own waste heat.

Solid-oxide cells are the choice because of temperature. The stack operates at 750 degrees Celsius, where part of the energy for splitting water comes in as heat rather than electricity, so the electrical demand per kilogram of hydrogen is about 20 per cent lower than a low-temperature alkaline or PEM system. The plant's reheating furnaces supply that heat without extra fuel.

The economics are decided by the oxygen. A solid-oxide stack produces pure oxygen alongside hydrogen, and the plant's basic-oxygen converters consume large volumes of it. Valued at the plant's own supply contract, the oxygen credit covers roughly a third of the stack's operating cost, which is what turns a marginal hydrogen project into a plausible one.

Scale is modest and deliberately so. Twelve megawatts is a pilot, sized to the plant's hydrogen demand for one reheating furnace, and the operators describe the target as proving the integration rather than the cost. A commercial installation would be 200 megawatts and would need a dedicated oxygen pipeline.

Degradation is the known weakness. Solid-oxide stacks lose voltage over time as the electrolyte and electrodes coarsen, and this unit's contract specifies a degradation rate below 1 per cent per 1,000 hours. The operators have run 2,000 hours and report 0.8, which is within specification and short of the 5,000 hours a bankable project needs.

Grid interaction is the second variable. A 12-megawatt load that responds within minutes is useful for absorbing surplus renewable generation, and the plant has signed an agreement to curtail when the local grid operator calls. Curtailment is paid at a fraction of the hydrogen value, which bounds how often the arrangement is worth accepting.

Cost of the hydrogen is 4.10 dollars per kilogram at current electricity prices, against 2.20 for the plant's incumbent steam-methane reforming, before the oxygen credit. With the credit the gap narrows to 0.60, and the plant's argument is that a carbon price of 40 dollars per tonne of emissions closes it.

A second constraint is water. The stack's feed is demineralized water at 90 per cent quality, and the plant's existing treatment supplies it at a rate that also serves the boiler house. Two thirds of the plant's hydrogen demand could be met before the water supply becomes the limit, which is a lower ceiling than the electrical capacity suggests.

The 12-megawatt figure is also the stack's minimum economic size. A smaller unit's balance-of-plant, including the heat exchanger and the control system, would cost more than the cells themselves, and the plant's engineers describe 40 megawatts as the size at which the unit stops being a demonstration.

For a reader, the interesting feature is the coupling. Electrolysis is usually discussed as a standalone plant producing hydrogen for a market; here it is a unit inside an industrial site, consuming that site's heat and supplying that site's gas and oxygen. That kind of integration is where the first viable projects are appearing, and it depends on the plant's own material balance rather than on a hydrogen market that does not yet exist.
