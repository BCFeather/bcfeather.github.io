Three days after the doors opened, the twenty-eighth China Hi-Tech Fair closed in Shenzhen on 28 November with 175 billion yuan of intended transactions and investment, 570 agreements signed on the floor, and 480,000 professional visits across three days, 45,000 of them from outside China. The organizers counted 1,365 buyer delegations from more than 120 countries and regions, the number that explains how a single floor produces a total of that size inside three days, and it is the figure that turns visits into meetings.

Divide the intent by the agreements and each is worth about 307 million yuan; divide it by the visits and each visitor accounts for roughly 364,000 yuan. Those ratios describe the fair's function: a matching floor where the unit of trade is a meeting that may become a contract, and where the aggregate is assembled from appointments and follow-up visits rather than from orders placed on the spot. What stands show is production lines, energy equipment and launch vehicles instead of consumer devices, which is why a single agreement can be worth so much.

Spread across 400,000 square metres at Bao'an were 5,000 exhibitors from more than 120 countries and regions, and 22 zones were arranged along supply chains, with more than 100 products launched for the first time and 200 forums, roadshows and summits running alongside the floor. It was the first international technology fair in Shenzhen after the city's year as host of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation meetings. Foreign exhibitors came from Russia, the United States, Argentina and Australia, among more than 20 countries with stands of their own.

Pairing was the organizing idea, and it showed in how stands were built. A robotics chain and an artificial intelligence chain were exhibited as chains, an open-source HarmonyOS stand carried 15 application scenarios, and more than 30 provincial delegations came to recruit projects. Capital followed the same logic: investment institutions and industrial funds met project teams in sessions built around commercial space, quantum technology and future industries. Buyers arrived with lists: NVIDIA, Foxconn, Samsung, Tesla, Microsoft and Siemens sent delegations looking at semiconductors, artificial intelligence systems and energy equipment.

What the word intent carries is the limits. Some of the 570 agreements are framework documents, a signed intention becomes a contract only after terms and delivery dates are fixed, and the fair publishes no audited value for what converts. Visitor counts include people who entered on more than one day, the exhibitor figure counts stands instead of companies, and a memorandum can be withdrawn without penalty. The distinction matters because the closing total is published as a headline and the signed value is not.

Conversion is what the next year will show. The organizers have opened an online matchmaking channel and placed unsigned intentions on a follow-up list, so each of the 570 agreements can be tracked to signature or to its removal. The buyers came with specifications and the sellers with sample units, and a review after the first year would show whether the fair's claim about matching holds up across industries as different as robotics and energy.
