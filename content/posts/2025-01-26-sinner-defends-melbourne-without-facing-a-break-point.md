Jannik Sinner retained the Australian Open title on 26 January with a 6-3 7-6(4) 6-3 win over Alexander Zverev that lasted two hours and forty-two minutes. He did not face a break point in the match, which is a statistic that describes both his serve and the passivity it induced. The second set tie-break was the only passage where the match was contestable, and he won it by taking the first point on Zverev's serve and never trailing.

The pattern repeats what won Sinner the title a year earlier. He stands on the baseline, takes the ball early on both wings, and returns from a position that leaves him exposed to a good first serve and dominant against a second one. Zverev served at 68 per cent in the first set and still lost it, because the rallies that followed his second serve were short and in Sinner's favour. Zverev's first serve percentage fell in the third set, and Sinner broke him in the fourth game with a backhand return that landed at his feet.

Zverev's problem is structural rather than mental. His forehand is a weapon when he has time and a liability when he does not, and the depth of Sinner's return removed the time. A tall player with a long swing needs a rally pattern that buys him a metre of space, and Sinner's ball speed does not grant it.

The women's final a day earlier went the other way. Madison Keys beat Aryna Sabalenka 6-3 2-6 7-5 in two hours and four minutes, at twenty-nine, in her thirty-eighth Grand Slam appearance, and she had saved a match point against Iga Swiatek in the semi-final. Sabalenka was chasing a third consecutive title in Melbourne and had won the previous two finals without dropping a set.

Keys won the match with the shot that had failed her for a decade. Her backhand, long a weakness that opponents attacked, held up under pressure in the third set, and she broke Sabalenka in the final game with a return that landed on the baseline. The technical change behind that improvement was a racquet and a grip adjustment made in the previous off-season, and the visible result arrived at the age when most players have stopped improving. Keys had lost four previous semi-finals in Melbourne, and the one she won required saving a match point at 5-6 in the third set.

Both finals say something about the current tour. The men's game has a player who can win a major without being threatened on serve, and the women's game has a champion who spent fifteen years losing the matches she now wins. Neither result was a surprise to the betting market, and both were surprising to anyone who had stopped watching. Two finals in two days produced one champion who never lost serve and one who finally won the tournament she had been losing for a decade.
