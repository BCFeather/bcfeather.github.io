Mohammed bin Salman gave an interview to Fox News on 20 September in which he said that his country was getting closer every day to an agreement with Israel, and that the question of the Palestinians was very important and needed to be resolved. He did not say what resolution he required, and he did not repeat the Arab League's condition that normalization follow the creation of a Palestinian state, which the kingdom had itself proposed in 2002. The interviewer asked twice, and the answer was the same both times.

American officials have been assembling the package. Talks with Riyadh have covered a security guarantee that would resemble a treaty, cooperation on civilian nuclear power including the possibility of uranium enrichment on Saudi soil, and advanced weapons sales that Congress has been slow to approve. Saudi officials have said the nuclear programme is the part they will not give up. The Israeli government has been told that an agreement with Saudi Arabia would be the largest step in regional recognition since the Abraham Accords, and that it may require concessions to the Palestinians that its coalition has refused so far.

Palestinian question is the one that cannot be deferred quietly. Saudi officials have said that any deal must give the Palestinians something that can be described as a state or as a pathway to one, and the Israeli government has said it will not freeze settlements or revive the peace process. The American president has said that the two can be reconciled, and the negotiating record of the past year suggests that the formula has not been written, in public at least.

The economics of the deal are real and insufficient. Saudi Arabia wants a defence relationship that deters Iran, and the United States wants a partner that keeps oil prices stable and distances itself from China. Both have been discussing a corridor linking India to Europe through the Gulf and Israel, which would need Saudi participation to work. A normalization agreement would unlock investment, would let Israeli firms operate in a market that has been closed to them, and would give the American navy more access to Saudi ports.

Domestic politics on all three sides constrain the timing. The Israeli government depends on parties that oppose Palestinian statehood, the Saudi crown prince has built his legitimacy on a break with the old Arab consensus, and the American Senate must ratify any treaty and has been hostile to Saudi nuclear enrichment. Each of the three leaders has reason to want the agreement and reason to wait, and none of them has said which reason is stronger.

A crown prince has said the talks are advancing without saying what they will produce, and the package that would make them work has not been assembled. The region's alignment is shifting slowly, and the Palestinians are the part of the equation that everyone describes as essential and nobody has priced, which is why the file has moved from the foreign ministries to the White House.
