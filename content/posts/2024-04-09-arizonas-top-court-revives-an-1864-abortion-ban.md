description: "The Arizona Supreme Court ruled on 9 April that a territorial law of 1864 banning almost all abortions could be enforced, and the legislature repealed it three weeks later after the decision became a national political issue."
---

Arizona Supreme Court ruled on the ninth of April that a law passed in 1864, from the time when Arizona was still a territory, could be enforced, and that it took precedence over a statute from 2022 that allowed abortions up to fifteen weeks. Opinion was written by four of the seven justices, and it held that the older law had never been repealed and that the legislature had not intended to create a right to abortion when it passed the newer one.

Content of the 1864 law is severe even by the standards of the nineteenth century. It banned abortion at every stage of pregnancy except to save the life of the mother, and it provided no exceptions for rape, incest or fetal abnormality. It also made it a felony for a doctor to perform the procedure, with a sentence of two to five years. Enforcement was suspended for fifty years by the decision in Roe against Wade and then by the 2022 statute, which had been blocked by a court while the case was argued.

Practical effect was a near-total ban in a state of about seven million people. Clinics in Phoenix and in Tucson said they would stop performing the procedure, and providers in neighbouring states reported calls from patients within hours. The state's attorney general, a Democrat, said her office would not prosecute anyone under the law, which left enforcement to county prosecutors who said they would apply it.

Political reaction was immediate and cross-party. Several Republican legislators who had voted for the 2022 fifteen-week limit said the older law went further than voters wanted, and one called it a disaster for the party. The Democratic governor, Katie Hobbs, called on the legislature to repeal it, and she issued an executive order that shielded providers from extradition to other states. National Democrats used the ruling in advertising within days.

Repeal came on the first of May, after three weeks of votes and one failed attempt in which two Republicans blocked the measure. The legislature passed a bill restoring the fifteen-week limit, the governor signed it, and the attorney general said enforcement of the 1864 law would be suspended until the repeal took effect in the autumn. Abortion-rights groups said the delay would leave a gap of several months in which the ban could still be applied.

Consequence of the ninth of April is an issue that moved from the courts to the ballot. Arizona voters approved a constitutional amendment protecting abortion access in November, and similar measures were on the ballot in several other states. The ruling showed that laws written before women could vote were still available to courts that wanted them, and it produced a coalition of providers, employers and Republican legislators who had not previously agreed on the subject.

What the decision did was to make the question political in a way that the courts could not settle. The case had been in the courts since December 2022, when a lower court judge ruled that the 2022 statute should take precedence over the older law.
