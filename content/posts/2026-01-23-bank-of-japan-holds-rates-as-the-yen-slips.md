description: "The Bank of Japan kept its policy rate at 0.75 per cent on an 8-1 vote, raised its growth forecasts and left the yen near 160 to the dollar with no signal of renewed bond purchases."
---

Markets went into the January meeting expecting nothing and got nothing, which is not the same as learning nothing. The Bank of Japan's policy board voted 8-1 to keep the overnight call rate around 0.75 per cent at its meeting on 23 January, and the single dissenter argued for an increase to 1.0 per cent on the grounds that the price stability target had largely been met and that risks to prices were skewed upwards. The majority did not follow, and the vote is a measure of how divided the board is at this level.

The forecasts in the quarterly outlook report moved more than the rate did. Growth for the fiscal year ending in March was revised up to 0.9 per cent from 0.7, and the following year to 1.0 per cent from 0.7, with the revision attributed to the government's economic measures and to a supportive financial environment. Core inflation for the coming fiscal year was revised up to 1.9 per cent from 1.8, while the current year's forecast stayed at 2.7.

The yen is the complication. It weakened to nearly 160 to the dollar earlier in the month, a level last seen in July 2024 when the Ministry of Finance conducted record-scale yen-buying intervention. Long-term Japanese government bond yields rose at the same time, which is an unusual combination: a currency falling while bond yields rise suggests that investors are pricing higher inflation and questioning the policy rate rather than fleeing to safety.

Governor Ueda's press conference was read for a signal on bond purchases, and the signal did not come. He reiterated that future increases would depend on economic and price developments and said the bank would conduct bond market operations nimbly in exceptional cases to promote stable yield formation, which stops well short of restarting purchases. Markets took the absence of a commitment as permission to keep selling the currency.

Wages are the variable that decides the next move. The bank's view is that wage growth is now feeding into prices more than it did, and that the spring wage negotiations will supply the evidence. The national confederation has publicly called for a five per cent increase after last year's 5.25 per cent settlement, and firms are entering the talks with profit growth that has slowed from ten per cent to 6.5 per cent over the first three quarters.

Market pricing puts the next 25 basis point increase in July, with a second largely priced by December, which would take the policy rate to 1.25 per cent. April is the earliest plausible date, once the first round of wage results is available, and June or July is more likely because the comprehensive data arrive then. The gap between the board and the market is about timing, not about direction.

Longer-term, the question is what a 0.75 per cent policy rate means in a country with inflation above 2.5 per cent. Real policy rates remain negative, which is why the currency keeps falling and why the long end of the curve keeps steepening, and the bank's gradualism is being tested by the market every week. On 23 January, the test was passed without a change in policy.
