---

Editing a gene before birth is attractive because a disease that damages organs in the first months of life cannot be prevented by treating after birth. A paper published this month in *Nature Biotechnology* reports an approach that administers base editors to fetal mice through the amniotic cavity, and finds editing in the liver, the organ that produces the affected protein.

The mutation is in a haemoglobin gene, and the disease models a thalassaemia in which the defect causes anaemia from the first weeks. Base editing, which converts one nucleotide into another without cutting the DNA, was chosen over nuclease editing because a double-strand break in a fetal liver that is rapidly proliferating carries a higher risk of deletion or rearrangement.

Delivery is the scientific content. The editor protein, not the mRNA, was encapsulated in a lipid nanoparticle and injected into the amniotic fluid at a gestational age corresponding to the second trimester in humans. Uptake into fetal tissue occurred through the amniotic membrane and the umbilical vessels, which is a route that has been used for small molecules and has not been used for a protein editor.

Results are a compromise. Editing reached 27 per cent of alleles in sorted fetal liver cells, and the treated animals had haemoglobin levels about 60 per cent of normal at birth, against 25 per cent in untreated controls. The effect declined after birth as edited progenitors turned over, and no second dose was given.

Off-target analysis was thorough and reassuring. The team performed whole-genome sequencing on treated and control livers and found no guide-independent edits above the background rate, and the on-target edit was confirmed at the intended position in 94 per cent of edited alleles.

Immunology is the open question. A bacterial-derived editor protein administered to a fetus whose immune system is developing could induce tolerance or, worse, an immune response on repeat dosing. The study reports no anti-editor antibodies in the weeks after treatment, which is consistent with tolerance induction and not the same as proving safety.

Human translation is far away, and the paper says so. The amniotic route in humans is accessible and has a long obstetric history, but the dose scaling, the gestational window and the regulatory pathway for fetal gene editing are all undefined, and any first-in-human trial would need a disease with no postnatal option.

Dose scaling is the translation problem. Amniotic volume scales with gestational age and with body mass, and the lipid nanoparticle dose that produced 27 per cent editing in a mouse would need a proportional increase that has not been tested for toxicity in a larger fetus. Delivery to the fetal liver also depends on the umbilical circulation, which differs between species.

The comparative case is postnatal editing. Trials in adults have achieved therapeutic editing in the liver at doses that require hospitalization and immune suppression, and a fetal route would avoid the dosing scale problem by treating a much smaller patient. That is the real argument: not that the technique is elegant, but that a fetus is a tractable target size.
