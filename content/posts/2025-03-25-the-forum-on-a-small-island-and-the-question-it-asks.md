On the twenty-fifth of March the annual conference on the island of Boao opened, and for four days the resort hosted a few thousand people, the prime ministers and the finance ministers, the bankers, the academics and the journalists who follow them. The venue is small and the guest list is large, and the combination produces a kind of conversation that is harder to arrange in a capital, which is the point of holding it there.

A group of Asian governments and business figures founded the forum in 2001, and its stated purpose is to discuss the region's integration. What it has become is a place where the Chinese government sets out its view of trade and investment to an audience that includes the people who will decide whether to invest in the region. The speeches are watched for the parts that are not in the official text, and the officials who speak there are the ones whose words are likely to be repeated in a boardroom.

Tariffs set the mood this year. The trade measures that the American administration had announced in the preceding months were on every panel, and the Asian economies that export to the American market were calculating what the levies would do to their growth. The forum's answer, which is the answer the host government has been giving in every venue, is that the region should trade more with itself, and the trade data that supports the argument is real, since intra-regional trade has grown faster than the trade with the rest of the world for a decade.

What is interesting is the gap between argument and practice. A company that wants to sell into the region still faces different standards, different customs procedures, different rules on data, and the agreements that were signed to reduce those differences have been implemented unevenly. The forum produces communiqués about harmonization and the customs officials in each country implement their own rules, and the distance between the two is where the work is.

An island is a good setting for the argument about tourism and the environment. Hainan has been building resorts and duty-free shops for a decade and the coastline has paid for it, and the provincial government has been trying to move the growth inland and towards the less fragile parts of the island. The conference's guests stay in hotels that were built on that coastline, and the discussion of sustainable tourism happens in a room with a view of the sea.

What the forum does, in the end, is let a set of governments say in public what they have been saying in private. The declarations are not binding and the expectations they create are real, and a business that is deciding whether to build a factory in the region reads them as a signal about the next five years. That is the function of the event, and it is worth the four days on the island.
