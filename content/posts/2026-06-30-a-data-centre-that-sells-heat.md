A data centre campus in northern Europe started delivering heat to a municipal district heating network this month, under a twenty-year agreement that pays it for every megawatt-hour delivered. The heat is not a by-product in the ordinary sense. The facility was designed to produce it, with cooling loops that run at a high enough temperature to feed the network directly, and a heat pump array that boosts the temperature when the outdoor air is cold.

Data centres have been rejecting heat into the atmosphere for decades, and the reason is economic rather than technical. Air cooling is cheap, and the heat it produces is at thirty-five degrees, which is too cool to be useful to a district network that runs at seventy. Upgrading the heat costs money and adds a system that can fail, and for a facility whose product is compute, that is a cost with no revenue attached.

The change is that the revenue now exists. District heating networks in several European countries have been expanding, and the carbon price on the heat they produce from gas has made waste heat attractive. A data centre that can deliver forty degrees can feed a heat pump, and a heat pump that runs on the same electricity as the servers is cheaper than a gas boiler when the carbon price is included. That is the arithmetic that turned a cost into a contract.

The contract is the hard part, and it is where most projects fail. A district network needs a supplier that will still be there in twenty years, and a data centre needs a customer that will take heat at three in the morning. The agreement has to specify a minimum temperature, a flow rate, a price that escalates with something, and a penalty if the facility goes offline. None of that is standard, and each project negotiates it from scratch.

The technical consequence of the contract is that the facility's cooling design is now driven by the customer's requirements rather than by the servers'. A heat exchanger that would otherwise be sized for the peak summer load has to be sized for the peak winter delivery, and a failure in the heat delivery system is a failure in the district network. That is a different risk profile from a data centre that simply vents heat.

The scale question is what decides whether this spreads. One campus delivering a few megawatts of heat can supply a few thousand homes, which is useful for a small city and marginal for a large one. A cluster of facilities in the same area could supply a city, and that requires coordination between competitors who do not usually talk. Some municipalities have started to require it in planning permission, which is the mechanism that will matter most.

The broader point is that waste heat is a resource that only exists if someone builds the connection. The engineering for a single facility is well understood, and the constraint is the institutional arrangement that makes the heat a product rather than a nuisance. The campus that signed this month is a demonstration of the arrangement, and the next few years will show whether it is a template.
