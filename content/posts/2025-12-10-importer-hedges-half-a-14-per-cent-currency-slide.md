A currency's fall is a cost for an importer and an opportunity for an exporter, and the importer's hedge is supposed to convert a volatile cost into a fixed one. An importer's disclosure this month shows the hedge covering half the move, and the reason is the pricing of the forward market rather than a failure of nerve.

The importer buys 60 per cent of its inputs abroad and prices its products domestically. A 14 per cent fall in its currency raises its input cost by 14 per cent and its selling price cannot follow immediately. Interest rate differentials set the hedge's price. A forward contract's price embeds the difference between the two currencies' interest rates, and when the domestic rate is 6 points above the foreign one, a 12-month forward costs 6 per cent more than the spot rate.

That premium is why the importer stopped. It hedged the first 6 months at an average premium of 4 per cent, and when the premium widened to 7 it judged the hedge too expensive. The currency then fell a further 8 per cent. Accounting shapes the story as much as the market does. A hedge that qualifies for hedge accounting defers the gain or loss, and a hedge that does not is marked to market and hits earnings. The importer's second-half hedges did not qualify, which is the reason the treasurer cites.

The alternative is a natural hedge. An importer with foreign revenue offsets its foreign cost, and the importer has begun invoicing 15 per cent of its domestic sales in the foreign currency to a multinational customer. A competitor hedges everything and pays a premium for it. That competitor's margin is stable and its cost is 3 per cent higher on average, and the stable margin is what its lenders require under a covenant.

A hedge's cost is visible and an unhedged loss is not, which is why programs stop early. A treasurer who pays a 6 per cent premium records the cost this quarter, and a treasurer who declines records nothing until the currency moves. The asymmetry is behavioral and it is also how boards read the two.

The coverage ratio is the policy variable. A program that hedges 50 to 80 per cent of a 12-month exposure reduces variance by more than half and never eliminates it, and a program that hedges 100 per cent has converted a hedging desk into a forecasting one. A hedge's accounting is a set of rules about documentation. A hedge that is designated at inception, documented and tested for effectiveness is deferred; one that is not is marked to market. A treasurer's first task is the paperwork rather than the trade.

Protection has a price, and that price rises when the risk does. A forward market that charges a premium for the direction everyone expects is a market that makes protection expensive precisely when it is wanted.
