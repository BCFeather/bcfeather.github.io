A design tool that was worth 20 billion dollars in an abandoned acquisition was worth about 56 billion at the close of its first session, and the arithmetic of that gap is the whole story of the listing. Figma sold shares at 33 dollars, opened at 85, traded as high as 124 and closed at 115.50, which is a 250 per cent gain on the offer price.

The raise was modest against the demand. The company and its existing shareholders sold enough stock to raise about 1.2 billion dollars, the book was oversubscribed many times over, and retail buyers reported allocations of a single share, which is what happens when an offering is priced for the underwriter and not for the market. A first-day gain of that size is a transfer from the company's treasury to the investors who were allocated shares, and the size of the transfer, roughly several billion dollars, is the clearest evidence that the pricing process is still designed to leave money on the table.

Business model is subscription software with a viral loop. Designers invite developers and marketers into the same file, the seats multiply without a sales visit, and net revenue retention has run above 100 per cent, which means the customers who stay spend more each year than they did the year before.

Numbers behind the story are mixed. Revenue grew quickly and the company is not yet consistently profitable on a GAAP basis, because it has been hiring and because its stock-based compensation is large, and a 250 per cent first-day gain is a bet on the growth rate and not on the current earnings.

The listing is also a signal about the market. After three years in which technology offerings were postponed, Circle in June and Figma in July both opened far above their offer prices, and the sequence is what reopens a window: bankers can now tell a founder that the discount is no longer punitive. A design platform that sells seats to teams is a business whose revenue depends on how many people touch a file, and the number of monthly active users, about 13 million at the listing, is the number that will decide whether the growth rate holds.

Competition is the risk that the prospectus names last and that the market should name first. Adobe, which walked away from its bid after regulators blocked it, still sells the incumbent tool, and a dozen start-ups sell AI-assisted design, so the moat is the collaborative file and the habits built around it.

What would settle the year is whether the stock holds above 100 once the lock-up expires and whether revenue growth stays above 30 per cent for two more quarters. A hold would make the pop a repricing, and a slide would make it a transfer of value from the company to the flippers. A lock-up that expires in January will test the price against the supply of stock that employees have been waiting three years to sell, and the outcome of that week will say more about the valuation than the first day did.
