Accretion disks in strong gravity produce winds, and the spectra of both stellar-mass binaries and supermassive black holes show blueshifted absorption lines where the wind crosses the line of sight. Theory divides these winds into two families: thermal-radiative winds driven by X-ray heating of the outer disk, and Eddington winds driven by radiation pressure in the inner disk, which should be fast and carry enormous kinetic power. Both families appear in the same sources, and which one dominates is set by the Eddington ratio and the geometry of the disk.

A paper in *Nature* reports the XRISM Resolve spectrum of GX 13+1, a neutron star binary in the Galaxy, and the wind it finds belongs to neither simple category. The absorption is the densest seen in such a source, Compton-thick in the authors' wording, and it attenuates the flux enough that the system looks faint although it is intrinsically brighter than its Eddington limit. The column density is high enough to hide the inner disk, so the observed luminosity is a lower bound on the emitted one.

Speed is the discriminator. The wind is extremely slow for a system radiating above Eddington, and the velocity and ionisation are consistent with a thermal-radiative wind launched by X-ray irradiation of the outer disk. Radiation pressure from the inner disk, which should drive the fastest outflow, is not what the line profiles show at all. The ionisation parameter points to gas far from the illuminating source, which is where the outer disk sits.

The contrast with supermassive black holes is the point of the paper. Recent Resolve observations of a black hole at a similarly high Eddington ratio found a wind at about 0.3 times the speed of light, and the two systems cannot be explained by one launching mechanism on this evidence. The authors argue that the ultrafast winds need an origin that the neutron star case excludes. A neutron star and a black hole at the same Eddington ratio differ in the size of the emitting region, and the wind depends on that size.

Resolve's spectral resolution is what makes the measurement possible. Line ratios separate the density from the ionisation parameter, and the velocity width separates a slow thick wind from a fast thin one, so the claim rests on spectroscopy instead of on an inference from the source's luminosity. Resolve reaches a resolving power that turns the line profiles into a measurement instead of a detection, which is why the density estimate survives scrutiny.

One source at one epoch is a narrow base for a general statement about wind launching. The inclination is uncertain, the disk geometry is not resolved, and a wind that varies on years would look different in a second observation. What the paper establishes is that Eddington luminosity does not guarantee an ultrafast wind, which is enough to unsettle the feedback arguments built on that assumption. A campaign on the same source at another orbital phase would test the geometry, and a second neutron star would test the generalisation.
