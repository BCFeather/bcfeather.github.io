Soil carbon credits are sold on a simple promise: a farm changes how it manages the land, the soil holds more carbon, and a company buys the difference. The promise is attractive because it is cheap, it does not require a factory, and it is hard to check. A trial reported this month by a consortium of soil scientists and an independent auditor tested the checking part in three countries, with paired plots and repeated sampling over four years.

Design was deliberately unglamorous. Each participating farm had a field where the new practice was applied and a neighbouring field where it was not, with the same soil type and the same crop rotation, and both were sampled to a metre depth at the start, the middle and the end of the trial. Sampling to depth matters, because the carbon that a cover crop adds near the surface can be offset by losses deeper down, and shallow sampling is the most common source of overstated gains in the literature.

The result: the measured increase in soil organic carbon was positive on most farms, and it was roughly a third smaller than the modelled estimate that the crediting schemes use. The gap was not uniform. Farms with a long history of tillage and low starting carbon gained more than the models predicted. Farms that had already adopted reduced tillage gained less, because the easy gains had been taken. The models assume a fixed response to a practice; real soils respond to where they started.

Straightforward and awkward, the accounting consequence follows. A credit is denominated in tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent, and it is sold against a modelled baseline rather than a measured one. If the models run high, the buyer is paying for carbon that is not there, and the seller is exposed if the soil loses the carbon later. The trial's authors recommend that credits be issued against measured change with a discount for uncertainty, and that contracts include a clawback clause for reversal.

Science caveats are substantial. Four years is short for soil carbon, which turns over slowly, and the measurement itself has uncertainty that is larger than the effect on individual fields, which is why the trial pooled results across farms. Sampling to a metre is expensive and slow, and the labs that do it are not numerous. A scheme that requires measurement on every field would cost more than the credits are worth for most smallholders.

The interesting question is what a credit is supposed to be. As a financial instrument, it needs to be standardized, verifiable and cheap to administer. As an environmental claim, it needs to represent a real change in the atmosphere. Those two requirements pull in opposite directions, and the trial shows the tension in numbers rather than in argument: the measured gain is real, smaller than advertised, and expensive to prove. The schemes will have to choose which requirement they care about, and say so.
