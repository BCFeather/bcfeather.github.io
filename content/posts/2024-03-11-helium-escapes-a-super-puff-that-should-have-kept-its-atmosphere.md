Super-puffs are planets with densities below about \(0.1\ \mathrm{g\ cm^{-3}}\) and core masses under 5 Earth masses, and their envelopes are large enough to be measured during a transit. Theory divides them by mass: below about 10 Earth masses a planet is expected to lose its envelope to photoevaporation or boil-off, while above that the deeper potential well should hold the gas in place for billions of years. That division has been an expectation rather than a result, since the massive ones are rarely observed with the filters that show gas leaving.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 8 March 2024 tests it on TOI-1420b, a warm super-puff that sits comfortably on the massive side. The team observed one full transit and one partial transit with the metastable helium filter on Palomar/WIRC and compared the helium light curve with the broadband transits recorded by TESS, which measures the radius at one wavelength, where an escaping atmosphere shows, and not the radius of the solid body.

The comparison gives the paper its number. Helium transits came out 0.671 plus or minus 0.079 per cent deeper than the TESS transits, a difference at 8.5 sigma. The planet itself measures 25.1 Earth masses and 11.9 Earth radii, a density of \(0.08\ \mathrm{g\ cm^{-3}}\) at an equilibrium temperature of 960 K, so it is both puffy and warm, and the radius is wide enough that a small excess in absorption is measurable.

Metastable helium absorbs at 1083 nm in the upper atmosphere, and an excess of absorption during transit means gas standing above the limb where the broadband radius does not reach. Modelling the excess with a self-consistent one-dimensional hydrodynamics code constrained the thermal structure of the outflow under different assumptions about the stellar XUV spectrum, and a three-dimensional simulation built on those results matched the observations at a mass-loss rate of \(10^{10.82}\ \mathrm{g\ s^{-1}}\).

That rate puts the atmosphere in perspective. Dividing the planet's mass by the loss rate gives about 70 Gyr, far longer than the star's age, so a super-puff of this mass appears able to keep its envelope over long timescales. The authors draw the consequence plainly: planets like this may have formed with the large envelope fractions they show today, which would make them odd at birth, and not merely unlucky afterwards.

They also keep a second explanation alive. Tidal circularization could have heated and inflated these planets, and that route would bring their envelope fractions into better agreement with what core-nucleated accretion predicts. Two readings that point in different directions are a reason to measure again, not a reason to pick one, and the paper presents them side by side instead of choosing.

What the observation cannot do is settle the question on its own. One full transit and one partial transit give a single epoch, the partial event constrains the outflow less than a complete chord would, and the thermal structure depends on an XUV spectrum that was assumed rather than measured. Repetition at other orbital phases would show whether the excess helium is steady, a mass measurement independent of the model would fix the loss rate instead of inferring it, and a second super-puff observed the same way, WASP-107b being the obvious candidate, would turn one case into a comparison.
