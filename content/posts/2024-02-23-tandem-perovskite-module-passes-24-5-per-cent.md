Perovskite photovoltaics have spent a decade setting records on squares a centimetre across, and the industry has spent the same decade asking what happens when the square grows. The answer has usually been a fall in efficiency, because a film that crystallizes neatly in a dish does not crystallize the same way across a plate. Tan Hairen's group at Nanjing University reported progress on that gap in *Science* on 23 February 2024, with an all-perovskite tandem module of 20.25 square centimetres certified at 24.5 per cent.

A tandem stacks two absorbers. The wide-bandgap cell takes the blue end of the spectrum and passes the rest to a narrow-bandgap cell beneath it, and the pairing can in principle beat the limit that a single junction faces. The group had already reached 28 per cent on a small cell and 21.7 per cent on a module, and the module figure was the one that mattered commercially.

Crystallization was the obstacle. Narrow-bandgap perovskite films form too quickly, and at scale they form unevenly, with the top and bottom of the film solidifying out of step and leaving a defective interface at the base of the layer. Adding glycinamide hydrochloride to the precursor solution slows the process roughly tenfold and lets the film heal from the substrate upward.

Certification came from an independent laboratory and entered in the solar-cell efficiency tables, where no module of this class stood higher when the paper appeared. That the number came from a module and not a cell is the point. Efficiency that survives scaling is the only efficiency a factory can use.

Current matching is the other constraint. The two subcells are in series, so the poorer of the two sets the current of the whole, and a narrow-bandgap layer with a defective base drags the module below the sum of its parts. Slowing crystallization raises the weak cell, which is why a deposition fix shows up as a module record.

The certified measurement also fixes the area, and area is the variable that separates a promising result from a saleable one. Perovskites remain fragile in the ways that matter outdoors, since moisture, heat and ultraviolet light all attack the crystal, and a tandem adds the complication that two absorbers must age together and stay in current match as they do. The paper addresses deposition and leaves durability where it stood.

What comes next is a module that keeps its efficiency through damp heat, and that test has broken more perovskite records than any competitor. Independent certification matters here because the efficiency tables accept no self-reported figure, and the entry is what the industry reads. Even so, a 20 square centimetre module above 24 per cent moves the technology from a laboratory curiosity into a candidate that manufacturers can price, and the salt additive that made it work is cheap enough to survive a bill of materials.
