Apple began taking orders for its mixed reality headset on 19 January, with deliveries to follow on 2 February. The device costs 3,499 dollars and requires a fitting at a store, because the light seal around the eyes has to match the shape of the face. The orders placed in the first weekend were estimated at a few hundred thousand, which is close to the number that the suppliers had been told to prepare. The orders opened on 19 January and the first deliveries were scheduled for the following week, and the company said the initial stock had sold out within an hour.

Price is the strategy. A device at that level is sold to the developers and to the enthusiasts rather than to the mass market, and the company has said the first version is for the people who will make the software. A product that is priced for the makers is a product that is being sold as a kit. The fitting is done at the stores, and the company has said the appointment is required rather than optional because the device cannot be returned for a different size. The reviewers who tried the device described the weight and the battery as the parts that would decide whether it became a product for the many or for the few.

Manufacturing is the constraint. The displays and the lenses are hard to make in volume, and the suppliers have said the yield is low, which is why the company expects to ship fewer than a million units in the first year. A component that is difficult to produce is a launch that is deliberately small. The display suppliers have been working on a panel that packs more pixels into a smaller area than any television, and the cost of that panel is the largest part of the bill.

Software is the open question. The headset runs the applications that the developers write for it, and the ones that have been shown so far are mostly the work of the company itself, which is the pattern of a platform that has not yet been adopted. A device without applications is a device that the buyer uses once. The applications that the company demonstrated in the launch were mostly video and the reproduction of a desktop, and the developers have said the tools are good and the audience is small.

Comparison with the phone explains the patience. The company has said the headset is a platform that will take a decade, and its history with the watch and the earbuds suggests that the first versions were also sold to a narrow audience. A platform that arrives slowly is a platform that the company can afford to wait for. The company's services revenue has been growing faster than the hardware, and a new platform is a way to sell the subscriptions that run on it.

What the launch shows is that the company has entered a market that the rivals have tried and failed to make mainstream, and its answer is to sell the hardware to the people who will build the demand. A headset that is sold as a computer rather than as a toy is a bet that the work will move into the device, and the bet will be decided by the software rather than by the screen.
