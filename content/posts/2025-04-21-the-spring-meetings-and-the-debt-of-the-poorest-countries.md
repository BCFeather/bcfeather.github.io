The spring meetings of the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank open in Washington on the twenty-first of April, and for a week the finance ministers and the central bank governors of most of the world are in one city. The meetings are part conference and part negotiation, and the agenda this year is set by two questions that are connected, the growth of the rich economies and the debt of the poor ones.

The debt question has an arithmetic that is easy to state. A low-income country that borrows in dollars pays an interest rate that is set by the market plus the spread that the market demands, and the spread depends on the risk that the lenders perceive. When global rates rose, the cost of the existing debt rose with the refinancing, and the countries that had borrowed during the cheap decade found that the interest bill was larger than the health budget. The fund's own analysis puts a number of countries in that position, and the number has grown since the pandemic.

The restructuring process is the part that is slow. A country that cannot pay negotiates with its creditors, and the creditors include other governments, the multilateral banks and the private funds, and the private funds have an incentive to wait because a delay costs them nothing and a concession costs them something. The framework that the group of twenty built in 2020 was meant to speed this up, and the cases that have gone through it have taken years, which is a long time for a government that is paying interest instead of teachers.

The growth question is the other half. The fund's forecasts for the rich economies have been revised down since January, because of the tariffs, and the revisions matter for the poor ones because a slower rich economy buys less from a poor one. The export that a low-income country sells is often a commodity or a garment, and the demand for both depends on the income of the buyer, and the fund's forecast is therefore a forecast about somebody else's consumption.

What the meetings produce is a communiqué and a set of numbers, and the numbers are the part that the participants use. A government that is negotiating a loan uses the fund's assessment as the third-party opinion that the lenders accept, and a government that is defending its budget uses the bank's projections to argue that the shock is external. The meetings are where those documents are agreed, and the agreement is the product.

What the week does not produce is a solution to the arithmetic. A country whose interest bill exceeds its health bill has three options, to grow faster, to restructure, or to cut, and the first is not in the gift of a finance minister and the third is a decision that no government wants to make in public. The meetings are where that choice is discussed, and the discussion is the most that a meeting can do.
