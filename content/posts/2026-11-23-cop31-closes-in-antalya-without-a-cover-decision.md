The thirty-first conference of the parties to the UN climate convention closed in Antalya on Sunday, two days after its scheduled end, without a cover decision. The session ran 44 hours past its Friday deadline, the longest in the convention's history and beyond the record set at Madrid in 2019. The text that failed was the cover decision for what the presidency had called the Antalya Agreement.

What the conference did produce were decisions on adaptation, mitigation, the just transition and finance, the items developing countries had pressed hardest. Those texts stand, and the multilateral machinery they describe continues. What is missing is the single political document that would have bound the package together and set the direction for the next round of national pledges.

Fossil fuels supplied the fault line. A bloc of parties insisted that the cover text name fossil fuels and commit to moving away from them; producers resisted wording they read as a phase-out by another name. The dispute widened to take in the response to a temporary overshoot of 1.5 degrees, and the size and channels of climate finance. Murat Kurum, the Turkish minister who shared the presidency with Australia's Chris Bowen under the conference's dual arrangement, said some delegations had fixed on a word or a phrase and brought in subjects unrelated to climate.

It is the third time a climate conference has ended without a final political decision, after COP6 at The Hague in 2000 and COP15 at Copenhagen in 2009. António Guterres, the UN secretary general, called the outcome deeply disappointing and said the science leaves no ambiguity; he argued that no one can put out a fire while pouring fuel on it, and that every delay raises the cost of the task. His statement also pressed developed countries on the finance they have promised.

Li Gao, the Chinese delegation's head and a vice minister of ecology and environment, said his side was deeply disappointed, having worked for a successful outcome, and argued that the failure should not cost anyone faith in the multilateral process. He said recrimination does not build trust and that cooperation remains the only route through a problem that belongs to everyone.

The practical consequences will be felt in the national pledges that parties are due to submit. A month before the conference, UN Environment Programme's emissions gap report found that current policies and announced pledges leave the 1.5 degree target out of reach, and the arithmetic has not changed with the closing speeches. Adaptation finance is the other ledger: the decisions taken in Antalya set out how money should be counted and delivered, and those paragraphs survive even as the headline text does not.

For all the disappointment, the conference did not end the process. The convention's standing bodies keep working, the next presidency takes over a mandate that includes the overshoot response, and the parties leave with a set of sectoral decisions and a missing centre. Whether a later conference can supply the centre is the question the week left open.
