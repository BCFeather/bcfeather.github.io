The Constitutional Court of Thailand removed the prime minister on 14 August over an appointment of a minister who had served a prison sentence, and the governing coalition replaced him within two days with the daughter of a former leader whom the army had overthrown. The transition took less than a week. The minister whose appointment triggered the ruling had been convicted years earlier, and the court held that the premier had acted improperly in proposing him.

Mechanism is the court. A constitutional provision that disqualifies ministers for a past conviction was used against a premier who had been in office for less than a year, and the decision dissolved his party's leadership without an election. A court that can remove a government is a court that the coalition has to negotiate with before it forms one. The coalition's largest partner is the party that the army had backed in the previous election, and it accepted the new leader without a public argument.

Family is the continuity. The new prime minister is the youngest in the country's history and the third member of her family to hold the office, and the party that nominated her is the one that the army had dissolved twice. A dynasty that returns through the ballot is a dynasty that the institutions have learned to live with. The family's parties have won every election since 2001 and have been removed by the courts or the army four times, which is the pattern the country lives with.

Progressive party was the other casualty. A party that had won the most seats in the previous election was dissolved by the same court earlier in the year, and its members were banned from politics for a decade. A party that wins and is dissolved is a party whose voters have no route to representation. The party that was dissolved had won the most seats, and its voters have no obvious alternative that the courts will allow to compete.

Economy is waiting for a budget. A coalition that changes its leader twice in a year is a coalition that has not passed the spending plan it promised, and the stimulus that was to be delivered through a digital payment has been delayed. A government that is negotiating with a court has less time for a budget. The digital payment scheme was the flagship policy and it has been delayed by legal challenges, which is a policy that costs money whether or not it is delivered.

What the week shows is that the formal institutions decide who governs and the voters decide who is popular. A country in which the two diverge is a country that will keep replacing leaders without changing the direction of policy. The pattern of elections followed by removals is the arrangement that the institutions have chosen, and the arrangement has outlasted several constitutions. Voters who chose the dissolved party have not been asked whether they accept the arrangement, and the next election will put the question to them.
