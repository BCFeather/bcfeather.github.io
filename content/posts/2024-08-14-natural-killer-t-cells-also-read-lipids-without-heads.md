Natural killer T cells are restricted by CD1d, a molecule that presents lipids rather than peptides, and the antigens that activate them have been assumed to be glycolipids, with a sugar head that anchors the lipid in the binding groove. Their repertoire is small and their targets are lipids, which makes the identity of those lipids the central question in the field.

A paper in *PNAS* on 14 August 2024 tests that assumption with a scan rather than a candidate list. The authors built a lipidomics system that captures the lipids held between CD1d and the T cell receptor, separates complexes by interaction strength, and then elutes the bound molecules for mass spectrometry. Separating complexes by interaction strength is the step that turns a binding assay into a ranking of antigens.

The approach is unbiased in the sense that it does not ask whether a particular lipid is present. It asks which lipids are there, and it does so in cells, with endogenous material, under normal conditions and after inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress. Inducing stress is included because a cell under stress presents a different set of lipids, and the antigen repertoire may shift with it.

Fifteen molecules came out, and they fell into two groups. One group contains the expected phospholipid antigens, whose headgroups are familiar from earlier work. The other contains neutral lipids, identified as ceramides, deoxyceramides and diacylglycerols. Two families from fifteen molecules is a narrow result, and it argues that the repertoire is more constrained than a random lipid would suggest.

Those neutral lipids are headless. They carry no polar headgroup, and their presence in a CD1d groove that was thought to need one for anchoring is the paper's surprise, since the structural logic of presentation had been built around the head. A ceramide has a hydroxyl and an amide but no sugar, and a diacylglycerol has even less, so neither resembles a glycolipid antigen.

How the groove accommodates them is a structural question the paper raises rather than answers. A lipid without a head could sit deeper, or the groove could open, and the answer would change which antigens are worth looking for in disease. A structure of CD1d with a ceramide bound would settle the question, and none is reported here.

The immunological reach is wide. NKT cells sit at the junction of innate and adaptive immunity, and their antigens are being developed as adjuvants and as vaccine components, so a broader antigen catalogue is a broader set of handles. The scan is limited by what it can solubilize and by the CD1d constructs used, and a lipid that binds weakly may be missed. What it shows is that the receptor's chemical range is wider than the glycolipid textbook. If headless lipids are antigens, then lipid metabolism feeds directly into immune recognition, and that link is worth tracing in disease. Whether the headless antigens appear in tumours or in autoimmune tissue is a question for the next survey.
