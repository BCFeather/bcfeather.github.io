---

Bulk cocoa trades as a commodity with a single price reference and a tolerance for origin. Single-origin contracts trade as a product: a named cooperative, a named harvest, a fermented and dried lot with a flavour profile the buyer has tasted. European buyers moved about a fifth of their volume into such contracts over the past three seasons, and the change has moved money and risk down the chain.

Price tells the first part. A single-origin lot from a well-run cooperative in Ghana or Ecuador has sold at premiums of 15 to 40 per cent over the nearby futures price, before the 2024 spike in the underlying market that lifted everything. The premium pays for selection, separate fermentation, hand sorting and the paperwork that proves the lot is what it claims.

Working capital tells the harder part. A cooperative that sells into a bulk contract receives payment against delivery, often with a pre-season advance from the exporter. A cooperative that sells single-origin must hold stock for longer, finance fermentation infrastructure, and wait for a buyer's quality approval before the lot is priced. The premium covers part of this; the financing cost is usually invisible in the contract.

Traceability is the third. European due-diligence rules, phased in since 2023, require a geolocation for the plots that produced the beans. A cooperative with 400 members can produce that record with a tablet and a boundary walk; a cooperative with 4,000 members, scattered plots and no digitized land register cannot, and those cooperatives lose access to the premium segment entirely.

Consolidation follows from that arithmetic. Buyers prefer to contract with cooperatives large enough to fill a container and organized enough to document it, and the number of such organizations in any producing region is small. The farmers outside them continue to sell bulk, at a price that increasingly reflects the discount for unverifiable origin.

Chocolate makers describe the shift as quality differentiation. The economics is closer to vertical coordination: a buyer that specifies fermentation, drying and traceability is managing a supplier, not buying a commodity, and the management costs are real even when they appear on the supplier's side of the ledger.

Certification costs are the third transfer. A single-origin lot must be documented against a standard, and the audit, the sampling and the laboratory analysis of cadmium and pesticide residues are paid by the seller. A cooperative that ships four containers a year absorbs these costs across a smaller volume than an exporter that ships four hundred, and the per-tonne cost can consume a fifth of the premium.

Bargaining is where the arrangement is most fragile. A buyer that has specified a fermentation protocol and approved the lot knows more about the cooperative's capacity than the cooperative knows about the buyer's alternatives, and the contract that results is usually take-it-or-leave-it on price. Transparency rules help at the top of the market, where a cooperative with a reputation can sell to several buyers, and do little at the bottom.

For a reader following food supply chains, the lesson generalizes. Every traceability mandate transfers a documentation cost to the least capitalized node in the chain, and the node that cannot absorb it exits the high-value market rather than investing in it. The premium does not fix that; it selects for the producers who already had the capacity.
