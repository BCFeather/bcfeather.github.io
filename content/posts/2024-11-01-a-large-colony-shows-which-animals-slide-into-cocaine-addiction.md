Addiction is usually described by three behaviours, and laboratory models have had difficulty producing all three in one animal. A paper in *eLife* on 1 November 2024 reports a study large enough to ask whether they co-occur, and finds that they do only in a minority. Extended access is the paradigm's central variable, because a session of one hour cannot produce the escalation that a session of six hours reliably does, whatever the dose.

More than five hundred outbred animals were given extended access to cocaine rather than the short sessions that most earlier work used. Extended access is the design variable that permits escalation, and a session too short to permit it has already excluded one of the three criteria. Three measures that load on separate factors are three traits, and a study that reports a single score has decided in advance that the question has one answer.

Three measures were followed over the study: how quickly intake rose, how hard an animal worked for the drug while a punishment was available, and whether use continued when it became expensive. The measures load on separate factors rather than on a single one, which is the result the sample size was chosen to detect. Punishment is what separates compulsive seeking from seeking that is merely intense, since an animal that avoids a punished lever is still behaving adaptively.

Sex differences appear in the pattern rather than in the average. Animals of both sexes reach all three criteria, and the ones that do are the ones whose intake escalated fastest rather than the ones that consumed most in total. The animals that reach all three criteria are a minority of the colony, which is roughly the proportion that a clinical sample of people with substance dependence also contains.

Genetic diversity is the reason for an outbred colony. An inbred strain removes the variation that a study of individual differences needs, and a colony of this size supplies enough of it for the three measures to be related to one another within a single experiment. An outbred colony is expensive and the cost is worth paying, because individual differences are the phenomenon and an inbred strain removes them before the experiment begins.

The paper does not identify a cause for the transition, and its measures are behavioural rather than physiological. Whether the animals that escalate share a neural signature is the question that recordings would answer, and the behavioural map is what those recordings would be designed around. A behavioural map without a neural one is a lead rather than a mechanism, and a lead of this kind is what the next study inherits along with the paradigms it defines.

What the study establishes is that addiction-like behaviour is a combination of traits rather than a single threshold, and that a model reporting only intake has measured the easiest of the three and has assumed the answer to the question it is asking. What a model reporting only intake has measured is the easiest of the three criteria, and the ease is the reason it was measured first rather than the reason it is sufficient.
