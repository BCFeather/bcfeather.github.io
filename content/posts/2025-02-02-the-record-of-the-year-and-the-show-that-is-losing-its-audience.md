The music awards were handed out in Los Angeles on the second of February, in a ceremony that ran for more than three hours and was broadcast live to an audience that was a fraction of the one it had twenty years ago. The prize that the industry cares about most went to an artist who had been nominated for it four times before without winning, which the ceremony treated as its emotional centre.

The economics of the broadcast have changed faster than the economics of the prize. The television audience for an awards show has fallen every year since the mid-2010s, and the ceremony that once drew tens of millions now draws a number that the network describes as stable and the trade press describes as a decline. The advertising that pays for the show is priced on that audience, and the ceremony is now a smaller business than the streaming that it promotes.

The streaming has its own arithmetic. A song that wins a prize is a song that people already knew, and the catalogue that a listener returns to is the one that was built before the prize. The award therefore does less for the revenue of the winner than the industry's own story suggests, and the labels know it.

The prize still matters for a different reason. An award is a credential that a musician can put on a poster for the rest of a career, and the credential is worth more to the artist who is not yet established than to the one who already is. The categories that the ceremony schedules in the afternoon, off the main broadcast, are the ones where the newer artists appear, and the afternoon is where the prizes do the most work.

The ceremony also has a function that the audience figure does not capture. The industry gathers in one room once a year, the executives meet the artists, and the deals that will be announced in the spring are discussed in the intervals. The show is a trade fair with a red carpet, and the trade fair part is still useful.

The criticism that the ceremony attracts is about the distance between the prize and the audience. The categories that the general public follows are a small share of the awards that are given, and the evening is organized around the ones that the broadcast can sell. The argument about that is as old as the show and it is renewed every year.

What the ceremony shows is an institution adapting to a smaller room. The prize is still the most valuable thing a musician can win, the broadcast is still the largest stage the industry has, and the two are no longer the same size. The show continues because the industry needs a night, and the audience it reaches is now a bonus rather than the business. That is a smaller claim than the ceremony makes for itself, and it is the one the ratings support, which is why the trade press and the network describe the same evening in two different tenses.
