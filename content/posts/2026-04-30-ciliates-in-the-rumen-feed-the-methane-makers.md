Methane from livestock is a climate problem with a microbiological solution somewhere inside it. The rumen is a fermenter containing bacteria, archaea, protozoa and fungi, and the methane is produced by methanogenic archaea, which use hydrogen and carbon dioxide to make it, so the gas is the end of a chain that begins with hydrogen. Anything that changes the hydrogen supply changes the methane.

Ciliates are the large eukaryotes in the rumen, and they have been suspected of colluding with the archaea since the 1970s, when methanogens were found living on and inside ciliate cells. The suspicion was hard to test because ciliates are difficult to culture and their genomes are difficult to assemble, being large, repetitive and full of bacterial sequence.

A study in *Science* in late April by Xie and colleagues assembled a catalogue of ciliate genomes across a range of ruminants, and used the catalogue to work out what the ciliates are doing in methane metabolism. The answer is that they carry their own organelle, bounded by a single membrane, that produces hydrogen gas.

The organelle is the interesting part, because it is a eukaryotic solution to a problem that bacteria usually solve. A hydrogenosome, as the structure is called in other organisms, oxidises substrates and releases hydrogen as a waste product, and in an environment where methanogens are waiting for hydrogen, a waste product is a feedstock. The ciliates are running a hydrogen pipeline into the archaea's factory, and the pipeline is inside the ciliate and not in the fluid around it.

What the genomes show is that the arrangement is general and not incidental. Different ciliate lineages in different ruminants carry the organelle and the genes that support it, and the same genes recur in hosts that have been separated by continents and by millions of years of herbivore evolution, which suggests that the partnership is old and that it has been maintained across host species and geography, and not acquired recently by one lineage in one herd.

The practical consequence is a target. Interventions that reduce methane from livestock have concentrated on the archaea, on feed additives and on vaccines, and a host-side target that removes the hydrogen supply is a different kind of intervention, because it acts upstream of the step that produces the gas and because it can be reached through the animal's own biology.

Precision is the challenge. The rumen is an ecosystem of hundreds of species, and removing a ciliate that also helps digest fibre would trade methane for productivity. A targeted intervention would need to remove the organelle or its output and leave the ciliate's other functions intact, and that level of specificity is not available today, which is why the near-term interventions are feed additives, oils and vaccines that act on the archaea or on the rumen's overall fermentation. What the paper establishes is the mechanism and the scale. Methane from ruminants is a large source of anthropogenic emissions, the microbiology that produces it has been studied for a century, and a eukaryotic organelle that feeds the process had been hiding in plain sight, in genomes that nobody could assemble until the sequencing got good enough.
