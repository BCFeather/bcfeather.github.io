description: "The Labor Department reported on 1 November that employers added just 12,000 jobs in October, the weakest monthly figure since 2020, as two hurricanes and a strike at Boeing held back hiring."
---Employers in the United States added twelve thousand jobs in October, the smallest monthly increase since December 2020, according to the report published by the Labour Department on the first of November. Unemployment rate held at 4.1 per cent, and average hourly earnings rose by 0.4 per cent on the month. Figure was so far below the recent trend, which had been running at about 150,000 a month, that the department attached a note to the release explaining the distortions that had produced it.

Two shocks accounted for most of the shortfall. Hurricane Helene struck the south-east at the end of September and Milton crossed Florida in October, and the department said the storms had prevented several hundred thousand people from working during the survey week. Strike at Boeing, which by then had idled thirty-three thousand machinists, removed a further large block from the payroll count. Neither effect is permanent, and both were expected to reverse in November. Department's establishment survey counted jobs rather than people, so workers who were paid for part of the month still appear in it, which is why the storms damaged the figure less than the household survey suggested.

Revisions went in the other direction. Department cut its estimates for August and September by a combined 112,000 jobs, which turned a picture of steady hiring into one of slowing hiring. Over the past three months the economy has added an average of 104,000 jobs, about half the pace of a year earlier, and the sectors that drove the post-pandemic recovery, leisure, hospitality and health care, are now the only ones adding staff in volume.

The Response in markets was to treat the report as noise and the revisions as signal. Yields on two-year Treasury notes fell, and futures markets raised the probability of a rate cut in December. Economists at several banks said the underlying trend was consistent with an economy that is cooling rather than contracting, and they noted that the unemployment rate has risen only gradually from its low of 3.4 per cent in 2023.

Political reading of the numbers was immediate and unavoidable. Report came four days before the election, and both campaigns cited it. The Republican candidate said it proved the economy had been mismanaged, and the Democratic administration pointed to the hurricane and strike effects and to the strength of consumer spending. Independent economists were careful to say that a single month, distorted by weather and a labour dispute, says little about the state of the labour market.

Report also completed a pattern that has run through the year. Hiring has narrowed to a few sectors, job openings have fallen back to pre-pandemic levels, and the number of people quitting has dropped, which reduces wage pressure. Federal Reserve officials had already cut rates in September and were debating a second cut when the report landed. Weak payrolls, with unemployment still low, is the combination that gives a central bank room to ease without appearing to panic. Economists will look to the November report, published after the election, for evidence of whether the distortions were temporary, and the answer will shape the pace of cuts through the winter.
