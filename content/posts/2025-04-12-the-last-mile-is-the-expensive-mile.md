Broadband subsidies are designed around a number that is easy to state and hard to achieve: every household connected. The programmes that fund the last mile pay per location passed, and the locations that remain are the ones where the cost per passing is highest. A fibre build in a suburb costs a few hundred dollars per home; a build in a valley with twelve houses costs tens of thousands. A programme that pays per passing has an incentive to count passings, and the counting is where the audits find problems.

The technology mix has changed to reflect that arithmetic. Fibre reaches where density justifies trenching, fixed wireless covers where a tower can serve a radius, and satellite serves the remainder. A household with a satellite dish has a connection that works and a latency that makes video calls difficult, which is a different product from the one the subsidy promised. A tower that serves a valley is a tower with a backhaul link, and the backhaul is a fibre run that costs as much as the tower.

The subsidy programmes have also acquired a preference for fibre. A funding rule that pays for a technology that will last thirty years is a rule that pays more per home than one that pays for a tower, and the difference is a political choice about what a connection is for. A household that needs a connection for schoolwork needs bandwidth and reliability, and a fibre line delivers both. A thirty-year asset is a promise about a technology that may be obsolete in ten, and the promise is what the subsidy is buying.

Take-up is the metric that the programmes do not measure well. A line that passes a house is not a customer, and the households that do not subscribe are often the ones the programme was designed for. A connection costs forty dollars a month, and a family that cannot afford it has a line it does not use. A household that does not subscribe is a household that the programme counts as served, and the difference is the gap between a map and a policy.

Construction is the binding constraint. A build requires permits, poles, conduit and a crew, and the crews are scarce. A programme that commits ten billion dollars in a year cannot spend it in a year, and the money that is unspent becomes a number in a report. A crew that can splice fibre is a crew that is booked for two years, and the booking is what a programme cannot accelerate with money.

What the last mile exposes is that connectivity is a distribution problem rather than a technology problem. The technology to connect a remote house exists and is affordable at the device level, and the cost of reaching the house is a cost that someone has to decide to pay. A country that decides to pay it has made a choice about equity, and a country that does not has made a different one. A connection that is available and unaffordable is a connection that has changed nothing for the family it was meant to reach.
