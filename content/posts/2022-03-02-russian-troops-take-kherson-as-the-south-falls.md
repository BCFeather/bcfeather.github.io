Russian armour entered the centre of Kherson on 2 March after a week of fighting on the approaches, and the city of 280,000 people on the Dnieper became the first major Ukrainian city to come under Russian control. Columns advanced from the Crimea along the road that runs west, and they met resistance at the bridges over the river before the defence pulled back. By the evening the Russian defence ministry said the city was secured, and the Ukrainian authorities said the fighting had moved to the north of the region.

The surrender was negotiated by the mayor, Igor Kolykhaev, who told residents to stay indoors after curfew and said he had agreed to fly no Ukrainian flags and to accept the conditions the commander set. He described the arrangement as an attempt to keep the city from being destroyed, and he stayed in his office through the spring. A Russian administration was installed in April under the name of a military-civilian authority, the rouble arrived in May as the second currency alongside the hryvnia, and passports were issued from the summer, which meant that a resident who took one had accepted the new state in writing.

Kherson mattered for the map. The city stands at the mouth of the Dnieper and on the canal that carries water to the Crimea, and the road west from it runs towards Mykolaiv and then to Odesa. Control of the crossing gave the Russian army a route to the coast for the land bridge it was building from Donetsk to the Crimea, and it gave it a way to threaten the ports that Ukraine still held.

For three weeks the streets were quiet and then they were full. Crowds gathered in the main square on 5 and 6 March with Ukrainian flags, Russian troops fired into the air and then at the crowd, and several people were wounded. The protests continued through the spring at a smaller scale, and by the summer the authorities were describing them as a security problem. Teachers who refused the Russian curriculum were dismissed, and journalists who kept filing for Ukrainian outlets were detained.

What the occupation looked like in Kherson became the template for the rest. A military administration took the tax receipts, the pension system was moved onto the Russian budget, and schools were told to teach in Russian. Food arrived in humanitarian convoys that the Russian ministry organized and distributed through the new administration, medicine ran short in the pharmacies, and residents who wanted to leave could cross to Ukrainian-held territory, where the queues at the checkpoints ran for kilometres and the wait could take a day and a night.

The city was retaken in November, and the retreat across the river cost the Russian army one of the few gains it could defend. What those eight months established was that a city taken in a day can be held for a year without being won, and that the administration of an occupied place is where a war of this kind is decided. Kherson was the first test of that argument, and it was fought twice: once in March with armour, and once afterwards with paperwork.
