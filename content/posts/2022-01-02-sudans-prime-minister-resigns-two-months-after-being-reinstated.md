Abdalla Hamdok resigned as Sudan's prime minister on 2 January, two months after the generals who had detained him in October reinstated him under a deal brokered by the United Nations. He said in a televised address that the arrangement had failed to end the violence against protesters and that he could not hold together a transitional government whose partners disagreed about everything.

It came after a fortnight in which the streets of Khartoum stayed full despite a renewed state of emergency. Security forces had used tear gas and live ammunition against demonstrators, medical groups reported scores of deaths since October, and the prime minister's own office had become a place where decisions were announced rather than taken. He had been kept under guard for weeks after the coup and released on condition that he form a cabinet of technocrats.

His argument for stepping down was procedural and it was also personal. The deal he accepted in November gave the military a veto over the cabinet, kept the sovereign council in the hands of the armed forces, and left the committees that run the police and the intelligence services untouched. A prime minister who cannot appoint a police chief can be blamed for what the police do, and that is the position he described in his resignation. He had spent the previous year arguing that a cabinet of technocrats could hold the transition together while the army and the protest movement negotiated a new settlement. That argument lost its audience when the committees meant to draft the settlement stopped meeting.

Generals accepted the news without drama. The sovereign council said it would appoint a replacement, and the military commander, Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, praised the outgoing prime minister in public while the protest movement called the resignation a confession that the partnership had been a fiction. Within days the security services had arrested two civilian politicians who had been part of the transition.

Diplomacy now has less to work with. The United Nations mission, the African Union and Western governments had built their strategy around Hamdok as the civilian face of a transition that the army tolerated, and his departure removes the person through whom pressure could be applied. The World Bank and the International Monetary Fund had suspended disbursements after the coup, and the economic crisis that had made the army unpopular in the first place deepened through the winter. Western capitals had also made the prime minister the condition for resuming budget support, which meant that his resignation removed the largest lever they held over the generals.

Sudan's transition has now gone through a coup, a reinstatement and a resignation in under three months, and each step was announced as a compromise. The army has kept prosecutions of its own officers inside military courts, and the civilians left in the government have no power to change that. What the sequence shows is that a power-sharing arrangement which leaves the guns where they were does not share power; it rents it, and the tenant can always be evicted.
