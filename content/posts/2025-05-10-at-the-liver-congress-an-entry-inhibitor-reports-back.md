Chronic hepatitis B is the most common cause of liver cancer in the world, and it has been treatable for twenty years without being curable. The drugs that exist, nucleoside analogues and interferons, suppress the virus so effectively that its DNA becomes undetectable in most patients. Stop taking them and it returns. The reason is a piece of viral housekeeping that sits upstream of everything the drugs can touch: covalently closed circular DNA, cccDNA, a stable loop that the virus parks in the nucleus of a liver cell and uses as the master template for everything it makes.

At this year's congress of the European Association for the Study of the Liver, which opened in Amsterdam on 7 May, a Chinese research group presented phase 2 data for a drug that attacks a different step, the one before cccDNA exists. Entry inhibitors block the receptor the virus uses to get into a hepatocyte. If it cannot get in, it cannot establish its nuclear template, and if the template is never established, the infection does not take hold in the first place.

The concept is not new. Bulevirtide, an entry inhibitor approved for hepatitis delta, proved that the target is druggable. What the Amsterdam presentation adds is a randomized, double-blind trial in chronic hepatitis B, the harder and much larger population, with the endpoint that matters most in this field: whether the drug does something that the existing treatments cannot. The reported result was described by the investigators as a breakthrough in the clinical programme, and the details presented included a reduction in a marker of viral activity alongside a safety profile that the trial was designed to test.

What entry inhibition implies about the shape of a cure is where the scientific interest lies. Current therapy holds the virus down; an entry inhibitor locks the door for new infections of new cells. Combined, the two do different jobs: one reduces the output of the existing templates, the other stops the templates from multiplying across the liver. Whether that combination can eventually let a patient stop treatment is the question that will define the next five years of hepatitis B research, and it is a question that requires long trials, not press releases.

The practical obstacles are the usual ones for a new class. Entry inhibitors are injectable, which is a real cost for a disease treated with a daily tablet. The phase 2 population is modest, and the endpoints are surrogate markers rather than the functional cure that regulators will eventually want. And chronic hepatitis B is concentrated in exactly the health systems that can least afford a second expensive injectable on top of a cheap generic.

Still, the direction is right and overdue. A field that spent two decades optimizing the same mechanism now has a clinical result for a different one, in the population that needs it most. If the phase 3 programme confirms what the Amsterdam data suggest, the treatment of hepatitis B will stop being a lifetime of suppression and start being a course of therapy with an end date.
