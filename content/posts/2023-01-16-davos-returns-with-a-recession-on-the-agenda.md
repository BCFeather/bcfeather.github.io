January 16 opened the annual meeting of the World Economic Forum in Davos, the first winter session held in person since 2020. About 2,700 people registered, fewer than the 3,000 of the last full gathering, and the hotels along the promenade were half empty on the first morning. The theme printed on every panel card was cooperation in a fragmented world, which is the sort of phrase that gets written when the drafters cannot agree on anything more specific. Registration fees rose again this year, and organisers said the cost of security had doubled since the last winter meeting.

Attendance from the largest economies was thinner than usual. Several heads of government stayed away, and the American delegation was led by officials rather than by the president. China sent a vice-premier, its most senior presence in years, and he used the slot to argue that the reopening would lift global demand. European leaders came in numbers, mostly to talk about energy and the price cap on gas that had just taken effect. The delegations that did come were larger than usual, because ministers brought their officials rather than travelling alone.

Chief economists published their annual survey on the opening morning, and two-thirds of the respondents expected a global recession in the year. That number had been rising all autumn and it set the tone for every panel afterwards. The managing director of the International Monetary Fund said the year would be tougher than the one before, and she put growth at about 2.7 per cent for the world economy, a downgrade from the October forecast. The survey has been published for three years, and its recession call has not always been right, which the authors acknowledged.

Energy was on more panels than any other subject. European industrialists described the cost of gas as a permanent handicap, and American suppliers talked about liquefied cargoes and the ships that would carry them. A session on the energy transition drew a standing crowd at the back of the hall, and the questions were about permits and grids rather than about targets. American exporters said they could ship perhaps 10 per cent more this year, limited by terminal capacity rather than by gas.

Technology firms arrived with a smaller footprint than in previous years. The large platforms sent their policy people rather than their founders, and the parties were fewer and shorter. Several venture investors said privately that they were spending the week raising money rather than announcing it, which is the opposite of what Davos is for. Recruitment firms reported fewer meetings than usual and said the mood among technology clients had not recovered.

What the meeting produced, as usual, was language. Communiqués mentioned resilience, safety nets and the need to avoid a race in industrial subsidies. None of the governments represented committed new money, and none of the chief executives promised to hold investment through the downturn. The value of the week lies in the corridor conversations, and those do not appear in the summary. The next meeting is scheduled for January 2024, and the organisers have already booked the same halls.
