A tournament that was announced as a controversy ended with a result that was not in doubt, and Chelsea's 3-0 win over Paris Saint-Germain in the final at MetLife Stadium on 13 July gave the expanded club World Cup a champion and its organisers an argument. Palmer scored twice and set up the third, and the English side was ahead within twenty minutes.

Palmer was the difference in a match that had been billed as a contest between two midfield projects. He finished a low cross in the eighth minute, scored again from the edge of the area before the half hour, and played the pass that released the third, and a player who had been left out of the England squad in the spring was the best player on the pitch in July. A final that was decided inside half an hour is the outcome that a tournament of this shape produces when one team has played together for a month and the other has played together for a fortnight.

Paris were the more expensive team and the less coherent one. Their midfield was overrun in the first half, their full-backs were caught high, and the substitutions at half time changed the shape without changing the scoreline, which is what happens when a plan depends on one player and he is marked out of it.

The money is the part that will be remembered. The winner took about 114 million dollars, the total prize fund was about a billion, and the tournament was financed largely by an American broadcaster and a sovereign fund, which is a model that European leagues have objected to and that the next edition will have to test again. A prize of that size for seven matches is more than the entire annual revenue of most clubs in the competition, and the distribution, weighted heavily towards the finalists, is the reason the smaller confederations signed up and the reason the European leagues objected.

Format is the argument that the result does not settle. Thirty-two clubs from six confederations played a month of matches in the American summer, players arrived from a season that had just ended, and two of them tore hamstrings in the group stage, which is the evidence that the players' union will bring to the next negotiation.

Crowds were decent and not full. The final drew a crowd above 80,000, the group matches in smaller cities drew far fewer, and the pricing was high enough that a family of four was a significant purchase, which is a commercial model that works in a World Cup year and may not work in an ordinary July. A player who tears a hamstring in a group match in New Jersey misses the start of his domestic season, and the club that pays his wages bears the cost of a tournament that pays the federation and not the employer.

What would settle the format is whether the European clubs send full squads in 2029. A full squad would mean the money has bought legitimacy, a rotated one would mean the tournament has become a pre-season tour with a trophy, and the answer will be visible in the team sheets and not in the press releases.
