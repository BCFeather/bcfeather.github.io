Perovskite solar cells have been the most efficient laboratory devices in photovoltaics and the least durable. A paper published this month in *Joule* reports a tandem module that survived the damp-heat test that the industry uses as the gate to commercial qualification: 1,000 hours at 85 degrees Celsius and 85 per cent relative humidity, with 93 per cent of initial efficiency remaining.

What matters more than the efficiency number is the test. Damp heat is where perovskites fail, because moisture enters the crystal, reacts at the grain boundaries and produces irreversible decomposition, and the standard encapsulant used on silicon modules does not keep it out. Passing the test means the device stack has been engineered so that moisture ingress is slower than the test's timescale.

What changed is the contact layer. The team replaced the organic hole-transport material, which is the weak point at the top of the stack, with a doped inorganic layer deposited by sputtering and annealed at low temperature. The layer is hydrophobic, blocks ion migration and does not decompose at 85 degrees.

Efficiency figures are 28.4 per cent on a 24 square centimetre aperture, which is below the best laboratory tandems at 33 per cent and above anything a commercial module achieves today. The team argues that the trade is the point: a device that loses three points of efficiency and passes the reliability test is closer to a product than one that sets a record and fails.

Measurement is careful. The module was tested in an independent chamber, with current-voltage sweeps at standard conditions before and after, and the reported degradation is from a single module rather than a batch, which the paper acknowledges as a limitation.

Remaining failure modes are the ones the test does not reach. Damp heat does not capture thermal cycling, ultraviolet exposure or the mechanical stress of a rooftop installation, and perovskites are known to degrade under all three. The qualification sequence has further tests, and the paper reports two of them at shorter durations.

Manufacturing is the other gate. A tandem cell requires a textured silicon bottom cell, a perovskite top cell deposited in a controlled atmosphere, and a contacting step that must not damage either. Pilot lines exist and run at low volume; the yield and the cost at gigawatt scale are unproven.

Ultraviolet stability is the next gate. Perovskites degrade under UV in the presence of oxygen, and the standard test sequence includes 1,000 hours of UV conditioning before the damp-heat step. The paper reports 500 hours at reduced intensity, which the authors describe as preliminary. Thermal cycling from minus 40 to 85 degrees is the third gate, and it has broken tandems that passed damp heat.

For the field, the result shifts the argument from whether perovskites can be stable to how much stability costs. A tandem module at 28 per cent efficiency and a 25-year lifetime is a product that changes the arithmetic of rooftop installation; the same module at 15 years is not, because the balance-of-system costs dominate.
