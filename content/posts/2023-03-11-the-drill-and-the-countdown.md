The drill began at twenty past six, and by March the school had stopped pretending that it was optional. Three years of standing in rows on the concrete had produced a kind of muscle memory, so that the whole form could find its place without being told and could keep a straight line while the megaphone counted. The physical training had been reduced in the final year, on the theory that the time was better spent on papers, and what remained was the running and the standing and the exercises that could be done in place.

Mornings started earlier than they had in the autumn. The first light arrived at half past six by the middle of March, and the boys who came from the east gate walked in with the sky behind them turning from grey to white. The classroom was unlocked at six, and there were always four or five people already inside, and the ones who arrived first had a routine that they did not describe to anyone. The people who came in first had a way of not looking up when someone else arrived, which was a politeness that nobody had taught them.

Evenings were the longest part of the day. The last period ended at half past nine, and the walk from the school gate to the flat took eleven minutes if the light at the corner was green. My mother had begun to leave a covered bowl on the table with a note that said how long to heat it, and the note was always the same four words, and the sameness was a comfort that I did not recognize until much later. The note was written in pencil and the bowl was always the same bowl, and the two of them together were the most reliable part of the day.

The countdown on the board was at ninety-five by the second week of March. It had been written in red at the start of the term and re-written every Monday, and the number had begun to appear in other places: on the corner of a test paper, on the back cover of a notebook, in the margin of a dictionary. Nobody said the number aloud, and everybody knew it.

Spring arrived in the middle of the month and made the building look different. The trees along the wall of the playground came out within a week, and the light in the afternoon changed so that the classroom's windows showed the colour of the leaves rather than the colour of the sky. The change was unwelcome in a way that was hard to explain, because it made the room feel like a place that would be looked at from outside.

What March did was shorten the distance between the ordinary and the last. The drill, the bowl under the note, the eleven minutes to the flat, the number on the board: each of them was still what it had always been, and each of them had acquired a second meaning that nobody had asked for. The second meaning was the one that stayed, and the ordinary one was the one that the year would take away.
