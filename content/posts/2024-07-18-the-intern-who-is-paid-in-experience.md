---Summer internships begin in July. A student in the second or third year of a degree applies in April, interviews in May and starts in the first week of the holiday. Placement lasts six or eight weeks. The pay varies more than the work. A bank pays a daily rate and provides a canteen card. A media company pays a transport allowance and nothing else. A technology firm pays a proper salary and expects the intern to be productive in the second week.

The Cost of the room is what the advertisements leave out. A student who moves to a city for eight weeks rents a room at a monthly rate that a landlord sets for a year. Rent is a month and a half of a graduate salary, paid by a family. The work is often administrative. A student files documents, formats a spreadsheet, sits in on meetings and takes notes. Tasks that the firm would not give a permanent employee go to the intern, which is the reason the internship exists.

Certificate at the end is the product. A stamped page that says the student worked from this date to that date is what a graduate application asks for. Content of the work matters less than the existence of the paper. The conversion to a job is the hope. A firm that takes ten interns in July makes offers to two in September. The two are chosen by the team they worked with, which is why an intern who is placed badly has a worse chance than an intern who is placed well.

A dormitory is the cheap alternative. A university that keeps its halls open in July lets a student stay for a fee that is a tenth of the market rent. A student who takes a placement in another city loses that option. The internship is also a form of screening. A firm that runs a summer programme uses it to see how a candidate behaves in an office before it commits to a salary. Eight weeks are cheaper than a probation period.

Narrow funnel makes the application a competition. A firm that advertises ten places receives a thousand applications, screens three hundred by a test, interviews sixty, and takes ten. Tests are a set of numerical questions that a student can prepare for in a week.

Reference letters are the other product. A supervisor who writes one for an intern writes twenty in August, and the letters are similar. A recruiter who reads them learns little beyond the fact that the student turned up. A student who takes two placements in two summers has two references and one line of experience, which is a better position than a student with neither. The students who do not get a placement take a course or a trip. A recruiter reads the gap on a form as a lack of initiative, which is a judgement that the student cannot argue with. By September the placements are over, the references are filed, and the students who were paid in experience are applying for the jobs that the experience was meant to open.
