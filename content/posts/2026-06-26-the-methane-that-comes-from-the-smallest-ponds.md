Methane is the second most important greenhouse gas, and the hardest to account for. Its sources are numerous and small: wetlands, rice paddies, landfills, livestock, leaks from gas infrastructure. Each is modest, the sum is large, and the measurements are sparse enough that the global budget has an uncertainty of tens of per cent. A survey published this month adds a category that has been systematically undercounted: ponds.

The study compiled chamber measurements from several hundred water bodies smaller than a hectare across a range of climates, and extrapolated using a global inventory of small water bodies. The finding is that these ponds emit methane at rates per unit area several times higher than lakes and reservoirs, and that their combined contribution is a significant fraction of the wetland total. The reason is that a small pond has a large shoreline relative to its volume, shallow water, and abundant organic sediment, which is precisely the setting in which anaerobic decomposition produces methane.

The mechanism is worth spelling out because it explains why the small water bodies were missed. Methane is produced in anoxic sediment, and it escapes either by bubbling or by diffusing through the water column, where a fraction is oxidized by bacteria before it reaches the air. In a deep lake, the diffusive pathway is long and the oxidation efficient. In a pond half a metre deep, the path is short, the bubbles reach the surface directly, and the oxidation has little time to work.

The survey also found that the emission depends strongly on how the pond is managed. Ponds that receive fertilizer runoff or livestock waste produce far more than ponds in untouched catchments, and ponds that dry out seasonally produce a burst of methane when they rewet, as the sediment that accumulated during the dry period decomposes anaerobically. Both are common in agricultural landscapes, which is where most small water bodies are.

The accounting consequence is about inventories. National greenhouse gas inventories are built from categories, and a pond that is smaller than the mapping threshold is not a category. It is either counted as wetland, or not counted at all. The survey's authors argue that the inventory methods should add a term for small water bodies, with an emission factor that depends on climate and management, and they provide the data to calibrate it.

The scientific caveat is that chamber measurements are local and the extrapolation is global. A pond in a temperate catchment and a pond in a tropical one behave differently, and the survey's sampling is denser in some regions than others. The authors are explicit that their estimate is a first cut, and that the uncertainty is large.

The reason the result matters beyond the budget is that small water bodies are easy to create and easy to drain. Farm ponds, ditches, and the puddles that form behind a blocked drain are all part of the same category, and their number is a function of land management. A greenhouse gas inventory that ignores them is ignoring a source that policy can actually influence, which is an unusual combination in climate accounting.
