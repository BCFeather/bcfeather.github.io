The 2014 Toledo drinking water crisis, when the city told 400,000 people not to drink its water, was caused by a cyanobacterial bloom in Lake Erie. The bloom was dominated by *Microcystis*, and the question of which strain dominated it turned out to be a question about oxidative stress. A drinking water crisis is the kind of event that sets a policy target, and the target was a phosphorus load that would keep the bloom below the threshold at which a treatment plant can cope.

A study combined a mechanistic model of competition between toxigenic and non-toxigenic strains with a simpler empirical model fitted to the lake's monitoring record. The paper appeared in *Science*, 26 May 2022.

Two strains cope with oxidative stress differently. The toxigenic strain makes microcystin, which protects the cell against reactive oxygen species, while the non-toxigenic strain relies on enzymes that degrade those species. The enzymes are more sensitive to nitrogen limitation than the pigment is, which is why a shift in the nitrogen supply can shift the outcome of the competition. A strain that pays the metabolic cost of making a toxin must be receiving a benefit, and the benefit here is protection against the oxidative stress that light and nutrient limitation create.

A planned phosphorus reduction would lower biomass, which is the intended effect. It will also make nitrogen and light more available per cell, because a smaller bloom consumes less of both. The models predict that this will favour the toxigenic strain and increase toxin concentrations even as the bloom shrinks. A bloom that is smaller also shades the water less, and the extra light reaching the cells is a subsidy for the strain that can tolerate it.

That is a policy-relevant inversion: a measure that reduces the visible nuisance could increase the health risk. The management target has been phosphorus because phosphorus limits the total biomass, and the toxin is not proportional to biomass. A manager who has been told to reduce phosphorus and who reads the paper is being told that the metric and the goal have come apart. The regulatory framework has been built around a total maximum daily load, and that is a framework that cannot distinguish a lake with a large harmless bloom from one with a small toxic one.

Both models are calibrated to one lake and one set of blooms, and the empirical model is fitted to a record that includes only a few years of the relevant transition. The prediction is a hypothesis as much as a forecast, and it has the merit of being testable against the monitoring data of the next decade. The mechanism is the kind that a monitoring programme could test directly, by measuring the ratio of toxigenic to non-toxigenic cells as the load falls.

A wider lesson concerns nutrient management has to consider the stoichiometry of the organism as well as the total load. A lake that is nitrogen-limited selects for a different community than a lake that is phosphorus-limited, and the toxin is a property of the community rather than of the load. The result also argues for measuring the toxin and not only the chlorophyll, because a bloom that is smaller and greener can be a bloom that is more dangerous. A lake is a system with a nitrogen cycle and a phosphorus cycle that are coupled through the organisms, and a policy that touches one of them moves the other in a direction that the models can predict but the permits cannot.
