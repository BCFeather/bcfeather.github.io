A used car's price is set by the supply of cars coming off lease and the demand of buyers who cannot afford new ones, and the two have moved in opposite directions this year. Prices fell 9 per cent in six months, and the supply's origin is a lease written three years ago.

The leases were signed when new-car prices were at a peak and inventory was scarce, and their residual values were set high. A leased car returning today is worth 12 per cent less than its contract's residual, and the lessor absorbs the difference and prices the next lease higher.

Dealers' inventory is the visible measure. Days' supply rose from 34 to 58, and a lot with 58 days of supply discounts 4 per cent more than one with 34, which is the mechanism that turns an inventory build into a price fall. New-car incentives are the second channel. A manufacturer's 8 per cent incentive on a new car competes with a three-year-old car at 65 per cent of its price, and a buyer with financing compares the two monthly payments. Electric vehicles are the segment with the largest fall. A three-year-old electric car lost 24 per cent of its value in a year, against 9 for a petrol car, and the difference is the battery's state of health report, which a buyer cannot verify.

The previous cycle sets the reference. The 2021 shortage pushed used prices up 30 per cent, and the fall since has returned them to a level 5 per cent above the pre-shortage trend rather than below it. The dealers' response is a pricing model. Two large groups now price a car within 24 hours of acquisition using a real-time transaction feed, and their inventory turns three days faster than a group that prices weekly.

A used car's price is set by a comparable sale and not by its cost. A dealer prices from a transaction feed of similar cars within a region, and a feed that reports falling prices produces a discount within days rather than weeks. Financing transmits the rate. A buyer's monthly payment is the price they compare, and a rise in the loan rate makes a 9 per cent price cut worth less than it looks. The two move together, which is why price cuts and incentive offers arrive in the same week.

A used car's residual value is the lease market's input, and the two markets feed each other. A fall in used prices raises the next lease's payment, which reduces new-car demand, which raises incentives, which pushes used prices down again. The loop is the reason a 9 per cent fall is not a stable number.

A used car's price is a lagged contract rather than a spot market. The lease signed three years ago sets today's supply, and the supply sets the discount that a buyer negotiates this week.
