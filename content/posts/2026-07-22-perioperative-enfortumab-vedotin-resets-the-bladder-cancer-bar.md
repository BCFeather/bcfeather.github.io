Muscle-invasive bladder cancer is treated by removing the bladder, and the chemotherapy given around the operation has been cisplatin-based for two decades. The regimen is effective and badly tolerated: a substantial share of patients cannot receive cisplatin at all, and those who do often arrive at surgery weakened. Antibody-drug conjugates have changed the arithmetic in metastatic disease, and the obvious question was whether moving them into the perioperative setting would beat the old backbone. That is the gap between a regimen that works in a trial and one that patients can complete, and it is the number oncologists will weigh against the survival gain.

The trial that answered it randomised patients with muscle-invasive, cisplatin-eligible disease to enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab or to cisplatin plus gemcitabine, given before and after surgery. It reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 22 July 2026. The primary endpoints were event-free survival and overall survival, with pathological complete response at cystectomy as a secondary measure of how much tumour the drugs removed before the operation.

At two years, event-free survival was 79.4 per cent with the antibody-drug combination against 66.2 per cent with chemotherapy, a hazard ratio of 0.53 with a confidence interval from 0.41 to 0.70. Overall survival was 86.9 per cent against 81.3 per cent, a hazard ratio of 0.65. Pathological complete response occurred in 55.8 per cent and 32.5 per cent of patients, and grade 3 or higher adverse events in 75.7 per cent and 67.2 per cent.

The pathological response rate is the part that explains the survival curve. Clearing tumour before surgery is a surrogate that has held up across solid tumours, and a jump of more than twenty percentage points implies that the combination is doing something the chemotherapy backbone could not. The toxicity is the cost of that activity: three quarters of patients on the new regimen had a serious adverse event, and the trial's discontinuation figures matter as much as its hazard ratios.

Whether the comparison is fair is the first objection. Cisplatin plus gemcitabine is the older standard, and many centres have already moved towards dose-dense or split-dose schedules. The trial also cannot say whether the benefit comes from the antibody-drug conjugate, from the checkpoint inhibitor, or from the pair, since both were given together and neither was tested alone in this setting. A three-arm design, with each component tested separately, is what would settle which drug is doing the work.

What changes is the default for cisplatin-eligible patients, and what remains open is the group that cannot take cisplatin at all, which was excluded by design. The next trials will test the combination in that population and ask whether the checkpoint inhibitor can be dropped for patients whose tumours do not express PD-L1. Both questions decide how widely the regimen travels.
