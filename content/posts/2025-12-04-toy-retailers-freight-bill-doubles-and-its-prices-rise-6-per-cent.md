A toy is a low-value, high-volume product, and its economics are set by how much air it occupies in a container. A retailer's disclosure this month shows freight at 14 per cent of cost of goods against 6 per cent three years ago, and its price rise of 6 per cent has covered less than half.

Category arithmetic explains the exposure. A plush toy fills a container at 40 dollars of cost per cubic metre and a building set at 400, so a freight increase hits the plush line hardest. The retailer's plush line is 40 per cent of its volume.

Container rates from Asia rose 2.4 times over the period, and the retailer's contracts covered 60 per cent of its volume at fixed rates. The uncovered 40 per cent is where the increase landed.

The response was a mix of three moves. The retailer consolidated shipments from five factories into two consolidators, which raised container fill from 68 to 82 per cent; it shifted 15 per cent of its plush volume to a nearer supplier at a 20 per cent higher unit cost; and it cut its SKU count by 30 per cent.

Margin effects show in the numbers. Gross margin fell from 34 to 29 per cent over three years, and the retailer's operating margin fell from 8 to 4.

Ownership of ships is the structural difference. Two large retailers have chartered their own vessels, which converts a volatile cost into a capital commitment. A mid-sized retailer cannot finance that.

The consumer's response is the constraint on pricing. A 20 per cent rise on a 15-dollar plush toy reduces purchase probability by 18 per cent in the retailer's own test, and a 6 per cent rise reduces it by 4.

A toy's freight share is unusually high because its value per cubic metre is low. A container of plush animals is worth 40,000 dollars and a container of electronics is worth 400,000, and the same freight bill is 2 per cent of one and 0.2 of the other.

The pricing gap is the interesting number. Freight doubled and prices rose 6 per cent, which means the retailer absorbed most of the increase and passed on a fraction. A retailer with a 30 per cent gross margin cannot pass on a 40 per cent cost increase without losing volume.

A toy's season is the constraint on pricing. Three quarters of a toy retailer's sales arrive in eight weeks, and a price rise in October is a rise that a customer can compare with last year's. A retailer that raises prices in November has fewer weeks to lose volume and less time to lose it in.

A freight rate is a product-design constraint, and a toy's shape is where it bites. A toy's shape and its air determine what a rate increase costs, and the cheapest response is often to change the toy.
