Superconductivity has been found in oxides, in iron-based compounds and in hydrides, and it remains rare among simple sulfides. Mercury sulfide in its cinnabar form is a semiconductor with a helical chain structure and no centre of symmetry, and the chains are the feature that a structural transition under pressure can remove.

A preprint posted on 27 December 2023 follows the material through that transition, at pressures that reach well above the reconstruction and well into the metallic phase. As pressure rises the helical chains approach each other and connect, and at 21 gigapascals the structure reconstructs into a centrosymmetric rocksalt arrangement. Superconductivity emerges just after the reconstruction, which places the onset at the structural change rather than at a separate electronic instability.

The critical temperature peaks at 11 kelvin near 25.4 gigapascals and falls with further compression, reaching 3.5 kelvin at 44.8 gigapascals, so the phase diagram has a dome and not a plateau, and the dome's descent is as informative as its peak. The upper critical field, fitted with two bands, is 5.65 tesla at zero temperature, and the two-band fit indicates that more than one sheet of the Fermi surface contributes to pairing.

Raman spectra point to the mechanism. A phonon softens as the transition is approached, and softening of that kind is the standard signature of a lattice instability that also strengthens electron-phonon coupling. The structural and the electronic changes are therefore linked by the same phonon, which is why the superconductivity appears with the reconstruction and not before it, and why the critical temperature tracks the soft mode once the new lattice is in place.

The material is the first group IIB metal sulfide reported to superconduct, and the only rocksalt-type metal sulfide with a critical temperature above 10 kelvin. Both claims are narrow, and they are the kind of first that matters for a family: mercury sits below zinc and cadmium in the same column of the periodic table, and the sulfides of those two metals have not shown the same behaviour under pressure.

Limits follow from the pressure. Every measurement is inside a diamond anvil cell, the phase that superconducts does not exist at ambient conditions, and the sample is a small grain whose purity and strain are not independently characterized, which leaves the width of the transition as a question for a second laboratory. A material that exists only under a press is a subject for physics and not for engineering, and the transition pressure is high enough that no device could use the phase without a cell around it.

For the search, the result suggests chiral structures as a hunting ground. A helical arrangement that can be driven into a denser symmetric one supplies both a structural transition and a phonon to soften, and the same recipe may apply to other sulfides and selenides whose chains are held apart by a lattice that pressure can close, which is a cheap criterion to screen with calculations before an experiment.
