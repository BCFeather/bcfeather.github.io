description: "Poland, Hungary and Slovakia kept national bans on Ukrainian grain after the European Commission lifted its restrictions on 15 September 2023, and Ukraine responded by filing a complaint at the World Trade Organization."
---

Brussels ended its temporary restrictions on imports of Ukrainian wheat, maize, rapeseed and sunflower seed on 15 September, on the argument that the blockages caused by the war no longer justified them. Poland, Hungary and Slovakia announced the same day that they would keep their own bans, and Bulgaria and Romania, which had also been covered, said they would not. Ukraine filed a complaint at the World Trade Organization on 18 September and threatened countermeasures, and its trade minister said the three countries had broken the rules of the single market.

Dispute is about prices and politics. Ukrainian grain has been travelling through Poland and Romania since the Black Sea route was closed, and part of it has stayed in the region rather than continuing to ports and to Africa, which has pushed down prices for local farmers. Polish farmers have blocked roads and border crossings, and the government has promised them support, compensation and a continued ban, delivered in a package announced at a campaign event in the countryside. The Polish election is on 15 October, and the governing party has been competing with a far-right party for the rural vote.

Legal position is awkward for both sides. The commission's decision was taken under a regulation that lets it act when a member state's market is disturbed, and it concluded that the disturbance had ended. The three governments that kept their bans have argued that their markets are still disturbed, and they have produced no data that the commission asked for, which is the weakness that Ukraine's complaint is built on. Ukraine's complaint will take months, and the commission has opened infringement proceedings against the three countries, which could end in the European Court of Justice.

Strategic cost is visible in the alliance. Poland has been one of Ukraine's most committed supporters, supplying weapons, hosting refugees and pressing its partners to do more, and the grain dispute has not changed that. Ukrainian officials have said they understand the political pressure and have asked for a solution that does not close the border, and Polish officials have said the ban protects farmers rather than the government, and that they will lift it when the market settles.

Wider trade picture is being renegotiated. Ukraine's exports through the European Union are governed by a temporary liberalization that expires next year, and the commission has proposed an extension with new safeguards. Farmers in the countries that border Ukraine have said they will accept the arrangement only if it includes a trigger that reintroduces restrictions when prices fall, which is the mechanism that the commission has been reluctant to write.

Two allies are arguing about wheat while they agree about the war, and the argument has moved from the market to the courts. The election in Poland will decide whether the ban ends in October, the harvest will decide whether the farmers stop asking for it, and the commission will decide whether the single market can survive an argument between its members and its candidate.
