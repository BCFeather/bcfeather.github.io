Green hydrogen depends on electrolysis, and electrolysis in acid depends on iridium. The metal is one of the rarest elements in the crust, and a technology that needs it at scale is a technology bounded by supply.

In *Science* on 12 May 2023, the metal is replaced. The authors dope a cobalt-based catalyst with a second metal and show that the doped material sustains oxygen evolution in acidic electrolyte, the reaction iridium currently performs.

Chemistry is where the difficulty lies. Acidic electrolysis is faster and more compact than alkaline, and the acidic environment dissolves most metals, so iridium survives because it is noble and a non-noble catalyst has to be protected. Doping supplies that protection, since a small fraction of a second element changes the surface's reconstruction and slows dissolution, and the electrochemical measurements show stability over hours.

Activity is the other metric, because a catalyst that is stable and slow is not a replacement, and the doped cobalt is compared against the benchmark. Mechanism stays debated, since a dopant can change electronic structure, surface composition or reaction pathway, and the spectroscopy addresses which of them applies.

Membrane needs follow from the chemistry. An acidic electrolyser needs a membrane keeping the gases apart, and a catalyst dissolving slowly still releases ions the membrane must tolerate, which is why a durability test in a cell rather than in a beaker is the relevant one. Supply chains shift the politics, because iridium comes largely from South African platinum-group refining while a cobalt route moves dependence to a metal with a different map of producers.

Current density sets the limitation, since a laboratory test at modest current is not a stack at industrial current, and durability at high current is what an electrolyser developer asks for first. Scale is the application's question, as a stack needs grams of iridium per megawatt and a cobalt catalyst would need its own supply chain and recycling route.

Lifetime of a full cell stays unshown, because hundreds of hours in a half-cell are a beginning and thousands in a stack are the target. Alkaline cells provide the honest comparison, since they avoid the noble metal entirely and pay for it in efficiency, and a cobalt catalyst in acid is an attempt to keep the efficiency without the iridium, with the trade decided by the price of electricity rather than by the paper. Energy materials gain a bottleneck removed, because a technology constrained by the rarest metal cannot scale. An activity measurement normalised to geometric area would separate the intrinsic gain of the doped surface from the gain that comes simply from a rougher one, and that normalisation is the point a sceptical reader should check first, along with the dopant's own cost and its own supply chain. A catalyst that performs for a thousand hours in acid earns a second look from the industry that has been waiting for one.
