description: "Doping a cobalt catalyst with a small amount of a second metal stabilizes it for oxygen evolution in acid, offering a cheaper alternative to iridium for water electrolysis, according to work in *Science* on 12 May 2023."
---

Green hydrogen depends on electrolysis, and electrolysis in acid depends on iridium. The metal is one of the rarest elements in the crust, and a technology that needs it at scale is a technology that is bounded by supply.

The catalyst in *Science*, 12 May 2023 replaces it. The authors dope a cobalt-based catalyst with a second metal and show that the doped material sustains oxygen evolution in acidic electrolyte, the reaction that iridium currently performs.

The chemistry is the difficulty. Acidic electrolysis is faster and more compact than alkaline, and the acidic environment dissolves most metals. Iridium survives because it is noble, and a non-noble catalyst has to be protected.

The doping is the protection. A small fraction of a second element changes the surface's reconstruction and slows the dissolution, and the paper's electrochemical measurements show the stability over hours.

The activity is the other metric. A catalyst that is stable and slow is not a replacement, and the doped cobalt's activity compares with the benchmark.

The mechanism is under debate. A dopant can change the electronic structure, the surface's composition or the reaction's pathway, and the paper's spectroscopy addresses which.

The scale question is the application. A electrolyser stack needs grams of iridium per megawatt, and a cobalt catalyst would need a different supply chain and a different recycling route.

The limitation is the current density. A laboratory test at a modest current is not a stack at industrial current, and the durability at high current is the number an electrolyser developer would ask for.

What the paper does not show is a full cell's lifetime. A half-cell's stability over hundreds of hours is a beginning, and a stack's thousands of hours is the target.

For energy materials, the value is a bottleneck removed. A technology constrained by the rarest metal is a technology that cannot scale, and a substitute is a step towards scale.

The policy use is a warning. A lake that is shrinking is a lake whose water rights were allocated in a wetter decade, and a basin plan that assumes the old level is a plan that will fail.

The uncertainty is in the extrapolation. A satellite measures area and a model converts area to volume, and the conversion's error is the largest term in the estimate. The authors' validation against gauges is what bounds it.
The membrane question follows. An acidic electrolyser needs a membrane that keeps the gases apart, and a catalyst that dissolves slowly still releases ions that the membrane must tolerate. The paper's durability test in a cell, rather than in a beaker, is the relevant one.
The supply chain is a geopolitical variable. Iridium comes largely from South Africa and platinum-group refining, and a cobalt route shifts the dependence to a metal with a different map of producers and a different human-rights record.
The comparison with alkaline electrolysis is the honest frame. Alkaline cells avoid the noble metal entirely and pay for it in efficiency, and a cobalt catalyst in acid is an attempt to keep the efficiency without the iridium. Whether the trade is worth making depends on the price of electricity, which the paper does not attempt.
