The International Monetary Fund published its quarterly update on 31 January and projected that the world economy would grow by 2.9 per cent in 2023, after an estimated 3.4 per cent in 2022, and by 3.1 per cent in 2024. The forecast was 0.2 percentage points higher than the one the fund had made in October, which was the first upward revision in a year. The document is the first of the year and the one governments use to set their own budgets, which is why its numbers move markets even when they are close to the previous ones.

Three developments produced the revision. China had abandoned its zero-Covid policy in December China had abandoned its zero-Covid policy in December, and the fund expected the reopening to lift consumption and to spill into the economies that export to China. Europe had got through the winter without the energy shortages that had been feared, because the weather had been mild and the storage had been filled. The American consumer had kept spending. The American labour market had added more than two hundred thousand jobs in December, which was the strongest argument against the recession the fund had feared in October.

By the fund's own standard the level was still weak. The average growth rate between 2000 and 2019 was 3.8 per cent The average growth rate between 2000 and 2019 was 3.8 per cent, and the forecast for 2023 was below it for the second year in a row. The fund's chief economist described the outlook as a return to the pre-pandemic pattern in which growth is slow, and added that the pattern had become the normal one. The estimate for 2024 was also higher than the previous one, and the two years together described a world growing at about three per cent, half a point below the century's average.

The inflation forecast was the other half of the document. The fund expected global inflation to peak in 2023 and to fall thereafter, which is the meaning of the title it gave the update, and it warned that the descent would be slower than the rise because services prices follow wages. The fund expected inflation in advanced economies to fall towards target by the end of 2024, and it warned that the last part of the descent would be the hardest.

In the order the fund always uses, the risks were listed. A resurgence of inflation would force central banks A resurgence of inflation would force central banks to raise rates further, debt distress would spread among low-income countries, and the property crisis in China could turn out worse than the official numbers suggest. The fund also noted that a recession in the United States or Europe would be felt everywhere through trade.

A forecast is a sentence about the future written in the present tense. The fund's sentence in January was that the world would avoid a recession and would not return to the growth rates of the previous decade, and the two halves of that sentence were the ones that governments had to plan for. The revision was two tenths of a point, the smallest the fund publishes and the one that changed the mood in the bond market.
