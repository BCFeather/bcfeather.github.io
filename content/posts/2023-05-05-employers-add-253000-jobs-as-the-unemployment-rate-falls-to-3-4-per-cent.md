---

American employers added 253,000 jobs in April, above the forecast of about 180,000 and a faster pace than the winter. The unemployment rate fell to 3.4 per cent from 3.5, which matches the low of January and the lowest reading since 1969. The labor force participation held at 62.6 per cent. The measure of the prime-age participation was about 83.3 per cent, which is the highest since 2008.

Revisions took away the strength of the winter. The jobs created in February and March were cut by about 149,000, which brings the three-month average to about 222,000. The pattern of the overstated winter has been consistent since the turn of the year, and the statisticians have been correcting it month by month. The statisticians have also been adjusting the seasonal factors, and the spring revisions have been larger than usual for three years.

Hiring was broad. The health care added about 77,000 jobs, the leisure about 31,000, and the professional services about 43,000. The construction added about 16,000 despite the mortgage rate, and the manufacturing added about 11,000. The temporary help fell again, which is the line that usually turns first. The government added about 23,000 jobs, and the retail shed about 8,000 as the stores closed.

Wages rose 0.5 per cent on the month and by 4.4 per cent over the year. The monthly gain was the fastest since the autumn, and the annual figure edged up from 4.3. The economists had expected a slowdown, and the surprise is the part that the Fed will read as the evidence of the tight market. The wage growth for the leisure and the hospitality was about 5.8 per cent, and the one for the information sector about 3.

Markets moved the odds. The traders priced about a 50 per cent chance of another quarter-point rise in June, up from about 30 before the report. The two-year yield rose by about 15 basis points, and the dollar strengthened. The ten-year yield rose to about 3.5 per cent. The five-year yield rose by about 10 basis points, and the dollar rose against the yen by about 0.5 per cent.

This report is the last before the meeting in June that the Fed has said would decide the pause. A job market that adds 253,000 jobs and pays 4.4 per cent more is a job market that is not yet cooling, and the number is the one that the committee will weigh. The committee meets on 13 and 14 June, and the statement will be the first since the banking failures of March. The committee has signalled that a pause is possible if the credit conditions tighten further, and the survey of the loan officers published this week showed the demand for the credit falling.
