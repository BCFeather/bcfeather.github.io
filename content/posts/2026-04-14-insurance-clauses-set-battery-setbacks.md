On 1 April, one of the three large marine and cargo underwriters active in northern Europe amended its loss-prevention schedule for battery energy storage. The new clauses are not law. They are conditions attached to cover, and a project that fails them can still be built, financed, and operated, as long as its owner is willing to carry the fire risk uninsured. In practice, very few owners are.

Substance, in this agreement, is specific. Containerized storage installations above a certain energy density must now sit at least 24 metres from a building line rather than the 12 metres common in national fire codes, must have a deluge system certified to a standard the insurer names, and must provide a thermal-runaway detection system with an independent power supply. Gas detection is required in the enclosure, not merely in the corridor. Each condition has a technical rationale, and each has a cost.

Why an insurer would take on the role of regulator is an arithmetic question. Property and business-interruption claims from storage fires have risen faster than premiums could follow, because a fire in a lithium enclosure is slow to detect, hard to extinguish and expensive to clean up. Restricting the population of insured assets is cheaper than pricing the tail risk. Writing the conditions into the policy is how that restriction is executed without refusing cover outright.

The effect on project economics is immediate. A 12-metre setback is often the difference between fitting a given capacity on a leased plot and needing more land, and land near a substation is the scarce input. Developers describe the arithmetic as a 6 to 9 per cent increase in capital cost per megawatt-hour, with the siting clause responsible for most of it.

Legal status is the interesting part. National codes are written through a notice-and-comment process, with technical committees, published drafts and a minister's signature. An insurer's schedule is written by a risk department, reviewed by a committee of underwriters, and communicated by email to brokers. Both bind behaviour, and only one of them can be challenged in court.

That asymmetry is familiar from other sectors. Cargo insurers have effectively set the container-weight and lashing standards that ports enforce; hull underwriters drove double-hull tankers years before the convention that mandated them. Private standards become public infrastructure whenever the cost of non-compliance is a risk nobody will carry.

Claims data behind the schedule are not public, and the insurer describes them only in aggregate: fire losses in storage above a certain energy density ran at roughly three times the rate assumed when the earliest policies were written. That ratio is the whole argument. A risk department that observes a loss ratio moving in the wrong direction has two instruments, price and terms, and price alone would have to rise far enough to make the cover unsellable.

The mortgage market offers the comparison. Lenders' requirements on flood resilience, cladding and fire safety became binding on construction long before building codes caught up, for the same reason: the party that pays for the loss can dictate the conditions of the risk, and it does so faster than a standards committee can meet.

For a reader in China, where storage build-out is faster than anywhere else, the transferable lesson is about who holds the pen. Domestic projects are largely insured through state-linked carriers whose schedules follow national standards, so the private tightening has no equivalent. The mechanism still exists, though: the moment an underwriter decides a risk is uninsurable at scale, the constraint arrives without any regulation being drafted.
