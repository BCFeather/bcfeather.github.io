description: "TALE-linked deaminases combining a Burkholderia DddA variant with an engineered TadA converted adenine to guanine at 17 mitochondrial sites in human cells, with frequencies up to 49 per cent."
---

Mitochondrial DNA has been the last compartment to be edited, because the guide RNA machinery that CRISPR uses does not import into the organelle and because the double strand breaks that Cas9 makes are lethal in a genome with no repair by recombination. Mitochondrial diseases are caused by point mutations in a genome of 37 genes, and most of the pathogenic variants are transitions that a base editor could in principle reverse.

Earlier mitochondrial editors used a cytidine deaminase fused to transcription-activator-like effectors, converting C to T in a narrow sequence context and therefore addressing about one eighth of the transitions that cause disease. A new construct extends the chemistry to adenine.

TALEDs combine a custom TALE array, a catalytically impaired or split DddA from *Burkholderia cenocepacia*, and an engineered deoxyadenosine deaminase derived from the *Escherichia coli* TadA protein. The paper appeared in *Cell*, 25 April 2022. Heteroplasmy above 60 per cent is usually needed for a clinical phenotype, and an editing frequency of 49 per cent in a cell line is a step towards that threshold.

Its construct catalysed A-to-G conversions at 17 target sites across several mitochondrial genes, with editing frequencies up to 49 per cent. The frequency is high enough to change the heteroplasmy of a cell line, which is the parameter that matters for a mitochondrial disease. A TALE array binds a 15 to 20 base pair sequence, which gives the specificity that a guide RNA would give in the nucleus and avoids the need for a PAM.

The DddA component is the enabler, because it deaminates without breaking the DNA, and its impaired catalytic activity means that it depends on the TadA domain for the adenine chemistry. The engineering is a division of labour between a DNA-binding array, a nick-making enzyme and a base-changing enzyme. A split DddA reduces the activity of the deaminase until the two halves meet on the DNA, which is a safety feature that limits editing to the intended site.

Off-target editing in the mitochondrial genome is the concern, and the authors report sequencing of the whole mitochondrial genome to look for it. A 16.6 kilobase genome is small enough to sequence completely, which makes the off-target assessment more tractable than it is in the nucleus. A complete mitochondrial genome sequence from a single cell would be the ideal off-target assay, and the study reports bulk sequencing of the population instead. A base editor that changes adenine to guanine can correct a variant that changes guanine to adenine, and the list of pathogenic mitochondrial transitions is long enough that both directions are needed.

These constructs are delivered as protein or as mRNA, and the delivery problem for mitochondria in a living animal is the same problem that every mitochondrial therapy faces. The paper establishes the chemistry and leaves the pharmacology to the next step. An animal model with a pathogenic mitochondrial variant would be the test of the approach, and the mouse mitochondrial genome differs enough from the human one that the constructs need re-engineering for it.
