Stellantis said on the first of December that its board had accepted the resignation of Carlos Tavares as chief executive, with immediate effect. Company gave no reason beyond a statement that the board and the chief executive had differed in recent weeks, and it named an interim executive committee chaired by John Elkann, the chairman of the holding company that controls the group. Process to appoint a permanent successor will run into the first half of 2025, and Stellantis confirmed its financial guidance for the year in the same announcement, which is unusual for a company losing its chief executive.

Departure ends a tenure that began in January 2021, when Fiat Chrysler and PSA Peugeot merged into a group of fourteen brands. Tavares had run PSA before the merger and was the architect of the cost programme that followed it, closing plants, cutting engineering headcount and pushing common platforms across marques. Achievements were real: the group's margins were among the highest in the industry for the first three years, and it returned large amounts of cash to shareholders. Reputation built in that period made him one of the most quoted executives in the European car industry.

Trouble arrived in 2024 and came from two directions at once. Sales in North America, the group's profit engine, fell sharply as prices rose and dealers accumulated inventory, and the company was forced to cut production at plants in the United States and to offer discounts it had previously avoided. In Europe, the group's Italian plants ran at low utilization while the Italian government pressed it over job guarantees, and the resulting argument became public. Profit warnings followed in the autumn, and the shares fell to levels not seen since the merger.

Board's patience ended with a dispute about strategy for the coming year. Executives close to the company said the disagreement concerned the pace of investment in electric models and the treatment of the American dealer network, and that the chief executive had wanted a faster restructuring than the family holding was prepared to accept. Elkann's statement thanked him for his work and said the group would be run by the interim committee until a successor was chosen.

Successor will inherit a group that is profitable and under pressure. Fourteen brands is more than the market can support, and the question that has hung over the company since the merger is which of them survive independently. Maserati has been the weakest, and the American brands have lost share. European operations have too much capacity, and the Italian government has made clear that it will not accept closures without a plan for employment.

Industry context makes the timing awkward. European manufacturers are cutting jobs across the sector as demand for electric cars disappoints and Chinese competitors take share in the segments where they are strongest. Volkswagen has been negotiating plant closures with its works council, and suppliers have been announcing reductions almost weekly. Loss of a chief executive at a company of Stellantis's size is therefore read less as a personal matter and more as a signal about how quickly the European car industry is being forced to change.
