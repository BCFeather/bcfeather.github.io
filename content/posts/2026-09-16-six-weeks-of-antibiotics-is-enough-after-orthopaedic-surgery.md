Bone and joint infections have been treated with four to six weeks of systemic antibiotics since the 1970s, a duration chosen when imaging was poor and surgical debridement was less complete. Implanted antibiotic-loaded carriers have since been added on top, on the theory that local delivery reaches concentrations that systemic dosing cannot, and the combination has become standard without anyone testing how much systemic therapy the local therapy makes unnecessary. Patients pay for the assumption with weeks of intravenous lines, renal monitoring and gastrointestinal disturbance.

SOLARIO randomized 500 patients who had undergone orthopaedic surgery and received local antibiotics to a short or long course of systemic treatment, with 475 assessed in the primary analysis. Definite treatment failure by twelve months occurred in 34 of 241 patients, 14.1 per cent, in the long-duration group and 26 of 234, 11.1 per cent, in the short-duration group. The risk difference was minus 3.0 percentage points, with a 95 per cent confidence interval from minus 9.0 to 3.0, which met the prespecified noninferiority margin. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 16 September.

Sensitivity analyses in the included population and in the per-protocol population agreed with the primary result, with risk differences of minus 2.4 and minus 2.7 percentage points. Side effects told a clearer story than the efficacy endpoint: by six weeks after surgery, symptoms potentially related to treatment had occurred in 45.2 per cent of the long-duration group and 17.2 per cent of the short-duration group, a difference of 28 percentage points.

That gap is the point of the trial. Antibiotics given for weeks cause candidiasis, diarrhoea, renal injury and line infections, and each of those is a real event in a patient recovering from orthopaedic surgery. A short course that is not worse at curing infection and much better at avoiding harm is a change in the ratio of benefit to cost, and the ratio is what clinicians weigh at the bedside.

Generalizability depends on the local therapy. Every patient in the trial received an antibiotic-loaded carrier implanted at the time of surgery, so the result describes a strategy in which local delivery does part of the work, and it does not justify a short course in a patient whose infection is being treated with systemic drugs alone. Surgeons who do not use carriers should not read the trial as permission to stop early.

The deepest uncertainty is the definition of failure. A composite that includes clinical signs, imaging and reoperation is a reasonable proxy for cure, and it cannot capture the patient who takes six weeks of tablets and feels unwell for all of them. Noninferiority trials of duration rarely measure the burden of the treatment itself, and here the side-effect data supply what the endpoint omits.

Twelve months is a short horizon for a bone infection, and late recurrence is the outcome that would undo the conclusion. The trial will be followed for longer, and its result already gives surgeons a defensible reason to shorten a course that patients have endured for fifty years without evidence that the extra weeks did anything.
