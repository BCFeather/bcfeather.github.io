Thirty-one of the largest American banks would remain above their minimum capital requirements in a hypothetical severe recession, the Federal Reserve said on 26 June, one in which unemployment reaches ten per cent and commercial property prices fall by forty per cent. The exercise took the banks through a path in which output falls and the rate stays high, which is the combination that the previous tests had treated as unlikely. The test also included a scenario in which the funding markets close for a period, which is the risk that the failures of last year exposed.

Projected losses came to about 685 billion dollars, larger than in the previous exercise, and most of them came from the credit card portfolios and from the loans to companies. A loss that is concentrated in one book is a loss that the supervisors can measure rather than guess. The credit card losses were the largest single category, because the borrowers with the weakest scores are the first to default when the jobs disappear.

Scenario is the variable that the banks cannot control. The hypothetical combines a rise in unemployment with a fall in asset prices, and the exercise asks whether a bank can keep lending through it rather than whether it would survive. A test that is about capacity is a test that the supervisors use to set the buffer. The banks that hold the most office loans are the ones that were given the harshest assumptions, and three of them ended the exercise close to the minimum.

Commercial property is the exposure the exercise isolated. The loans against offices have lost value as the tenants left, and the regional banks hold a larger share of them than the largest institutions do, which is why the smaller banks were tested on a tougher path. A concentration in one asset is the reason the test exists. The supervisors have said they will use the results to set the capital surcharge for the largest institutions, and the proposal is expected in the autumn.

Results set the payouts. The banks that clear the test can return capital to the shareholders, and several announced increases in dividends and buybacks within hours of the release. A test that is passed is a permission. The payouts are larger than in the previous year, and the supervisors have said they will not object as long as the plans do not reduce the capital below the buffer. The banks that failed the qualitative part of the exercise in previous years were not among the thirty-one this time, because the supervisors had already required them to fix the processes.

What the exercise shows is that the capital is adequate for a recession that the models describe, and the risk that remains is the one the models do not describe. A test that is published is a test that the market prices, and the disclosure has made the capital of the largest banks a public number rather than a supervisory secret.
