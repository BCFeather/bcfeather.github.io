Rare immune disorders caused by a single gene have been the most tractable target for gene therapy since the 1990s, and the field's history is a sequence of successes followed by insertional complications. A Phase 2 trial reported in the *New England Journal of Medicine* on 6 May adds a careful entry to the success column: 14 of 15 infants with a defect in a cytokine receptor gene achieved functional immune reconstitution.

The disease is severe combined immunodeficiency caused by mutations in the interleukin-7 receptor alpha chain, a condition in which T cells fail to develop. Untreated, infants die of infections within the first two years. Haematopoietic stem cell transplant from a matched donor cures most, but a matched donor is available for well under half.

The vector is a lentivirus carrying the correct gene, introduced into the patient's own CD34-positive stem cells after harvest and brief culture. Unlike the retroviral vectors used in earlier trials, lentiviral integration favours active genes and the construct includes insulator elements intended to reduce the chance of activating neighbouring oncogenes.

Conditioning is the part that changed practice. The protocol used low-dose busulfan at roughly a quarter of the conventional dose, enough to open marrow niches without the full toxicity of myeloablation. Twelve of the fifteen children were treated as outpatients after infusion; the longest hospital stay was eleven days.

Results are measured in cells and time. T-cell counts reached age-appropriate ranges in 14 patients within six months, and the fourteenth at nine months. Immunoglobulin replacement was discontinued in 13. Two patients had infectious episodes during the first hundred days and recovered. Three years after the first infusion, the transduced cell fraction remains stable, and vector copy number per cell has not risen, which is the marker that would suggest insertional expansion.

Safety findings are the usual set and one anomaly. Two patients developed transient neutropenia attributed to busulfan; one had a grade 3 elevation of liver enzymes that resolved. No clonal expansion has been detected in integration site analysis of more than 60,000 insertion sites, and the trial's independent monitoring board has continued it.

Limits are honest ones. Fifteen patients is a safety cohort, and the youngest is now three years old, so the durability of a stem cell correction across a lifetime is unknown; children who received a transplant have the same uncertainty. Cost is unaddressed in the paper and is the practical barrier: a single-dose therapy priced like other approved gene therapies would exceed what most health systems pay for a rare disease.

What the trial establishes is that low-dose conditioning can be enough when the vector delivers the gene efficiently, which reduces the early mortality that has made these therapies risky in infants. That is a technical result with a moral shape: it moves a therapy from the intensive care unit to a day ward, and the number of centres able to offer it rises accordingly.
