Cosmic rays arrive at Earth with energies no known thermal process can produce, and the accelerators have been sought for a century. Supernova remnants, pulsar winds and shocks around galaxy clusters are candidates, and the problem with all of them is that the acceleration happens far away and cannot be watched as it occurs. Spacecraft have flown through shocks at Earth, at Jupiter and at Saturn, and each pass produced particle spectra that theorists tried to fit after the fact.

Jupiter offers a nearby laboratory. The planet's magnetic field and its interaction with the solar wind create a bow shock, a surface where the flowing plasma abruptly slows and heats, and a spacecraft passing through that surface can measure particles as they are being energized. Jupiter's magnetosphere is the largest structure in the solar system and its shock is correspondingly vast, which makes the crossing long enough for instruments to sample the transition. The crossing took hours rather than the milliseconds a terrestrial shock allows.

Observations reported in *Nature* on 4 June caught electrons gaining energy at the shock, and the mechanism is the one theorists have proposed: a fraction of particles are reflected at the shock front repeatedly, gaining energy with each crossing, until they escape upstream as a suprathermal population. The energy gain per crossing is small, and a particle needs many crossings to reach the energies that appear in cosmic rays, which is why the turbulence that keeps particles returning to the shock is as important as the shock itself.

That mechanism, known as diffusive shock acceleration, is the standard explanation for power-law particle spectra at shocks. Testing it requires resolving the reflection and the repeated crossings, which is what a spacecraft inside the shock transition can do and what a telescope looking at a distant remnant cannot. That mechanism is also invoked for the shocks driven by supernova remnants, where the same power-law spectrum appears at energies a million times higher.

A general rule follows. If the same process appears at a planetary bow shock, at an interplanetary shock and at a supernova remnant, then the acceleration does not depend on the details of the object and can be scaled to the systems that produce the highest-energy cosmic rays.

The limits are spatial and temporal sampling. A spacecraft crosses a shock in minutes and samples a thin slice of a three-dimensional structure, so the spectrum measured at one crossing may reflect local turbulence rather than the steady accelerator. Clusters of spacecraft flying in formation at Earth's bow shock are the natural follow-up, because there the same physics can be measured at several points simultaneously. The measurements also constrain the injection problem, which asks how a thermal particle enters the acceleration process at all, and that step is the one that models handle most arbitrarily. Jupiter's shock is also a natural laboratory for the plasma instabilities that fill in the spectrum below the injection energy.
