Rate-setters in Tokyo raised its policy rate to about 0.25 per cent on 31 July, the second increase this year, and announced a plan to reduce its bond purchases. The yen weakened after the decision, which is the opposite of what a rate increase is supposed to do. The bank had spent years defending a ceiling on long-term yields, and the decision to let the ceiling go is a larger change than the rate itself.

Carry trade is the explanation. An investor who borrows in a currency with a low rate and invests in one with a high rate earns the difference, and the difference between Japan and the United States was still large after the increase. A trade that survives a small move is a trade that continues until the move is large. The gap between Japanese and American yields had widened through the year, and the yen had fallen to levels that the government described as disorderly.

Bond purchases are the other half of the policy. A central bank that has bought a large share of the government's bonds is a central bank that is holding down long-term rates, and a plan to reduce the holdings is a plan to let them rise. A balance sheet that is unwound is a balance sheet that the market has to absorb. The reduction will be gradual and announced in advance, which is the method the bank has chosen to avoid a shock in a market where it is the largest holder.

Politics is quiet and real. A rate rise makes the government's debt more expensive and strengthens a currency that exporters do not want strong, and the central bank's independence was being tested by a government that had been criticizing it in public. A decision that the government dislikes is a decision that has to be explained. The government's debt is the largest in the developed world relative to the size of the economy, and every basis point of higher yield costs the budget money.

Reaction was violent within days. The increase, combined with weaker American data, produced a sharp unwinding of the carry trade in early August, and the stock market fell further than it had in years. A position that is crowded is a position that exits through one door. The unwinding in early August removed a position that had been built by hedge funds and by retail investors in Japan, and the margin calls arrived within a week.

What the decision shows is that a small rate move can be large when it changes a belief. The bank did not intend to move the world's markets, and the belief that the yen would not rise was the thing that it broke. A central bank that normalizes policy is a central bank that has to accept a stronger currency and a weaker export sector, and the trade-off is the test of its independence.
