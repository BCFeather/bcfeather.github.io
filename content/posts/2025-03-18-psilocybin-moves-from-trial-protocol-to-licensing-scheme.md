Psilocybin is a compound that a handful of clinical trials have shown to produce large and rapid reductions in depression scores when it is given with psychological support. The trials are small, the follow-up is short, and the blinding is difficult because the drug produces an unmistakable experience. A regulatory system that decides what to license has to work with evidence of that quality, and two American states have decided to license the service anyway. A compound that produces a five-hour experience is a compound whose effect is inseparable from the room it is given in.

Oregon built the first framework under a ballot measure passed in 2020. The programme licenses manufacturers, testing laboratories, facilitators and service centres, and it requires a preparation session and an integration session around a supervised dosing appointment. A client does not need a diagnosis, and a facilitator does not need to be a clinician, which is the part that psychiatrists have criticized. A facilitator who is not a clinician cannot diagnose a contraindication, and the training that Oregon requires is a hundred and twenty hours.

Colorado followed with a broader scheme that adds a medical model alongside the general one. The state licenses healing centres and requires training for facilitators, and it allows a limited number of conditions to be treated under medical supervision. Both states discovered the same operational problem: the training pipeline is slower than the demand for facilitators, and the cost of a session is high enough that the service is priced for the affluent. A session costs between a thousand and three thousand dollars, and insurance does not cover a service that is not a treatment.

The clinical evidence is the part that the licensing schemes have to interpret. A phase two trial with a hundred and four participants reported a sustained response at six weeks, and a follow-up paper reported that a large share of the responders were still improved at a year. A trial of that size cannot establish rare adverse events, and the reports of difficult experiences that require intervention come from exactly the settings that a trial monitors closely. A trial with a hundred participants is a trial that can detect a large effect and not a small one.

The FDA has not approved psilocybin for any indication, and it rejected a related application for MDMA-assisted therapy in 2024 on the grounds that the trial data did not support the claim and that the blinding had failed. That decision set the tone for the field: a regulator that has looked closely at the evidence and found it thin is a regulator that will ask for larger trials with better controls. A regulator that has rejected one application is a regulator that will want a second trial with an active placebo.

What the states have created is a natural experiment in regulation without approval. A service that is legal in Oregon and Colorado, illegal in most other states and unavailable under insurance is a service that will generate data on safety and on who pays, and those data will decide whether the federal agencies move. The science is promising and unfinished, and the licensing schemes are running ahead of it. A state that licenses a service without evidence of efficacy is a state that has decided the evidence is close enough.
