Gravitational-wave astronomy has spent a decade measuring the inspiral and merger of black holes. The ringdown that follows, the brief oscillation as the merged object settles into a rotating hole, carries information about the horizon itself, and that part of the signal has been much harder to extract because it is short and faint. The signal is a fraction of a second long and its amplitude falls exponentially, which makes the measurement a test of how quickly a perturbed horizon radiates away its deformation.

GW250114 produced a detectable component in the ringdown, reported in *Nature* on 24 June, with a matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio of 15.8 in the LIGO Hanford detector and 17.1 in Livingston. Those numbers are high enough for the component to be treated as a measurement rather than a hint.

Measured properties agree with what general relativity predicts for a Kerr black hole, the exact solution describing an uncharged rotating hole. Agreement is not the same as a test of alternatives, but it does establish that the signal can be read as horizon physics rather than as instrumental noise. If the ringdown frequencies and damping times had come out differently, the object would not be a Kerr hole, and alternatives such as horizonless compact objects predict different spectra.

The interest lies in what such a wave would let observers do. Frame dragging, the dragging of spacetime around a rotating mass, is a prediction that has been tested weakly in the weak-field regime by satellite experiments and hardly at all near a horizon. A wave emitted close to the ergosphere samples exactly that region.

Analysis is delicate because the ringdown overlaps with the merger signal and with the noise that follows. Choosing where the inspiral ends and the ringdown begins is a modelling decision, and different choices shift the recovered parameters. The paper's confidence intervals reflect that, and independent analyses with different waveform models will be the test of robustness. Different waveform models place the boundary between merger and ringdown at different times, and the recovered frequency shifts with that choice.

Two detectors at one observatory is a modest basis for a claim about strong-field gravity, and the next observing run with the Virgo and KAGRA instruments will add the triangulation that turns a detection into a sky location. The result nonetheless marks a shift in what gravitational-wave observatories are for: not only counting black holes, but watching one settle and checking whether the settling follows the mathematics that predicted it. The next generation of detectors, with longer arms and lower seismic noise, will see many more ringdowns and turn a single measurement into a distribution, which is when a test of gravity becomes statistically meaningful. A distribution also allows a test of whether the spectrum depends on the mass and spin of the remnant, which is where modified theories of gravity would first diverge from the standard account.
