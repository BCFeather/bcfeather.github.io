Powell appeared before the banking committee on 7 March for the semi-annual testimony. He said the recent data had been stronger than expected and that the terminal rate would probably be higher than the committee had projected in December. He also said the pace of increases could return to half a point, which the committee had slowed to a quarter in February. The remarks were prepared, and the question period was where the market found its signal. The testimony is required twice a year by the law that created the committee's oversight role.

Two days later he told the House committee that no decision had been made about the size of the March step. The December projections had put the peak at 5.1 per cent, and Powell said that figure was now likely to be revised up. The distinction mattered because the first day moved the market and the second did not. The projections are published four times a year, and the December set had assumed a peak of 5.1 per cent.

Bond yields rose sharply. The two-year yield passed 5 per cent for the first time since 2007, and the ten-year reached 4 per cent. The curve had been inverted since the previous summer, and the inversion deepened to about a point, the widest since the 1980s. Mortgage rates followed, and the average thirty-year rate reached 7 per cent in the following weeks. The average thirty-year mortgage rate had been 6.4 per cent in January and reached 7.1 by the end of the year.

Equity markets fell, and the technology indices lost 2 per cent over the two days. The dollar rose against most currencies, and gold fell. Futures priced a half-point increase at the March meeting with a probability of about 70 per cent, up from 30 before the testimony. The dollar index rose 1.5 per cent over the two days, its largest move since the autumn. The labour market data published in the same week supported the chairman. Job openings fell but remained above 10 million, and the quits rate stayed high. Payrolls for February, published three days later, came in at 311,000, above the forecast and below the January figure. The combination suggested the labour market was cooling slowly rather than breaking. The quits rate was 2.6 per cent, down from the peak of 3 per cent but above the pre-pandemic level of 2.3.

Powell was asked about the debt limit and said the central bank would not be able to protect the economy from a default. He was asked about crypto regulation and said the committee had no authority over the commodity classification of ether. The answers were short, and both were read as refusals. The debt limit was reached in January, and the Treasury was using extraordinary measures to keep paying its bills.

Markets repriced everything within a week, which is why the testimony is remembered for the direction rather than for the numbers. The March meeting was held two weeks later, in the middle of a banking crisis that had not existed on the day of the testimony. The committee raised rates by a quarter point and signalled one more, and the terminal rate it projected stayed at 5.1. The banking crisis began on 10 March, when a lender in California was closed by regulators.
