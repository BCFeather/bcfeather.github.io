---

Last holdout among the three big rating agencies gave way on a Friday afternoon. Moody's lowered the long-term issuer and senior unsecured rating of the United States to Aa1 from Aaa, one notch on a 21-notch scale, and changed the outlook to stable from negative. Standard and Poor's had moved in 2011 and Fitch in 2023, so the country now has no top grade from any of them.

The reasoning was arithmetic and not politics. Moody's said the downgrade reflected an increase over more than a decade in the ratios of government debt and interest payments to revenue, to levels significantly higher than other sovereigns with the same rating. It projected the federal deficit widening to nearly 9 per cent of gross domestic product by 2035 from 6.4 per cent in 2024, and the debt burden rising to about 134 per cent of output from 98 per cent.

Interest is the mechanism that makes the projection self-reinforcing. Mandatory spending including interest was about 73 per cent of the total in 2024 and is projected at 78 per cent by 2035, which leaves a shrinking share for everything a government chooses to do. An extension of the 2017 tax cuts would add about 4 trillion dollars to the primary deficit over a decade on the agency's own estimate, and the debt stock is already above 36 trillion.

The stable outlook is the part that deserves attention. A stable outlook means the agency does not expect another change soon, and its explanation rested on strengths that have nothing to do with the budget: the size and dynamism of the economy, the dollar's role as the reserve currency, and an expectation that the Federal Reserve will remain independent. A rating that depends on institutional behaviour is a rating that can move if the behaviour does.

Markets had priced a downgrade, and the reaction was small. Treasury yields had already risen through May on the same fiscal arithmetic and on the tariff uncertainty, and the ten-year yield had touched 4.5 per cent. The absence of a sharp move is the useful signal: a downgrade that the market already believes is a confirmation and not an event.

Politics is the reason the arithmetic does not get fixed. Deficits of this size are the result of tax cuts and entitlement promises that majorities of both parties have voted for, and a rating agency cannot negotiate. Moody's said it did not believe material multi-year reductions would result from the fiscal proposals then under consideration, which is a sentence about Congress and not about the White House.

What would settle the rating is a fiscal path that stabilises debt as a share of output, and no such path exists in the legislation that was moving through the House that month. A downgrade that changes nothing in behaviour is a warning that has been received and filed, and the next test will come from a Treasury auction rather than from an agency.
