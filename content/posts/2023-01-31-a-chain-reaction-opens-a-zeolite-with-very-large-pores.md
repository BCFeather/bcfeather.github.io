Zeolites are crystalline aluminosilicates with pores of molecular dimensions, and their pores decide what they can do. A catalyst that has to admit a bulky molecule needs a pore larger than the twelve-membered rings that most frameworks offer, and making one has been a synthetic problem for decades. The material reported in *Science*, 19 January 2023 has an extra-large pore, and the route to it is unusual. Alvaro Mayoral, Miguel Camblor, Fei-Jian Chen, Jihong Yu and colleagues start from a chain silicate precursor and drive chain-condensation reactions that form new Si-O-Si bridges, converting the chains into a framework.

Condensation is the standard chemistry of zeolite synthesis, and what is new is the control. In a conventional synthesis, the framework assembles around a template, and the pore size is set by the template's size. Here, the connectivity is built by reacting chains that already have a defined structure.

The product is low-density. A framework with very large pores has fewer atoms per unit volume, and the paper's structure is correspondingly open. Low density is the signature of a large pore, and it is also the reason such materials are fragile.

The pore's size is measured by adsorption and by diffraction, and the paper reports both. A pore is only useful if a molecule can enter it, and the adsorption of a probe molecule whose diameter is known is the test that the structure is what the diffraction says.

The application is catalysis of large molecules. Petrochemical cracking works on small molecules and the existing frameworks are adequate; the pharmaceutical and fine-chemical industries work on molecules that are larger than a twelve-ring pore admits, and a larger pore would let a solid acid catalyst replace a homogeneous one. The stability question is the one that decides whether the material leaves the laboratory. A low-density framework with large pores is prone to collapse on calcination, and the paper's reported stability under the conditions it tested is a first indication rather than a guarantee.

The synthetic route is the more general contribution. If chains can be condensed into frameworks with designed connectivity, then the pore size is a design parameter rather than a consequence of the template, and a family of materials becomes accessible.

What remains is the scale. A synthesis that works in a laboratory gram does not necessarily work in a reactor, and the crystallization conditions that produce a single phase are often narrow. The next step is a recipe that another laboratory can repeat. For the field, the result is a proof that the pore-size ceiling is not fixed. Frameworks with larger pores have been made before, and the chain-condensation route is the one that can be aimed at a target rather than found by screening. Acidity is the property that matters for catalysis, and an aluminosilicate with a large pore has fewer acid sites per volume. A catalyst that admits a large molecule but offers few sites is a compromise, and the aluminium content is a variable the synthesis can tune.
