Emmanuel Macron won the second round of the French presidential election on 24 April with 58.55 per cent of the vote, against 41.45 for Marine Le Pen, according to the interior ministry's count, which the Constitutional Council certified three days later. The result gave him 18.77 million votes to her 13.29 million, and it made him the first French president to be re-elected since Jacques Chirac in 2002. The count was published the same night, and the result was never close enough for doubt.

The margin was comfortable and narrower than it looked. In 2017 the same pairing produced 66 per cent for Macron, so he lost roughly eight points of the electorate in five years, and Le Pen gained about seven. Abstention rose to 28.01 per cent, up from 25.4 in the first round, and among the young and the working class it ran far higher, which means the winner's mandate rests on people who chose him over the alternative rather than for him. The rise was concentrated among younger and poorer voters.

Those transfers were the campaign. Melenchon's 7.7 million first-round voters split three ways, some holding their noses for Macron to keep the far right out, some abstaining, and a slice voting for Le Pen on the argument that she had softened her party. Macron spent the fortnight between the ballots on the theme of a vote against the extremes, and Le Pen spent it on purchasing power, which was the issue that had made her competitive. He had also promised to push the retirement age up, which the runoff gave him a mandate to defend.

Results came at eight in the evening and the square in front of the Eiffel Tower filled within the hour. Le Pen conceded quickly and said the result was a defeat for her party but that the campaign had changed the terms of the argument, a formulation that acknowledged the National Rally's best presidential score while conceding the office. She won a clear majority among manual workers, a bloc her party had courted for a decade.

Governing is the harder part. Legislative elections were six weeks away, the president's party needed a majority to pass pensions and energy legislation, and the coalition that had returned him was assembled for a runoff rather than for a programme. Turnout in the legislative contest would be lower, and the opposition parties that had backed him grudgingly had no interest in lending him seats. The legislative vote would decide whether he could legislate at all, and the runoff had given him no coattails.

This runoff also set a pattern that would repeat across Europe. An incumbent won, the far right advanced, and the left's votes decided the outcome without being represented in the government, which is a stable arrangement only while the alternative remains unacceptable to a majority. What it produced was a presidency that could win national elections and still struggle to assemble a majority for a single bill. The abstention rate, 28.01 per cent, was the highest recorded in a French presidential runoff.
