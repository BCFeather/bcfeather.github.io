title: "Standards are committees that agree to stop arguing"
date: "2025-04-18T09:20:00+08:00"

description: "Technical standards look like engineering documents and are actually negotiated treaties between firms that would rather not need them."
---

A technical standard is a document that says how something should work, and the reason it exists is that two devices have to talk to each other. The engineering is usually the easy part. The hard part is deciding which firm's preferred approach becomes the one that everyone else implements, and that decision is made in a committee where each firm has a commercial interest. A document that specifies a connector is a document that decides which connector factories will build for a decade.

The process is deliberately slow. A working group drafts a specification, the draft goes through several ballots, and the comments that arrive are from companies that have built a product around a different answer. A standard that takes four years is a standard that has given everyone time to accept a compromise, and the compromise is often a provision for both approaches. A ballot that comes back with three hundred comments is a ballot that has been read by three hundred engineers with a commercial interest.

Patent policy is the mechanism that makes the compromise possible. A firm that contributes a technology declares the patents that read on the standard and commits to license them on fair and reasonable terms. The commitment is what stops a standard from becoming a toll road, and it is also the reason the licensing disputes are so bitter. A patent that is declared essential is a patent whose owner has agreed to a royalty that a court may later find unreasonable.

Consensus is not unanimity. A committee that requires every member to agree gives a veto to the smallest participant, and a committee that requires a majority votes and moves on. The bodies that have lasted tend to require rough consensus and to record the objections, which lets a dissenting firm implement the standard and complain about it. A veto for the smallest member is a veto that a large member can sometimes buy, and the buying is what the process is designed to prevent.

The alternative to a standard is a de facto one. A product that becomes popular enough defines the interface without a document, and that is cheaper and faster and gives the defining firm a great deal of power. The formal process exists mostly to prevent that outcome, and it succeeds when the market is large enough to support several vendors. A de facto standard is a standard that one firm can change, and the firms that depend on it have no vote.

What a standard cannot do is settle whether the technology is any good. A specification can encode a mediocre design as thoroughly as a good one, and the market discovers the difference afterwards. The committee's job is interoperability, and interoperability has nothing to say about quality. A committee that produces a mediocre specification produces a specification that the market will implement anyway, because interoperability is worth more than elegance.
