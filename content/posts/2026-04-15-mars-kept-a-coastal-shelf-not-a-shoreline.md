title: "Mars kept a coastal shelf, not a shoreline"
date: "2026-04-15T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A comparison of terrestrial and Martian topography argues that a global ocean leaves a flat band of coastal plain and shelf rather than a recognisable shoreline, and that Mars has such a band."
---

Case for an ocean on Mars has rested for thirty years on features that look like shorelines. Terraces in the northern lowlands were mapped as wave-cut platforms, and the argument was that a body of water must have stood at their level. The problem is elevation: the candidate shorelines sit several kilometres apart in height, and a sea surface is an equipotential surface, so a set of terraces that disagree by kilometres cannot all mark the same water level.

Two researchers at the California Institute of Technology approached the question from the other side, asking what a global ocean does to a planet's topography and not what a shoreline looks like from orbit. They first analysed the Earth, where the answer is known, and found that the most prominent topographic signature of a global ocean is not a shoreline at all.

On Earth the signature is a band of low slope and low curvature that runs around every continent, comprising the coastal plain and the continental shelf, at elevations between about 410 metres below sea level and 15 metres above it. The band exists because wave action and sediment transport plane the margin of a continent, and because a shoreline migrates across that plane as sea level changes, erasing its own earlier positions.

Applying the same measure to Mars produces a comparable flat zone, between roughly 1,800 and 3,800 metres below the datum, wrapped around the northern lowlands. Whether that zone is a coastal shelf or an artefact of lava flows is the question the paper addresses, and the answer turns on what else sits inside it.

Inside the candidate shelf sit river delta deposits, coastal sediments, thick sequences of layered rock and aqueously altered minerals, all of which are the kinds of material that accumulate at a continental margin and none of which are the kinds that a lava flow produces. The convergence of several independent observations on one elevation band is a stronger argument than any single feature.

The result, published in *Nature* on 15 April as an open-access article by Zaki and Lamb, is that the northern plains probably held an ocean bounded by a shelf. It is a probabilistic conclusion, since a planet can produce flat topography in more than one way, and the paper's contribution is to show that the shelf hypothesis explains the whole set of observations better than the alternatives.

Timing remains unsettled, and it is the part that planetary scientists most want. A shelf records that water stood at a level for long enough to plane the margin, and it does not record when, and the valley networks and deltas that date the wet period give a range and not a date. Without samples, the chronology of Martian water rests on crater counts and on inference. Sample return is the instrument that would settle it, and the Mars Sample Return programme has been redesigned more than once for cost. Until material comes back, the shape of the terrain is the evidence available, and a paper that reads the shape correctly is worth more than a decade of arguing about terraces.
