The first sustained heat of the year arrives in June, and with it the annual argument about the electricity bill. Residential tariffs in most cities rise in steps, with a monthly threshold above which the unit price increases and a second threshold above which it increases again. A household that runs one air conditioner overnight in early summer stays in the first band. A household that runs three units through July and August crosses both, and the difference in the bill can be a factor of two even when consumption rises by half.

The step structure is deliberate. It is meant to protect the small user and to price the last units of consumption at something closer to what they cost. The effect is that air conditioning, which is the largest single item in a Chinese household's summer demand, is also the thing that turns a bill into a surprise every year. The surprise arrives in July, when the second tier engages, and it is followed by a conversation about which rooms can be left alone.

The technology has improved steadily and quietly. Inverter compressors, higher seasonal efficiency ratings, better insulation in new buildings, and a price war that has pushed the cheapest models down to a level where a household replaces a decade-old machine without much thought. Twenty years ago an air conditioner was a major purchase, and the decision to run it was a decision about money. Now the machine is cheap, the electricity is cheap relative to income, and the comfort is treated as ordinary rather than as consumption.

That change has a physical counterpart, and the counterpart happens at night. When every unit in a district runs through the evening, the outdoor air in that district warms by two or three degrees, which raises the load on the units, which raises consumption. The urban heat island is partly a consequence of air conditioning and not only of asphalt and concrete. Districts with a high density of housing and a low density of trees are the ones where this feedback is strongest, and they are also the ones where the households are least able to pay for the extra load.

The grid sees the same thing from a different side. Summer peak demand in the east of the country is driven by cooling, and the peak arrives in the late afternoon, which is precisely when the photovoltaic capacity that has been added at enormous expense is starting to fall away. Storage fills part of that gap and the coal and gas fleet fills the rest. The result is that a hot evening is expensive for reasons that have nothing to do with the household that turned on a unit.

None of this argues for making cooling harder to obtain. In an increasingly hot country, air conditioning is closer to public health than to luxury, and the households that defer it are the ones most exposed to heat. What it argues for is honesty about the design of the tariff, the shading and the trees, and the fact that comfort in one district is partly paid for in another.
