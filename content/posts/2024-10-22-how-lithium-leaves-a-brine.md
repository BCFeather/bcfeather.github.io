Lithium is extracted from brine because the brine is already a solution, and the work is a matter of concentrating it. The salt flats of the Andes and of the Tibetan plateau hold groundwater that has circulated through volcanic rock for millions of years, dissolving what it passed, and the lithium in it sits at a few hundred parts per million beside much larger amounts of sodium, magnesium and potassium. Pump the brine to the surface, put it in a shallow pond, and let the sun and the wind do the first part of the separation. The concentration is low enough that the chemistry has to be cheap, since every tonne of lithium moves a great deal of water before it is ready to be sold. The brine is pumped from wells that reach the aquifer below the salt, and the pumps run continuously because the pond system cannot be restarted once it stalls.

The ponds are arranged in a sequence, and each step removes a salt that crystallises before the lithium does. Halite comes out first, then sylvite, then a mix of salts that has to be controlled by the concentration and the temperature at which the pond is held. By the time the brine has lost most of its water the lithium is concentrated enough to be precipitated as carbonate, and the ponds at that end of the sequence are small and carefully managed instead of large and passive. The order in which the salts come out follows the solubility curves, and a pond run too fast deposits a mixture that has to be dissolved and separated again. The pond sequence is a chemical plant laid out on the ground, and its footprint is measured in square kilometres rather than in square metres.

Direct extraction is the alternative, and it skips the ponds. An adsorbent, usually a manganese oxide or a titanium-based ion sieve, takes lithium out of the brine while leaving the sodium and the magnesium behind, and the loaded material is washed with acid to release the lithium into a clean solution. Membranes and ion-exchange resins do something similar, and the advantage is recovery: a sorption plant can take a larger fraction of the lithium than a pond can, in hours and not months. The sorbent has to survive thousands of cycles, and the loss of capacity between them is the number that decides the operating cost of a plant. Direct extraction also tolerates a brine that ponds cannot use, which is why the oilfield brines of North America have become a feedstock instead of a waste.

The water question is where the argument is sharpest. Brine is not freshwater, and the two are connected in an aquifer that has a freshwater lens above the brine, so pumping the brine down can draw the fresh water towards it. The operators report the volumes they extract, the regulators set the limits, and the communities that farm and graze around the salars ask whether the limits account for the years when the basin does not refill. Reinjection is part of the design, because a brine that is not returned is a brine that is lost, and the pressure in the aquifer has to be held for the wells to keep flowing. Regulators in the Andean basins have begun to require monitoring wells around a project, and the data from those wells is the evidence in the dispute.

Specification is set by the battery industry, and a shipment that misses one impurity is returned. Battery-grade carbonate allows very little magnesium, iron or boron, and the purification after precipitation is a chemical plant and not a pond. That is why the industry is moving from carbonate to hydroxide for some cathodes, and why the cost of the last step is where the margins are decided. The impurities that matter are the ones picked up from the rock the brine crossed, and the magnesium in the Andean brines is high enough to complicate the carbonate step. Battery-grade material is a specification with a number attached to each impurity, and a shipment that misses one of them is returned.

What the technology cannot fix is the geography. A salar is a closed basin, the brine in it is old, and the evaporation that concentrates it is the same process that will concentrate whatever is left when the lithium has gone. Direct extraction reduces the footprint and the time, and it does not change the fact that the resource is a finite volume of water in a place where water is the scarcest thing there is. A plant also produces a waste stream that has to be managed, and the management is a permit condition and not an engineering afterthought. The economics of a project therefore depend on the price of the last step as much as on the grade of the brine, which is why two salars with similar chemistry can have very different costs.
