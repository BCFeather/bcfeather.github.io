Insulin is a peptide, and the stratum corneum was built to keep peptides out. Transdermal delivery has therefore meant either piercing the skin or using electrical or ultrasonic tricks, none of which has displaced the syringe. A polymer that can itself cross intact skin, carrying a drug with it, would change the geometry of the problem: instead of opening a route, the carrier becomes the route.

Shen Youqing, Zhou Ruhong, Chen Rongjun and colleagues at Zhejiang University report such a material in *Nature*, 20 November 2025. Its formal name is poly[2-(N-oxide-N,N-dimethylamino)ethyl methacrylate], or OP. In its protonated form it is cationic and concentrates in the acidic sebum and the fatty-acid-rich intercellular lipids of the stratum corneum, then diffuses through the lipid layers. Below the stratum corneum, at the physiological pH of the epidermis and dermis, it becomes a neutral zwitterion and moves across cell membranes rather than remaining lodged in them.

To make the conjugate, the team attached OP to insulin to form OP-I. In a mouse model of type 1 diabetes, a transdermal dose of 116 U kg\(^{-1}\) rapidly brought blood glucose into the normal range. In diabetic miniature pigs, 29 U kg\(^{-1}\) did the same. The authors report that the effect was comparable to that of a subcutaneous injection of insulin at a matched dose, which is the relevant comparison for a delivery claim: the question is not whether the drug arrived, but whether it arrived in an amount that matches the established route. The same dose of free insulin, without the polymer, had no such effect.

Two properties explain the performance. The polymer's charge switch lets it move through the lipid barrier while avoiding the tendency to stick permanently to membranes, and the conjugation keeps insulin protected while the complex is in transit. Both are required; a carrier that crossed the skin but released its cargo too early would not reach the circulation, while one that crossed without switching charge would accumulate in the tissue. The authors' account of the path, through the dermis and into lymphatic vessels, rests on imaging, and not on a direct measurement of the amount absorbed.

The animal work stops at pigs. The experiments are in diabetic rodents and a small number of diabetic miniature pigs, and the key outcome is a short-term reduction in blood glucose, not long-term glycaemic control. There is no human pharmacokinetic data, no measurement of variability between individual animals, and no comparison with the injection schedule that people actually follow. Skin permeability also varies with site and thickness, so a dose that works on mouse abdominal skin is not yet a dose that works on a person's forearm.

What the authors have demonstrated is therefore a delivery mechanism, not a therapy. A transdermal polymer that carries a peptide through intact skin is a genuine departure from the methods tried before, but it is still four species away from a product: patient variability, long-term absorption, skin tolerance and manufacturing consistency all remain to be measured. The test that follows is a human pharmacokinetic study with multiple skin sites, showing that the variability of transdermal absorption is narrow enough to be dosed safely. Until then, the claim is that a skin-permeable polymer can carry a peptide through skin, not that injections have been replaced.
