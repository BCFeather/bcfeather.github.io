Core consumer prices in Japan rose 4 per cent in the year to December, the fastest pace since December 1981, and the third month above the central bank target of 2 per cent. Headline inflation was 4 per cent as well, and the measure that strips out both food and energy rose 2.8 per cent, its quickest since 1991. Tokyo prices, published a week earlier and watched as a leading indicator, rose 4.4 per cent, the fastest in four decades.

Food did much of the work. Prices for processed food rose 7.4 per cent, with flour, cooking oil and dairy products leading, and restaurants raised menu prices as they passed on the cost of gas and imported grain. Electricity bills were up 21 per cent after the utilities began recovering the cost of fuel bought at last year's prices. Rice prices rose 12 per cent after the poor harvest, and eggs rose 20 per cent after an outbreak of avian influenza.

Wages are the piece still missing. Nominal earnings rose about 2 per cent over the year, well behind prices, and real household spending has fallen for three quarters. Firms surveyed by the central bank in January reported that they intended to raise pay in the spring wage round, and the size of that round has become the argument inside the bank. The spring wage round opened in February with the largest union asking for 5 per cent and the employers' federation offering 2.5.

Policy stayed where it was. The Bank of Japan kept its cap of 0.5 per cent on ten-year bond yields at the meeting that ended on 18 January, after a December adjustment that doubled the band and set off the largest monthly move in the market since 2016. The governor repeated that easing would continue until wage growth and prices settled near 2 per cent in a sustainable way. The bank now holds about 55 per cent of the outstanding stock of government bonds, up from 40 per cent a year earlier.

Markets tested the cap repeatedly. The ten-year yield traded near the ceiling for much of the month, and the bank bought record quantities of bonds to defend it, with holdings now above half of the outstanding stock. The currency, which had weakened through 2022 to 150 to the dollar, recovered to about 128 after the December change of band. Foreign investors sold Japanese bonds in the month, and the finance ministry intervened in the currency market for the first time since 2011.

Kuroda's term ends in April, and the choice of successor became the way the market talked about policy. The government nominated Kazuo Ueda in February, an economist who had warned publicly about the difficulty of exiting an easing programme, and the yen moved on the nomination before any policy question was settled. Core inflation for January, published in February, was 4.2 per cent, so the December figure was not the peak.
