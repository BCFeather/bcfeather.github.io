description: "A compound that penetrates the blood-brain barrier treated late-stage sleeping sickness in 26 of 28 patients in a trial reported this month, and it is an oral tablet rather than an infusion."
---

Late-stage sleeping sickness, caused by a parasite that has crossed into the brain, requires drugs that cross the blood-brain barrier, and most available compounds do not. A trial reported this month tested an oral compound in 28 patients with late-stage disease, and 26 were cured at six months.

Achievement lies in the molecule's design. It is a small, lipophilic compound with a molecular weight of 340 and a low affinity for the efflux pumps that remove drugs from the brain. The team optimized those two properties across 400 analogues before selecting the candidate.

Trial design was single-arm and open-label, with a historical control, because the disease is fatal without treatment and a placebo is not permissible. The cure criterion was the absence of parasites in cerebrospinal fluid at six months.

Pharmacokinetics are the key data. The compound reaches 1.2 micrograms per millilitre in cerebrospinal fluid at a 400-milligram dose, which is 40 per cent of the plasma concentration, against 3 per cent for the older infusion.

Safety was acceptable for a fatal disease. Three patients had a transient rise in liver enzymes, and one developed a rash that resolved on withdrawal. No patient died during the trial.

The comparison with the current regimen is stark. The existing treatment is an intravenous infusion over two hours with a 30 per cent rate of serious adverse events, and it requires a hospital admission of a week. An oral tablet changes the delivery model.

Access is the open question. The disease affects about 6,000 people a year, mostly in rural areas of two countries, and a commercial price is unlikely. The developers have committed to a not-for-profit price and to a donation programme, which is the pattern for neglected disease drugs.

Resistance is the future constraint. The parasite's transporter can mutate, and the compound's dependence on a single uptake route makes resistance plausible. The team selected a backup compound with a different transporter and has it in preclinical testing.

Diagnosis is the missing link. Most patients present at a stage when the parasite is in the blood and not the brain, and a lumbar puncture is required to stage the disease. A blood-based staging test would let the oral drug be used earlier, and one is in validation.

What a health system can manage is the dosing schedule. One tablet twice a day for ten days is a regimen a clinic can supervise, and the older infusion needed a hospital bed. That change is why the health ministry's cost model fell by 60 per cent in the trial's own projection.

The trial's follow-up is twelve months and incomplete. Relapse in this disease can occur at eighteen, and the paper reports six-month data with a commitment to publish the full follow-up. A cure at six months is a claim about parasites and not about patients.

For a reader, the result is an argument about how a molecule's properties determine where a disease can be treated. A drug that reaches the brain is a drug that can be given at a clinic rather than in a hospital, and that difference is the public health value.
