Four trillion dollars became a club rather than a record on 31 July, when Microsoft closed above the mark a day after quarterly results that beat guidance on every line that matters, and the two companies that now sit at that valuation are the seller of the chips and the renter of the computing.

Cloud did the work. Azure and the related server products grew about 39 per cent in the quarter, faster than the company had guided three months earlier, and commercial bookings rose by a third, which is the number that tells an investor whether contracts are being signed or merely renewed. A growth rate of 39 per cent in a cloud business of that size is a rate that adds several billion dollars of annual revenue in a single quarter, and it is a rate that only three or four companies in the world can fund the hardware for.

The quarter was large and profitable at the same time. Revenue rose 18 per cent to about 76 billion dollars, operating income rose faster, and the capital spending line rose fastest of all, because a company that sells computing has to buy it first and the depreciation arrives two years later.

Margins are the argument that the bears make. Gross margin fell slightly as the mix shifted towards infrastructure, and free cash flow fell as capital expenditure doubled, so a company at four trillion dollars is a company whose earnings are growing and whose cash is being poured into buildings and chips. A company that buys accelerators and depreciates them over five years is a company whose earnings today are a promise about demand in 2028, and the promise is the part of the valuation that a change in the depreciation schedule can quietly rewrite.

The order of events matters for the narrative. NVIDIA crossed four trillion on 9 July as a supplier, Microsoft crossed it on 31 July as a customer, and the pair between them describe the shape of the boom: one sells the accelerator, the other sells the hour of computing, and both are valued on the assumption that the queue at the door does not shorten.

Concentration is now a policy question. The two companies together are worth more than the entire energy sector, and the index funds that hold them hold the same bet twice, which is a structural feature of passive investing that regulators have begun to discuss and that no investor can diversify away.

What would settle the valuation is whether the capital spending produces revenue at the same rate next year. A year in which depreciation rises faster than cloud revenue is a year in which the multiple compresses, and the guidance that the two companies give in the autumn is the only evidence that will matter. A year in which the rental price of computing falls faster than the cost of the chips would be a year in which the model breaks, and that is the scenario the bears describe when they say the margin is a temporary scarcity and not a franchise.
