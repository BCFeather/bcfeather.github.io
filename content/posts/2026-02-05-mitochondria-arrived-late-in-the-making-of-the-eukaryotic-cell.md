The origin of the eukaryotic cell is the largest single transition in the history of life, because it produced the compartment that made complex multicellularity possible. Two details have never been settled: how long the process took, and when the mitochondrion arrived. The textbook version has an archaeal host acquiring a bacterial symbiont and becoming a eukaryote in a single evolutionary step, and the version that has gained ground recently stretches the process over hundreds of millions of years.

An analysis published in *Nature* on 5 February approaches both questions through gene duplications. Duplications accumulate in genomes at rates that can be estimated, and a duplication shared by two lineages dates the divergence of those lineages more precisely than a single orthologous gene can. Applied to genes that trace back to the earliest branches of life, the method reconstructs a timeline with internal checkpoints.

The reconstruction suggests a long eukaryogenesis, with the eukaryotic lineage separating from its archaeal relatives well before the cell had any of the features that define a eukaryote. The mitochondrial acquisition appears relatively late in that sequence, and the timing coincides roughly with the Great Oxidation Event between 2.43 and 2.22 billion years ago. A late mitochondrion is a different story from an early one. Oxygen was a poison before it was a resource, and the timing of its accumulation is exactly what makes a late symbiont plausible.

Interpretation has support from the fossil record and from the physiology of modern organisms. A host that lived without mitochondria for hundreds of millions of years would have been an anaerobic archaeon of the kind that still exists in oxygen-poor sediments, and the arrival of an oxygen-using symbiont would have become advantageous only when oxygen became available. The Great Oxidation Event is the moment when that happened.

Duplication rates vary across lineages and across gene families, which is the method's weakness. A rate calibrated on one group applied to another can shift a date by hundreds of millions of years, and the paper reports ranges rather than points for that reason. Dates this old carry uncertainties that dwarf the differences under discussion, and a hundred-million-year range is normal for a clock this old., and the argument is about order, not about arithmetic.

What the paper changes is the shape of the story. A single leap implies one rare event, and a long process implies many intermediate forms that left no trace and can be reconstructed only from the genomes of lineages that stopped partway. The second story is more testable, because intermediates leave relatives. The intermediate lineages are the evidence to look for, and the search for them is now a genomic problem instead of a philosophical one.

For the wider field, the result puts the eukaryotic cell in the same category as photosynthesis and multicellularity: a transition that took a long time, involved several innovations, and left the evidence of its stages in the genomes of organisms that never completed it.
