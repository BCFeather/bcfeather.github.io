Elon Musk said on 13 May that his agreement to buy Twitter for forty-four billion dollars was temporarily on hold, pending details about how many of the platform's accounts are fake or spam. The announcement was made in a single tweet, posted before dawn on the American east coast and linked to a Reuters report from 2 May that repeated Twitter's own disclosure, that fewer than five per cent of its monetizable daily active users are false or spam accounts. The shares fell before the market opened and stayed well below the offer price for the rest of the day.

The figure in the filing is the company's own estimate, produced by sampling accounts and reviewing them by hand, and it had been disclosed in every annual report for years. Musk's argument is that the sample is too small, that the estimate is stale, and that the real number could be several times larger, which would change the value of a business whose worth rests on how many people see its advertising. Twitter's board said it remained committed to the transaction, and the company's lawyers began explaining that a buyer who has signed a merger agreement cannot suspend it because he has changed his mind about a number in a filing.

Instability had been visible for weeks. The offer of 54 dollars and 20 cents a share was made on a take-it-or-leave-it basis in April, at a premium of about thirty-eight per cent, without financing lined up and without the due diligence that a purchase of that size normally involves. Twitter's board accepted it after an auction it had run in parallel produced nothing better, and the two sides had spent the intervening weeks arguing in public about the platform's moderation rules, the fate of its staff and the meaning of the phrase free speech.

A hold announced on social media has no legal form, and that is the point of the manoeuvre. If Musk wants out, the agreement provides for a termination fee of a billion dollars and for specific conditions under which he can walk away, and a dispute about spam accounts would have to be proved material to the business rather than to the buyer's mood. Twitter's shares trading well below the offer price measured the market's view of the probability that the deal would close as written.

For the employees, the effect was a summer of uncertainty. Hiring had been frozen, some executives had left, and the company's advertising business was being managed by people who did not know who their ultimate boss would be. For the users, the argument about false accounts was oddly familiar, because Twitter had been publishing estimates of them for a decade and nobody had previously asked whether the number was small enough to matter.

That tweet poses a real question for any platform that sells attention: how many of the accounts counted are people. What Musk did with it in May was convert an industry-wide measurement problem into a contractual lever, and the price of the company on any given morning became a reading of his intentions rather than of its advertising revenue.
