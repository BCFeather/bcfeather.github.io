A nitrate print found in an archive's basement in 2024 has been restored and screened this month, ninety-eight years after its release. The film is a 42-minute documentary about a river port, shot on location with a hand-cranked camera, and its restoration raises the question that archives argue about more than any other: which version of a film is the film.

The print was the only surviving copy of a cut that was released in one territory and withdrawn after a month. A second version, released nationally six months later, survives in three copies and is the one that has been shown since the 1970s. The two differ by eleven minutes of footage.

Restoration work began with the physical object. Nitrate decomposes, and the print had lost 30 per cent of its frames at the reel joins; the team scanned at 4K, stabilized the image digitally, and reconstructed missing frames by interpolating from adjacent ones, which is a technique that works for static shots and fails on movement.

The colour is the contentious part. The film was released with tinted sequences, amber for daylight and blue for night, applied by hand to the print. The restorers reproduced the tints from the surviving frames, and the result looks unlike the black-and-white version that has circulated for fifty years.

Presentation choices were documented rather than decided quietly. The restoration's notes list every intervention, from frame interpolation to the tint reproduction to the decision to keep the original soundtrack's surface noise rather than filter it. That documentation is what separates a restoration from a remake.

Archival politics sit underneath. The archive that holds the print is a different institution from the one that holds the national version, and the two disagreed about which cut to present. The screening used the 1928 cut and the national version will be shown next season, which is a compromise that leaves the argument open.

For a reader, the useful point is that a film is not a single object. It is a set of prints, each with its own history of cuts, tints and damage, and the question of which is canonical has no technical answer. The restorers' contribution is to make the differences visible rather than to resolve them.

Sound restoration raised a separate argument. The film was released with a synchronized disc system, and no disc survives for the 1928 cut. The restoration's soundtrack was assembled from a 1931 reissue disc and from the national version, and the notes mark which passages are reconstructed rather than original.

Rights are the barrier that keeps most restorations off screens. The film's copyright status is unclear in two jurisdictions, and the archive's screening was licensed for one territory and six showings. That is typical: the restoration exists and the audience is limited by law rather than by interest.

The commercial angle is small and real. Restorations of this kind cost 60,000 to 200,000 dollars and reach an audience of a few thousand in cinemas, which means they are funded by archives and foundations rather than by distributors. That is why the documentation matters: it is the only part of the work that outlasts the screening.
