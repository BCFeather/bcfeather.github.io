The Yangtze finless porpoise is a small cetacean with no dorsal fin, a rounded head and a habit of surfacing in a way that looks like a smile. It is the only cetacean that lives entirely in fresh water in China, and its population has been counted five times since 2006, using a method that combines visual line transects with acoustic monitoring. The acoustic part matters more than the visual one, because the animal is small, dark and surfaces briefly, and a boat-based count that relied on eyes alone would miss most of them in murky water.

A 2022 survey estimated 1,249 animals in the main stem of the river, up from a few hundred in the 1990s. The earlier numbers explain why the rise matters: the 2006 count found roughly 1,800, and the 2012 count found about 505, a decline that looked terminal. The recovery since then is the result of a set of measures that began with a fishing ban. From 2021 the river's commercial fisheries were closed, and the gillnets that killed porpoises as bycatch came out of the water; the number of boats on the main stem fell by more than half.

The ban is the largest of them. From 2021 the river's commercial fisheries were closed, and the gillnets that killed porpoises as bycatch were removed from the water. A porpoise that drowns in a net is not a rare event: the animal feeds in the same channels that boats use, and it cannot see the filament. The ban removed the nets but not the risk, because the river also carries the propellers of a shipping trade that has grown every year.

Shipping rules changed too. Speed limits in the parts of the river where the animals concentrate, restrictions on night navigation, and a requirement that vessels use propellers designed to reduce noise. Porpoises navigate by echolocation, and a river full of engine noise is a river they cannot read. The rules are enforced by the maritime safety administration, which has more experience with collisions than with cetaceans, and compliance is uneven along the upper reaches.

River engineering has reshaped the channel as well. Dams, revetments and the shipping channel have narrowed and deepened the water, and the animals have moved into the side channels and the confluences where the flow is slower. The survey found them concentrated in a few stretches, which is efficient for counting and worrying for the species. A population squeezed into a few stretches is exposed to a single spill, a single dry season, or a single new port.

What the numbers do not show is the future. A population of 1,249 animals is small enough that a single disease outbreak or a bad year of water quality could reverse the trend, and the genetic diversity of a population that has passed through a bottleneck is lower than its size suggests. The count is a reason for cautious optimism, and the caution is doing as much work as the optimism.
