description: "The surface of ice carries a film of liquid that thickens as the temperature approaches zero, and the slipperiness of a skate or a boot comes from that film and from the friction that keeps it there."
---

Ice is slippery only when it is warm enough, and the reason is a layer at its surface that is neither solid nor liquid. At a temperature well below freezing the surface molecules are held in the lattice, and a skate on the ice grinds rather than glides. Within a few degrees of the melting point the surface molecules can rearrange without breaking the lattice beneath them, and the layer they form is a few nanometres thick at rest and thicker under a load.

The layer exists because the molecules at a surface have fewer neighbours to bond to than the ones inside. A molecule at the surface has lost about a quarter of its hydrogen bonds, and the energy it would gain by joining the lattice is not available. The result is a surface that is disordered in the same way as a liquid, and the thickness grows as the temperature approaches the melting point because the free energy difference between the two arrangements shrinks.

Friction changes the layer while the layer changes the friction. A skate blade presses on the ice and the pressure melts a little more, and the sliding motion shears the film and generates heat, which melts more still. The three effects are coupled, and the friction coefficient of ice is therefore not a constant but a function of speed and of load. A slow skate at a low temperature has a high coefficient and a fast skate has a lower one, which is why the first few metres of a cold rink feel different from the rest.

The measurement is difficult because the layer is thin and disappears when the sample is prepared for a vacuum. Techniques that work at a buried interface, such as a sum-frequency spectroscopy or a force balance between a tip and the ice, give a thickness that depends on the temperature in the way the theory predicts. The same techniques show that the layer is not water in the bulk sense, since its viscosity is higher and its structure is oriented by the ice beneath.

Consequences reach beyond skating. A glacier slides on its bed because the water at the base lubricates it, and the same film decides how fast a frozen road is, how an aircraft wing ices and how a snowpack settles. Avalanche forecasting uses the temperature of the snow surface, and a warming of a degree or two changes the strength of the bonds between the grains. The slipperiness that a pedestrian notices is the same phenomenon at a different scale.

What remains uncertain is the thickness at a given load and the way the film behaves when the sliding is fast enough that the water cannot drain. A film that thickens beyond a few hundred nanometres behaves like a film of bulk water, with a viscosity that is lower and a friction that rises again. The transition between the regimes has been measured and the theory that connects them is still being written.
