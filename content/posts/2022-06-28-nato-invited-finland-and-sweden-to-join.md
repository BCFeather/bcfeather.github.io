At a summit in Madrid on 28 June, NATO invited Finland and Sweden to become members, hours after Turkey withdrew the objections that had blocked them for four weeks. The three governments signed a memorandum in which Finland and Sweden promised to treat Kurdish groups as security threats, to lift restrictions on arms sales to Turkey and to amend their laws on extradition. Accession protocols were due to be signed in Brussels the following week, and ratification by thirty parliaments would take the better part of a year.

Strategic significance of the change is hard to overstate. Finland shares a border of about thirteen hundred kilometres with Russia, and its army maintains conscription and a reserve large enough to give the alliance a new depth in the northeast. Sweden brings a modern air force and a navy suited to the Baltic, and the two countries together control the approaches to the Gulf of Bothnia and the sea lanes that supply the Baltic states.

Both had applied in May, within weeks of the invasion of Ukraine, and both had spent decades describing themselves as militarily non-aligned. Finland's decision was the more consequential, because the country had built its security policy around the assumption that membership would provoke Moscow, and the war changed the calculation overnight. Public support in Finland rose from about a quarter to more than three quarters within a few weeks.

Turkey's demands were specific. It wanted Sweden and Finland to stop supporting the Syrian Kurdish militia that Turkey regards as an extension of the PKK, to extradite individuals it accused of terrorism, and to end arms embargoes imposed after its incursion into northern Syria in 2019. Sweden's government agreed to a joint mechanism for reviewing cases, and both countries changed their laws on arms exports before the summit ended.

Response from Moscow was predictable and limited. The Kremlin said that the decision would worsen security in the region and that it would respond with unspecified measures, and Russian officials threatened to move nuclear-capable missiles closer to the Finnish border. Analysts noted that Russia had already deployed most of its available forces in Ukraine and that the threats were political rather than practical.

The summit's other business was equally significant. The new strategic concept named Russia as the most direct threat to the alliance and described China as a systemic challenge, which put the two in a single document for the first time. Leaders agreed to put more troops on standby and to strengthen the eight battlegroups that had been established in the east since 2014, and they restated the target of spending two per cent of national income on defence. What the invitation changes is the map that the alliance has lived with since 1949. A Baltic sea that was half neutral becomes an alliance lake, and a border that was quiet becomes a frontier, and the two countries that chose this outcome did so because a war on their doorstep made the older calculation obsolete; the ratification process will be slow and will be used by every member to extract something, which is how alliances work.
