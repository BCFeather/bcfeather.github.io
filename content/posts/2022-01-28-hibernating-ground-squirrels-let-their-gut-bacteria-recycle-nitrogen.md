description: "Animals that fast for months cannot afford to lose protein, and a study of thirteen-lined ground squirrels shows their microbes turning urea back into amino acids while the body is shut down."

Hibernation is a strategy for surviving a season without food, and it creates a problem that is easy to miss. An animal that stops eating also stops taking in nitrogen, and nitrogen is what proteins are made of. Muscles left unused for months should waste away, and in hibernating mammals they do not, which has been one of the quiet puzzles of the field. The authors measured the gene activity of the microbes as well as of the host, and the microbial genes for urea breakdown rose in the months when the animals were fasting.

Textbook answer involves the liver and the kidneys. During fasting, mammals break down protein and produce urea as waste, and the cheapest response is to excrete it. Hibernating animals slow their kidneys and store urea instead, which is why their blood urea rises through the winter, and the assumption was that the store was a buffer rather than a supply. That result also explains why hibernators wake with their muscles intact, a fact physiologists had documented without being able to account for the nitrogen budget.

New work reported in *Science* on 28 January looked at the bacteria in the gut of thirteen-lined ground squirrels across the hibernation season, and found that the community changed with the season in a way that increased its capacity to break urea down. Microbial urease splits urea into ammonia and carbon dioxide, and the ammonia can be used by the host to make amino acids. In hibernating animals, the pathway was running harder, and the nitrogen that came out of it appeared in the animals' tissue protein. The team also found that the animals' kidneys slowed their filtration during torpor, which is what makes the urea available to the gut in the first place.

Experiment connects three systems that are usually studied separately. The host shuts down, its kidneys conserve urea, and the gut microbes convert the urea it delivers into a form the host can reuse. Each part is simple, and the coordination is harder to explain: the animal's metabolism has to keep supplying urea to the gut even while it is barely eating or moving, and the microbial community has to be maintained on a diet that consists of almost nothing. The community's composition shifted towards species that carry urease genes, and the shift reversed when the animals resumed eating in the spring.

Medical interest is speculative and it is the reason the paper was published in a general journal. Human patients on prolonged fasting, and patients whose muscles waste during illness, lose nitrogen they cannot spare, and a process that lets the gut flora recycle it would be worth understanding even if it could not be copied. The squirrel does it for a living, every winter, on no food at all.

Nothing about the finding is mysterious in principle. Bacteria have been recycling nitrogen in rumens and soils for as long as those habitats have existed, and what is new is the discovery that a hibernating mammal recruits that chemistry into its own nitrogen economy. The animal does not hibernate alone; it hibernates with its microbiome.
