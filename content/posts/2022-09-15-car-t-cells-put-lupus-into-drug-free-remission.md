description: "Five people with refractory systemic lupus erythematosus received anti-CD19 CAR T cells and all reached DORIS remission within three months, with drug-free remission maintained for a median of eight months and naive B cells returning after about 110 days."
---

Lupus is a disease driven by B cells that produce autoantibodies, and a therapy that removes the B cells is a therapy that attacks the source. The removal has been attempted with antibodies and with cyclophosphamide, and neither has produced a durable drug-free remission in refractory disease. Depth and duration of depletion are the two variables the earlier approaches could not control.

Five patients with active systemic lupus erythematosus refractory to several immunosuppressive drugs in a compassionate-use programme, using autologous T cells transduced with a lentiviral anti-CD19 CAR and reinfused at one million CAR T cells per kilogram after lymphodepletion. The paper appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 15 September 2022. A compassionate-use programme is a programme for patients who have exhausted the approved options, and the exhausted population is the one a new therapy has to be tested in first.

CAR T cells expanded in vivo, depleted B cells deeply, improved clinical symptoms and normalized laboratory parameters, including seroconversion of anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies. Remission by DORIS criteria was reached in all five patients at three months, and the median disease activity index fell to zero. A median disease activity index of zero is the numerical form of a clinical impression, and the impression is that the patients improved.

Drug-free remission was maintained during a median follow-up of eight months after administration, and it persisted even after B cells reappeared, which happened after a mean of 110 days. A remission that outlasts the return of the target cells is the observation that makes the result more than a prolonged depletion. B cells returned at a mean of 110 days, and the disease did not return with them.

The returning B cells were naive and carried non-class-switched receptors, which is the phenotype of a reconstituted repertoire rather than a reactivated one. A repertoire that comes back naive may be a repertoire that has not yet learned to make autoantibodies. Whether the reconstituted repertoire ever learns to make them is what a longer follow-up will show. The therapy was tolerated with only mild cytokine-release syndrome, which is unusual for a CAR T therapy in a population with autoimmune disease and no prior lymphodepletion. A mild toxicity profile is the property that would allow the therapy to be tested earlier in the course. Mild cytokine release in five patients is a small number, and the number is the limit of the claim.

A series of five patients is a case series, and a case series cannot establish that the remission is caused by the therapy and not by the natural history or by the concomitant drugs. The authors present the result as a proof of feasibility and call for a trial. Five patients cannot establish causation, and the paper presents the result as feasibility rather than as efficacy.

The wider implication is CAR T cells directed at a lineage marker can reset an autoimmune repertoire, and the reset is a different therapeutic logic from immunosuppression. A therapy that rebuilds the immune system and not suppressing it is the kind that rheumatology has wanted. Rebuilding the repertoire is a different therapeutic logic from suppressing it, and the difference is the reason the paper is read.
