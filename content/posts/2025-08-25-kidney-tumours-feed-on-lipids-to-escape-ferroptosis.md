---

Ferroptosis is a form of cell death that depends on iron and on the peroxidation of lipids in the membrane. It is unusual among death pathways in that it can be turned on by starving a cell of cysteine or by blocking the enzyme that clears lipid peroxides, and it has become a target for therapies aimed at tumours that resist conventional drugs. The pathway is unusual in that it can be triggered by removing a single amino acid from the medium, which makes it easy to study and hard to deploy.

A paper in *Nature* on 21 August describes a resistance mechanism that runs through lipid uptake. The authors show that cancer cells can take up lipoproteins through glycosaminoglycans, the long sugar chains that decorate the cell surface, and that the lipids obtained this way protect the membrane from peroxidation. The effect was strongest in tumours that are already lipid-rich, with kidney cancer given as the clearest example. Clear-cell tumours accumulate lipid droplets visible under a microscope, and the droplets were long treated as a storage symptom rather than as a supply line.

The mechanism matters because it connects three things that are usually studied separately: diet, the extracellular matrix, and the chemistry of the membrane. Lipoproteins carry cholesterol and fatty acids; glycosaminoglycans are the docking structures that many viruses and growth factors use; and the lipid composition of a membrane determines how easily its fatty acids oxidize. Putting them together gives a route by which a tumour's environment can supply exactly the material it needs to survive.

It also suggests a reason for a clinical observation. Clear-cell kidney cancer stores lipid droplets and is often resistant to ferroptosis inducers, and the new work implies that the resistance is not only a matter of antioxidant enzymes but of supply. If the tumour can import lipid faster than it peroxidizes it, then blocking the import becomes a way to make the therapy work.

The obvious experiment is to combine a ferroptosis inducer with an inhibitor of lipoprotein uptake or of glycosaminoglycan synthesis. Both classes of drug exist, though neither was developed for this purpose. The paper reports that the protection was reduced when the sugar chains were removed, which is a proof of principle rather than a treatment. Removing a sugar chain from a cell is not something a clinician can do, but the result identifies the step that a drug would have to block. It also explains why dietary fat might matter more in some tumour types than the metabolism literature has assumed.

There is a broader lesson about how cancer metabolism is described. The standard account is about glucose and glutamine, the fuels that a cell burns. This work is about what a cell builds with, and about the fact that membranes are made of material that can be imported, stored and spent. Ferroptosis happens in that material, and so does resistance to it.
