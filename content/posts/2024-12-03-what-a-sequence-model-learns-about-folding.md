description: "A network trained only to predict the next amino acid ends up encoding which residues touch in the folded protein, and that representation can be used and also misread."
---

A protein language model is trained on sequences and nothing else, and the question is what that training can produce. The corpus is every protein sequence that has been deposited, several hundred million of them, and the model is asked to predict the next amino acid in a sequence given the ones before it. That objective is the one a language model uses for text, and it forces the network to represent the regularities of the data in its hidden states. The regularities of protein sequence are the grammar of folding: which residues appear next to which, which patterns recur across families, and which positions tolerate substitution. A model that has seen a family of sequences learns which positions are conserved and which are free to change, and that distinction is the raw material of every downstream prediction. A protein is a string of twenty letters and the model treats it as one, which is a simplification that costs it nothing and gains it the whole corpus.

The model that came out of that training is useful in ways the objective did not promise. A representation learned from sequence alone turns out to encode structure, because the contacts that hold a fold together leave a trace in the pattern of substitutions across evolution. Two positions that touch in the folded protein tend to change together, and a network that has seen enough sequences can learn that correlation without ever being shown a structure. The result is that a model trained on letters can be asked about shapes, and the answer is better than a model that was trained on shapes. The scale at which those correlations appear is what a deeper model can capture, since a pair of positions that touch in three dimensions may be a hundred residues apart in the linear sequence. Contact prediction from a single sequence was thought to be impossible before the models existed, and it is now a routine first step.

Attention is the mechanism that makes the correlation learnable. Each position looks at every other position in the sequence and weights them, and the weights that emerge after training concentrate on the positions that matter for the prediction. The pattern of attention in a trained model has been mapped against the contact map of the folded protein, and the two agree closely enough that the attention can be read as a crude structure prediction. The agreement is not a proof that the model contains a structure, and it is evidence that the representation is organised around contacts. The attention maps are also unstable between training runs, and two models with the same architecture and different seeds produce different maps that predict the same structure. Reading the attention as a structure is a diagnostic, and a structure predicted that way has not been solved to atomic resolution.

Uses follow from the representation. An embedding from the middle of the network is a vector for a protein, and vectors for proteins with similar function end up near each other even when the sequences have diverged beyond alignment. A model can be fine-tuned on a small set of labelled examples to predict a property, which is the standard way that a foundation model is adapted to a laboratory's problem. A model can also be asked to generate a sequence that satisfies a constraint, and the generated sequences have been made and tested in the laboratory. A fine-tune needs fewer examples than a model trained from scratch, and the small labelled sets that a laboratory can produce are the reason this approach has displaced older supervised methods. A property model built this way is shipped as a service and used by groups that never see the weights.

Generation is where the claims get strong. A network that has learned the grammar of a family can propose sequences that are far from any natural member and still fold, and the proposals have been expressed and crystallised. The success rate is low by the standards of a design method that starts from a structure, and the sequences that work are diverse in a way that a structure-based design is not. The diversity is the point, since a family that has been explored from one starting point tends to stay near that point. The sequences that fail are usually the ones that violate a constraint the model never learned, such as a disulfide pattern or a metal site that the family requires. A design that is proposed by a model and made by a laboratory is a test that the model cannot fake, and that is the strongest evidence the field has.

What the model cannot do is the part that matters for a laboratory. A sequence model has no notion of the experiment that produced the sequence, so it cannot tell whether a protein is abundant, toxic, or difficult to express, and those properties decide whether a design is usable. The model also inherits the biases of the corpus, which is dominated by proteins from a small number of organisms and by the families that were studied because they were interesting. A prediction for a protein from an under-sampled family is a prediction from very little evidence. The under-sampled families are also where the experimental structures are missing, so the model's prediction there cannot be checked against anything. A model can also be wrong with confidence, since a likelihood is not a measurement.

Training cost is the constraint on the next step. A model of this size needs a cluster of accelerators and a month of training, and the community that uses it does not have that, so the field has settled on a few released models and a large number of fine-tunes. The fine-tunes are where most of the biology is being done, and the result is a division of labour between the groups that can train and the groups that can measure. That division is the same one that structural biology has had for decades, and the sequence models have not changed it as much as the announcements suggested. A released model is also a fixed point that other work is measured against, and the field has begun to report improvements relative to it rather than against a benchmark that was designed for the task. A model that is trained on public data and evaluated on public data is a model whose failure modes are the ones the public data does not contain.
