An optical clock counts oscillations of light rather than of microwaves, and its accuracy has been demonstrated in laboratories for a decade. A transportable version ran for 30 days at a field site this month, and the report describes a device that a metrology institute can move in a van.

The clock uses a single ion of ytterbium held in a trap and interrogated by a laser. The laser's linewidth is 1 hertz, and the ion's transition is at 518 nanometres, which gives 5.1 times 10 to the 14 oscillations per second.

The instability reached 3 parts in 10 to the 18 over a day, which means the clock would lose a second in 10 million years. A cesium fountain's 3 parts in 10 to the 16 is a hundred times worse. Size and power are the engineering achievement. The clock fits in three racks of 1.2 cubic metres and draws 400 watts, against a laboratory system's 12 racks and 3 kilowatts. The vacuum system's ion pump and the laser's frequency comb were the components that shrank. Temperature and vibration are the two disturbances a road imposes, and the clock's enclosure holds both within the range the laser can tolerate.

Transport was tested deliberately. The clock was driven 600 kilometres and restarted in 4 hours, and its frequency agreed with the laboratory reference to 2 parts in 10 to the 17 after the move. The application is geodesy. An optical clock's frequency shifts with gravitational potential at a rate of 1 part in 10 to the 18 per centimetre of height, so two clocks at different altitudes can measure the height difference without a levelling survey.

Price separates the two clocks by an order of magnitude. A satellite's rubidium clock costs 20 million dollars to orbit and a field optical clock costs 3 million to build, and the ground instrument's accuracy is a thousand times better. An optical clock's advantage is its stability over an interval, and its disadvantage is that the interval must be measured against a reference at the other end. A portable clock taken from a laboratory to a distant site and back tests both: the transport's disturbance and the comparison's residual.

Geodesy uses the sensitivity that a clock's tick rate has to gravitational potential. A 10-centimetre change in a clock's altitude changes its rate by one part in 10 to the 16, which is within the demonstrated stability. A survey that measures gravity this way needs no gravimeter. A transportable clock's market is a laboratory that needs a reference it cannot build. A national metrology institute's customers are universities and observatories, and a clock that travels replaces a shipment of an atomic standard that costs more than the trip.

A laboratory instrument becomes infrastructure when it can travel. A clock that can be moved is a clock that can define a standard at a site rather than at an institute, and that changes who can measure what.
