Malaria control has been a story of two interventions: nets and drugs. Both have been eroded by resistance, and both act on the human side of the cycle. The mosquito is the other host, and it is where a parasite spends the part of its life that is easiest to interrupt, because the numbers are small and the environment is contained.

A paper in *Nature* on 21 May 2025 reported a screen built for that target, and the design of the screen is half of its contribution. The authors assembled a large library of antimalarial compounds, including molecules already in development for human use, and tested them for their ability to stop the parasite developing inside the mosquito and not in the blood. Twenty-two compounds emerged as the most effective.

Best of them did something a net cannot. When mosquitoes landed on material treated with the compound, every parasite in their guts was dead within six minutes, and the effect held in laboratory colonies that had been selected for resistance to the pyrethroids used in bed nets. A six-minute kill is a transmission block that happens before the mosquito can take a second blood meal, and a mosquito that dies or clears its parasites before the next bite cannot pass the infection on.

Chemistry is deliberately cheap. The compounds identified in the screen are simple structures that are easy and inexpensive to synthesize, which matters because the countries that need them are not the countries that would pay for a complex molecule. Simple chemistry also means a shorter path from a laboratory to a factory, and a molecule that a contract manufacturer can make in tonnes is a molecule that a procurement agency can buy, which is the difference between a paper and a product in a market where the buyer is a ministry of health.

Duration was tested and reported. Treated netting retained its effect for a year, which is roughly the service life of a bed net in a programme, and the compounds share no target with the drugs used to treat people, so a parasite that evolves resistance to one would not gain resistance to the other.

Limits are those of any screen. The work was done in mosquitoes and in the laboratory and not in villages, and a compound that kills a parasite in an insect still has to pass an irritancy test, because a mosquito that avoids the net is a net that does not work. Cost per square metre at scale is the number that decides deployment, and it has to come in below the pyrethroid it would sit beside.

What would settle it is a field trial of treated netting alongside standard nets, measuring infections in children and not parasites in insects. A tool that blocks transmission at the mosquito is the kind of tool that could make elimination possible in places where treatment keeps failing, and the paper is the first half of that case.
