Spotify told staff on 4 December that it would cut about 1,500 jobs, roughly seventeen per cent of its workforce, its third round of redundancies in twelve months. The shares rose about seven per cent on the day, which is the reaction that cost cuts now produce at companies whose investors have spent two years asking for profit. The company had already cut six per cent in January and two per cent in June, and had told the market in October that it would report a small operating profit for the fourth quarter.

The letter to employees was written by the chief executive and posted publicly, a form that has become standard since the pandemic. It argued that the company had hired for a growth rate that did not arrive, that the difference between the plan and the outcome had grown rather than closed, and that the only honest way to face the gap was to reduce the number of people. It also said that the cuts would fall across departments, including the teams that build the product, and that the company would offer severance of about five months.

The financial story behind the decision is one of the strangest in the industry. Spotify has 574 million users, of whom 226 million pay, and it pays out roughly two thirds of its revenue to the labels and publishers whose catalogues it licenses. Growth in paying subscribers has slowed to single digits in mature markets, the price rises of the past two years have been absorbed rather than celebrated, and the company has spent heavily on podcasts and on an advertising business that has never reached the scale promised at its investor day.

Competition has moved into the same corner. Apple Music, YouTube and Amazon take a smaller share of the market but pay no attention to profitability in the way an independent company must, and TikTok has become the place where a large part of the under-thirty audience discovers music at all. The labels, meanwhile, have their own interest in the streaming economics and have begun licensing directly to platforms that did not exist when the model was designed.

Inside the company the cuts land on a workforce that had been told a year earlier that the era of expansion was over. Engineers in the podcast division, editorial staff who build playlists, and the sales teams that sell audio advertising all lost roles, and the people who remained were asked to take on the work of those who left. The pattern is familiar from the rest of the sector: the reductions arrive in December, the bonuses are calculated in January, and the strategy document for the following year is written by fewer hands.

Spotify will probably end 2024 as a profitable company, which is what the market wanted and what the letter promised. What it will not be is the company that told investors in 2019 that scale in users would eventually convert into scale in profit, because the conversion has arrived at a smaller size than the plan assumed, and the difference has been paid for by about three thousand people who no longer work there.
