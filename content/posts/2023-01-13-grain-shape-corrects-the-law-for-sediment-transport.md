Rivers move gravel, coasts move sand, and deserts move dust, and the rate at which they do it is one of the oldest quantitative problems in geomorphology. The standard laws relate transport to the shear stress of the flow and to the size of the grains, and they have been fitted to flume experiments for nearly a century.

The paper in *Nature*, 11 January 2023 adds a variable that the laws left out. Eric Deal, Jeremy Venditti, J. Taylor Perron and colleagues measure how grains of different shapes move under the same flow and find that shape changes both the drag on a grain and the friction between grains, so that a law calibrated on spheres mispredicts natural sediment.

The physics is in the two forces. Drag depends on the projected area a grain presents to the flow, and a flat grain presents more area per unit mass than a round one. Friction depends on how grains interlock, and angular grains interlock more than smooth ones. Both effects scale with shape rather than with size, which is why a size-only law cannot absorb them.

The group's correction is a theory with a shape parameter, verified in a flume and then applied to natural grains. The claim is not that previous measurements were wrong but that they were made on materials whose shape differed, and the differences between laboratories that have puzzled the field may be shape effects in disguise.

The applications are practical. River restoration depends on predicting how much sediment a channel will carry, and a law that overestimates transport by a factor of two leads to a design that scours a bed it meant to protect. Coastal nourishment has the same problem in reverse: a beach that loses sand faster than predicted is a beach that has to be renourished sooner.

Planetary science is the other audience. Sediment transport on Mars and Titan is inferred from landforms, and the inference runs through a transport law calibrated on Earth. If shape matters, then the rounded grains of a terrestrial river and the angular grains of a crater rim do not obey the same relation, and a Martian estimate made with an Earth law carries a bias.

What the work does not settle is the range of shapes. Natural sediment spans plates, blades and rods, and the theory's parameter has to be measured for each population. A single number per site is a manageable requirement, and it is still a requirement that field studies will have to meet.

The broader lesson is about how empirical laws age. A law fitted to a laboratory material becomes a law about that material, and the step from the laboratory to the field is a step in the variables, not only in the coefficients.

Field calibration is the next requirement. A transport law with a shape parameter asks a survey to measure shape as well as size, and shape is currently reported in words rather than numbers. A standard descriptor, such as a perimeter-area ratio from a sieved sample, would make the correction usable by engineers who never run a flume.
