World Alzheimer's Day falls on 21 September, and the organizations behind it have settled into a routine that is both necessary and slightly wearying: publish the prevalence estimates, note that the numbers are rising, and ask governments to treat dementia as a health system problem rather than a family misfortune. The estimates are familiar. Around 55 million people live with dementia worldwide, a figure expected to reach 139 million by 2050, with roughly two thirds of cases in low- and middle-income countries, where the median age is lower and the health budget is smaller.

The theme the campaign has pressed hardest in recent years is diagnosis. In high-income countries perhaps half of cases are formally identified; in much of the rest of the world the figure is far lower, and the reason is rarely a shortage of neurologists alone. Dementia is still treated as an ordinary consequence of age, something a family manages at home until it cannot, and the point at which a name is put to it comes late or never; a great many death certificates still record pneumonia or a fall rather than the condition underneath.

Blood tests have changed the terms of that argument. Measures of phosphorylated tau in plasma now perform well enough to be used as a first step in specialist settings, and the two anti-amyloid antibodies approved in several countries require a confirmed amyloid signature before they can be prescribed. A test that costs a fraction of a PET scan and does not need a cyclotron makes the diagnostic step cheaper, which is not the same as making it available, and the difference between the two is where most health policy on dementia now lives.

Availability is where the argument returns to earth. A blood test needs a laboratory, a pathway and a clinician willing to act on the result, and none of the three is evenly distributed. Treatments that require infusions every two weeks and regular MRI monitoring add a further requirement, and the health systems most short of geriatricians are the least able to meet it, which is the definition of a technology that widens the gap it was meant to close.

For families the practical questions are older than the science. Who provides care, who pays for it, and what happens when the person who remembers the household's arrangements stops being able to. Every campaign produces the same list of asks: respite services, training for carers, and a diagnosis delivered early enough to be useful, and every campaign reports the same shortfall against it, in every language and on every continent.

The research pipeline justifies some of the optimism and none of the triumphalism. A disease with a twenty-year preclinical phase is a disease that gives medicine a long window, and medicine has only recently begun to look through it. Whether looking earlier helps more people, or merely sells more tests to people who will not be treated, is the question the next decade is supposed to answer.
