Two crania were dug out of a terrace at Yunxian in Hubei in 1989 and 1990, and for most of three decades they sat in a drawer as fragments of an awkward shape. The second, known as Yunxian 2, was the more difficult: crushed, distorted, missing its base, with the bones of the face pushed out of alignment and the two halves of the cranium splayed against each other. A paper in *Science* on 25 September presents a digital reconstruction of it and a phylogenetic placement, and the placement is the news, because it moves a branch of the family tree rather than adding a specimen to the end of it.

Reconstruction was done by mirroring and by correcting the distortions that fossilisation imposes, a process that took years and a great deal of computed tomography. What emerged is a cranium that is low and long, with a broad braincase and a face that projects further forward than a modern human's. The authors, led by Ni Xijun of the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing with Chris Stringer of the Natural History Museum in London, date the fossil to roughly a million years ago.

Placed in a comparison with other East Asian fossils, the skull groups with *Homo longi*, the species named in 2021 from a cranium found at Harbin. That grouping matters because of what sits inside it, and because of what it excludes: the Denisovan fossils from Siberia, known mainly from a finger bone and a jaw, fall within the same branch on the authors' analysis. If the reading holds, Denisovans are not a separate sister lineage to *Homo longi* but a population within it, and the Harbin and Yunxian skulls become the face of a group previously known only from DNA.

Plausible and contentious in equal measure is the geography that follows. East Asia would have held a long-lived lineage, distinct from *Homo sapiens* and from Neanderthals, ranging from the temperate north to the subtropical centre of the continent, and one that the authors place close to the divergence of the two lineages that produced modern humans and Denisovans, which would make it older than either. Other specialists will want more than one skull before accepting a species boundary drawn at a million years.

Weak point is the dating, as the authors acknowledge. The Yunxian terrace has been difficult to date directly, and the age rests on associated fauna and on the stratigraphy rather than on a single radiometric measurement. A skull that is a million years old and one that is 800,000 years old support different stories about who was where when.

Hominin palaeontology advances slowly and in lumps, because the evidence arrives as fragments and the arguments are made in public. This paper adds a face to a name, which is a real advance, and it rearranges a tree on the strength of one skull, which is the kind of advance that gets tested by the next excavation.
