date: "2022-10-15T10:20:00+08:00"

description: "In two simulated galaxies the vertical breathing modes share the pattern speed of the spiral arms and not that of the bending waves, which puts the two vertical motions on different drivers."
---

A galactic disc supports several kinds of wave at once, and a star near the Sun can be moved by all of them. Spirals compress the disc in its plane, bending waves warp it up and down, and breathing modes change its thickness without moving its mid-plane. Measuring which wave travels at which speed is how the drivers are told apart, and the measurement is hard in a real galaxy because a star's vertical motion mixes all three modes into one line-of-sight velocity.

Two isolated simulations supply the measurement in a preprint posted to arXiv on 1 October 2022. The authors run a star-and-gas model of a disc galaxy, track the vertical velocity field over intervals of a gigayear, and use a windowed Fourier transform to extract pattern speeds from each mode. The windowing matters, since a spiral's speed can change with radius and with time, and a transform taken over the whole run would blur the very differences the comparison is looking for. Intervals of a gigayear are long enough to hold several rotations and short enough to keep the pattern steady. The transform is computed on a grid of radii, so a pattern speed is reported as a function of position rather than as a single number for the disc.

The comparison gives a clean separation. Breathing modes and spirals share a pattern speed across the disc, while bending modes travel at their own speed, and the bar's speed matches the breathing pattern where the bar dominates. A shared speed is evidence of a common driver, and the paper reads the breathing motion as part of the spiral rather than as a separate oscillation that happens to be nearby.

What makes the identification convincing is the range over which it holds. Matching a single radius would say little; matching the pattern speed across the disc says that the two motions are locked, so a change in the spiral's pitch should show up in the breathing field. The authors present that consistency as the main evidence, and they note that the bar enters the same relation.

Left open is the observational side. Pattern speeds in real galaxies are inferred from Tremaine-Weinberg integrals and from streaming motions, and neither method separates a breathing component from a bending one. Whether the simulated coupling survives in a disc with gas fractions and feedback that differ from these two runs is a further question, and the two simulations are isolated models without a cosmologically motivated accretion history, so a disc that formed from a merger may organize its vertical motions differently from a disc that grew quietly.

A measurable prediction is what the result offers: where a spiral's speed is known, the breathing motion should carry the same figure, and a survey that maps vertical velocities across a face-on disc could test that with existing integral-field data. No such test has been run, and the prediction is the part of the paper that a survey team can act on.
