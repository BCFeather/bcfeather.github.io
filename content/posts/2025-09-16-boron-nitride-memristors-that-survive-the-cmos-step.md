A memristor needs a switching layer thin enough to change resistance under a few volts, and integrating one onto a finished wafer needs that layer to be deposited without damaging the transistors underneath. Two-dimensional materials are attractive for the thickness and the sharp interface, and awkward because they are usually transferred. A switching layer on top of finished logic has to be grown at a temperature the metal wiring can survive, and most oxides and perovskites fail that test before their switching is measured.

The authors deposit hexagonal boron nitride directly onto a one-inch silicon wafer by electron cyclotron resonance plasma-enhanced chemical vapour deposition. The film comes out polycrystalline, without wrinkles, and without the contamination that transfer leaves behind, which is what allows the next step. The deposition is a plasma process at a temperature low enough for aluminium metallisation, and the film is characterised by microscopy and spectroscopy before any device is patterned. The plasma is struck at low pressure, so the film grows without a seed layer.

Memristors patterned from the film yield at 92.5 per cent, retain their state for 50,000 seconds at 85 degrees Celsius, and endure 700 cycles in the discrete device. Those numbers are respectable for a laboratory process, and retention is the one that matters for a non-volatile claim. The 700-cycle figure applies to discrete devices, and the integrated cells are the ones that reach millions of cycles, which is the reverse of the usual order of reporting. The yield figure is measured on a one-inch wafer, small for a process claim, large enough to show the film is uniform.

The integration demonstration is stronger than the device numbers. One-transistor-one-memristor cells were built by depositing the nitride on tungsten vias inside 180-nanometre CMOS, and the integrated cells endured millions of programming cycles. A one-transistor cell is the least a memory array needs, since the transistor isolates the lines that would otherwise share the switching layer. The one-transistor cells were measured for endurance instead of retention, so the report draws two different claims from two different test structures.

Nothing in the report addresses the parts of a memory that carry data. Switching variability between cells, the resistance window at the array level, and the disturb behaviour of a half-selected cell are unmeasured, and 180 nanometres is two generations behind the nodes that would use an embedded memory. A memory also needs a selector that passes the switching current without disturbing its neighbours, and the tungsten via in the demonstration is a large one.

Value lies in the process order. A switching layer that can be grown on top of finished logic without a transfer step fits the back end of a CMOS line, and that is the constraint most emerging memories fail. Whether the nitride survives the thermal budget of a more advanced node is the next question. The process argument is the durable part: a two-dimensional insulator that can be grown on the back end removes the transfer step that has kept these materials out of production.
