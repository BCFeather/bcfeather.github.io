Adhesion is one of the harder tricks in animal defence. A predator that bites a frog and finds its own mouth glued shut learns quickly, and several frog lineages have arrived at the same solution independently. The chemistry behind the trick had not been worked out for any of them, which left the repeated evolution unexplained as well as unmeasured. Several of those lineages are only distantly related, which makes the repeated outcome a natural experiment in how far evolution can reuse the same molecular parts.

A paper in *Nature Communications* on 10 July 2024 supplies it for the Madagascan tomato frog, *Dyscophus guineti*. The secretion's stickiness rests on two proteins that interact: a highly derived member of a widespread glycoprotein family, and a galectin, the sugar-binding protein class that cross-links glycoproteins in many contexts.

Neither component is novel. Homologues of both proteins occur in other amphibians, and they have been present in skin for far longer than glues have existed, so the raw material predates the innovation by a wide margin. What changed is how much of them is made, and how the glycoprotein is built.

Expression is the first lever. In the glue-producing glands the two genes are transcribed at levels far above those in ordinary skin, which supplies the quantity a defensive secretion needs, since a glue that is not present in bulk cannot coat a predator's mouth. Structure is the second. The glycoprotein in *Dyscophus* carries substitutions that increase its disorder and its glycosylation, and a protein with more unstructured regions and more sugar behaves like a gel former rather than like a folded globular protein. The gland itself is built for bulk release, with large secretory cells packed against one another, so transcript levels are matched by a tissue architecture that can deliver the proteins quickly.

The same two changes appear in a second lineage that evolved glue independently, which is the paper's central comparative claim. Recruitment of ancient templates, with a raise in dose and a shift in shape, is what makes the repeated evolution of a complex secretion possible. Invention of new genes is not the mechanism here.

For materials science the result reads as a formula. Two proteins, one disordered and heavily glycosylated, one a lectin that cross-links it, give an adhesive that sets on contact with water, and both components are ones a synthetic biologist could express in quantity. The ecological case rests on inference, since the work does not identify the predator species actually deterred, and it does not measure adhesive strength under controlled conditions. A synthetic version would need the disorder tuned rather than maximized, since a chain that is entirely unstructured may flow instead of setting.

Even so, the evolutionary point is clean. Innovation in this instance was not a new gene but a new schedule and a new shape for two old ones, and that is a route to novelty that genomes take more often than the textbooks suggest.
