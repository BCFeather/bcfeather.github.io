A mammoth that died in what is now Yakutia about 52,000 years ago left skin behind, and the skin preserved something a fossil does not: the way the chromosomes were folded inside the cell. The animal was found in 2018 near a settlement on the Indigirka, its front half intact in the permafrost, and the sample that reached the laboratory was not bone or tooth but a patch of hide with hair still on it. The paper that followed reported a genome arranged in chromosomes hundreds of millions of base pairs long, and the arrangement agreed with what living elephant skin shows. The hide had been buried in silt that froze around it within a season of the animal's death, and that speed is part of why the tissue survived at all: a carcass that lies exposed for weeks loses its cells to bacteria before the cold can fix anything.

DNA outside a cell falls apart quickly. Water hydrolyses the backbone, microbes consume what is left, and oxidation finishes the work, so an ancient sample usually yields fragments of a few hundred base pairs with the architecture long gone. Cold slows all three reactions and desiccation stops them, and the hide had been both frozen and dried, which is the condition under which tissue can enter a glass-like state instead of a crystalline one. The authors call that state a chromoglass, and the word is doing real work: in a glass the molecules stop moving without arranging themselves, and a structure caught that way can persist for a very long time. Earlier work on ancient samples had recovered fragments and short reads, and a few studies had reported contact maps at low resolution from bone, but none had reached the scale of whole chromosomes.

What the paper measured was contact. A Hi-C library built from the sample records which pieces of DNA lay close to which others in the nucleus, and the map that comes out shows the loops, the compartments and the chromosome territories a living cell maintains. On that map the mammoth's twenty-eight chromosome pairs could be separated, and the inactive X chromosome showed the two-domain partitioning that mammalian skin shows, which is evidence that the folding survived and was not rebuilt by the analysis. The resolution of the map is coarse by the standards of a cell-culture experiment, tens of kilobases at best, so what it shows is the layout of the nucleus and not the position of individual promoters. The contacts behind the map number in the millions, which is what makes a reconstruction at this resolution possible from a sample that has been dead for fifty millennia.

Comparison with a living elephant is the part that carries weight. Skin from an Asian elephant, processed the same way, gave a map with compartments in the same places, so the mammoth's map could be read against a reference instead of alone. The authors then asked where the two differed, and they could ask it because both maps were built by the same pipeline with the same parameters. The differences they report sit in the regulation that separates a mammoth from an elephant rather than in the letters of the sequence. The mammoth's chromosome count of twenty-eight pairs is the elephant count, and the map confirms it directly, where read depth had allowed only an inference. The same comparison identified the chromosome that carries the genes for the sex of the animal, and the map placed it where a male's would sit.

None of this rescues the sequence. The DNA is still fragmented at the level of individual bases, and the chromosome-scale map is a map of contacts among fragments and not a read of the text. The specimen is one individual from one site, preserved by one unusual combination of cold and dryness, and no second sample of comparable quality has been described. Inferences about regulation drawn from it are inferences about the shape of a genome, and the shape is what the method can see. The method also assumes that the state survived the extraction intact, and that the contacts it records are the ones the nucleus held in the living animal, and not ones made as the tissue dried. The usual authentication standards for ancient DNA were applied, and the damage patterns at the ends of the fragments are the ones expected from an old sample, unlike those of modern contamination, and every library was built and sequenced with the controls that the field now requires.

Why the result matters is that it changes what a fossil can be asked. Palaeogenomics has worked from fragments, assembling a genome from short reads and inferring the chromosomes from the sequence. A sample like this one offers a direct measurement of arrangement, and arrangement is where much of the regulation lives. Enhancers act on promoters that the folding brings near them, and a map of the territories is a map of which genes could have been talking to which. If other specimens hold the same state, the method could be applied to animals with no close living relative, and that is a different kind of evidence from an alignment. A specimen that fails one of those checks is not a mammoth skin with preserved contacts, and the paper reports the checks because the claim is the kind that a single bad sample would discredit.

The next step is a survey rather than a study. Museums hold hides, and the question is whether any of them dried fast enough and stayed cold enough to have entered the same state. The conditions that produced this specimen are unusual but not unique, and the search for a second one has begun in collections assembled for other purposes. What would settle the question is a specimen from another species with a map that can be compared, and until then the mammoth stands alone, one animal whose skin remembered the shape of its chromosomes. Permafrost covers a large part of the northern hemisphere, and the number of carcasses that have emerged from it in the past two decades is small but not negligible, which makes the survey a finite and plausible project instead of an open-ended hope.
