Tsetse flies transmit trypanosomes, and the disease they cause in people and in cattle has shaped the settlement of a large part of Africa. Control has relied on traps and on insecticides, and both have limits, since a trap that catches few flies changes little.

In *PNAS* on 7 March 2023, the problem is approached through the fly's nose. The authors identify the volatile compounds that tsetse flies respond to and the blend that attracts them, and the blend is the basis of a bait that can be used in a trap.

Mixture is the chemistry. A single compound is a weak attractant, and a blend drawn from a host's breath and skin is a strong one, so the analysis decomposes the blend and tests the components. Physiology supplies the foundation, since tsetse respond to carbon dioxide, to host odours and to visual cues, and the attractive blend is layered on top of a basic response to a breath.

Because a fly's response can be measured in a wind tunnel or in a field trap and the paper uses both, behaviour is what separates a laboratory preference from a field attraction. Geometry matters as much as smell, since a visual target with a blue and black pattern catches more flies than a plain one, and odour adds to a visual trap rather than replacing it, which makes the two modalities complementary.

Transmission gives the disease relevance, because a fly attracted to a trap is a fly that does not bite a person, and fewer flies reduce the reproduction rate of the parasite, which makes a trap a public health intervention. Strategy is where the insecticide question lands, since treated targets work but depend on a chemical supply chain and on resistance management, while an odour bait that can be used without insecticide is a lower-risk tool.

Specificity is the safety property. A bait that attracts tsetse and not honeybees can be deployed in a farming landscape, and the specificity tests are the basis of that claim. Cost is the practical question, because a synthetic blend of several compounds is a product with a supply chain, and a trap that costs more than the disease it prevents is one donors fund and communities abandon.

Field reduction stays unestablished, since a trap that catches flies in a trial has to be deployed at scale, maintained and replaced, and operational trials are a separate project. Vector control gains the lesson that behaviour is a target, because a fly's attraction to a host is a vulnerability that evolution did not remove, and a bait exploits it without the toxicity that insecticide carries. Seasonality is the next unknown, since fly populations swell with rain and a bait that works in a dry month may be swamped when numbers peak, so a trapping programme needs a calendar as well as a chemistry. A trap also has to compete with a host, since a hungry fly chooses between a bait and a cow, and the choice is a behaviour that no odour test alone can decide.
