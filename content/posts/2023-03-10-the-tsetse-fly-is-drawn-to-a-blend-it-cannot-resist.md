Tsetse flies transmit trypanosomes, and the disease they cause in people and in cattle has shaped the settlement of a large part of Africa. Control has relied on traps and on insecticides, and both have limits: a trap that catches few flies is a trap that changes little.

The study in *PNAS*, 7 March 2023 approaches the problem through the fly's nose. The authors identify the volatile compounds that tsetse flies respond to and the blend that attracts them, and the blend is the basis of a bait that can be used in a trap.

The chemistry is a mixture. A single compound is a weak attractant, and a blend of compounds from a host's breath and skin is a strong one. The paper's analysis decomposes the blend and tests the components.

The behavioural assay is the evidence. A fly's response to an odour can be measured in a wind tunnel or in a field trap, and the paper uses both, which is what separates a laboratory preference from a field attraction.

The disease relevance is the transmission cycle. A fly that is attracted to a trap is a fly that does not bite a person, and a reduction in fly numbers reduces the reproduction rate of the parasite. The trap is a public health intervention.

The insecticide question is the strategic one. Insecticide-treated targets work and they depend on a chemical supply chain and on resistance management. An odour bait that can be used without insecticide is a lower-risk tool.

The blend's specificity is the safety property. A bait that attracts tsetse and not honeybees is a bait that can be deployed in a farming landscape, and the paper's specificity tests are the basis for that claim.

What the paper does not establish is the field's reduction in disease. A trap that catches flies in a trial is a trap that has to be deployed at scale, maintained and replaced, and the operational trials are a separate project.

The cost is the practical question. A synthetic blend of several compounds is a product with a supply chain, and a trap that costs more than the disease it prevents is a trap that donors fund and communities abandon.

For vector control, the lesson is that behaviour is a target. A fly's attraction to a host is a vulnerability that evolution did not remove, and a bait exploits it without the toxicity that insecticide carries.

The fly's sensory physiology is the mechanism's foundation. Tsetse respond to carbon dioxide, to host odours and to visual cues, and the attractive blend is layered on top of a basic response to a breath. The paper's design reflects that hierarchy.
