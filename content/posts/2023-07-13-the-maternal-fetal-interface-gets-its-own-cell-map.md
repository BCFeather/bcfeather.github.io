The placenta is the interface between two genetically different individuals, and that interface is where tolerance is negotiated. Its anatomy is unusual: fetal cells invade maternal tissue, and maternal immune cells reside among them. In *Nature* on 13 July 2023, the arrangement is resolved. The authors use MIBI, an imaging method labelling many proteins at once, to identify cell types and their positions in the decidua and in the placental villi.

An architecture is the finding. Trophoblast subsets occupy distinct zones, and maternal immune cells follow the trophoblast's invasion front rather than distributing evenly. Subtypes carry the map's central distinction, since a villous trophoblast exchanges nutrients while an extravillous one invades, and the two have different markers and different positions.

Tolerance is the implication. An interface where the mother's immune cells are positioned rather than uniformly mixed is one where proximity is regulated, and that regulation is a candidate mechanism for tolerance. Composition of the immune population is the second result, because decidual natural killer cells and macrophages dominate and their ratio changes with gestation, which a single time point cannot show.

Vasculature belongs to the map. Spiral arteries are remodelled by trophoblast, and the map shows where that remodelling sits relative to the immune population. Pathology supplies the motive, since pre-eclampsia and fetal growth restriction involve a failure of that remodelling, and a map is the reference against which a pathological specimen can be compared.

Panel strength is the method's advantage. A dozen or more proteins labelled in one section distinguishes cell states a hematoxylin and eosin stain cannot, and the selection of markers is the design, with trophoblast, immune subsets and vasculature each represented. Fragility is a practical constraint, because a decidua is soft and a section hard to cut, and the preparation is what preserves the arrangement. Sample sets the limitation. A placenta delivered at term is not one at twelve weeks, and the map's window is the deliveries available. Imaging depth constrains further, since a section is thin and a structure extending through the tissue must be reconstructed from serial sections.

Dynamics is what the paper does not show, because an interface is a moving front and a fixed section shows a moment. Reproductive biology gains an anatomy previously described in words, since the interface's zones were known qualitatively, and a map with coordinates and markers is a quantitative reference. Mouse models invite caution, because their placental architecture differs from the human one, and a mechanism validated there needs a human check; a placenta from a complicated pregnancy can be scored against the map, which makes descriptions comparable between centres.

A series of placentas across gestation would convert the static map into a timeline, and the ratio of immune subsets that the authors measure at term is the sort of quantity a timeline would make interpretable. A comparison with a placenta from a pregnancy that ended early would show which parts of the architecture depend on gestation and which are laid down before it, and that split is the question the paper leaves for others.
