Metastatic prostate cancer has a standard backbone of androgen deprivation with an androgen receptor pathway inhibitor, and for a decade the targeted radionuclide lutetium-177 PSMA-617 has been reserved for patients whose disease had already progressed on that backbone. Reserving it meant sequencing the drug last, which is where a therapy with a survival benefit tends to be least useful. The question was whether moving it forward, into the first-line combination, buys more time. Sequencing questions are the hardest ones in oncology, because every trial that moves a drug forward removes evidence about what it would have done later.

A phase 3 trial led from Weill Cornell Medicine with investigators at several institutions tested that move, and the results were published in *The Lancet* on 6 August. Patients with PSMA-positive metastatic disease received the radionuclide together with the backbone shortly after diagnosis, and the comparison was against the backbone alone, with crossover permitted for patients in the control arm whose disease progressed.

Adding the radionuclide reduced the risk of radiographic progression or death by 28 per cent. The trial's design matters for reading that number, since crossover means the control arm eventually received the drug, which compresses any survival difference that might otherwise accumulate. A progression endpoint measured under crossover is therefore a conservative reading of the benefit, and the ongoing follow-up will show whether overall survival separates. Absolute figures will follow in the fuller report, and the hazard ratio is what the news coverage has, which is why the shape of the curve matters more than the headline percentage.

Nearly nine in ten metastatic prostate cancers express PSMA, which is what makes the drug's targeting biological rather than incidental. The trade is tolerability: dry mouth occurred in 46 per cent of patients on the triple regimen, a consequence of radioligand uptake in the salivary glands, and fatigue was common. More adverse events in the experimental arm is the expected price of adding a third active agent.

The regulatory sequence has already moved. The Food and Drug Administration approved earlier use of the drug in late July, before the paper appeared, which is now the normal order for a trial whose result was public at a conference. Clinicians will have to decide whether the progression benefit justifies starting a radioligand in patients who may do well on the backbone alone for years. A radioligand is also a logistical commitment, since supply is tied to reactor schedules and a fixed shelf life, and a first-line indication multiplies the number of patients who need it at once.

The open question is duration. A radionuclide given at diagnosis competes with the same drug given later, and the trial cannot say whether the total benefit is larger when the drug is used early or when it is held in reserve. That comparison would need a second randomization, and nobody has run one yet. The trial's crossover design was a concession to ethics, and it has cost the field a clean answer about survival, which is the price of not randomizing patients to a placebo after progression.
