description: "Growth rings and isotope records in fossil fish that died at the Cretaceous-Palaeogene boundary place the Chicxulub impact in boreal spring, when northern ecosystems were entering their growing season."
---

Whether a catastrophe kills evenly depends on when it arrives. The impact at Chicxulub removed about 76 per cent of species, and the selectivity of that extinction has been attributed to body size, to diet and to geography, with the season of the year left out of the account because no measurement existed. Selectivity is the detail that makes the extinction interesting, because a catastrophe of that size might have been expected to kill everything and it did not.

A deposit in North Dakota preserved fish that died within hours of the impact, buried by a seiche wave that swept the inland sea. Those bones record the last months of their lives in the same way that tree rings do, and a group read that record to establish when the asteroid struck. The work appeared in *Nature*, 23 February 2022.

Osteohistology, the study of bone microstructure, shows annual growth bands whose spacing reflects seasonal food supply, and the stable isotope composition of the same bone records the chemistry of the water and the animal's metabolism across the year. Combining the two in acipenseriform fish from the Tanis deposits gave a chronology spanning the final years of the Cretaceous. Growth bands in these fish are laid down seasonally, with a wide band in the months of plenty and a narrow one in the months of scarcity, and the outer band records the season of death.

The fish were in a phase of active growth, with newly deposited bone and a isotopic signature characteristic of spring, when they died. The authors therefore place the impact in boreal spring, which is austral autumn, and argue that the timing shaped which lineages survived.

Seasonality changes the arithmetic of survival. An impact in spring destroys the newborns and the juveniles of that year in the northern hemisphere, while an impact in autumn destroys the stored reserves that carry a population through winter, and the two losses fall on different species. Northern spring is when plankton blooms, when juveniles are small and when migrants are arriving, so the loss of that cohort removes a year class from every food web that depends on it.

The claim rests on a small number of exceptional specimens, and the interpretation of the growth band as annual is calibrated against modern relatives rather than demonstrated in these fossils. The seiche deposit is also a single site, and what it records about the season locally is not a global statement.

Even with those limits, the result supplies a variable that extinction models had treated as unknown. If the season of the impact matters, then the same asteroid arriving six months later would have produced a different survivor list, and the pattern of who died becomes partly a matter of timing. A season that can be measured also allows the fossil record to be compared with climate models of the last Cretaceous year, which is a test that neither field could perform alone.
