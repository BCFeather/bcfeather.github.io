Harbin opened the ninth Asian Winter Games on 7 February, with competition running to the fourteenth across ice events in the city and snow events in the Yabuli hills two hours away. More than thirty national Olympic committees sent athletes, several of them from countries with no snow of their own and a handful from tropical Asia. The event is small by Olympic standards and large by the standards of the sports involved. Ice hockey and figure skating drew the largest crowds, and the speed skating oval in the city sold tickets for finals weeks in advance.

The venue plan is the interesting part. Harbin already had an indoor ice complex from the 1996 games, and Yabuli has been the country's main training base for alpine and cross-country skiing since the 1990s. Organisers renovated rather than built, and the only significant new construction was a curling hall and an athletes' village that converts to student housing. Converting the village to student housing is the standard trick, and it works only when a university is willing to take the buildings on the schedule the organisers need.

That choice reflects a lesson that winter sports have learned expensively. A ski resort built for a two-week event has to support itself for the other fifty weeks, and the ones that failed are visible in the Alps and in eastern Europe. Yabuli's resorts run on domestic tourism, which is a business that exists because the hill is near a city of ten million people. Machine-made snow is denser than natural snow, which makes a race course faster and harder, and athletes noticed the difference in training.

Snow is the operational risk. The region has cold winters and modest snowfall, and the courses were prepared with machine-made snow stored from the previous season. A warming climate has pushed the snow line upward, and the International Olympic Committee has begun to talk about rotating winter host cities rather than building new ones. A Thai skier who trains in Japan and a Hong Kong curler who practices in a mall are the visible end of a pyramid that barely exists at home.

The competition itself is a development event for several delegations. Thailand, Singapore and Hong Kong sent athletes who train abroad, and the Philippines has a curling team that practices in a mall. For those programmes, an Asian games is the only multi-sport winter event they can reach, and the participation is the point rather than the medal table. Medal tables at a regional games are a poor guide to a programme's health, because the strong delegations send their second team.

What the games will leave behind is the question every host answers differently. Harbin gets a renovated ice hall and an upgraded training base, and it gets a fortnight of hotel occupancy in a season that already attracts visitors for the ice festival. The bill is smaller than a new resort would have been, and the benefit is proportional to a city that already had the infrastructure. A city that already sells ice lanterns in January did not need a games to fill its hotels, and that is the strongest argument for the model.
