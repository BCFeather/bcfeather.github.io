A semiconductor firm that sells accelerators spends more on software than on any single block of silicon, and the acquisitions it makes are increasingly software companies. The reason is that a chip is a component and a platform is a stack, and the stack is what a customer buys when it commits a data centre to one vendor. A developer who has written a training loop for one vendor's library will rewrite it only if the alternative is clearly better.

A library is the moat. A collection of kernels that have been optimized for one architecture over five years is a collection that does not port in a quarter, and a competitor that enters with faster hardware still has to reproduce the library. An acquisition that brings a compiler, a runtime or a set of domain libraries is an acquisition that buys time for the hardware roadmap. A compiler that produces code ten per cent faster than a rival's is a compiler that has been tuned for one architecture for years.

Prices reflect that. Software companies with modest revenue have been bought for sums that imply a strategic value rather than a financial one, and the buyers are the firms with the largest data centre ambitions. A purchase that looks expensive on a revenue multiple is cheap if it prevents a customer from switching. A purchase that keeps a customer on the platform for another three years is a purchase that pays for itself twice.

Building it is the alternative. A large chipmaker can hire several hundred engineers and write the library itself, and it takes years and produces a product that is judged against an incumbent that has been refining its own for a decade. The acquisitions are a shortcut around that decade, and shortcuts are what a company with a closing window will pay for. A hired team that leaves after two years leaves a library that nobody understands, which is the risk of building.

Open source complicates the strategy. A library that is open can be used by a competitor, and a vendor that contributes to an open project gains adoption and loses differentiation. The firms that have done best are the ones that keep the compiler proprietary and open the kernels, which gives developers a reason to stay and rivals a reason to lag. An open kernel that a rival can adopt is a kernel that improves the ecosystem and erodes the advantage.

What the pattern shows is that the value in computing has moved from the transistor to the toolchain. A fab that can print a smaller feature is a fab with an advantage of a year, and a toolchain that a developer already knows is an advantage that lasts as long as the developer's habits. A toolchain that is already installed is the reason a new chip needs a reason to switch, and the reason has to be large.
