A quarter-point cut with a four-way split is a committee that disagrees about the direction of travel as well as the speed. The Bank of England's May decision lowered Bank Rate to 4.25 per cent, the fourth reduction since the peak of 5.25 per cent, and the vote was five in favour, two for a half-point cut to 4.0 per cent and two for no change at 4.5 per cent.

Case for the cut rested on progress. Twelve-month CPI inflation had fallen to 2.6 per cent in March from 2.8 per cent in February, slightly below the path in the February report, and pay growth, while elevated, was expected to slow significantly over the rest of the year. Underlying GDP growth had slowed since the middle of 2024 and the labour market had loosened, with vacancies falling and unemployment drifting up from a low base, which is what a loosening labour market looks like before it looks like a weak one.

Argument against rested on what is coming. The committee expected CPI inflation to rise to about 3.5 per cent in the third quarter as previous energy increases passed through regulated prices, and it judged the outlook for inflation to be unusually uncertain because of trade policy. Two members thought a pause was warranted until that pass-through was visible.

Two members who wanted a half point were looking at the other side of the same evidence. A small margin of slack had opened in the economy, business investment was weak, consumption had not followed real incomes up, and productivity growth was poor. On that reading, a quarter point was too little to matter before the autumn, when the energy effect drops out of the annual comparison.

Bailey's framing in the press conference was about autopilot. He said rates could not be on one, because the committee had to respond to circumstances rather than to a schedule, and the phrase is a warning to anyone who extrapolated a cut at every meeting. The committee has cut four times in a year, and the intervals have not been regular: August, November, February, May, with a pause in between when the data did not cooperate.

The growth background is soft. The Office for Budget Responsibility had cut its forecast, the economy had grown barely at all in the previous quarter, and the employer National Insurance increase that took effect in April was raising costs for the firms most likely to hire. A rate cut of 25 basis points is a small offset to a payroll tax rise, and the committee knows it, which is one reason the vote was close and one reason the Governor talked about slack.

What would settle the next meeting is the inflation print for April and May, when the energy pass-through arrives, and the wage data that follows. A 3.5 per cent peak that behaves as forecast would allow another cut in August; a peak that overshoots would validate the two members who voted to hold.
