description: "Diversions around the Cape of Good Hope add a fortnight and three thousand miles to an Asia-Europe voyage, and the bill is paid by whoever has the weaker contract."
---

Container ships that used to transit the Suez Canal are now sailing around Africa, and the diversion adds roughly three thousand nautical miles and ten to fourteen days to a voyage between Asia and northern Europe. The cause is the risk of attack in the southern Red Sea, which insurers have priced as a war zone, and the consequence is a shipping schedule that has been rebuilt around a longer loop. A canal transit that costs a hundred thousand dollars in tolls is cheap against a diversion that costs ten days of a ship's revenue.

The canal was the reason the Asia-Europe route worked. A passage that saves nine days is a passage that lets a ship make two more voyages a year, and the toll is paid out of that gain. A carrier that routes around the Cape needs more ships for the same service, which absorbs capacity that was delivered by a record order book in 2023 and 2024. A fleet that was built for a canal route is a fleet with the wrong mix of ship sizes, because the longer route rewards a bigger hull.

Freight rates moved first and then settled. Spot rates on the route more than tripled in early 2024, and by the end of the year they had fallen back as the fleet absorbed the longer distance. A rate that is set by capacity rather than by a surcharge is a rate that reflects the number of ships, and the number of ships is now large. A surcharge that lasts a year becomes a line in the contract, and a line in the contract is a cost that cannot be removed quickly.

Contract structure decides who pays. A shipper with an annual contract at a fixed rate is protected for the term of the contract and exposed at renewal, and a shipper buying on the spot market pays the market every week. The negotiations in early 2025 produced contracts with fuel clauses and with diversion clauses, which are the mechanisms that shift the risk. A shipper that negotiates in January is negotiating with a carrier that has just published a record profit, and the balance of power is visible.

Emissions are the second bill. A longer voyage burns more fuel, and a shipping company that reports its carbon intensity is reporting a number that the diversion raised. The industry's efficiency rules are measured per unit of transport work, so a longer route is a worse ratio, and the carriers have begun to buy biofuel blends to offset it. A carrier that buys a biofuel blend pays a premium that a customer refuses to absorb, so the cost sits in the carrier's margin.

What the diversion has changed is the geography of inventory. A retailer that planned on a thirty-day lead time is now planning on forty-five, and the working capital that sits on the water is the cost that does not appear on a freight invoice. A supply chain built for a nine-day canal is a supply chain that has to be rebuilt for a longer world, and the rebuilding is slower than the ships. A supply chain that holds six weeks of stock instead of four is a supply chain with more cash tied up and less ability to respond to a demand shift.
