description: "Seven years of follow-up in more than ten thousand older Europeans finds that lonelier people begin with worse memory scores but decline at the same rate as everyone else."
---

Loneliness is treated as a public health problem because it correlates with mortality and with poor mental and physical health. Whether it damages the brain, and how, is a messier question, and the literature has been inconsistent because most studies are small, cross-sectional and full of people with other illnesses. The distinction also matters for how the finding gets reported, because a headline about loneliness accelerating decline would be a claim the data do not support.

Venegas-Sanabria and colleagues followed more than 10,000 adults aged 65 to 94 across Europe for seven years, reported in *Science* on 7 May. The size and the duration are what make the result worth reading: a seven-year window can separate a difference that exists from the start from a difference that accumulates. European cohorts also differ in how loneliness is measured, and a single question about feeling left out is not the same instrument as a validated scale.

Lonelier participants scored worse on memory tests at baseline. During the follow-up, their rate of decline was the same as that of less lonely participants. Loneliness marked a lower starting point rather than a faster slide. The baseline difference may also be a product of the same social conditions that produce loneliness, which would make the association a marker rather than a mechanism.

That distinction has consequences for how the problem is framed. If loneliness accelerates decline, then reducing it should slow the trajectory, and interventions would be measured over years. If it is associated with a lower level that then follows the normal path, the association may reflect something upstream: depression, poorer sleep, less cognitive stimulation, or the health conditions that make people withdraw. A lower starting point is still a difference worth taking seriously, because a brain that begins further down has less reserve when a vascular event or a depressive episode arrives.

Observational data cannot settle which explanation is right. People who are lonely differ from people who are not in income, housing, chronic disease and bereavement, and a cohort study can adjust for those variables only to the extent that they are measured. The finding narrows the claim rather than eliminating it, which is a useful thing for a field with a lot of large claims. Ageing itself is the largest of those upstream factors and the hardest to adjust for.

What would move the question forward is an intervention trial with cognition as a prespecified outcome, and those are rare because loneliness programmes are social rather than clinical. The result here suggests such a trial should measure baseline cognition carefully and follow people long enough to see a slope, not just a level, because a slope is the thing that a social intervention could plausibly change. The measurement problem is the other obstacle: loneliness is reported by the person feeling it, and a trial that randomizes support has to decide whether the outcome is the feeling, the contact or the cognition.
