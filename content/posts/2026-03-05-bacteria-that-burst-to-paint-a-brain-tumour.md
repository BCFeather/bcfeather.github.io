Chimeric antigen receptor therapy works well in blood cancers and poorly in solid tumours, and glioblastoma is the hardest case of the solid tumours. The barriers are anatomical, since the blood-brain barrier keeps cells out, and biological, since a tumour that expresses one antigen will lose it under the selective pressure of a single-target therapy.

A strategy reported in *Science* on 5 March attacks the barrier of delivery and the barrier of specificity in one construct. Zhang and colleagues converted the macrophages already resident in the brain into CAR-M cells, which avoids the problem of trafficking cells across the blood-brain barrier, because the cells were there in the first place and only had to be reprogrammed.

The second part of the design is the antigen. A strain of *Escherichia coli* was engineered to lyse at the tumour and to deposit, as it breaks open, two targets simultaneously, EGFRvIII and IL-13Rα2, both of which are expressed on glioblastoma and neither of which covers the whole tumour. Coating with two antigens makes escape by antigen loss much less likely.

The bacterial vector serves as the factory, the carrier and the switch. It delivers the antigen to the tumour and not to the liver, it provides an inflammatory stimulus that recruits and activates myeloid cells, and it is dose-scalable in a way that cell therapy is not, which matters for a disease whose incidence is small and whose manufacturing capacity is smaller. Each is a reason that a bacterium is a reasonable chassis for this problem, and together they are a reason that no other chassis would do.

Design is a departure from the usual antigen choice. Most CAR work selects the antigen that is most uniformly expressed, and this work selects two that are not, on the theory that a tumour has to lose both to escape and that losing both is costly for the tumour. Whether that theory holds in patients is the trial question that the preclinical work sets up.

Translation faces the obstacles that all bacterial therapies face. The same lipopolysaccharide that provides the inflammatory stimulus is toxic at doses that produce it, and the attenuation required to make a strain safe is attenuation that may make it ineffective. Sterile inflammation in a brain is also less tolerable than sterile inflammation elsewhere, because the compartment has no room.

Regulatory questions follow the biology. A therapy that combines a genetically modified bacterium with a genetically modified macrophage is two advanced therapy products in one protocol, and the manufacturing, release testing and pharmacovigilance of each must be solved separately before they can be combined. What the paper establishes is a principle and not a product. A tumour that is attacked from two antigens, by cells that are already on site, with a delivery system that grows in a fermenter, is a design that addresses the three reasons glioblastoma has resisted cell therapy. The principle is worth testing, and testing it will take years.
