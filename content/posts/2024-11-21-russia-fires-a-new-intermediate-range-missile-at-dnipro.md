Russia struck the city of Dnipro on the twenty-first of November with a missile that President Vladimir Putin described afterwards as a new intermediate-range system, and he said the attack was a response to Ukraine's use of American and British long-range weapons against targets inside Russia. Statement was made in a televised address hours after the strike, and it named the weapon as Oreshnik, a name that had not appeared in public before. Ukrainian officials said the target was an industrial complex that had produced rocket engines and other military equipment since the Soviet period.

Weapon itself is the part that analysts have argued about since. Putin said it flew at hypersonic speed, carried conventional warheads and could not be intercepted by existing defences. American officials said the missile was based on the RS-26 Rubezh, an intermediate-range ballistic missile that Russia had developed before a treaty banned the class, and that it had been fired from a launch site in the Astrakhan region. Range of the system, if it is deployed in numbers, would cover all of Europe.

Strike caused limited damage by the standards of the war and produced a large political effect. Six people were reported injured and a factory was damaged, and the attack did not change the military situation on the front. Its purpose appeared to be signalling: the missile was launched at a city far from the front, videos of the impact circulated within hours, and the Kremlin briefed foreign correspondents about the weapon's characteristics the following day. Warning to European capitals was implicit in the range rather than stated in the text.

Legally, the episode reopened a question that had been settled by treaty in 1987. Intermediate-range nuclear forces treaty banned ground-launched missiles with ranges between five hundred and five thousand five hundred kilometres, and the United States withdrew from it in 2019, citing Russian violations. Deployment of a new missile in this class is the consequence the treaty was designed to prevent, and arms control specialists said it removes a category of restraint that had held for three decades.

Western reaction combined condemnation with caution. NATO said the strike would not change its support for Ukraine, and the American defence department said it had been notified through nuclear risk reduction channels before the launch, which is a procedure designed to prevent a misunderstanding. Officials stressed that the missile carried conventional warheads, and that no change in the nuclear posture of either side had been detected.

Consequences for the war's next phase are uncertain and depend on deployment. If Oreshnik is produced in quantity and stationed within range of European cities, it becomes a permanent feature of the security argument on the continent and a bargaining chip in any negotiation. If it remains a small number of demonstration shots, it will be remembered as a message rather than a capability. Russia has said it will continue testing, and the next launch will be watched for whether the weapon is used against military targets or against cities.
