American and Iranian delegations met in Doha on 1 July with an agenda of memorandum clauses and no plan for a handshake, and the sessions were held in separate rooms with Qatari and Omani officials carrying texts between them. Both governments described the day as constructive, and President Trump said afterwards that progress had been made, while Iranian officials said the talks had addressed the implementation of the memorandum signed earlier in the year. Photographs released that evening showed two conference tables and no shared frame.

Format was the part the two capitals had argued about for weeks. Washington had pressed for a meeting of principals and Tehran had declined one until the sanctions clauses were implemented, so the encounter was arranged as proximity talks, with mediators walking between rooms. Officials from both sides briefed journalists afterwards on condition that the arrangement stay vague, and the vagueness carried a message of its own: a government that will not sit at a table with another government has told its public that the table has no legitimacy yet. Qatari officials have hosted such encounters for years, and they debriefed both delegations separately at the end of each session.

What the memorandum contains is narrower than the headlines suggested. It covers the release of frozen assets, a schedule for oil exports, and a mechanism for verifying that neither side moves armed groups across the borders it shares with Iraq and with the Gulf states. Implementing those clauses requires banks, insurers and shipping companies to act, and none of them will act on a promise that the two governments might disown within a month.

Mediators had their own reasons to keep the format. Qatar and Oman have built a role out of being the rooms where rivals can meet without meeting, and both have an interest in a settlement that keeps the Gulf's shipping lanes quiet. Their officials also know that a collapse would be blamed on the venue rather than on the parties, which is the sort of risk a small state tries not to carry. Oman also holds a channel to Tehran that no Western government can use, and its diplomats have kept it open through three rounds of escalation.

Market reaction was small, which is a verdict of its own. Brent settled at 76 dollars and one cent on the Friday after the talks, and most of the risk premium that had pushed prices higher in June had already come out of the curve. Traders had priced the meeting as a scheduling event rather than as a settlement, and the flat response suggested they were right.

What Doha established is that the two governments can talk about implementation and cannot yet talk about terms. Within three weeks the same two governments were exchanging missile strikes across the Gulf, and the memorandum survived as a document that both sides cited while doing the opposite. The next round was pencilled in for August, and by then the agenda had shifted from frozen assets to the ships in the strait.
