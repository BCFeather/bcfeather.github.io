Ammonia is a candidate fuel and a fertiliser feedstock, and storing it is awkward. It is a gas at room temperature, it liquefies only under pressure or at low temperature, and it attacks many of the materials that would hold it. A solid that absorbs ammonia reversibly at moderate conditions would change how the chemical is handled.

The material reported in *Nature*, 11 January 2023 is a metal-organic framework that takes up ammonia by a mechanism the field had not used for this gas. Benjamin Snyder, Ari Turkiewicz, Jeffrey Long and colleagues find that ammonia inserts into the metal-linker bond of the framework, a cooperative reaction that converts the three-dimensional structure into a dense one-dimensional coordination polymer.

Cooperative insertion is a different process from physisorption. In a porous solid, gas molecules sit in pores and the uptake is a smooth function of pressure. In an insertion reaction, the first insertion makes the next one easier, and the uptake jumps at a threshold pressure, which is the signature of a phase change rather than of filling.

The capacity that follows is high, and the thermal behaviour is the practical part. An insertion reaction releases heat, and a material that manages its own heat during charging avoids the temperature spike that degrades beds of adsorbents. The paper's claim of intrinsic thermal management is a statement about the reaction's enthalpy being absorbed by the structural change.

Reversibility is what makes it a storage material rather than a getter. The framework has to release the ammonia on demand and return to its original structure, and the paper reports cycles that do so. A material that binds ammonia irreversibly is a scrubber, which is a different product.

The stability question is the one the field will press. Ammonia is a small, polar, reactive molecule, and many frameworks hydrolyse or amorphise under it. The insertion mechanism avoids some of that chemistry by consuming the bond rather than exposing the metal site, and the reported crystallinity after cycling is the evidence.

The engineering gap is the bed. A powder with a phase change has to be shaped into pellets that survive many cycles and conduct heat, and the volume change of insertion is a mechanical stress that a pellet may not tolerate. Those are the questions between a material and a tank.

For ammonia's role in a low-carbon economy, the result is a candidate answer to the storage problem. If the material works at the pressures and temperatures of a real system, then ammonia becomes easier to keep, and a fuel that is hard to store is a fuel that stays in the chemical plant.

For the storage calculation, the relevant number is volumetric rather than gravimetric. A framework that takes up a large mass fraction of ammonia but collapses to half its volume is a worse tank than a denser material with a smaller uptake, and the paper's structural change has to be measured under pressure to settle that.
