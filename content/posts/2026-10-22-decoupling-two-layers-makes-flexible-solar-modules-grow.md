Flexible perovskite modules have trailed their rigid cousins for a reason that is chemical rather than architectural. A paper in *Science* on 22 October reports a fix for it, and the numbers are the argument: a certified 26.86 per cent on a small flexible cell, 22.31 per cent on a 5 by 5 centimetre module, 20.63 per cent on a 10 by 10 centimetre one and 20.15 per cent on a panel larger still. Wu and colleagues at Soochow University led the work, reported in *Science* on 22 October.

The weak layer is the self-assembled monolayer that sits between the transparent electrode and the perovskite and selects which charge carrier leaves. On a rigid glass substrate the monolayer forms cleanly. On a flexible substrate, surface hydroxyl groups desorb during processing and interfere with the assembly of the monolayer, so the layer is patchy, the contact is poor and the perovskite grown on top inherits the damage.

Separating the two steps is the fix. The authors anchor the monolayer first, with a passivator that accelerates the anchoring and suppresses the interference, and only then deposit the perovskite, so the solvent and the thermal budget of one step no longer decide the quality of the other. Each step can then be optimized for its own failure mode: the monolayer for coverage, the perovskite for grain size and phase. They call the approach decoupled deposition, and it treats the interface and the absorber as two processes with two sets of conditions rather than one compromise.

Stability is the half of the result that decides whether the efficiency matters. A 25 square centimetre flexible device kept 92.5 per cent of an initial 22 per cent efficiency after 1,044 hours of maximum power point tracking at 85 degrees Celsius and 65 per cent relative humidity. The modules also passed the full sequence of IEC TS 63,163, the standard written for flexible photovoltaic products, with certification by TUV SUD.

Flexible photovoltaics are wanted where weight and shape matter: vehicles, portable power, building surfaces that cannot take glass. The obstacle has been that efficiency fell as area grew, so a laboratory cell proved a chemistry that a factory could not reproduce. A certified module at 20 per cent on a commercially relevant size, and a standard test passed, moves the argument from chemistry to process engineering, which is where the remaining cost lies. Coating speed, drying uniformity and the yield of a kilometre of film are the numbers that a customer will ask for.

Three questions stay open. The certified numbers come from a laboratory line, and the yield of a roll-to-roll process is not the yield of a batch. The transparent electrode is still indium tin oxide on a plastic film, which sets cost and flexibility limits of its own, and the absorber contains lead, so encapsulation and end-of-life handling carry the same weight as efficiency. The paper answers the interface problem; the manufacturing problem is next.
