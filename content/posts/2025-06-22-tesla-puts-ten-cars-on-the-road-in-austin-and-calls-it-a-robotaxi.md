A decade of promises met a geofenced rectangle of Austin on 22 June, and the result was a service that a passenger could use and a company could describe as launched. Tesla began offering rides in a small fleet of Model Y cars, at a flat fare of 4.20 dollars per trip, to invited users who had been selected from shareholders and social media accounts.

Safety architecture is the part the company does not advertise. A Tesla employee sits in the front passenger seat as a monitor, the cars run on the company's Full Self-Driving software, and the operating area is restricted to a mapped portion of the city, with rides limited to daylight and to favourable weather. A monitor in the car is an admission that the system is not yet trustworthy enough to be alone.

The first customers were not a random sample. Most of the accounts that posted videos were Tesla enthusiasts with a company mention in their profile, and several livestreamed the entire trip, which is a form of marketing that a regulated taxi operator would not be allowed to rely on. Anecdotes from that population are evidence about enthusiasm and not about safety.

The economics are the harder question. A flat fare of 4.20 dollars is below the cost of the trip in most cities, which makes the launch a marketing expense rather than a business, and a fleet of ten cars cannot generate the utilization that makes a robotaxi cheaper than a human driver. The company says the software needs no mapping and no specialized hardware, which is the claim that would make scaling cheap if it holds.

Regulation is the gate that the company has to pass next. Texas has a light touch regime that allows autonomous vehicles to operate without a permit, and the states that matter for revenue, California among them, require incident reporting and a longer record. A service that is legal in one state is a pilot, not a product.

Competitors have been running paid driverless services for years. Waymo operates in several cities with no monitor in the car, and its safety record is published in reports that regulators can read, which is the standard that a new entrant will be measured against whether it likes it or not.

What would settle the year is whether the fleet grows beyond the invite list and whether the monitor leaves the front seat. A service with a hundred cars and a safety driver in each is a test with a revenue line, and the market has already begun to price the difference. The gap between a demonstration and a service is measured in cars and in incidents, and only one of those numbers is published by the company that owns the fleet. A fleet of ten cars in one city is a laboratory with a fare box, and the laboratory has already produced a public record of interventions that regulators will read before they permit anything larger.
