---

Most drug programmes aimed at the ubiquitin system try to stop a ligase. A paper in *Science* on 29 October does the opposite, and the result suggests the opposite direction is worth a programme of its own. Chandrasekhar and colleagues identified a small molecule that switches on CUL2-FEM1B, an E3 ligase that cells use under reductive stress, and the switch improves mitochondrial protein import and neuronal survival in models of mitochondrial disease. The paper appears in *Science* 394, 6823.

The biology runs through the import gate. Proteins destined for the mitochondrial interior have to pass the TOM complex, and when that gate is obstructed the organelle cannot renew itself. CUL2-FEM1B removes a family of proteins, the BEX proteins, that block substrate binding; under normal conditions the ligase is held inactive by one of those proteins, so the cell keeps a degradation system in reserve until stress arrives.

Finding an activator meant screening for a molecule that changes what the ligase binds, not one that changes what it degrades. The team ran a differential screen and found BD1, a compound that prevented CUL2-FEM1B from binding BEX proteins while leaving substrate recognition largely intact. Crystal structures placed BD1 in a pocket the ligase uses for BEX binding and not for the substrates it turns over in reductive stress.

A cell-active analogue, BD2, displaced the BEX proteins from the ligase and let the enzyme work. In cells, activation improved the import of proteins into mitochondria and raised energy production, and in neuronal models of mitochondrial and neurodegenerative disease, including Friedreich's ataxia, it restored survival. The effect comes from removing an obstruction rather than from adding a function, which is why the authors describe the compounds as displacers. Dose, timing and the fraction of ligase molecules occupied all matter more here than they would for an inhibitor, because the enzyme is being asked to run faster rather than to stop.

Degraders have dominated the field for a decade because it is easy to imagine a molecule that tags a protein for destruction. The other direction, a molecule that releases a ligase from its own inhibitor, has been harder to design and easier to dismiss. What this paper shows is that a ligase can be treated as an enzyme with a gain-of-function pharmacology, and that the reserve systems a cell keeps for stress are legitimate drug targets.

Clinical translation is not in the paper. The compounds are tool molecules, their selectivity across the ligase family is not settled, and the BEX proteins have normal roles that displacing them everywhere would disturb. Dosage is the deeper problem: a ligase that clears an import gate is useful for a few hours of stress and dangerous as a permanent setting, so the next question is how to switch it on briefly and in the right tissue. An activator with a short half-life and a tissue that already carries an import defect would be the sensible first candidate.
