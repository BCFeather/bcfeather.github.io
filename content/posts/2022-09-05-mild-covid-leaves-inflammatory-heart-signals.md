A patient who reports breathlessness after a mild infection is a patient whose heart may be inflamed, and the imaging that can see inflammation is magnetic resonance. A serial study of people without previous cardiac disease is the cleanest test. A clinic-based cohort of people who sought assessment is the population in which the question can be asked first.

Serial cardiac assessments in 346 individuals with COVID-19 and no previous cardiac disease or notable comorbidities, measuring blood biomarkers and imaging at a median of 109 days and again at 329 days. The paper appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 5 September 2022. A median of 109 days after infection is late enough for an acute effect to have passed and early enough for a chronic one to persist, and the timing is the design.

At baseline, 73 per cent reported cardiac symptoms, including exertional dyspnea in 62 per cent, palpitations in 28 per cent, atypical chest pain in 27 per cent and syncope in 3 per cent, and symptomatic individuals had higher heart rates and higher imaging values or contrast accumulation. A rare structural finding in a symptomatic group redirects attention to function and to inflammation, and the redirection is the interpretable result.

Structural heart disease and high levels of injury or dysfunction biomarkers were rare, which is the result that separates this cohort from myocarditis. At follow-up, 57 per cent still had symptoms, and diffuse myocardial edema was more pronounced in those who remained symptomatic than in those who improved. Female sex and diffuse myocardial involvement at baseline independently predicted symptoms at follow-up. A hypothesis that explains a correlation is the best an observational imaging study can produce, and the paper presents it as one.

A diffuse signal is the kind that a focal reading of magnetic resonance would miss, and the pattern is what the authors emphasize.
