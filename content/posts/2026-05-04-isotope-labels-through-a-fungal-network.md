The idea that trees trade carbon through fungal networks has become one of the most repeated claims in popular ecology: the wood wide web, mother trees feeding seedlings, a forest as a cooperative. A paper in *Nature Ecology and Evolution* on 28 April reports an experiment designed to test the transfer directly, and the answer is that it happens, at a scale far smaller than the stories imply.

Design was a labelling experiment in a temperate mixed stand. The team fed carbon-13 labelled carbon dioxide to six mature trees of one species, then measured the isotope in the tissues of neighbouring seedlings of a different species at distances from one to twelve metres. Mesh cores allowed fungal hyphae through while excluding roots, and control plots with trenches cut around them tested whether transfer required an intact network.

The isotope arrived. Within two weeks, seedling leaves contained carbon-13 at levels above background, and the enrichment declined with distance as expected for diffusion through a hyphal network. In trenched controls the enrichment was roughly a fifth of the connected plots, which is consistent with transfer through fungi being the dominant pathway but not the only one.

Magnitude is where the interpretation narrows. The transferred carbon accounted for between 2 and 6 per cent of the seedlings' total carbon in the first month, and the authors estimate an annual figure below 5 per cent for shaded seedlings and near zero for seedlings in full light. A seedling that gets 4 per cent of its carbon from a neighbour is not surviving because of the network; it is surviving despite a marginal subsidy.

Species specificity complicates the general claim. Transfer was strongest between ectomycorrhizal trees and seedlings sharing the same fungal species, and negligible between arbuscular mycorrhizal partners, which form different structures and exchange phosphorus more than carbon. The popular account treats the network as a single system; the data describe a set of pairings with different rules.

Alternative explanations were tested and not fully excluded. Carbon can move through soil water after root exudation, through fine-root grafts, or through fungal hyphae that connect to dead roots rather than living ones. The trenching control addresses the first and the mesh design addresses the second, and the authors concede that distinguishing a living pathway from a decaying one is beyond the method.

The paper's value is corrective rather than dismissive. Mycorrhizal networks matter for nutrient exchange, for seedling establishment in harsh soils and for the spread of signalling molecules, and the evidence for those roles is independent of this result. What the labelling shows is that carbon transfer, the most cinematic of the claims, is quantitatively small.

Reading the literature on this topic requires holding two facts: fungi connect trees, and connections are not the same as subsidies. Enthusiasm for the first has repeatedly been read as proof of the second, and a number with a decimal point is a better guide than a metaphor.
