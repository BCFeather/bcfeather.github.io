Hindenburg Research published a 106-page report on 24 January accusing the Adani group of stock manipulation, undisclosed related-party dealings and accounting fraud across its ports, power, coal and infrastructure companies. Gautam Adani, then the third richest person in the world, denied every allegation and called the document an attack on India. The report was published on a Tuesday and the shares fell before the Indian market opened the next morning.

Markets answered within hours. Shares in Adani Enterprises fell 20 per cent on the day, and the group companies lost close to 100 billion dollars of combined market value over two sessions, with the ports and transmission units down by a fifth. Seven listed firms were affected, and the fall wiped out most of the wealth the founder had accumulated in the previous year. Dollar bonds issued by the group fell to 70 cents on the dollar, and the cost of insuring its debt against default rose above 500 basis points.

Timing made the damage worse. A follow-on share sale of 2.5 billion dollars was already open to retail buyers, and the group had marketed it as a demonstration of confidence. Subscription from institutions covered the book, but the retail portion was met only with the help of the group's own companies, and the shares were priced at the bottom of the range. The share sale had been priced at 3,112 rupees, and the group said it would use the money for airports and hydrogen plants.

Questions in the report concerned audit and related parties. It alleged that four offshore entities with ties to the family had traded in the group's shares, that some auditors were small firms with few staff, and that the group had borrowed against assets through a structure involving a Mauritius-registered vehicle. The group's reply called the entity question a misunderstanding of how export finance works. The audit question turned on a firm with twelve partners, which had signed the accounts of several group companies for a decade.

Rating agencies and index providers moved next. Credit ratings on three group companies were put on watch, index compilers reviewed the free float, and several banks paused new lending while they checked exposure. The group said it had repaid 1.1 billion dollars of loans against shares in the previous year and that its leverage was within the covenants it had agreed. Banks in France and Japan were reported to have lent against the group's ports, and they declined to comment on their exposure.

Disputes over disclosure reached the regulator and the courts. The securities board began an examination of the share-sale disclosures, and opposition politicians demanded a parliamentary committee while the government, which had allowed the group to run several infrastructure concessions, said it had no comment on a private company. What the episode established is how little of the group's finances sat in public view. The Supreme Court later appointed a panel to look into the disclosure questions, and it reported back in the spring.
