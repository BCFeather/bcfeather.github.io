Monte Verde sits in a peat bog in southern Chile and has been the anchor of an argument for thirty years. Excavations there produced plant remains, hearths and a footprint, dated to roughly fourteen thousand years ago, and the site became the standard evidence that people reached the southern cone before the Laurentide ice sheet began to retreat.

A paper in *Science* on 19 March, by Surovell and colleagues, re-examines the stratigraphy and reports that the archaeological layers date to the mid-Holocene, several thousand years younger than the original interpretation. The news section of the same issue describes the reaction as a debate that has exploded, which is the tone that this kind of revision produces.

The technical dispute is about how the deposits formed. A peat bog accumulates slowly and irregularly, and material can move within it as the water table shifts. The original interpretation treated the archaeological horizon as a surface on which people lived, and the new one treats it as a lens of material that has been reworked, which changes the age of the objects without changing the age of the sediment around them.

Dating methods are the second front. Radiocarbon dates depend on which carbon is measured, and a sample taken from a plant fragment that has exchanged carbon with younger groundwater will give an age that is too young. The new work argues that the relevant samples are affected by this, and the counter-argument is that the original laboratory work controlled for it and that the site's internal consistency is too strong to be an artefact of contamination.

What is at stake is larger than one site. South America's settlement history is reconstructed from a small number of well-dated sites, and the removal of the earliest one moves the arrival of people on the continent later and narrows the window between arrival and the occupation of the Amazon and the Andes. A chronology built on a single site is a chronology that one paper can change, and a continent's prehistory resting on one peat bog is a prehistory with a single point of failure.

Archaeology has been here before. Sites that defined a timeline have been redated, and some have survived the redating and some have not. Monte Verde survived one round of skeptical examination, when critics argued that the artifacts were geofacts and that their association with human activity was weak, and it emerged with stronger evidence than it went in, partly because the skeptics forced a better documentation of the context than the original excavators had produced.

Further excavation is the answer both camps can accept, because the argument is about how the deposits formed, and that is a question a larger exposure can settle. Until it is settled, the site remains the best-documented early occupation in the southern cone and a reminder that a chronology is a hypothesis about a place, and that places are harder to read than the objects found in them.
