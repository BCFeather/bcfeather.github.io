title: "American inflation falls to eight point five per cent as petrol gets cheaper"
date: "2022-08-10T23:40:00+08:00"

description: "The July figures show the first monthly fall in prices for two years, and the core measure keeps rising."
---

Consumer prices rose by eight point five per cent in the year to July, down from nine point one per cent in June, and the monthly change was flat. It was the first month without an increase since the start of the pandemic, and the reason was petrol. The average price of a gallon fell by about eight per cent during July, which is the largest monthly fall in the index since the spring of 2020. The price of used cars, which had driven the index for a year, also fell.

The core measure, which excludes food and energy, rose by five point nine per cent, and the monthly increase was smaller than in June and larger than the economists had expected. Shelter costs, which are the largest component and the slowest to move, continued to climb. Food prices rose by more than ten per cent over the year, and the price of eggs and of dairy products reached levels that the department described as unusual. Services, which include rents and medical care, are the part of the index that the central bank watches most closely.

The market reaction was relief rather than triumph. Equities rose by two per cent on the day and the two-year Treasury yield fell, which means that traders reduced their expectations for the next increase. The central bank has raised by three quarters of a point twice in a row, and the officials had said that they would need to see a series of declines before they slowed. One month is a start and it is the kind of number that can reverse when the base effects change in the autumn.

At the same time, the politics of the number are unavoidable in a year with an election. The president said that there was zero inflation in July and that the figures showed progress, and the Republicans said the annual rate was still above eight per cent and that the monthly figure had been produced by a fall in the price of fuel. The White House has been releasing petrol from the strategic reserve since the spring, and the reserve is now at its lowest level since 1984. Economists have argued about how much of the fall in the pump price is due to that release.

Meanwhile, the global picture is different from the American one. Inflation in the euro area is close to nine per cent and rising, and the price of gas in Europe is five times what it was a year ago. The Bank of England expects a rate above thirteen per cent in the autumn, and the Bank of Japan has kept its policy loose. The fall in the American number is a national story, and the forces that produced the rise are still at work everywhere else.

What the report does is give the central bank room to slow without giving it a reason to stop. The officials have said that they want the rate to reach a level that restrains demand and that they will keep it there for a while. The next two reports will decide whether the July number was a turning point or a pause, and the answer will depend on the price of energy and on the cost of shelter. Both of those are set outside the bank, and both of them are still rising.
