Whether money buys happiness has been an empirical question with two answers. One analysis found emotional well-being rising with income up to a threshold and then flattening, while another, using a larger sample and a different question, found that it kept rising.

In *PNAS* on 7 March 2023, the disagreement is resolved with data. Two of the authors of the earlier studies analyse a combined dataset and a set of measures, and the resolution turns out to depend on which part of the distribution is examined.

Two findings carry the answer. For most people well-being rises with income across the whole range, and the flattening that the earlier work reported does not appear, whereas among the least happy group the flattening does appear and additional income beyond a level adds little.

Because a question about the previous day's emotions captures a different construct from a question about life satisfaction, measure matters, and the paper separates the two rather than averaging them. Sample matters more, since the study that found a plateau had few observations at the top of the income distribution and a thin tail produces a flat curve that is an artefact, while the combined dataset has enough high earners to fill it.

Interpretation is not that money is everything, because the curve's slope is modest and the gap between a comfortable income and a large one is smaller in well-being than in money, so the point is about shape rather than magnitude. Policy reads the slope at the bottom, since if well-being rises with income at low incomes then transfers to the poorest buy more well-being per dollar than transfers to the middle do.

Methodology is the lesson. Two careful analyses disagreed over sample size and measure, and a third that combines them identifies which assumption produced which result, which is what a resolution looks like.

Causation is what the paper cannot establish, because income and well-being are correlated and a happier person may earn more, and the natural experiments that would settle it are rare and narrow. Happiness measurement is the weakest link, since a single question about yesterday is consistent and crude while a battery of questions is richer and harder to compare across surveys, and the choice made here is the compromise the literature has settled on. The threshold's location, where the curve of the least happy group flattens, is a number policy can use: beyond it the marginal dollar does not improve their emotional lives, and below it the dollar does. The curve also leaves out non-monetary income, since a household's time, health and family support all bear on well-being and none of them appears in the measure, and a slope estimated from cash alone is a partial derivative rather than the whole effect. A replication in a country with a different income distribution would test whether the threshold is a property of money or of the place where it is spent, and surveys in poorer countries are where that test would run.
