Whether money buys happiness has been an empirical question with two answers. One analysis found that emotional well-being rose with income up to a threshold and then flattened; another, using a larger sample and a different question, found it kept rising.

The paper in *PNAS*, 7 March 2023 resolves the disagreement with data. Two of the authors of the earlier studies analyze a combined dataset and a set of measures, and the resolution turns out to be about which part of the distribution is examined.

The findings are two. For most people, well-being rises with income across the whole range, and the flattening that the earlier work reported does not appear. For the least happy group, the flattening does appear, and beyond a level the additional income adds little.

The measure matters. A question about the previous day's emotions captures a different construct from a question about life satisfaction, and the two behave differently with respect to income. The paper separates them rather than averaging.

The sample matters more. The earlier study that found a plateau had a smaller sample at the top of the income distribution, and a thin tail produces a flat curve that is an artefact of few observations. The combined dataset has enough high earners to fill the tail.

The interpretation is not that money is everything. The curve's slope is modest, and the difference between a comfortable income and a large one is smaller in well-being than in money. The paper's point is about the shape, not the magnitude.

The policy reading is about the bottom. If well-being rises with income at low incomes, then transfers to the poorest buy more well-being per dollar than transfers to the middle, and the curve's slope at the bottom is the relevant number.

The methodological lesson is about replication. Two careful analyses disagreed because of sample size and measure, and a third analysis that combines them identifies which assumption produced which result. That is what a resolution looks like.

What the paper cannot establish is causation. Income and well-being are correlated, and a person who is happier may earn more. The natural experiments that would settle it are rare and narrow.

For the field, the value is a curve that both camps can accept. A debate that ran for a decade ends with a figure that has a slope and a plateau, and the plateau is located where the least happy respondents live.

The measurement of happiness is the weakest link. A single question about yesterday is reliable in the sense of consistency and crude in the sense of content, and a battery of questions is richer and harder to compare across surveys. The paper's choice is a compromise that the literature has settled on.

The threshold's location, where the least happy group's curve flattens, is a number that policy can use. It says that beyond a certain income the marginal dollar does not improve the emotional lives of the least happy, and below it the dollar does.
