The capsule entered the atmosphere shortly before ten in the morning local time and hit the water about six minutes later. It came down off the coast of Baja California, within a few kilometres of the target point that the recovery teams had chosen months earlier. Parachutes deployed in the last minutes, and the splashdown was photographed from a ship that had been waiting since dawn. Divers were alongside within an hour, and the capsule was lifted onto the deck by the following morning.

Re-entry was the part the engineers had worried about most. The heat shield met the atmosphere at nearly twenty-five thousand miles an hour, close to Mach thirty-two, and the temperatures on its surface rose above anything a capsule built for people had experienced. The shield is designed to char and to shed material rather than to conduct heat inwards, and the sensors behind it recorded temperatures that the team will spend months analysing. The capsule had spent twenty-five days in space, and the last of them were spent waiting for weather that would allow a landing.

Twenty-five and a half days in space had taken the spacecraft about one point four million miles. Most of that distance was spent in a distant retrograde orbit around the Moon, a path chosen because it is stable and because it tests the navigation and life-support systems for longer than a short mission would. The capsule also flew further from the Earth than any spacecraft designed to carry people had gone before. Navy divers had practised the procedure in a pool in San Diego for two years before they tried it in the swell.

Recovery was its own test. The navy and the space agency had rehearsed the procedure in the Pacific for years, with a dummy capsule and with crews who practised opening the hatch in heavy swell. The sea state on the morning of 11 December was moderate, and the operation took longer than the rehearsal had, which is what always happens when the equipment is real.

The mission carried mannequins rather than astronauts, and the data they recorded will shape the next flight. Radiation exposure, vibration, temperature and the behaviour of the seats all have to be checked against models before a crew is allowed to sit in the same place. Engineers also want to know how the heat shield behaved at the seams, because a crewed return depends on those seams holding. The recovery ship carried a team of engineers who read the data off the capsule before it was lifted, and none of them found anything that surprised them.

A crewed fly-by is planned for 2024, and a landing on the surface for a date that has moved twice. What has been established is narrower: the rocket lifts, the capsule flies, the shield holds and the ship can find it in the water. Four facts, each of them necessary, and none of them sufficient. What the mission proved is that the chain of people and machines can hold together for a month, and that is the part of a lunar programme that takes longest to build.
