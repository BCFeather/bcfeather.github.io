Municipal outdoor pools shut at the end of August, whatever the temperature is doing. The closing date is usually the Sunday before the school term, and it is a staffing decision dressed as a seasonal one. The lifeguards are students, the students go back to class, and the pool that has been open since June closes on a day when the water is still twenty-six degrees and the afternoon is still thirty-three.

The people who notice are the ones who swim on a schedule. There is a group at every municipal pool that arrives at half past six, swims a kilometre, and leaves before the office. They know each other by their swimming costumes and not by their names. For four months they have shared a lane and a set of complaints about the water temperature, and for the rest of the year they will swim indoors at a pool on the other side of the district, at a different hour, with a different set of people.

Municipal pool economics are difficult, and have been for decades. The admission charge covers a fraction of the running cost, and the difference comes from a local budget that is always being asked to justify itself. Indoor pools cost more to run than outdoor ones and are open all year, which means the pool that closes in August is the one that is cheapest to keep and the one that serves the neighbourhood in the season when the neighbourhood needs it.

What has grown in the last few years is the private club in a hotel or a residential compound. It is clean, it is open in December, and it costs twenty times what the municipal pool charges. The people who use it are the people who were already going to swim somewhere, and the municipal pool is for the ones who were not.

What the summer season produces, in a pool, is a specific kind of social mixing that does not happen anywhere else in the city. A retired man and a delivery rider share a lane because there is no other lane. A child learning to swim is in the shallow end next to an adult doing stretches. The pool is one of the few rooms in the city where the only hierarchy is speed, and it lasts four months and then stops.

The closing week has its own atmosphere. The regulars swim longer than usual. Somebody brings a cake to the last morning. The manager says the pool will open again in June and everybody knows it will, and everybody also knows that the June opening will be with a new set of guards and a new set of prices.

What the pool costs to keep open through September is a sum that a district can calculate precisely, and what it is worth is a sum that nobody has ever calculated, because the people who benefit are not the ones who fill in the forms.
