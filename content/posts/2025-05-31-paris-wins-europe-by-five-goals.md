A club that had spent fourteen years and more than a billion euros trying to buy this trophy won it with a team built around a twenty-year-old. Paris Saint-Germain beat Inter Milan 5-0 in Munich on 31 May 2025, the widest margin in the history of the European Cup and Champions League finals, and the first title for a French club since Marseille in 1993.

Scoring started early and never stopped. Achraf Hakimi scored after twelve minutes, Desire Doue added two more at twenty and sixty-three, Khvicha Kvaratskhelia finished a move that began with a backheel from Ousmane Dembele at seventy-three, and Senny Mayulu completed it at eighty-seven. Doue was named player of the match, and at nineteen he became the first French player to appear in a final before turning twenty.

Shape of the game was unusual for a final. Inter had beaten Barcelona in a semi-final that went to extra time and had conceded four in that second leg, and Paris attacked the space behind a back three that never settled. A five-goal final is usually a match between unequal teams, and this one was, although the gap on the night was created by a system and not by a balance sheet, which is the detail that matters for next season.

Luis Enrique became the seventh coach to win the competition with two different clubs, having won it with Barcelona in 2015. His team had already won Ligue 1 by nineteen points and the Coupe de France, so the final completed a treble, and the club's trophy count since the Qatari takeover reached 37. Paris is the twenty-fourth club to win the competition, and the first from France in thirty-two years.

Economics behind the result are the part that football argues about. Paris spent a decade buying established stars and losing quarter-finals, then changed strategy towards younger players and a coach with a system. Doue, Mayulu and Warren Zaire-Emery came through or arrived as teenagers, and the wage bill fell while the squad improved, which is the opposite of what the first decade of the project did.

Eighteen thousand Paris supporters travelled to Munich, several thousand of them without tickets, and the footage of the final whistle is already the club's most valuable marketing asset. A first title after fourteen years of investment is a return on capital that a state-backed fund can measure, and it is also the moment a project stops being a project.

What would settle the argument is what comes next. A club that wins once with young players has proved a model; a club that wins twice has built one. The next season starts with the same question every champion faces, which is whether the system or the squad was the reason. The city that waited thirty-two years for this will not be satisfied with one. A treble in the first season of a new method is the kind of result that makes a director of football famous and a coach poachable.
