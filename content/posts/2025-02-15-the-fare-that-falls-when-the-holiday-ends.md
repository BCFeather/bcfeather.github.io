description: "Domestic air fares collapse in the week after the festival, and the fall is a pricing model rather than a discount. The airlines are selling the same seat to a different passenger."
---

Domestic air fares collapse in the week after the festival, and the fall is steep enough that a route which cost a thousand yuan on the fourth of February costs two hundred on the tenth. The airlines describe it as a normal seasonal curve and the passengers describe it as a bargain, and both are reading the same numbers.

The mechanism is the way a seat is priced. An airline sells the same seat at a series of prices that rise as the departure approaches and as the cabin fills, and the model is built on a forecast of the demand. In the holiday the forecast is high and the cheap fares are withdrawn early, and in the week after it the forecast is low and the fares start at the bottom of the ladder.

The demand that disappears is the visiting demand. A person who flew home on the fourth has no reason to fly again in the following week, and the business travel that fills the cabin in a normal February has not resumed. The two segments are what the airline plans for, and the gap between them is the week in which the fare is decided by the empty seats rather than by the cost.

The capacity is the other half of the arithmetic. An airline cannot easily remove a flight from a schedule that was published in November, and the aircraft that was added for the holiday is still flying in the week after it. The seats that are empty are seats that cost the airline the fuel and the crew regardless, and a seat sold for two hundred is better than a seat sold for nothing.

The railway follows a similar curve and with a different shape. The high-speed trains keep a fixed price in most markets, and the discount appears in the form of the seats that are released late, and the passenger who is flexible enough to travel on a Tuesday in February is the one who benefits.

The hotels have their own version of the week. A room that cost three times its normal rate on the fourth returns to the normal rate on the sixth, and the resorts that depend on the holiday have a month of low occupancy afterwards. The travel industry calls the period the shoulder, and the shoulder is where the margins are thin.

The consumer behaviour that the curve produces is a set of strategies that the experienced traveller has learned. Book early for the holiday, wait for the week after it, and check the fare on a Tuesday rather than a Friday. The strategies are rational and they are also the reason the cheap seats disappear as soon as enough people learn them.

What the week shows is that a price is a statement about a forecast. The airline does not know how many people will fly on the tenth, and the fare is its guess, and the guess is revised every day by a computer that reads the bookings. The bargain that a passenger finds is the residue of a forecast that was too pessimistic.
