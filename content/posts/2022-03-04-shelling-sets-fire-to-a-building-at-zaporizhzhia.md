Shelling hit the Zaporizhzhia nuclear power station in the early hours of 4 March, and a fire started in a training building beside the reactor blocks. The plant is the largest in Europe, with six reactors and a capacity of 5,700 megawatts, and the fighting came within a few hundred metres of the containment structures. Russian forces took the site the same day, and the Ukrainian staff went on operating the reactors under armed guard.

Inspectors at the International Atomic Energy Agency said the safety systems had not been affected, that the fire had damaged a building used for training and administration, and that radiation levels at the site were normal. One reactor was shut down, the others ran at reduced power, and the agency asked for access that took six months to arrange. Its director general said the situation had moved from the risk of a serious accident to a serious safety concern.

Engineers spent the week explaining why a shell near a reactor matters. A water-cooled reactor needs offsite power to run its pumps, and the plant keeps diesel generators for the case when the grid goes down. If the pumps stop, the fuel heats up, the zirconium cladding reacts with steam, and the water boils, which is what happened at Fukushima after the tsunami and why the agency counts the hours that a diesel tank can run before somebody has to drive more fuel through a checkpoint. A reactor shut down for years still produces heat from the decay of its fission products, and that heat still needs water.

Diplomacy produced statements rather than access. The Security Council met in an emergency session, the agency's board asked Russia to stop fighting near the plant, and the seven pillars of nuclear safety that the agency had published in 2021 were repeated by every government that spoke. Ukraine asked for a no-fly zone over the site and was refused, and the United States said the matter belonged to the agency and to Ukraine.

What came in the summer was worse than that night in March. Shelling in August hit a switchyard and cut the plant from the grid, and the reactors were left on their own power with the diesel generators running for weeks. A mission of the agency visited in September and reported that the staff were working under the orders of a Russian commander, that two of the three external power lines had been lost, and that the arrangement could not continue.

The last unit was placed in cold shutdown that September, and the plant has produced no electricity since. What the year proved about nuclear stations at war is that the structures are hard to damage and the systems that make them safe are soft. A containment vessel can take a rocket, and the grid that feeds the pumps, the switchyard that connects it and the diesel tanks that stand in for it cannot. The warnings of 2022 became the operating assumptions of every reactor that sits on a front line.
