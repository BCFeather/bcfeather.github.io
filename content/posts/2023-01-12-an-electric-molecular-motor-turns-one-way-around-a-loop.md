Molecular machines have been built from many parts, and the hardest part has been direction. A motor that spins randomly is a rotor; a motor that spins one way is a machine, and the difference requires a ratchet, a fuel and a sequence that never lets the system slip backwards.

The motor reported in *Nature*, 11 January 2023 is a [3]catenane, two macrocyclic rings threaded onto a larger loop so that they can slide around it. Long Zhang, Yunyan Qiu, Fraser Stoddart and colleagues drive the rings with redox chemistry rather than light or heat, and the movement is reported as highly unidirectional.

The design uses cyclobis(paraquat-p-phenylene) rings, which accept electrons reversibly. Reducing a ring changes its affinity for the stations along the track, so that the ring prefers one site when oxidised and another when reduced. Alternating the potential therefore moves the ring from station to station, and the order of the stations sets the direction.

A ratchet is what prevents the reverse step. Each station is separated from its neighbour by a barrier that is only passable in one redox state, so the sequence of states and the sequence of positions are locked together. The motor does work by moving against a load, and the paper's measurements of the directional bias are the evidence that the ratchet is doing its job.

Electric drive is the practical advantage. Light-driven motors need a photosensitiser and a wavelength; chemically driven ones accumulate waste. An electrode supplies electrons and removes them, and the fuel is a potential that a potentiostat can hold. For a machine that has to run for many cycles, that is the cleanest interface available.

The scale is the limitation. A [3]catenane is a few nanometres across, and its torque is measured in units that no macroscopic load can feel. The interesting applications are therefore not engines but switches: a ring that moves between two stations can change the fluorescence of a molecule, open a pore or expose a binding site, and the direction of the movement is what makes the switch reliable.

What the paper does not yet show is a motor coupled to a function. A ring that turns is a demonstration; a ring that turns a valve is a device, and the coupling is where molecular machinery usually fails, because the load reacts back on the mechanism.

The comparison with biological motors is instructive. ATP synthase and the bacterial flagellum achieve direction with a fixed structure and a chemical gradient, and their efficiency is set by evolution rather than by design. A synthetic motor that runs on a potentiostat is cruder and more controllable, and controllability is what a technology needs first.

The next steps are lifetime and speed. A motor that survives thousands of cycles without fatigue, and one that turns fast enough to move something visible, would mark the transition from chemistry to engineering. The paper's demonstration of direction is the precondition for both.
