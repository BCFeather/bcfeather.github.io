A benchmark posted to arXiv on 16 March proposes that the fastest way to measure an agent is to make it play a game that millions of people already understand. The PokeAgent Challenge is built on the battle system of Pokemon and on the role-playing games that surround it. Its authors come from several universities and from industry, and the paper runs to forty-one pages. The competition that produced the data ran at NeurIPS in 2025, and more than a hundred teams entered it. Its baselines are published with the scripts that produced them, so a later submission can be scored against the same numbers.

Two tracks carry the benchmark. The Battling Track supplies more than twenty million battle trajectories and a set of baselines from heuristics, from reinforcement learning and from language models. Battles are partially observable, because a player sees one active creature and has to infer the rest of the opponent's team from what appears. The Speedrunning Track asks for long-horizon planning in the role-playing game, where progress depends on a sequence of thousands of small decisions. It comes with an open-source orchestration system so that different harnesses can be compared on the same footing.

Results show three populations that behave differently. Specialist agents trained with reinforcement learning play competently inside the format they were trained on and struggle when the opponent's habits change. Generalist language models reason about the game in text and lose to the specialists on raw win rate, while explaining their moves better. Elite human players beat both, and the gap between them and the best agent is the number the benchmark exists to shrink. Submissions from the competition, described in the paper, fill in the shape of that gap.

One finding will interest people who build evaluations. The authors compare battling against a matrix of standard language-model benchmarks and find the two nearly orthogonal, which means that skill at Pokemon battles measures something the usual suites do not. A model can score well on reasoning tests and lose consistently to a simple scripted opponent. The reverse also occurs, and a specialist that wins battles cannot explain a joke or follow an instruction. A benchmark that captures a capability the others miss is worth having even when the capability looks like a game.

Design choices decide whether a benchmark lives or dies. This one moves to a live leaderboard for battles and to self-contained evaluation for speedrunning, with the code published so that others can run it. A living benchmark needs maintenance, because the frontier moves and a fixed test set becomes a target to be fitted. The paper's answer is to keep the environment open and to let the leaderboard absorb the improvements. Whether that works depends on people caring enough to submit.

What the benchmark measures is the part of intelligence that involves other agents. A battle is a game against an opponent who is also planning, with hidden information on both sides, and the reward arrives only at the end. Those conditions are closer to negotiation, to trading and to traffic than a multiple-choice examination is. A model that plays well under them has learned something about predicting another mind, and the paper's data make that comparable across systems for the first time.
