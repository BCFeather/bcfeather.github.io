description: "Polycrystalline samples of a layered nickelate lose all resistance below 6.6 kelvins at 9 gigapascals and reach 35.6 kelvins at 14.5 gigapascals, a preprint reports."
---

Nickelates joined the cuprates as high-temperature superconductors in 2023, and the question since has been whether the effect survives in ordinary polycrystalline material or belongs to a particular crystal. A superconductor that exists only in a single crystal is a laboratory object, and one that survives in a pressed pellet is a material. Bulk polycrystals are what applications need, and their grain boundaries are where superconductivity usually degrades first.

Samples of \(\mathrm{La}_{3}\mathrm{Ni}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{7}\) made by a sol-gel route without post-annealing under oxygen pressure were measured under hydrostatic pressure to 14.5 GPa, the authors report in a preprint posted on 29 September 2023. Resistivity carries the evidence, and magnetism plays no part in it. The synthesis avoids the high-pressure annealing that single-crystal growth demands, which makes the route worth testing if the transition survives it. A pressed bar with contacts in line is the standard geometry, and the pellet is measured as grown.

Density-wave order is what the pressure removes. The anomaly in resistivity weakens as the pressure rises, a drop appears at 7 GPa, and zero resistance is reached at 9 GPa below 6.6 K. By 14.5 GPa the transition temperature has climbed to 35.6 K, which puts the material within the range where nickelates have been discussed as cuprate analogues. The anomaly and the transition are measured in one four-terminal geometry, so the sequence rests on a single kind of data.

Suppression of a competing order, a partial drop, then a vanishing resistance, is the sequence the paper reads as superconductivity. Because the measurement is four-terminal resistivity on a polycrystal, the zero is a bulk path through connected grains rather than a single filament, and that is what makes the polycrystalline result worth reporting. Pressure was raised in steps and held, so the transition temperature is tracked rather than inferred from a single point, and the zero at 9 GPa gives the first temperature in the sequence. Whether the density wave disappears or only retreats is a question that resistivity cannot answer alone.

Vanishing resistance is not the Meissner effect, and no diamagnetism or specific heat is reported. The pressure medium and the grain microstructure both shape the transition, and the pairing symmetry remains unidentified. Hydrostatic conditions inside a cubic anvil cell are approximate, and non-hydrostatic stress can shift a transition by several kelvins. The upper critical field and the shape of the resistive transition are also left out.

A shielding-fraction measurement and a structural refinement under pressure would separate bulk superconductivity from a percolating path, and crystals grown at the same oxygen content would test whether the polycrystalline route changes the transition itself or only the path to it. Neither measurement is routine at 15 GPa, which is why the question has stayed open. A heat-capacity anomaly at the transition would be the cheapest confirmation of all.
