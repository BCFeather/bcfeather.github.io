Fern genomes are famously large and famously repetitive, and the standard explanation has been polyploidy: doubling the whole set of chromosomes, then losing some, then doubling again. The Salviniales, the water ferns, seemed to be an extreme case, with *Salvinia molesta* long described as a pentaploid. A paper in *Science* on 27 August sequenced two species and found the story does not hold. Genome size in plants is a running joke among botanists, and ferns sit near the top of the table with more DNA than some vertebrates.

Rizzieri and colleagues assembled genomes for *Salvinia molesta* and *Salvinia cucullata*. The second species turned out to have 34 chromosome pairs and the smallest genome known in any fern, which is a surprising place to find a record. The first turned out to be an obligately clonal diploid, not a pentaploid, which corrects a description that had stood for decades. Thirty-four pairs is an ordinary count for a fern, which makes the smallness of the genome a statement about how little repetitive DNA the species carries.

Neither genome showed evidence of whole-genome duplication events in its history. That removes the mechanism usually invoked to explain how ferns carry so much DNA and tolerate so much rearrangement, and it replaces it with repeated chromosome rearrangements: fusions, fissions and translocations that reshuffle the karyotype without doubling it. Rearrangement without duplication is a mechanism that cytology can see only indirectly, through the pairing patterns at meiosis, and the assembly is what makes the inference explicit.

Clonality explains why *S. molesta* can carry a stable odd-looking genome. A plant that reproduces vegetatively does not have to pair its chromosomes every generation, so rearrangements that would be lethal in a sexual lineage can persist. The species is also one of the world's worst aquatic weeds, and a genome that tolerates rearrangement may be part of why it spreads so readily. A weed that spreads by fragments carries its rearranged genome intact into every new pond it reaches, which turns a curiosity into an ecological advantage.

The comparison matters beyond ferns. If genomic plasticity can come from rearrangement rather than duplication, then the size of a genome is a poor proxy for its evolutionary history, and the same argument applies to other lineages where polyploidy has been assumed from chromosome counts alone. Other clonal plants may be hiding similar histories, since a genome that cannot be assembled cleanly tends to be described rather than examined.

Assembly quality is the limit on all of this. Repeat-rich genomes are hard to assemble, and a claim about absent duplication depends on seeing the whole sequence rather than the genic regions. The two Salvinia genomes are a strong demonstration that long-read assembly can settle questions that cytology could only pose. Cheap long-read sequencing has changed which questions are answerable, and this paper is an example of a question that was open for forty years and is now closed by a machine.
