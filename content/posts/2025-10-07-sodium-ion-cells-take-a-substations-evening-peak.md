description: "A utility has put a 10-megawatt-hour sodium-ion battery into a substation yard, and its performance over a summer of evening peaks is the first field data for a chemistry that has been promised as the cheap alternative to lithium."
---

Sodium-ion cells have been in laboratories for a decade and in vehicles for two years, and the argument for them is that sodium is abundant and the cells can be shipped at zero volts, which makes them safer to transport. A utility commissioned a 10-megawatt-hour container at a substation this month, and the first summer's data are a test of the argument in a place where the price of failure is an outage.

The duty cycle is a daily one. The container charges between 11:00 and 15:00, when the substation's feeders carry rooftop solar, and discharges between 18:00 and 21:00, when the feeder's load peaks at 8.2 megawatts. That is 700 full cycles a year, which is harder than most grid duties.

Round-trip efficiency is 88 per cent, against 94 for a lithium iron phosphate system, and the difference costs about 40,000 dollars a year at the local price of electricity. The chemistry's advantage is that it retains 92 per cent of capacity after 3,000 cycles, which the utility's model says is enough for a 15-year life without augmentation.

Thermal behavior is the safety claim the site cares about. The cells were driven to a 100 per cent depth of discharge 200 times, and the pack's maximum temperature rise was 12 degrees with the passive cooling the container's design allows. A lithium system of the same rating would need active cooling. Pricing follows the load, and the tariff the container displaces is the one that matters.

Cost is the reason the utility tried it. The container cost 1.4 million dollars, which is 15 per cent below the lowest bid for a lithium system in the same tender, and the tender's terms weighted lifetime cost rather than capital cost.

The constraint is energy density. The container weighs 90 tonnes against 60 for a lithium system of the same energy, and the yard had to be reinforced. A site with a smaller footprint could not use it, which is why the utility's next project is a four-hour system in a rural substation with space.

Siting decides the case. A substation serving 4,000 houses sees a peak between six and nine in the evening, when rooftop solar has stopped and demand has not, and a container parked beside it removes the need for a network upgrade that would cost 3 million and take four years to permit.

Cold weather is the technology's weakness. A sodium-ion cell loses a fifth of its capacity at minus 10 degrees, and the operator's container has a heating circuit that draws 3 per cent of its own throughput. A lithium-iron-phosphate unit would need less heating and costs 20 per cent more per kilowatt-hour.

Where a cheaper chemistry wins is the question worth asking here. On a grid duty with space and a daily cycle, the efficiency penalty is smaller than the capital saving. On a vehicle or a rooftop, the weight and volume reverse the comparison, and the chemistry's promise narrows accordingly.
