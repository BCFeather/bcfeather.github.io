description: "A trial in The New England Journal of Medicine finds that azithromycin does not reduce wheezing severity in preschoolers in the emergency department, even when it clears the bacteria."
---

Preschool children arrive in emergency departments with wheezing, and a substantial share carry bacteria that antibiotics could kill. The temptation to treat is strong, the guidelines are ambiguous, and the trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 18 May puts a number on the question. The trial was designed around that ambiguity, and its two-cohort structure is what allows the result to speak to both the child with a detectable pathogen and the child without one.

The study randomized children to azithromycin or placebo and separated them into a positive cohort, where a bacterial pathogen was detected, and a negative cohort where it was not. The primary outcome was a severity score measured over the treatment window.

Scores did not differ in either cohort. In the positive cohort the median was 9.59 with azithromycin against 9.72 with placebo, and in the negative cohort 9.30 against 9.10. The confidence intervals around those medians overlap, and the trial was sized to detect a difference that the data do not show. A severity score in a preschooler is also a coarse instrument, built from work of breathing and auscultation, and a drug that helps a narrow subgroup could be invisible in a median.

Bacterial clearance did differ, and by a lot: 58.7 per cent in the treated group against 11.4 per cent in the placebo group. The drug did what an antibiotic is supposed to do to the bacteria. The symptoms did not follow, which places the wheeze downstream of the infection or outside it altogether. Wheeze in this age group is often driven by viral infection and by airway calibre rather than by bacterial load, and clearing a bystander organism changes nothing about either.

Secondary outcomes behaved the same way, and resistance and adverse events were similar between groups. That last point matters because the argument for treating has often been framed as low cost, and a drug that provides no benefit while selecting for resistance is not low cost at all. The trial also collected resistance data, which is unusual for an acute-care study and useful for the antibiotic stewardship argument that follows a negative result.

The clinical reading is narrow and firm. A preschooler wheezing in an emergency department should not receive azithromycin for the wheeze itself, and the finding that a pathogen was detected does not change that. The broader reading is about how often a plausible mechanism survives a trial: clearing the organism is a mechanism, and the trial shows that the symptom has a different cause, which is worth remembering the next time an antibiotic looks obviously justified. Prescribing habits are the practical obstacle, because parents expect a prescription and clinicians dislike an empty consultation, and a negative trial changes practice only when it is paired with something to offer instead. That substitute is usually a clear explanation and a follow-up plan, neither of which costs anything.
