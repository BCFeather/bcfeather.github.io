A payroll report released on the Thursday before a holiday is usually a formality, and this one was not. Economists had expected about 110,000 jobs after a private survey had shown a loss of 33,000 positions two days earlier, and the bureau reported 147,000, which is the kind of surprise that moves a market in the thin trading of the third of July.

Composition was narrower than the headline. Private employers added 74,000, state and local governments added the rest on the strength of public education hiring, and manufacturing lost 7,000 for a second month. A number that is carried by the public sector is a number that says less about demand than the total implies, and a manufacturing loss in the month before the metals duties doubled is a warning that the tariff calendar has begun to reach the factory floor.

Wages were calm. Average hourly earnings rose 0.2 per cent on the month and 3.7 per cent on the year, which is a pace that sits below the 2022 peak and close to what a two per cent inflation target can absorb. The unemployment rate fell to 4.1 per cent, its lowest since February, and the broader measure that includes discouraged workers fell to 7.7 per cent.

The household survey told a different story. Employment measured there rose only 93,000, participation fell to 62.3 per cent, its lowest since late 2022, and 329,000 people left the labour force, which is how a falling unemployment rate can coexist with a slowing jobs market. Revisions to the previous two months added 16,000, which is small enough to be noise and large enough to show that the spring was better than the first prints suggested.

Markets read the report as a reason to sell bonds. The ten-year yield rose to about 4.33 per cent, and the probability that the Federal Reserve would cut at its meeting on 29 and 30 July fell from roughly a quarter to about five per cent in a single session. A stronger number is a stronger case for waiting, and a market that had been positioned for a cut spent the morning repricing the whole of the third quarter.

The Federal Reserve had spent the spring arguing that tariffs would raise prices before they slowed hiring, and a report like this one gives the committee room to keep that order of events intact. A committee that is worried about inflation and reassured about employment has no reason to move, and the political pressure for a cut does not change the arithmetic, which is why the odds of a July move collapsed rather than merely fell.

What the report settles is narrow. It says that the labour market is not collapsing in the month the tariffs took effect, and it says nothing about July, August or the autumn, when the higher duties show up in prices and in orders. A single month is a data point, and the argument about the second half is still open.
