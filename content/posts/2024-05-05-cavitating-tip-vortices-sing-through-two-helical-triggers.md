A hydrofoil tip vortex can cavitate into a slender gas tube that radiates a pure tone, and the phenomenon has been known as vortex singing since 1989. The tone is loud, narrow and tied to the operating condition, and its practical importance is that a propeller can sing across a band of speeds without anyone knowing why. The tone has been observed on propellers, on rudders and on isolated hydrofoils, which suggests that the mechanism belongs to the vortex and not to the blade.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 5 May 2024 proposes a mechanism. The authors describe the bubble dynamics with the desinent cavitation number and a viscous correction, and derive the dispersion relation of the waves that travel along the cavity interface. The correction is what keeps the wave speeds finite at the small radii that a tip vortex reaches.

Spectra from an experiment identify the modes. A wavenumber-frequency analysis of the cavity radius measured at the China Ship Scientific Research Center shows that the singing waves are the stationary double helical modes, with azimuthal wavenumbers of plus two and minus two, together with the breathing mode at zero. That rules out the earlier picture. Standing waves had been assumed, but a stationary helical mode is not one, so the trigger lies in amplification of a travelling interface wave and not in a resonance of the cavity length. Their amplitudes, not their existence, set the radiated level.

Two triggers follow from the dispersion curves. The twisted cavity, initially at rest, is set into motion by the corrected natural frequency of the twisted shape after a step change in the far-field pressure, and once moving it can excite the zero-group-velocity frequency of the breathing mode through the frequencies at which the dispersion curves intersect. A step in pressure is the manoeuvre that a propeller makes when its loading changes, and the mode that answers it is the one the experiment sees.

The intersections are what couple the modes. One crossing involves the breathing mode and the minus-two helical mode, the other the breathing mode and the plus-two mode, and each crossing gives a distinct singing condition with no fitted parameter.

Validation uses two independent facilities. The predicted conditions match the singing observed at the China Ship Scientific Research Center and at G.T.H., and the cross-spectral density shows a breathing wave travelling from downstream towards the tip, the signature the scheme predicts. Agreement at two facilities with different geometries is the strongest support the paper offers.

What the model does not attempt is the amplitude. It says when a tone appears and at what frequency it sits, and how loud the tone becomes is left to a nonlinear treatment of the interface wave. A nonlinear model would also have to explain the saturation that keeps the tone from growing without bound, which is the last part of the phenomenon to be accounted for.
