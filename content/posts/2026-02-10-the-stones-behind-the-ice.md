Curling's playing equipment is supplied by a single geological accident. Competition stones are cut from a granite from Ailsa Craig, a granite outcrop in the Firth of Clyde, because the rock has two properties no other source reproduces: a microcrystalline structure that resists water absorption, and enough toughness to survive repeated collisions without spalling at the running band.

That monopoly has been managed for decades. The quarry has been worked intermittently, most recently under a conservation agreement that permits limited extraction, and the stone is processed by one company that supplies essentially every championship. A stone set for a four-sheet club costs around 20,000 dollars, and a championship set considerably more.

Supply is finite in a literal sense. The accessible reserve on the island is estimated in decades rather than centuries, and the operators have begun blending stone from a second quarry for practice equipment, keeping the island material for competition. Nobody has announced a replacement, because the property that makes the rock good is the same property that makes it rare.

Expansion runs into that constraint. Curling federations have grown in Asia, South America and the Gulf, and each new member wants stones; the governing body has responded with a loan programme and a scheme that certifies synthetic practice stones, which behave differently at the running band and therefore teach the wrong draw weight.

Ice is the other variable. A curling sheet needs a pebbled surface, applied by spraying water through a fine nozzle and freezing the droplets, and the pebble profile depends on the water's mineral content and the hall's humidity. Championship ice is managed by a small group of specialists who travel between events, and their judgement is the reason a stone behaves consistently from one end to the other.

Technology has crept into judging. The hog line sensor, embedded in the stone's handle, detects whether the stone is released before the line, and the measurement replaced a human judgement in 2016. Its failure mode is well known: a stone whose handle is cold or whose battery is depleted can register late, and each event carries a calibration procedure and a spare set.

For an emerging programme, the barriers are cumulative. A new federation needs a hall with a reliable dehumidification system, stones it cannot buy at scale, and ice technicians it does not have. Money alone does not solve the third constraint, which is why the sport's growth has followed existing winter-sport infrastructure rather than demand.

Ice technicians are the constraint money cannot fix. A championship sheet needs a technician who can read the pebble profile by watching a stone's curl, and there are perhaps forty people worldwide who can do it. The governing body runs a certification course with a two-year apprenticeship, and the supply grows slower than the membership.

Read as a supply chain, curling is a small illustration of a general problem. When a sport's equipment depends on a single material source, expansion is capped by geology and the governing body becomes a rationing authority. The alternative, a synthetic equivalent that behaves identically, has been attempted for twenty years and not achieved.
