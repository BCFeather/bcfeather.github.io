The World Championships returned to Paris for seven days at the adidas arena, from 25 to 31 August, with 64 entries in the men's singles drawn from 35 countries and 64 in the women's singles from 34. The draw sizes are the same as in an Olympic year, and the field is the deepest the sport assembles outside the Games, and every federation sends its full quota of entries. The arena holds a crowd the sport rarely draws outside an Olympic tournament, and the seven days were sold as one event and not as separate sessions.

Men's singles final went to Shi Yuqi, the world number one, who lost the opening game to Kunlavut Vitidsarn and then took the next two, 19-21, 21-10, 21-18. At 29 he is older than most first-time champions in the event, and the win is his first world title after a career that has included a team gold and a long stretch of injuries. The first game went to the Thai player, the second to Shi by eleven points, and the third stayed close until the last few rallies.

The women's singles final was shorter and more one-sided. Akane Yamaguchi beat Chen Yu Fei 21-9, 21-13 for her third world title, and the match turned early: Chen, carrying an injury, could not sustain the rallies that her game depends on, and Yamaguchi attacked the space that opened as a result. The scoreline reflects the injury and not the ranking, since a fit Chen would have extended the rallies that decided the match.

China finished with two golds and three silvers. Liu Shengshu and Tan Ning, both in their early twenties, won the women's doubles, and the other finals went to opponents, which is a return that matches the country's recent record at the event and not the dominance of the previous decade. The women's doubles pair is young enough to be the base of the next cycle, which matters more than a count of medals in Paris.

Margins in the men's final are the part worth reading closely. A 21-10 second game and a 21-18 third suggest that Shi adjusted after the first game, and the drop in Kunlavut's scoring rate came as the rallies lengthened, which is the pattern of a player who solves a problem during a match instead of before it. Conditioning is the plausible explanation, since a 29-year-old who wins a third game after losing the first has paced the match instead of out-hitting his opponent.

What the week says about the season is limited. Form at a world championship is a single sample, and the next Olympic cycle will be decided by ranking points accumulated over two years. The two golds give China a base, and the three silvers show where the base has gaps. The ranking points from Paris count towards qualification, and the calendar between now and the next Games is what will decide the seedings.
