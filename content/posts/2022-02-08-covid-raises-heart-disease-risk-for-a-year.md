title: "Surviving COVID leaves a year of elevated heart risk"

description: "Among 153,760 veterans who survived COVID, the risk of new cardiovascular disease stayed elevated beyond the first 30 days, from arrhythmia and heart failure to stroke and thromboembolism, and rose with the severity of the acute illness."
---

The acute phase of COVID-19 is a vascular disease as much as a respiratory one, with clots, arrhythmias and myocardial injury appearing in patients whose lungs are only moderately affected. Whether that risk recedes when the infection clears has been an open question since the first wave. Clinicians noticed the pattern early, in patients whose oxygen saturation was normal and whose electrocardiograms were not. A cohort of this size can detect small relative risks, and the question is whether they are causal.

A cohort built from national databases of the US Department of Veterans Affairs followed 153,760 people with COVID-19 and compared them with 5,637,647 contemporary controls and 5,859,411 historical controls. The analysis estimated the risk and the one-year burden of prespecified cardiovascular outcomes beyond the first 30 days. It appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 7 February 2022.

Elevated risk covered cerebrovascular disorders, dysrhythmias, ischaemic and non-ischaemic heart disease, pericarditis, myocarditis, heart failure and thromboembolic disease. The burden was graded by the care setting of the acute illness, so people who had been managed at home carried a lower but still measurable excess risk, and those admitted to intensive care carried the most. Each outcome was prespecified, which matters because a database that size will produce a significant association for almost any diagnosis if the analyst is allowed to choose after the fact.

Controlling for a historical cohort is what separates this study from a simple before-and-after comparison. The historical controls account for the background trend in cardiovascular disease, and the contemporary controls account for the changes in care that occurred during the pandemic, so the difference is attributed to the infection rather than to the period. The historical cohort covers the same hospitals before 2020, so the comparison is between patients who were similar except for the pandemic.

The cohort skews older and male and largely White, because it is drawn from veterans, and its members had a high burden of risk factors before infection. A younger and healthier population would show a smaller absolute excess, though the relative pattern might be similar. A younger cohort with fewer baseline risk factors would give a cleaner estimate of the infection's own contribution.

Surveillance also matters. People who have had COVID may see a doctor more often in the following year, and more contact with the health system finds more arrhythmia and more heart failure, a bias that the authors acknowledge and cannot fully remove. This surveillance bias would inflate the early months most, when contact with services is highest. Emergency visits and hospital admissions are recorded, while an arrhythmia detected by a wearable and never reported is not.

For health services the implication is that recovery does not end at discharge. A patient who has been hospitalized with COVID belongs in a follow-up pathway that checks blood pressure, rhythm and renal function, and the guideline committees that wrote post-COVID care plans had little quantitative evidence to work with until this study arrived. The size of the excess risk per patient is small and the number of patients is enormous, so the population-level burden is substantial even where the individual hazard is modest.
