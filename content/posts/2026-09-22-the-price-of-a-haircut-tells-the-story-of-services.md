The official price data published this month continues a pattern that has held for the better part of two years. Goods prices are close to flat, and in several categories falling: electronics, clothing, and the durable household goods that arrive in containers. Services prices are rising at a rate that would have been called alarming in the previous decade. The gap between the two is the most important number in the economy, and it is best understood by looking at something small.

A haircut is a service with almost no imported content. The scissors may be imported, the chair may be imported, but the cost of a haircut is dominated by the barber's time and the rent of the shop. When wages rise, the price rises. When rents rise, the price rises. When either falls, the price falls slowly, because a shop that has signed a five-year lease and trained a staff does not cut its prices to win a price war it cannot see. The haircut is therefore a cleaner measure of domestic cost pressure than almost anything in the basket.

Across the service sector the same logic applies. A restaurant meal, a plumber's visit, a dental appointment, a school fee: each is mostly labour and premises, and each adjusts upward with a lag and downward almost never. This is why services inflation is described as sticky, and why central banks have found it so much harder to bring down than goods inflation. Goods can be sourced from somewhere cheaper. A haircut cannot.

In some places the mechanism now works the other way. Wages in the service sector have been rising faster than productivity for several years, and the gap has to close either through higher prices or through lower employment. In economies where the labour market has loosened, the second channel has begun to operate, and the service price data has started to soften at the margin. In economies where it has not, the first channel continues, and the price of a trim in a city centre has become a small symbol of a large problem.

Worth spelling out is the distributional consequence. Households that spend a larger share of their income on services, which is to say households that rent, eat out, use childcare and pay for medical care, experience inflation at the services rate. Households that own their homes and buy mostly goods experience something close to zero. An average inflation figure that blends the two describes neither, which is one reason the public argument about prices and the official statistics seem to be conducted in different languages.

There is a longer-run question underneath the cyclical one. Productivity in services has grown slowly for decades, and the sectors where it has grown quickly are the ones that can be automated or offshored. A haircut cannot be offshored and has so far resisted automation, which means its relative price will keep rising as the rest of the economy gets more efficient. Economists call this the cost disease, and it is not a disease so much as an arithmetic consequence. The barber is not overpaid. The goods he uses have simply become cheaper than his time.
