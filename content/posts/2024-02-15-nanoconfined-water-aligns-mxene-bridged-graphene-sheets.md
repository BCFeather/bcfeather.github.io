Two-dimensional materials keep promising more than they deliver, because the promise lives in a single flake and the deliverable is a sheet. Graphene conducts and bears load along its plane. The MXenes, the carbide and nitride layers that sit beside it, add surface chemistry that can be tuned. Assembling either into a free-standing film has meant heat, pressure or a polymer binder, and every one of those steps costs some of the property that made the flake worth handling.

Cheng Qunfeng at Beihang University and a group spanning the Chinese Academy of Sciences and the University of Texas at Dallas reported a room-temperature answer in *Science* on 15 February 2024. The route is water, held in a layer a nanometre thick between the platelets, and the alignment comes from the drying rather than from a press.

What the confined water does is give the flakes room to slide. As the film dries slowly, the nanoplatelets rotate into parallel order before the bridges set, and two kinds of bridge form at once. Covalent bonds tie MXene platelets to graphene edges, and π-π stacking holds the basal planes in register. The sheet comes out isotropic in plane, with no direction along which the flakes lie differently.

The numbers are mechanical and electrical together. In-plane tensile strength reaches 1.87 gigapascals and modulus 98.7 gigapascals, which places a water-processed film in the range of engineering composites. In-plane conductivity at room temperature reaches 1,423 siemens per centimetre, and the volumetric specific capacity reaches 828 coulombs per cubic centimetre, the figure an electrode needs if it is to store charge in a small volume.

The film also keeps its conductivity through bending cycles, which is the test a flexible electrode has to pass before anyone talks about a device. Isotropy matters more than the peak values. Layered assemblies usually come out strong along the flake and weak across it, and a device that bends in service will find the weak direction first. A sheet that behaves the same in every in-plane direction removes that failure mode, and it does so without hot pressing, which would damage the MXene surface chemistry that the conductivity depends on.

Earlier routes to the same end pressed the film at temperature or soaked it in polymer, and both leave the flakes with a preferred direction. Water does the aligning while the sheet is still soft, which is why the isotropy survives drying.

What the film lacks is a cheap precursor route, since MXene synthesis still starts from a hydrofluoric-acid etch, a step no factory will enjoy. The authors present the water route as a general method for other nanoplatelets, and the aerospace case is plain. Composites have been the route to lighter airframes, and a film that combines strength with conductivity opens the possibility of a structural panel that also carries current or stores charge. Whether the process survives a scale-up beyond a laboratory dish is the question the paper leaves to the next step.
