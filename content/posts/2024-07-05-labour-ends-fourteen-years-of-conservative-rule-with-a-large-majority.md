Labour won a large parliamentary majority in the general election of 4 July, taking about 410 of the 650 seats, and the Conservative party that had governed since 2010 was reduced to little more than a hundred. The result was expected from the polls and the scale of it was not. A party that had lost four elections in a row won the fifth by moving its programme towards the centre. A fifth election after four defeats is an election that a party wins by persuading the middle rather than by mobilizing its base.

Arithmetic is the story. Labour's share of the vote was about thirty-four per cent, and the electoral system turned that into a majority of more than 170 seats, because the opposing vote was split between two parties on the right. A system that rewards concentration is a system in which a modest lead becomes a large majority. A vote that is split on the right is a vote that produces a government on the left, and the new party's seats were few.

Conservative collapse was the other half. The party lost seats to the Liberal Democrats in the south and to a new right-wing party in the north, and a governing party that loses votes at both ends of the spectrum loses the coalition it had assembled. A coalition that was built on leaving the European Union is a coalition that has no further purpose. A governing party that governs for fourteen years accumulates a record that the opposition can attack line by line.

Campaign was economical. The incoming government promised to grow the economy, to reform planning rules and to invest in the health service without raising the main taxes, and the promises were costed against forecasts that assume growth. A programme that depends on growth is a programme that a chancellor inherits as a risk. A forecast that assumes growth of two per cent is a forecast that the first fiscal event will test.

Turnout was low. About six in ten registered voters cast a ballot, and the fall was sharpest among younger voters, who were the group most likely to support the outgoing government's opponents. A low turnout is a mandate that the winner will be reminded of. A young voter who stays home is a voter who does not appear in the mandate that the government claims. A turnout of six in ten is a turnout that a party of the young has to reverse before the next election.

What the election establishes is that the governing party changed after a period in which the country's problems did not. A new government with a large majority and a thin fiscal margin has the power to act and little money to spend, and the first budget will show which of the two it prioritizes. A budget in the autumn will set the tone, and the tone is what the first hundred days are remembered for.
