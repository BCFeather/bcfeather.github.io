---

The route of next year's grand tour was published this week, and the shape of it is now familiar: fewer passes, more summit finishes, and stages in the high mountains that are shorter than the ones ridden twenty years ago. A mountain stage that once ran two hundred kilometres with three cols now runs a hundred and thirty with two, and the second is often a climb of ten or twelve kilometres at eight or nine per cent rather than twenty kilometres at six.

The physiology explains part of it. A long stage at a moderate pace burns carbohydrate at a rate the body can sustain, and the decisive attacks come late, when glycogen is low and the strongest riders can still accelerate. A short stage ridden hard from the start depletes glycogen earlier and shifts the contest towards riders with a high threshold and fast recovery. Shorter, steeper climbs also favour a different rider: the power-to-weight specialist rather than the diesel engine who can hold a steady tempo for forty minutes. Route design is therefore a way of choosing which kind of champion the race produces, and the organizers know it.

The commercial explanation is just as strong. Television pays for the race, and a three-hour broadcast with a long flat approach and one decisive climb is harder to sell than a two-hour broadcast that is competitive from the first kilometre. Shorter stages also fit the way people watch: a summit finish at four in the afternoon is a better television product than one at six. The organizers do not say this in the press release, and it is visible in the route.

The logistical constraint is the one that decides the details. A mountain pass has to be closed to traffic, which means negotiating with the local authorities, paying for the closure, and finding a road wide enough for the caravan of vehicles that follows the race. Shorter stages reduce the number of kilometres closed and the number of passes that need agreements, and they let the race finish higher up, at a ski station with a car park and a cable car, rather than at a town in the valley.

The counter-argument comes from the riders. A shorter stage ridden at full intensity is not easier; it is more intense, and the cumulative load over three weeks is similar. Several teams have complained that the modern route produces more crashes in the first hour, because the whole peloton starts at threshold, and that the racing is more controlled rather than less, since the teams can calculate the whole stage from the first climb.

What has not changed is the uncertainty. The organizers can design a route that favours a type of rider, and they cannot design the weather, the crashes or the form. The last decade of grand tours has been won by riders of every description, on routes that were supposed to exclude them, which is a reminder that a route is a hypothesis rather than a result. The mountain stage is shorter now. Whether it is better is a question the riders answer every July.
