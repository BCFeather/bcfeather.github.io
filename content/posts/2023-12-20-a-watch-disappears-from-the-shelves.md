Apple announced on 18 December that it would stop selling two of its watches in the United States, days before the busiest week of the retail year, because of a patent dispute it had lost in October. The models affected were the Series 9 and the Ultra 2, the only two with the blood oxygen sensor that a tribunal had found infringed patents held by Masimo, a medical device company in California. Online sales stopped on 21 December and sales in stores on 24 December, and the import ban itself took effect on 26 December.

The case has a long file. Masimo sued in 2020, alleging that Apple had hired its engineers and copied its technology for measuring oxygen in the blood with light, and the trade commission found in favour of the smaller company in January 2023, in a decision that was confirmed in October. The remedy was an exclusion order on imports of the infringing models, which is the most serious sanction available in a patent case at the border, and Apple's attempts to work around it with a software update were rejected.

Apple's options were narrower than its public statements suggested. The company could remove the sensor, which would make the watch a lesser product at the same price, or it could settle, which would mean paying a company it has spent four years describing as a patent troll. It chose to keep selling until the order took hold, then paused sales rather than imports, and filed an appeal with the federal circuit. A court paused the ban on 27 December, and the watches went back on sale online within hours.

Masimo's side of the story is about scale. The company employs about 5,000 people, sells monitors to hospitals and had a market value of roughly seven billion dollars before the dispute, against Apple's three trillion. Its chief executive told reporters that the case concerned a principle rather than money: that a larger company cannot take a smaller one's invention and put it on a wrist. Its shares rose by about five per cent when the ban took effect, which is the market's way of counting the value of the injunction.

The timing cost Apple more than the models. The two watches represent a small share of the company's revenue, but they were the centrepiece of the holiday advertising, and the company had to tell customers in the last week of December that its newest product could not be bought. The wearables division had already been the slowest-growing part of the business, and the dispute gave the competition a talking point about the health features that had been the category's main selling argument.

Behind the argument about patents is an argument about what a watch is. Apple has spent a decade turning a consumer device into a regulated medical instrument, adding an electrocardiogram, an irregular rhythm notification, a fall detector and a blood oxygen reading, each of which required clearance from the food and drug administration and each of which borrowed technology that other companies had patented. The lesson of December is that a device which borrows from medicine will eventually be judged by the rules of medicine, in a court that does not care how many people bought the last version.
