The quarter that ended on 27 April contained a policy event that no forecast had. On 9 April NVIDIA was told that exports of its H20 accelerator to China, including Hong Kong and Macau, would need a licence, and on 14 April the company learned that the requirement had no stated end. A product line that had been designed to comply with the previous rules was removed from the market between two earnings calls, and the customers who had ordered it were told to expect nothing.

Revenue still rose. Total revenue was 44.06 billion dollars, up 12 per cent on the previous quarter and 69 per cent on the year, and data centre revenue was 39.1 billion, up 73 per cent. Networking revenue rose 64 per cent on the quarter to 5 billion as customers connected racks of Blackwell systems, which is the number that shows a platform being deployed and not sampled.

Charge was 4.5 billion dollars, taken for inventory and purchase commitments that could not be used. H20 sales had been 4.6 billion dollars in the quarter before the licence took effect, and a further 2.5 billion dollars of orders could not be shipped. The company's GAAP gross margin fell to 60.5 per cent from 78.4 per cent a year earlier, and would have been 71.3 per cent without the charge.

Net income rose 26 per cent to 18.78 billion dollars, and diluted earnings per share were 0.76 dollars on a GAAP basis and 0.81 on an adjusted one. A company that loses a market worth several billion a quarter and still grows net income by a quarter is a company whose other markets are larger than the one it lost, and the arithmetic of that sentence is the whole investment case for a company that is valued on the assumption that demand is not the constraint.

The guidance is where the policy shows up again. NVIDIA said second-quarter revenue would be about 45 billion dollars, plus or minus 2 per cent, and that the figure already reflected an expected loss of about 8 billion dollars of H20 revenue. Gross margin guidance of 71.8 per cent assumed Blackwell profitability improving through the year, which is a statement about manufacturing yield as much as about pricing.

Whether the Chinese market is replaceable is the strategic question. NVIDIA's argument has been that export controls accelerate Chinese domestic chip development, and that a market denied today is a competitor created tomorrow. The charge is the accounting version of that argument, and the 8 billion dollar guidance is the version that repeats every quarter the rules stay.

What would settle it is whether Blackwell demand from American and European cloud builders absorbs the lost volume, and whether a licensed H20 variant returns. The quarter says the first is happening, and the second is a decision in Washington and not in Santa Clara.
