title: "Gazprom cuts off Bulgaria and Poland over rouble payments"
date: "2022-04-27T23:45:00+08:00"
categories: [news]
description: "The two countries refused to pay under a March decree that required buyers to route money through a Russian bank and convert it into roubles, and Europe's gas price jumped by a fifth on the news."
---

Gazprom stopped supplying Bulgaria and Poland on 27 April, saying the two buyers had failed to pay under the terms of a presidential decree issued at the end of March. Both governments confirmed that deliveries had ceased, and both had said in advance that they would not comply with a scheme that would have required them to hold accounts at Gazprombank and to accept payment converted into roubles. Bulgaria's government called the suspension a breach of contract, and Poland's climate minister said the country had prepared for it.

The decree, signed on 31 March, created a mechanism in which buyers pay in their own currency into a special account at Gazprombank, which then converts the money into roubles and settles the contract. Moscow presented the arrangement as a response to sanctions that had frozen its foreign reserves, and European capitals read it as an attempt to undermine the payment terms written into long-term contracts, which specify currency and bank. European buyers had asked whether paying in euros into a Gazprombank account would breach sanctions, and the answer depended on whom they asked.

Bulgaria and Poland were the first to be cut off, and neither depended on Russian gas as heavily as Germany or Italy. Poland had spent years building a liquefied gas terminal at Swinoujscie and had lobbied against Nord Stream 2, and its storage was fuller than the European average. Bulgaria was more exposed, importing nearly all of its gas from Russia, and its government said it had arranged alternative supplies from Greece and from Azerbaijan. Bulgaria's long-term contract with Gazprom expired at the end of the year, and its government said it would not be renewed on these terms.

Prices moved within hours. The Dutch benchmark contract rose as much as 21 per cent and traded above 1,370 dollars per thousand cubic metres, and European Union officials accused Russia of using energy as a weapon. The commission's energy commissioner said the cutoffs were a breach of contract without justification, and the union began preparing guidance on how companies could pay without violating sanctions, a question that divided member states. Traders also pointed to lower flows through Ukraine, which had already reduced the volumes available to western Europe.

The wider significance lay in what it revealed about the remaining buyers. Several European utilities had already opened the accounts that Moscow demanded, arguing that the mechanism left the contractual currency unchanged from their side, and others were waiting to see whether the cutoffs would be extended. Germany, which had resisted an embargo, faced a choice between compliance and a loss of supply that its industry could not absorb quickly. Poland's link to Norwegian gas through Denmark was due to open in the autumn, which gave Warsaw a timetable for ending the arrangement.

Russia's leverage, though, ran in both directions. The share of its gas exports going to Europe was falling as buyers found other suppliers, and the revenue lost from two mid-sized customers was small against the political signal. What April demonstrated is that a payment dispute can turn a commercial contract into an instrument of coercion, and that both sides were willing to use it.
