A Long March 5B carrying a Yuanzheng-2 upper stage lifted off from the Wenchang space launch site in Hainan at 14:43 on 13 August and placed the eighth group of low-orbit internet satellites into their planned orbits. The launch was declared a success, and the mission was counted as the 588th flight of the Long March family.

The satellites belong to a constellation that is being assembled in batches, a pattern that has become familiar in the past two years. The low-orbit internet projects are designed to provide broadband from satellites a few hundred kilometres above the ground, where the round trip for a radio signal is short enough to support interactive traffic. The trade-off is that each satellite covers a small area, so the service only works once hundreds of them are in the sky at the same altitude and in coordinated planes. The satellites also have to hand traffic from one to the next as they move, which is a scheduling problem as much as a radio one.

Long March 5B is the heavy-lift member of the family, with four boosters around a core stage, and it is used when a batch is heavy enough that a smaller rocket would need several flights. The Yuanzheng-2 upper stage then does the distribution work: after the main stages have done their job, it relights to place the satellites into their individual slots rather than dropping them all at one point.

Wenchang, on the north-east coast of Hainan, is the site for the largest vehicles because their cores can be brought in by sea rather than by road. The site has been the departure point for the station modules, for the lunar and Mars missions, and now for a steady run of commercial constellation batches. Two pads serve the heavy vehicles, and the coastal humidity is a constant argument for the air-conditioning in the payload fairing.

The constellation build-out is not only a launch problem. Ground terminals, spectrum coordination and the manufacturing of user equipment all have to keep pace, and the satellites themselves have to be designed for a five-year life in an environment that includes atomic oxygen and frequent eclipses. Launch cadence is the visible part of the programme; the invisible part is the production line behind it. A batch of satellites is now assembled on a line with stations for integration and testing, in the way that cars are, and the schedule of launches is set by how fast that line can deliver.

August has been a busy month for Chinese launches. A Long March 4C put a space-environment test satellite into orbit from Xichang on 17 August, and a Long March 8A flew from the Hainan commercial spaceport on 26 August. The three missions used three different vehicles from three different sites, which is the point of a programme that now measures itself in flights per month rather than in milestones.
