description: "The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to between zero and 0.1 per cent on 19 March, ending the negative rate it had maintained since 2016 and becoming the last major central bank to begin tightening."
---

Bank of Japan raised its policy rate on the nineteenth of March to a range of zero to 0.1 per cent, ending eight years in which it had charged commercial banks for holding deposits at the central bank. Decision was taken by the policy board with a majority vote, and it was the first increase since 2007. Bank also abandoned its yield curve control programme, under which it had bought unlimited government bonds to keep long-term rates below a target, and said it would no longer intervene to cap them.

Move had been signalled for weeks and was still a change of regime. Governor Kazuo Ueda took office in April 2023 with a mandate to normalize policy cautiously, and he had said repeatedly that he wanted to see sustained wage growth before acting. Annual wage negotiations in the spring produced the largest increases in more than thirty years, with the main union federation reporting an average rise of 5.2 per cent, and that gave the board the evidence it said it needed.

Inflation had been above the two per cent target for almost two years. Consumer prices rose by 2.8 per cent in February, and the core measure that excludes fresh food was similar. Much of the increase came from imports and from energy, and the bank argued that the underlying trend was now being driven by domestic demand and by wages rather than by external prices. Growth had been weak, however, and the economy fell into recession in the second half of 2023.

Yen reacted by strengthening and then giving back the gain. Currency had fallen to about 150 to the dollar before the decision, and it rose to about 147 within hours as traders bet that other central banks would cut rates before the Bank of Japan raised them again. That divergence is the reason the currency has been weak for two years, and it remained the main uncertainty for Japanese exporters and for the bank's own projections.

Consequence for Japanese households is mixed. Savers will earn a small amount on deposits for the first time in years, and borrowers with variable-rate mortgages will pay more. Banks have been slow to pass on the increase, and the net effect on household budgets in the first year is likely to be small. The larger effect is symbolic: a generation of investors has been trained to expect zero rates, and the end of that expectation changes how they price assets.

What the nineteenth of March means is the closing of an era rather than the start of a tightening cycle. Japan was the last major economy still running the emergency policies of the 2010s, and its exit leaves none. The bank has said it will move slowly and that it does not intend to raise rates to levels that would be normal elsewhere, since the country's debt is the largest in the developed world relative to its economy. What changed was the direction. What remains uncertain is whether the economy can grow with positive rates, and whether the wage growth that justified the decision continues.
