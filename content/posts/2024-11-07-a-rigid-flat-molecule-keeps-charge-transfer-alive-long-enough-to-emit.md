Thermally activated delayed fluorescence made organic light-emitting diodes efficient by harvesting triplet excitons, and it did so with molecules that are deliberately twisted. A paper in *Nature Communications* on 7 November 2024 reaches the same end with molecules that are rigid and flat. Delayed fluorescence harvests triplets that would otherwise be lost, and every efficient organic emitter has to solve that problem in one geometry or another.

The twist is what separates the donor from the acceptor, and separation is what makes the charge-transfer state long-lived. The cost is that a twisted molecule is a fragile one, and device lifetimes have suffered for a decade. Separation of the excited state is the physical requirement, and a twisted donor and acceptor is only one of the arrangements that can supply it.

Rigid planar conjugated molecules are stable, and planarity is what impedes charge transfer, because a delocalized excited state is not a separated one. The problem is to obtain separation without twist. Planarity delocalizes the excitation, which is convenient for absorption and inconvenient for separation, and the rigid frame reconciles the two demands. A film is an ensemble, so the property a device exploits is an average over conformations rather than a property of one molecule.

Dispersion-free intramolecular charge transfer is what the authors report. The donor and acceptor are held in a rigid frame that prevents conformational dispersion, so every molecule in a film behaves in the same way. Conformational dispersion is a property of a film, so a molecule that is rigid at the single-molecule level makes the film behave as an ensemble.

Uniformity is what a device needs. A film in which some molecules emit and others do not wastes current, and a rigid frame removes the conformational spread that produces such mixtures. External quantum efficiency and lifetime are the two numbers a display manufacturer reads first, and a paper reporting both is addressed to that industry. Lifetime is the number that decides whether a material reaches a panel, and a stable frame is what a panel needs above all.

The photophysics and the device performance are reported together, and the second is what makes the first matter, since a stable emitter with poor external quantum efficiency is not a solution. Transferability is what distinguishes a design rule from a single good molecule, and the authors state the rule in terms a synthetic chemist can apply.

On the modelling side, the design rule is the transferable part. If rigidity and charge separation can be had together, then the trade-off that defined the field is not a trade-off but an artefact of the geometries that were tried. Colour tuning remains open, since the gap that sets the emission wavelength also sets the barrier that the charge transfer has to cross. A rule stated in synthetic terms is a rule a chemist can test, which is what makes the paper useful beyond its own molecule.
