Shares in Meta, the company that owns Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp, closed 26 per cent lower on 3 February, and about 232 billion dollars of market value disappeared in a single session. The fall began in the half hour after the earnings call on the previous evening and continued through the next day, from 323 dollars to 237.76 dollars, and it set a record for the largest one-day loss of value by an American company. The Nasdaq composite index fell 3.7 per cent the same day, and the other technology stocks fell with it.

Behind the drop was a set of numbers that investors had not expected from a business of this size. Net income fell 8 per cent in the fourth quarter to 10.3 billion dollars, the first decline since 2019, and earnings per share came in at 3.67 dollars against a forecast of about 3.85. Daily active users fell by about half a million to 1.929 billion, the first quarterly decline in the company's history, and the guidance for the first three months of 2022 put revenue at 27 to 29 billion dollars, which would be the slowest growth since the company listed.

Two arguments explain the shortfall, according to the executives. The privacy rules that Apple introduced on its phones in 2021 made it harder to follow users across applications and therefore harder to sell targeted advertising, and the finance chief put the cost of those changes at about 10 billion dollars for the year. Advertisers were also cutting budgets because of inflation and the disruption to supply chains, and the growth of TikTok had taken attention from the younger part of the audience.

Spending on the metaverse complicates the account further. The division that builds the virtual world lost 10 billion dollars in 2021, and the company has said the losses will grow. Mark Zuckerberg told analysts that the investment was the price of being early, and the market answered by valuing the advertising business as though it had stopped growing. His personal holding fell by about 29 billion dollars on the day, and he dropped out of the list of the ten richest people.

Nothing about the quarter was catastrophic in absolute terms. Revenue rose 20 per cent to 33.67 billion dollars, the year closed with sales of nearly 118 billion, and the company still made more than 10 billion dollars of profit in three months. The reaction measured something else, which is the change in expectation: a business that had grown at a fifth a year for a decade told its owners that the next quarter might grow at 3 per cent.

Other platforms have been through the same arithmetic since. Growth in users reaches a ceiling in every market that matters, regulation raises the cost of the data that made the advertising work, and a competitor with a better feed takes the time that the users no longer give. Meta's answer is to spend its way into a new medium, and the shares will be cheap or expensive depending on whether the next ten years look like the last ten.
