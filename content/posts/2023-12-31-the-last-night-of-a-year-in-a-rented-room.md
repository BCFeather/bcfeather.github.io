On the last night of the year the flat was warm and the window was open a hand's width, which is how the room is kept in winter when the radiator is on and the air must move. Fireworks started at eleven from the sports ground a few hundred metres away, and the flashes came through the gap in the curtains in colours that changed every few seconds. My mother had made dumplings in the afternoon and left them covered on the table, and my father had arrived on the afternoon bus with a bag of oranges from the market at home.

December had been the month of marks. The internal examinations came in the middle of it, four papers in three days, and the results were posted on the board by the end of the week. I had moved up eleven places from where the November test had put me, which sounds better than it is, because the November test had been a bad day and the December one had been an ordinary one. In the new class the teachers were different, the pace was different, and the first two weeks felt like learning to walk on a floor that had been raised slightly.

The year also ended with a question that had been answered in January and would be asked again in June. My class had been assembled in September and dissolved in January, and the classmates I had learned the corridors with were spread across three streams by February. Two of them wrote to me about the examinations in the key class, which were harder than the papers we sat, and I read those messages with the mixture that anyone would recognize: curiosity, a little envy, and the practical note that their notes were better than mine and could be borrowed.

Nothing about the evening was unusual, which is why it is worth writing down. The television in the corner showed a countdown from a stage in a city I have never visited, the neighbours in the next flat argued about whether to leave the door open for luck, and at some point the fireworks became loud enough that talking required raising the voice. Somebody upstairs sang the first line of a song badly and the whole building laughed, and the laugh was the loudest sound before midnight.

At twelve the noise peaked and then fell away within ten minutes, which is what happens in a town this size. The smoke drifted over the road and the lamps lit it from below, and the street that had been empty at half past eleven filled with families walking home in coats over pyjamas. My father said the same sentence he says every year about the fireworks being better when he was young, and my mother said the same sentence she says every year about the money they cost, and the two sentences together are the closest thing this family has to a ritual.

Three years remain of the school and two and a half of them were still ahead at midnight. The league table that had placed me in an ordinary class in January had not changed, and the work that would change it had not started. What the year left behind was smaller and more useful: a room five minutes from a gate, a bicycle with a broken bell, four subjects I had chosen and one examination I had failed, and a family that had moved across the city so that all of it could happen. At half past twelve the window was closed, the fireworks were over, and the year was simply gone.
