Rain falling on a city with no drainage finds the weakest ground and cuts a channel. In the cities of the Congo basin, that channel becomes a gully: a ravine tens of metres wide and deep, advancing uphill through streets and houses as each storm lengthens it. The process is familiar to the people who live beside it, and it has been almost absent from the statistics that governments use to plan. Planning around it has been difficult because a ravine grows during a storm, and the record of where it went is held by the households that moved.

A paper in *Nature* on 27 August 2025 builds those statistics. Its authors mapped 2,922 gullies across 26 cities, using satellite imagery to trace each ravine and to measure how far it advanced between observations. They then combined the growth with settlement maps to count the people whose homes lay in the path. Each ravine was traced in imagery taken at two or more dates, so growth was measured, not inferred from a single snapshot.

The displacement estimate is the headline. Between 2004 and 2023, about 118,600 people were displaced by gully expansion, with an uncertainty of plus or minus 44,400. The annual rate was around 4,650 people per year before 2020 and about 12,200 per year afterwards, an increase that coincides with heavier rainfall and with faster building on the slopes that feed the channels. The uncertainty is dominated by the difficulty of counting a household that moved to another plot inside the same district.

Exposure is the forward-looking number. The population living in areas where gullies could plausibly expand rose from 1.6 million, plus or minus 0.6, in 2010 to 3.2 million, plus or minus 1.3, in 2023. Most of that growth is in unplanned districts, where a household that loses its plot has no title to fall back on and rebuilds on the next vulnerable slope. Heavier rain is part of the explanation and construction is the other, since a new road concentrates runoff into the same channels the gullies follow.

Attribution is where the paper is careful. It reports the correlation between the post-2020 acceleration and rainfall and not a causal test, and it treats urban growth as a second driver, since a paved surface concentrates runoff and a new settlement puts people where the water arrives. That distinction matters for policy, because rainfall cannot be managed locally while drainage and settlement can. The paper states it plainly and leaves causal attribution to a study designed for it.

The limits are those of remote sensing. A gully narrower than the imagery can resolve may be missed, a house that was abandoned looks the same as one that was destroyed, and a displaced household that moved fifty metres stays invisible to the method. City boundaries also cut the survey, so a ravine that crosses into the next municipality is counted once. A gully stabilized with concrete and vegetation looks different from one that is still growing, and imagery separates them only if the dates are close.

What would help is a monitoring service instead of another retrospective count: repeat imagery every season, published as a map a city engineer can use, with the growth rate attached. That is cheaper than the displacement it would prevent. A map is also the only form in which the problem reaches a budget, since a ravine that is not on a plan is not in a line item. That is the authors' case for monitoring, and it is an argument about institutions, not about geology.
