Quasicrystals with eight-fold symmetry are easy to draw in two dimensions: the Ammann-Beenker tiling does it with squares and 45-degree rhombi, and the result has no translational period at all. Lifting that pattern into three dimensions is the harder problem, because the two-dimensional tiling has to be stacked so that local order stays coherent along the third axis. The octagonal case sits between the five-fold Penrose pattern and the twelve-fold square-triangle pattern, and its two-dimensional version has served as the standard testing ground for eight-fold order.

Kowaguchi, Mehta, Doye and Noya construct an ideal three-dimensional octagonal quasicrystal from the Ammann-Beenker tiling and then ask what a particle would have to look like to stabilize it. The answer comes from the local environments. Coordinations around each site, counted and sorted, tell a designer how many neighbours a patchy particle should want. That inversion is the useful part: instead of asking whether a given particle assembles a quasicrystal, the paper reads the tiling and asks what particle the tiling demands.

Their first design is a binary mixture: particles with eight patches, and particles with five. Simulated, the mixture assembles into a three-dimensional octagonal quasicrystal, which is the outcome the ideal structure predicted. The patches stand in for the bonds of the tiling, so the number of patches is the coordination number the structure wants.

The simulation also departs from the ideal. The assembled structure shows a narrower distribution of coordination numbers than the ideal tiling does, a small difference in local statistics that separates a real quasicrystal from a drawn one. A narrower spread means fewer distinct environments to satisfy, which is already a hint that one component might do.

Then the eight-patch component turns out to be unnecessary. A one-component system of the five-patch particles assembles into an essentially identical octagonal quasicrystal, so the binary mixture was carrying a component that growth did not need. That reads as a lesson in design as much as a result. What remains is the local rule, not the mixture.

The eight-patch particles do something else alone. A one-component system of them assembles into a cluster of crystalline domains, and the boundaries between domains are coherent enough that the cluster shows approximate eight-fold order without being a quasicrystal. Coherent boundaries let the domains share an orientation, and diffraction from such a cluster would show broad eight-fold peaks instead of the sharp spots of a true quasicrystal.

Realization is the point of the exercise, and the result says something about how much freedom a designer has. Patchy particles of this kind are what DNA origami or protein design can now build, and five patches are a simpler target than eight, and a single species is easier to make than two that have to be tuned against each other. The preprint was posted to arXiv on 9 August 2024 and has since appeared in *J. Chem. Phys.*
