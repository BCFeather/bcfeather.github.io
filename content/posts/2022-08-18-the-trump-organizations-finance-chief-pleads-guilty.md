Allen Weisselberg, who has been the finance chief of the Trump Organization for decades, pleaded guilty on Thursday to fifteen counts of tax fraud and agreed to testify against the company. He admitted that he had received about one point seven million dollars in benefits that never appeared on a tax return, including the rent on an apartment, the lease of two cars and the school fees of his grandchildren. The plea was entered in a state court in Manhattan, and the judge said he would serve five months in jail and pay about two million dollars in taxes and penalties. The sentence will be imposed after the trial of the company, which is due to begin in the autumn.

The case is about the way the company paid its executives rather than about the size of the payments. The benefits were recorded as expenses of the business rather than as salary, which meant that the company did not pay the payroll tax and the executive did not pay the income tax. The prosecutors said the practice went back fifteen years and involved other executives who have not been charged. The defence said the arrangement had been set up by the accountants and that the finance chief had relied on their advice.

The guilty plea changes the shape of the trial. The company is accused of the same conduct, and the man who kept its books will now be a witness for the prosecution. His agreement requires him to testify truthfully and to cooperate with the investigation, and it does not require him to implicate the family. The judge said that the plea does not prevent the prosecutors from charging him again if he lies, and that the sentence depends on his cooperation. The company's lawyers said the plea was the act of an individual and that the company had done nothing wrong.

At the same time, the wider investigation is the one the plea is attached to. The attorney general of New York has filed a civil suit against the family and the company, and the district attorney has empanelled a grand jury to look at the valuation of the assets. The finance chief's testimony could be used in both, and the documents that his plea describes give the investigators a map of the payroll. The family has called the inquiry a political pursuit, and the prosecutors have said that the evidence is documentary.

Meanwhile, the business effect is smaller than the legal one. The company runs hotels, golf courses and licensing deals, and its lending banks have been reviewing their exposure since the investigation began. The chief financial officer's departure from the day-to-day management was announced in the spring, and the family has installed a new team. The company's accounts remain private, and the value of the brand has been the subject of an argument between the family and the banks.

What the plea shows is that a case built on records can reach the people who keep them. It also shows how much of the company's operation depended on one man's discretion, and how little of it was written down. The trial in the autumn will test whether the prosecutors can prove that the company knew what he was doing, and the answer will depend on documents that the plea has now made available.
