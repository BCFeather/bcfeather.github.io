A particle that carries a temperature different from the fluid around it stirs that fluid without moving. The gradient it imposes makes the local viscosity vary in space, so the flow obeys an equation whose coefficients change from point to point, and the linear machinery that handles ordinary suspensions no longer applies in the usual form.

Arbib and Oppenheimer sidestep the variable coefficients. Using an energy dissipation argument together with the Lorentz reciprocal theorem, they compute the effective viscosity of a dilute suspension of such hot particles, with a correction that keeps separate bookkeeping for the bulk fluid and for the particles themselves. The reciprocal theorem supplies a way to move a solution of one flow problem onto another, so a hard problem with varying viscosity can be read off an easier one with a known answer.

Spheres with different heat distributions serve as the test cases. A sphere that is uniformly hot, or hot on one face, sets up a different flow field around itself, and each arrangement yields its own correction to the result a passive sphere would give, in which a small loading raises the viscosity by a fixed fraction of the volume the particles occupy; the correction then separates into a term the fluid contributes and a term the particle contributes, and only the second depends on how the heat sits on the surface.

The departure from textbook behaviour appears when the particles are non-Brownian and all point the same way. The viscosity then stops being a scalar: it depends on the angle between the imposed shear and the common orientation, so the same suspension can be thicker along one axis than along another. A sphere carries its heat field around with it, and the part of the temperature gradient that aligns with the flow adds to the dissipation while the part across it does not.

Fixing the orientation with an external field goes further. The stress tensor loses its symmetry, and the viscosity picks up odd components, the kind that turn shear into a response perpendicular to it. Odd viscosity is known from chiral fluids; here it comes from an anisotropic arrangement of heat sources rather than from molecular handedness.

Every result holds in the dilute limit, where particles stand far enough apart that each sees the bulk fluid and not its neighbours. Pair interactions at higher loading, the convection that a real temperature gradient drives through a real fluid, and the contrast in thermal conductivity between particle and fluid all sit outside the calculation, and each of them would change the coefficients that the dilute result supplies.

Read in practical terms, the claim is that a suspension of heated particles cannot be summarized by one number for thickness, and a device that relies on such a fluid would have to write the direction of the flow into its design. A measurement along two shear directions would be enough to see the effect. The preprint was posted to arXiv on 5 August 2024.
