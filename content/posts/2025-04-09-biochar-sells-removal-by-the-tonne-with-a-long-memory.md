Biochar is what is left when biomass is heated without oxygen, and it is one of the few ways to remove carbon from the atmosphere with a technology that already exists at farm scale. A tonne of biochar contains roughly two to three tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent, and it persists in soil for hundreds of years because the aromatic structure that makes it stable is the same structure that resists decomposition. A tonne of char that is buried in a field is a tonne that is not in the atmosphere, and the accounting is the whole product.

Production is a kiln problem rather than a laboratory problem. Slow pyrolysis at four to six hundred degrees produces char and a combustible gas, and the gas can run the process. A farm-scale unit that handles agricultural residue costs tens of thousands of dollars, and the equipment is simple enough that manufacturers in several countries build it. A kiln that runs on its own syngas is a kiln that does not need a fuel supply, which is why the technology spreads in places without gas.

The co-benefits are why farmers adopt it. Char improves water retention in sandy soil, raises pH in acidic soil, and provides a substrate for microbial activity. A farmer who applies it to a field is making an agronomic decision, and the carbon credit is a secondary revenue stream that makes the decision easier. A farmer who applies ten tonnes per hectare spends money that a carbon payment only partly covers, and the agronomic gain is the rest.

Verification is the weak link in the market. A credit has to prove that the biomass would otherwise have decomposed or been burned, that the char is in the soil, and that it will stay there. The methods that exist rely on sampling and on modelling, and the registries that issue credits differ in how strict they are. A registry that accepts a modelling approach is a registry that issues credits for char that may not stay in the field it was spread on.

Demand is thin and concentrated. A handful of corporate buyers have signed offtake agreements to meet voluntary targets, and the price has ranged from a hundred to two hundred dollars a tonne. A market that small is a market where one buyer's withdrawal changes the price, which is why the projects that survive are the ones with long contracts. A buyer that wants a durable removal is a buyer that will pay more for a verified tonne than for an estimated one.

The scale limit is biomass. A world that charred all of its crop residue would remove perhaps a gigatonne of carbon dioxide a year, and that residue is also used for animal feed, for fuel and for soil cover. A removal method that competes with existing uses is a method whose real potential is the fraction that is spare, and nobody has measured that fraction well. A residue that is already used for animal bedding is a residue that is not available for char, and the competition is local.
