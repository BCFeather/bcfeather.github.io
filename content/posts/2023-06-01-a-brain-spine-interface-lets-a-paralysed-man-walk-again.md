---

A spinal cord injury interrupts the pathway between the brain's intention and the muscles that would carry it out, and the interruption is usually permanent because the cord does not regenerate. What survives in many patients is the circuitry below the injury, which can still produce movement if it receives the right command.

In *Nature* on 1 June 2023, that command is supplied. The authors implant electrodes on the motor cortex and on the spinal cord, decode the cortical activity corresponding to walking, and stimulate the cord with patterns that reproduce the decoded intention.

Decoding carries the difficulty. A cortical signal is a mixture of many neurons' activity, and the mapping from that mixture to a limb's trajectory has to be learned for each patient, so the decoder is trained while the participant attempts movement in a rehabilitation setting.

Stimulation forms the second half. A spinal implant delivers bursts to the roots innervating the legs, and the timing and amplitude of those bursts have to match the phase of a step rather than the whole of one, which is what separates a controlled gait from a fixed pattern.

Natural gait is the result. The participant stands, walks and climbs stairs with a rhythm he controls, and a closed-loop interface lets intention set the movement, which can change from step to step. Training accompanies the implant, and its contribution to the outcome is a variable the study cannot isolate. Surgical burden is the practical consideration, because two implants mean two procedures and two sets of hardware, and a system a rehabilitation centre could deploy would have to be simpler than this laboratory instrument, smaller and wireless.

Durability stays unknown, as an implant working for a year may drift with electrode impedance and with the decoder's ageing calibration, and a chronic system needs a recalibration routine. Cohort size sets the limitation, because one participant with one injury level is a demonstration rather than a trial, and other levels and other times since injury are the next question. Regeneration is what the paper does not claim. The cord is not repaired, and the system bypasses the lesion rather than healing it, which matters for how the result is described to patients. Neurorehabilitation gains a route that works with existing circuitry, because a therapy recruiting what remains could be offered to most patients with a contusion.

Exoskeletons offer the comparison clinicians will reach for, since a wearable robot moves a limb while a brain-spine interface moves it through the patient's own muscles, and the second route preserves muscle tone and a sense of agency in a way the first does not. A failure mode worth naming is the decoder's own learning, since a system that adapts to the patient may also adapt to a habit, and a person who learns to lean on the stimulation could lose the intention the decoder is meant to read. Cost is the barrier a health system will weigh, because two implants and a rehabilitation programme are a large commitment for a therapy that has yet to show a benefit in a randomized comparison.
