title: "Brain-spine interface lets a paralysed man walk again"

description: "A brain-spine interface that reads cortical commands and stimulates the spinal cord restored standing, walking and stair climbing in a man with a chronic spinal cord injury, according to work published in *Nature* on 1 June 2023."
---

A spinal cord injury interrupts the pathway between the brain's intention and the muscles that would carry it out, and the interruption is usually permanent because the cord does not regenerate. What survives, in many patients, is the circuitry below the injury, which can still produce movement if it receives the right command.

The interface in *Nature*, 1 June 2023 supplies that command. The authors implant electrodes on the motor cortex and on the spinal cord, decode the cortical activity that corresponds to walking, and stimulate the cord with patterns that reproduce the decoded intention.

The decoding is the hard part. A cortical signal is a mixture of many neurons' activity, and the mapping from that mixture to a limb's trajectory has to be learned for each patient. The authors train the decoder while the participant attempts movement in a rehabilitation setting.

The stimulation is the second half. A spinal implant delivers bursts to the roots that innervate the legs, and the timing and the amplitude of the bursts have to match the phase of a step rather than the whole of one.

The result is a natural gait. The participant stands, walks and climbs stairs with a rhythm that he controls, rather than with the fixed pattern that a continuous stimulation produces.

The participant's own control is the departure from earlier work. A closed-loop brain-spine interface lets the intention set the movement, and the intention can change from step to step.

The rehabilitation is a component. The implant is paired with training, and the training's contribution to the outcome is a variable that the study cannot isolate.

The durability is unknown. An implant that works for a year may drift as the electrodes' impedance changes and as the decoder's calibration ages, and a chronic system needs a recalibration routine.

The limitation is the cohort. A single participant with a particular injury level is a demonstration rather than a trial, and the generalization to other levels and other times since injury is the next question.

What the paper does not claim is regeneration. The spinal cord is not repaired, and the system bypasses the lesion rather than healing it, which matters for how the result is described to patients.

For neurorehabilitation, the value is a route that works with existing circuitry. A therapy that recruits what remains is a therapy that most patients with a contusion could be offered, and the demonstration is a step towards that.
The surgical burden is a practical consideration. Two implants require two procedures and two sets of hardware, and a system that a rehabilitation centre could deploy would need to be simpler than the research prototype. The authors' hardware is a laboratory instrument, and a product would be smaller and wireless.
The comparison with exoskeletons is the frame that clinicians will use. A wearable robot moves a limb, and a brain-spine interface moves the limb through the patient's own muscles. The second route preserves the muscle's tone and the patient's sense of agency, and the first does not.
