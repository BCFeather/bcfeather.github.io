description: "Chains grafted onto a surface hold a layer of water that an oil droplet cannot displace, and the repulsion is stronger when the chains are dense and short."
---

A surface that repels oil under water is harder to make than one that repels water in air, and the reason is that water is on both sides of the interface. An oil droplet in water approaches a solid with a thin film of water between them, and the droplet is held away only if that film is stable. A coating that is merely hydrophobic gives the droplet a way to touch the solid, and the oil wins the contact because it has a lower interfacial energy against a hydrophobic surface than water does.

The coating that works is a brush of polymer chains tethered at one end to the surface. The chains are soluble in water and are stretched away from the surface by the excluded volume of their neighbours, and the layer they form is a few tens of nanometres thick. When an oil droplet presses on the layer, the chains are compressed, and compression costs entropy because the chains lose the conformations they had. The oil is excluded by that cost.

Density is the parameter that decides the strength. A sparse brush has gaps between the chains where the oil can reach the solid, and a dense one has no gaps. The thickness has to be large enough that the droplet cannot reach through it and small enough that the layer stays attached under shear. The coatings that have been reported use chains of a few thousand repeat units, grafted at a density of about one chain per square nanometre, which is close to the limit that the grafting chemistry allows.

The test is a contact angle measured through the water phase. A droplet of oil that sits on the coating with a high angle is held away, and a droplet that flattens is touching. The angle is sensitive to the history of the sample, since a coating that has been dried and rewetted can lose its hydration and recover it only slowly. A measurement taken on a fresh sample and one taken after a week of immersion can differ by tens of degrees.

Applications are marine and industrial. A hull that keeps oil and protein off its surface has less fouling, and a membrane that resists oil has a longer life in produced water. A heat exchanger that runs with an oil-water mixture fouls on the oil side, and a coating that keeps the oil in the flow rather than on the metal would remove a maintenance interval from the schedule. The same property is wanted in medical devices, where a catheter that resists biofilm is a catheter that resists infection.

Limits are mechanical. A brush is a soft layer, and a flow that exerts enough shear removes it, as does any abrasion. The grafting is done by surface-initiated polymerisation, which is a batch process that works on flat plates and is difficult on the inside of a tube. The chemistry is also sensitive to the substrate, since the initiator has to be attached to a surface that is clean and uniform, and a real surface is neither.
