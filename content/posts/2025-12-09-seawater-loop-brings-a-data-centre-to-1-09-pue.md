A data centre's electricity goes mostly to computation and the rest to removing heat, and the ratio between the two is the number operators compete on. A coastal facility has run a closed seawater loop for two years and reported a power usage effectiveness of 1.09, against 1.4 for its air-cooled sites.

The loop is closed on the seawater side. A titanium heat exchanger takes heat from a secondary loop into seawater that is drawn from 30 metres depth at 9 degrees, and the discharge is returned 4 degrees warmer at a diffuser that mixes it within 200 metres.

Titanium is the material choice that makes the loop durable. Seawater corrodes steel at 0.1 millimetres a year and titanium at nothing, and the exchanger's cost is 3 million dollars against a 400,000-dollar steel unit that would need replacement in a decade.

The servers are cooled by a dielectric liquid rather than by air. A single-phase immersion tank holds 40 servers in a bath that circulates to the secondary loop, which removes the fans from the servers and 12 per cent of their power draw. A dielectric liquid also removes the dust that an air-cooled hall filters, and a hall without filters loses one maintenance task and gains another.

The site's constraint is geography. A facility needs a coast, a deep-water intake and a discharge permit, and only three of the operator's 40 sites qualify. The rest are inland and will keep their chillers.

Heat reuse was evaluated and rejected. The 9-degree return can warm a greenhouse at 25 degrees, and the pipe to the nearest town is 6 kilometres at a cost of 5 million dollars, which the town's heating demand does not justify.

A free-cooling site is the honest baseline. An inland site in a cold climate achieves 1.15 with air handlers and no seawater, which is close enough that the coastal advantage is about land price rather than about efficiency.

Power usage effectiveness is measured at the meter and depends on the load. A facility at 30 per cent utilization has a worse ratio than the same facility at 80, because the cooling runs regardless. The 1.09 is an annual figure at an average load of 65 per cent.

Corrosion is the long-run risk. Titanium survives seawater and the welds are the weak point, and a heat exchanger's inspection interval is set by the weld's history rather than the metal's. A pinhole leak puts salt into the secondary loop and the servers' cold plates.

A coastal site's permit is the project's schedule. A discharge permit requires a mixing-zone study, and a study requires a year of current measurements. A developer that begins construction before the study starts is a developer with a building and no permit.

Efficiency here comes from siting rather than from equipment. The engineering is proven and the constraint is a coastline, and a fleet's average will improve only as fast as the coastline allows.
