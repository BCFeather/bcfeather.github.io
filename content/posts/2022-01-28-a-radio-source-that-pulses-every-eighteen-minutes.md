description: "Archival low-frequency data revealed a Galactic transient with an 18.18-minute period, reported in *Nature*, unlike any pulsar known."
---

Radio surveys usually find transients by looking for a burst that appears once and fades. The low-frequency sky had been comparatively quiet, with the known population dominated by pulsars whose periods run from milliseconds to seconds and by the slow scintillation of active galactic nuclei. A periodic signal with a period of minutes had no place in that taxonomy. A period of minutes sits between the seconds of a pulsar and the hours of an orbital cycle, and no known class occupied it. A slow rotation also strains the standard picture of magnetar emission, which assumes periods of seconds.

The discovery came from reanalysis rather than from a new telescope. A group examining archival data from the Murchison Widefield Array and the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope found a source that pulses every 18.18 minutes, bright, highly linearly polarized, visible across a broad frequency range. The paper appeared in *Nature*, 26 January 2022. The same data had been searched before for single bursts, which is why the periodic signal survived in an archive rather than in a fresh observation. Reanalysis of archived voltages is cheap, and this is the second discovery of its kind to come from a survey that had already been published.

Each pulse lasts 30 to 60 seconds. Some pulses contain shorter bursts below half a second, while others show a smoother profile, and the shape evolves over hours. Dispersion of the pulses with frequency placed the source inside the Milky Way rather than in a distant galaxy, which turns the object from a cosmological curiosity into a Galactic one. Dispersion also gives a crude distance, and the implied location is close enough that the source cannot be a distant galaxy.

The favoured interpretation is an ultra-long-period magnetar, a highly magnetized neutron star whose emission is beamed and whose rotation is slow enough to produce a minutes-long period. The polarization and the brightness temperature point to coherent emission, the process that drives pulsars, and the slow period would place the object in a region of the magnetar population that had been predicted and not observed.

Alternative explanations exist and are not excluded. An interacting binary, a magnetar with a precessing beam, or an object whose period is an alias of a faster rotation could all produce a similar signal, and each makes a different prediction for the polarization swing across a pulse. A precessing beam would sweep the polarization angle once per period, while a slow rotation would hold it steady, so the hypotheses are separable with a single well-timed observation.

The measurement rests on archival data, which limits what can be said about the source's variability over years. A single object also cannot establish a population, and the search that found it covered a narrow band of the sky. Archival data also carry no information about whether the source was active before the survey began, which removes the chance to measure a spin-down.

What would settle the question is a dedicated survey at low frequencies with timing precision sufficient to track the period over months, together with a search for the same signature elsewhere. If a population exists, the slowest pulsars would no longer be the slowest objects of their kind.
