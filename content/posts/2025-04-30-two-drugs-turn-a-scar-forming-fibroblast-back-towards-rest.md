description: "Inhibiting the kinase SRC alongside TGF-beta signalling reversed fibrosis in engineered heart tissue and in mice, a result that treats stiffness itself as a target."
---

A heart that has been through an infarct heals by scarring, and the scar is made by fibroblasts that have changed state. Activated fibroblasts deposit collagen, stiffen the matrix, and the stiffened matrix activates more fibroblasts, which is a loop that runs until the organ stops working. Drugs that block a single signalling molecule in that loop have repeatedly failed in trials, and the reason may be that the loop has more than one input.

A paper in *Nature* on 30 April 2025 argued that the stiffness signal itself can be targeted. Sangkyun Cho, Siyeon Rhee and Joseph Wu began with a meta-analysis of public single-cell sequencing datasets from human and mouse hearts, which identified the focal-adhesion kinase SRC as enriched in fibroblasts and as the node that transmits mechanical tension into the cell. SRC sits at the adhesion, where a stiff matrix is felt, and it is the kinase that converts tension into the phosphorylation cascade that keeps a fibroblast activated.

The pharmacological test used saracatinib, an SRC inhibitor, together with suppression of TGF-beta signalling. Separately, each drug produced a partial effect; together they repressed the profibrotic programme sharply, including a marked inhibition of the MRTF and SRF pathway that neither produced alone. Synergism is the result, because it says the two inputs, mechanical and soluble, have to be closed at the same time.

Functional readouts went beyond gene expression. In engineered heart tissue made fibrotic, the combination restored contraction, and in a mouse model of heart failure it alleviated contractile dysfunction. Engineered tissue is the right intermediate system here, because it lets the authors vary stiffness independently of the soluble signal, which is exactly the variable the paper claims to be targeting. A tissue that contracts less after treatment is the closest thing to an organ-level endpoint that a dish can offer.

Limits are the usual ones for a mechanism paper. Saracatinib is an oncology drug with a safety profile of its own, and nothing in the study establishes a dose that a cardiologist could give for months. Mice are not people, engineered tissue is not a ventricle, and fibrosis in the model is acute and controlled while clinical fibrosis accumulates over years. A drug that works on a young scar may do nothing to an old one, and most patients arrive with the old kind.

The conceptual shift is what lasts. Cardiac fibrosis has been treated as a consequence of inflammation and of neurohormonal activation, and trials that followed that model reduced afterload and blocked aldosterone without reversing the scar. A model in which mechanics is an input, and not only an outcome, gives a different set of targets, of which SRC is the first one tested.

What would settle it is a chronic dosing study in a large animal with an established scar, and a formulation of an SRC inhibitor that a heart patient could take. Neither exists yet. The paper's contribution is to show that the loop can be interrupted on the mechanical side, and that interrupting only the chemical side is not enough.
