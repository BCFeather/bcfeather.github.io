Muscle declines with age, and mitochondria decline with muscle. The CDGSH iron-sulfur proteins, known as CISD or NEET, sit at that intersection, and two of the three family members have been assigned roles in muscle cells. CISD1/mitoNEET and CISD2/NAF-1 live on the outer membrane and the endoplasmic reticulum. The third, CISD3/MiNT, sits inside the mitochondrion, and until this year nobody had shown what it does there.

A paper in *PNAS* on 21 May 2024 supplies the answer. Mice without CISD3 develop muscle atrophy, and their proteomic profile overlaps with that of Duchenne muscular dystrophy, which is a strong statement about how much the loss costs a muscle fibre. The mitochondria in those animals are structurally abnormal and functionally impaired.

Molecularly, the link runs through complex I, the first enzyme of the respiratory chain. CISD3 interacts with NDUFV2, a core subunit of complex I, and donates its \([2\mathrm{Fe}\text{-}2\mathrm{S}]\) clusters to it. Structural modelling with coevolutionary analysis places the donor and acceptor clusters 10 to 16 ångström apart, with contact residues that could conduct a transfer, and the geometry is consistent with a hand-off rather than a stable complex.

Muscle is the tissue where the effect shows most clearly because a fibre cannot dilute a defective mitochondrion by dividing. A neuron might compensate, and a myocyte carries the damage for as long as it lives. Assembly of complex I requires its iron-sulfur clusters to be inserted in the right order, and a shortage of donor clusters would stall the process before the enzyme ever reaches the membrane. That reading explains why the knockout phenotype looks mitochondrial rather than cytosolic.

Aging is the context that makes the finding interesting to clinicians. CISD3 abundance falls with age, complex I activity falls with age, and muscle mass falls with age, and the paper puts the three on one line without claiming that the line is causal in people. Clinical implications are tentative and should be read as such. If CISD3 limits complex I biogenesis in ageing muscle, then raising its activity might slow a decline that has no current treatment, and the same argument applies to dystrophies in which mitochondrial capacity is secondary. Redundancy is likely, since cluster transfer is usually catalysed by dedicated carriers and CISD3 could be one of several.

The paper does not show that CISD3 is the only donor, and other cluster-trafficking proteins could cover part of the demand in different tissues. It also cannot say whether the atrophy in the mice comes from complex I alone or from the wider metabolic consequences of a sick mitochondrion.

The experiments also give the field a reagent. A mouse that lacks CISD3 is a tool for asking how much complex I activity a muscle needs before it starts to waste, which is a question that no pharmacological inhibitor answers cleanly. What it does establish is a missing link in a pathway that textbooks describe in outline. A protein nobody had placed now sits between the cluster pool and the respiratory chain, and muscle maintenance depends on it.
