description: "A Nature Medicine analysis of 6.1 million people in 118 countries maps pain by body area, age and country income, and the patterns differ sharply."
---

Pain research usually counts diagnoses, which means it counts the people who reached a clinic. A study published in *Nature Medicine* in October took the other route and analysed self-reported pain from 6,125,459 people aged five to over a hundred, in 118 countries and territories, with data collected between 1990 and 2025. The result is a map, and the map is not flat. Reporting pain rather than seeking care removes the filter that clinics impose, though it adds one of its own: surveys reach the people who answer them.

Prevalence ranges from about 2 per cent for facial pain to about 40 per cent for back pain, and the spread is the first sign that the condition has been treated as one thing for too long. Women report more pain than men in all eleven body areas the authors studied, and the largest gaps appear for facial pain, headaches and stomach or abdominal pain, differences that are large enough to change how a clinic triages. Those differences are consistent across countries, which makes them harder to attribute to reporting habits alone.

Age changes the shape of the burden. Across all body areas, prevalence rises most sharply before 55, and different areas peak at different times: headaches and abdominal pain peak earlier in life, while back, hip and knee pain keep climbing. The common assumption that pain is a problem of old age is right about the total and wrong about the timing of the increase.

Country income enters the picture after 40. From that age, pain anywhere in the body, joint pain and back pain all increase faster in countries with lower Human Development Index values. By 80 or older, the prevalence of any bodily pain is about 31 percentage points higher in low-HDI countries than in high-HDI ones, and low back pain is nearly twice as common, a gap that no adjustment for the usual risk factors closes. The pattern is not explained by older populations in poorer countries, because the increase holds within age bands.

That finding is the uncomfortable one. Smoking, obesity and low income are known to drive pain, and the authors report that these factors explain less of the burden in lower-development settings than in richer ones. Something else is operating, and the candidates are physical work, injury, untreated conditions, air pollution and access to care, none of which the dataset can separate. A self-report dataset records the pain and not its cause, and the paper is explicit about that limit.

Prevention follows from the shape of the curve: if pain rises fastest before 55, waiting until retirement to address it is too late. The paper also makes a case for regional rather than global targets, since a programme designed for back pain in a high-income country does not match a burden dominated by joint pain at 40 in a country where the same condition goes untreated for longer. Counting diagnoses would have hidden all of this, which is the argument for asking people directly.
