description: "The Food and Drug Administration approved suzetrigine on 30 January for moderate to severe acute pain, the first new class of analgesic in more than twenty years and the first that blocks a sodium channel found only in peripheral nerves."
---

Acute pain after surgery or an injury is treated with opioids, with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and with acetaminophen, and the combinations have been standard since the 1990s. The opioid crisis made the limits of that standard visible: a drug that works on the same receptors as morphine relieves pain and produces dependence, sedation and respiratory depression, and a surgeon who prescribes a week of tablets sends some of them into a household where they will be misused.

Its target is a sodium channel called NaV1.8, which carries the electrical signal in nociceptors, the nerve fibres that report damage from skin, muscle and viscera. The channel is expressed in those fibres and hardly anywhere else, and the genes that encode it are the ones that, when mutated, produce inherited conditions in which people feel no pain or feel too much of it.

Suzetrigine blocks that channel. Because the channel is peripheral, the drug reduces the signal at its source and leaves the central nervous system alone, which is why the label lists no dependence, no respiratory depression and no sedation. Vertex sells it as Journavx, and the FDA approved it on 30 January for moderate to severe acute pain in adults, as a schedule IV controlled substance on the basis of the class rather than of the data.

Trials behind the approval were in abdominoplasty and in bunionectomy, two operations that produce a predictable and measurable amount of pain. Patients who received the drug reported less pain over the first two days than those who received placebo, and the effect was comparable to a combination of hydrocodone and acetaminophen in one of the studies, which is the comparison that a prescriber will care about.

Two limits define the label. The first is that the indication is acute pain, which means the drug has not been shown to work in chronic pain, in neuropathic pain or in the pain of cancer. The second is that a peripheral sodium channel blocker does not treat inflammation, so a patient with a swollen and inflamed wound still needs the anti-inflammatory component of the regimen.

Economics will decide how widely it is used. The list price is about fifteen dollars a tablet, which is far above a generic opioid and far below the cost of a dependence that follows from one. The NOPAIN Act, which Congress passed in 2022, pays hospitals and surgery centres extra for non-opioid pain treatments in outpatient settings, and Vertex expects the drug to qualify, which turns a price objection into a budgeting one.

What the approval shows is that the sodium channel family is druggable after thirty years of failed attempts. The first compounds were too toxic or too unselective, and the ones that reached trials produced cardiac effects or neurological ones. A selective blocker of a channel that exists only in pain fibres is the version that works, and the same logic is being applied to NaV1.7 and to the channels involved in chronic pain.
