Japan spent twenty-five years with a policy rate at or below zero, and the exit from that regime has been the most cautious tightening cycle of any major central bank. The Bank of Japan raised the rate to 0.1 per cent in March 2024, to 0.25 per cent in July and to 0.5 per cent on 24 January, which is a pace of one small step every few meetings and a total increase of half a percentage point in ten months.

One member dissented, and the decision was otherwise unanimous. Toshimitsu Nakamura dissented, arguing that the bank should wait for the next meeting and confirm through the corporate survey that firms had regained their pricing power before changing the policy setting. The dissent is the kind that a central bank publishes when the argument is about timing and not about direction.

Reasoning in the statement was about the cycle between wages and prices. Japanese inflation has run above the 2 per cent target for most of the past two years, driven first by imported energy and food prices and then by services, and the spring wage negotiations have produced increases above 5 per cent for two consecutive years. A central bank that sees wages and prices rising together has the evidence it said it needed.

Yen was the constraint that shaped the timing. The currency had weakened past 160 to the dollar in the summer of 2024, and the Ministry of Finance intervened, and a rate that stays at a quarter of a per cent while the Federal Reserve holds at four and a quarter is a rate that invites carry trades. The January decision narrowed the gap by a quarter point and left it wide.

What the increase does not do is make Japanese money expensive. A policy rate of 0.5 per cent against core inflation of about 2.5 per cent leaves the real rate negative, and the bank said as much in its communication, describing financial conditions as accommodative and the adjustment as a reduction in the degree of accommodation. Mortgage rates and corporate borrowing costs remain low by the standards of the past thirty years.

Reaction was muted because the decision had been priced. Markets had assigned a high probability to a January move after the December meeting and the wage data, and the yen moved little on the day, which is the response that a well-signalled central bank produces. The interesting part of the reaction was in the forward curve, where traders reduced the number of increases they expect for the rest of the year.

Next question is how far the bank can go. Japan's government has a debt stock above twice gross domestic product, and every quarter point of rate adds to the interest bill, which is why the fiscal authority watches the bank's meetings as closely as the markets do. The bank's own projection is that inflation will settle near target in the medium term, and a projection of that kind is a promise about the endpoint of the cycle rather than its pace.
