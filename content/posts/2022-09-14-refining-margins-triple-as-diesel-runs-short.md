description: "Distillate stocks in America fell to their lowest since 2000, and the crack spread for diesel passed 60 dollars a barrel."

Inventories of distillate fuel in the United States fell to 111 million barrels in the first week of September, the lowest since records began in 2000. The figure covers diesel and heating oil, and it was 20 per cent below the five-year average for the season. Stocks in Europe were about 10 per cent below normal, and the two markets were competing for the same cargoes. Gasoline stocks were also 7 per cent below normal, and the driving season had ended early in the west.

Refiners were earning three times their normal margin, and their shares rose 40 per cent over the quarter. The crack spread, which is the difference between a barrel of crude and a barrel of diesel, passed 60 dollars in the same week. The spread had been 20 dollars in the spring and 10 in 2021. Supply had fallen because of closures. Six refineries in the United States and Europe shut between 2020 and 2022, removing 1.5 million barrels a day of capacity, and the pandemic had accelerated decisions that were already being taken. No new refinery had been built in the United States since 1977, and the permitting process was the reason. Refiners in the Midwest earned 70 dollars a barrel, and the coastal plants earned less because of shipping costs.

Demand had changed rather than fallen. Diesel is the fuel of freight, agriculture and construction, and the reopening of the economy had pushed it back to pre-pandemic levels. The loss of Russian refined products to European buyers added 1 million barrels a day of demand for cargoes from elsewhere. Gas-to-oil switching in power generation added more.

In Europe, the wholesale price of heating oil doubled, and the winter outlook for households that heat with oil was the worst in a decade. The diesel shortage fed into prices at the pump. The average price of a gallon of diesel reached 5 dollars in the United States, a record, and it was 2 dollars a gallon in 2021. Jet fuel demand had returned to 90 per cent of its 2019 level, and that competed for the same crude.

Governments responded with releases from reserves and with rules. The United States offered 10 million barrels of low-sulfur diesel from the strategic reserve in September, and Europe discussed a cap on the price of Russian refined products. Neither was enough to rebuild stocks before the winter. The shortage eased in the spring. Warm weather in Europe cut heating demand, and Chinese exports of refined products rose after the government raised the quota. Refining margins fell back to 25 dollars by March 2023, and the diesel crack stayed above its historical average through the year.

A new plant takes five years and a billion dollars, and no one was building one. The episode was a lesson in how long refinery capacity takes to respond. The industry's answer was to run existing plants harder, and utilisation rates reached 95 per cent in America, which is close to the physical limit. Refinery utilisation in Europe was 88 per cent, and several plants had postponed maintenance to keep running.
