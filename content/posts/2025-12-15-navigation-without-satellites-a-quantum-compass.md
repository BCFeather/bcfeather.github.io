Satellite navigation fails when the signal is jammed or absent, and the fallback is inertial navigation, whose error grows with time. A cold-atom accelerometer demonstrated on a moving vehicle this month held a position error of 3 metres over 40 minutes, against a conventional inertial unit's 900.

The instrument measures acceleration by splitting an atom cloud with laser pulses and reading the phase difference when the paths recombine. An atom's phase shifts with acceleration at a rate set by fundamental constants, which is why the measurement needs no calibration.

The 40-minute run was on a van on a closed circuit with a known trajectory. The atom interferometer's error was 3 metres at the end of the run, and a fibre-optic gyroscope of the same class accumulated 900.

Size is the engineering limit. The vacuum chamber, the lasers and the optics occupy 1.2 cubic metres and draw 250 watts, which fits a truck and not a phone. A compact version at a fifth of the volume is in development.

Vibration is the hard problem. An atom cloud is disturbed by acceleration during the interferometer's sequence, and the team's design keeps the sequence to 20 milliseconds and applies a vibration correction from a classical accelerometer. A jamming transmitter's cost is a few thousand dollars and a satellite's replacement is a few hundred million, which is the asymmetry the program is buying against.

The application is military and civil at once. A vehicle that can navigate without a satellite can operate under jamming, and a survey vessel can work where satellite corrections are unavailable. A vehicle's own wheel odometry is the third input, and the atom interferometer's job is to correct the drift that odometry accumulates.

Measurement of gravity is the second product. The same instrument measures the local gravitational field, and a gravity map at a metre's resolution reveals buried pipes and tunnels, which is a market utilities already pay for.

An inertial unit's error grows with the square of time because its gyroscope's bias is integrated twice. An atom interferometer's error grows linearly because it measures acceleration directly, which is the difference that makes a 40-minute run comparable to a day of a conventional unit.

Sizing is the commercial question. A unit at 0.2 cubic metres and 80 watts would fit a truck and a survey vessel, and a market for 5,000 units a year at 200,000 dollars each is not enough to justify a chip-scale development program.

A quantum sensor's competitor is a better gyroscope rather than a satellite. A ring-laser gyroscope of the same accuracy costs a third and occupies a tenth of the volume, and its drift is the reason the atom interferometer's linear error is worth paying for.

Size traded for independence is this instrument's bargain. A quantum accelerometer is a worse clock than a satellite and a better reference than nothing, and the value of that difference depends on how much a jamming transmitter costs.
