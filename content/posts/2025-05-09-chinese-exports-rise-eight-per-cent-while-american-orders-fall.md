First Chinese trade report after the April tariff escalation was expected to show damage, and it showed a split. Exports in dollar terms rose 8.1 per cent from a year earlier to 315.7 billion dollars, imports fell 0.2 per cent to 219.5 billion, and the surplus was 96.2 billion. In yuan, exports rose 9.3 per cent and imports 0.8 per cent, so the difference between the two measures is the exchange rate.

American line was the damage. Exports to the United States fell 21 per cent to 33 billion dollars, after a rise of 8.9 per cent in March, which is the sharpest fall since the pandemic. Shipments to every other destination rose 13 per cent, and those to the Association of Southeast Asian Nations rose 21 per cent, which is the rerouting that a tariff on one border produces.

Timing explains part of the split. The reciprocal rate of 145 per cent took effect in the middle and later part of April, and a large share of the month's shipments had been booked before it, so a single month cannot separate the effect of the tariff from the effect of the announcement. Firms also moved goods through third countries, which shows up as exports to Vietnam and as imports from Vietnam.

Composition is the structural story. Mechanical and electrical products were 60.1 per cent of exports in the first four months and grew 9.5 per cent, which is the category that includes the consumer electronics that received an exemption in April. A country whose exports are increasingly machinery is a country whose customers are factories, and factories buy through cycles.

First four months put the year in context. Total trade was 14.14 trillion yuan, up 2.4 per cent, with exports up 7.5 per cent and imports down 4.2 per cent. Imports falling while exports rise is the signature of weak domestic demand and of a tariff that makes imported components more expensive, and it is the imbalance that a trade negotiation is about, since a surplus is the number the other side counts.

Surplus is the political number. A bilateral deficit with the United States that shrinks because American orders fell is a deficit that will shrink in the statistics and not in the politics, since the goods arrive through a third country and the final demand is still American. Customs data cannot see the last border.

What would settle the year is the May and June reports, when the pre-tariff shipments have been consumed and the 90-day truce is in force. A rebound in exports to the United States would show that the truce worked; a continued fall with rising shipments to Southeast Asia would show that the trade has moved and not stopped. What the customs figures cannot show is which of those containers were opened in Los Angeles and which were repacked in Hanoi and sold on.
