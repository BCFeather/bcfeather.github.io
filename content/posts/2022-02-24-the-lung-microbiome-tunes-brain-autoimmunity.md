Multiple sclerosis is a disease of the brain that has environmental risk factors residing in the lungs. Smoking and respiratory infection raise the risk, and T cells that attack myelin survive and mature in lung tissue before they migrate to the central nervous system, which is a connection that has resisted explanation. Explaining why the lung should matter at all has been difficult, because the organ most exposed to the environment is not the organ that the T cells eventually attack.

A study in rats has now traced that connection to the bacteria living in the lung. Treating the animals with neomycin shifted the pulmonary microbiota towards phyla rich in lipopolysaccharide, and the shift changed the reactivity of microglia, the immune cells resident in the brain. The paper appeared in *Nature*, 23 February 2022.

Microglia in the treated animals entered a state primed by type I interferon, and in that state they responded less strongly to the type II interferon signal that drives an autoimmune attack. The downstream effect was a smaller proinflammatory response, less recruitment of immune cells into the brain and milder clinical signs of disease. Type I interferon is the signal that cells produce in response to viral RNA, and microglia that have seen it behave as though a virus had already passed through the tissue. The state is reversible, since withdrawing the antibiotic restored the original reactivity within weeks.

The experiment ran in both directions. Suppressing lipopolysaccharide-producing lung phyla with polymyxin B aggravated the disease, and adding those phyla or lipopolysaccharide alone reproduced the protective effect of neomycin, which establishes the bacterial signal rather than the antibiotic as the active ingredient.

A lung-brain axis of this kind implies that the pulmonary mucosa is not a passive barrier but a sensor that reports the microbial environment to the central nervous system. The route is likely to be through circulating microbial products and through the priming of monocytes that then enter the brain. That route would explain why an inhaled signal can change the behaviour of cells that never leave the skull.

Rodents are the obvious limitation. The animals were housed under controlled conditions with a defined microbiota, and the human lung contains a different and more variable community, so translating a phylum-level shift into a clinical recommendation is premature. Human lungs are also sampled by lavage, which recovers the airway community and not necessarily the tissue-associated one that the rodent study examined.

The finding does, however, give smoking and infection a common mechanism. Both disturb the pulmonary community, both alter the state of microglia, and both are associated with flares of demyelinating disease, which suggests that the microbiome is the intermediate variable that epidemiology had been missing. Whether the same phyla sit in the human lung at the same abundance is unknown, and the next step is a study that correlates the pulmonary community with relapse rates in patients.
