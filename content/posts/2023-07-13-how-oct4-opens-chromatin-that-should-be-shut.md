Most transcription factors need open chromatin, and a nucleosome is closed. A small class of factors binds a nucleosome anyway, and that class matters because it is what makes a cell's identity changeable. In *Nature* on 13 July 2023, how one of them manages it is explained. The authors determine cryo-electron microscopy structures of OCT4 bound to nucleosomes carrying native human sequences, and of OCT4 and SOX2 bound together.

Distortion is the binding mode. OCT4 contacts the nucleosome where the DNA's helical path is altered, and that alteration lets the factor read a sequence otherwise facing inward. Asymmetry is what the factor exploits, since a nucleosome has two faces and a dyad, and binding one face at a defined position gives a geometry sequence-specific recognition can use.

Cooperation is the second result. SOX2 binds adjacent to OCT4, and the two stabilise a nucleosome conformation neither produces alone. Histone tails play the other half, because a factor's binding can displace a tail or change its contacts, and these structures show a tail repositioned rather than evicted.

Mechanism for pioneering follows. A factor binding a closed nucleosome can recruit remodellers and open the site, and the structure shows where recruitment would happen. Affinity is modest by design, since a pioneer binds weakly and transiently, which lets it sample many sites and open the ones that matter.

Sequence dependence is the detail. Native sequences rather than a synthetic strong site are used, and the structures show why a particular motif matters. Comparison with other pioneers frames it, because related DNA-binding domains may use a related mechanism, and this structure is a template for that comparison; the glucocorticoid receptor binds nucleosomes too, and comparing the two would show whether the mechanism is shared or convergent.

Resolution is the method's power. A cryo-EM map of a nucleosome-particle complex resolves the DNA's path and the factor's domains, and the path is what a biochemical assay cannot see. Static picture sets the limitation, as a structure is one conformation and the opening that follows is dynamic.

The opening itself is what the paper does not show, because a bound factor is not an open nucleosome, and the transition needs a time-resolved measurement. Chromatin compaction raises the harder obstacle, since a factor binding a single nucleosome has not yet met a fibre. Stem cell biology gains a structural basis for reprogramming, because a factor entering closed chromatin is the first step of a conversion, and a cell converted to pluripotency depends on it. A single-molecule imaging experiment would show the dwell times that the structures imply, since a pioneer that binds transiently spends most of its time unbound, and the residence time is what sets how quickly a closed region opens. Whether other members of the family use the same distortion is the comparison that would turn one structure into a mechanism, and a set of structures across the family is what the field would need for that.
