---

Most transcription factors need open chromatin, and a nucleosome is closed. A small class of factors can bind a nucleosome anyway, and the class is important because it is what makes a cell's identity changeable.

The structures in *Nature*, 13 July 2023 explain how one of them does it. The authors determine cryo-electron microscopy structures of OCT4 bound to nucleosomes containing native human sequences, and of OCT4 and SOX2 bound together.

The binding is a distortion. OCT4 contacts the nucleosome at a site where the DNA's helical path is altered, and the alteration is what lets the factor read a sequence that would otherwise face inward.

The cooperation is the second result. SOX2 binds adjacent to OCT4, and the two together stabilize a nucleosome conformation that neither produces alone.

The consequence is a mechanism for pioneering. A factor that can bind a closed nucleosome can recruit remodellers and open the site, and the structure shows where the recruitment would happen.

The sequence dependence is the detail. The authors use native sequences rather than a synthetic strong site, and the structures show why a particular motif matters.

The comparison with other pioneers is the frame. A family of factors with related DNA-binding domains may use a related mechanism, and the structure is a template for that comparison.

The method's power is the resolution. A cryo-EM map of a nucleosome-particle complex resolves the DNA's path and the factor's domains, and the path is what a biochemical assay cannot see.

The limitation is the static picture. A structure is one conformation, and the opening that follows is a dynamic process that the structure only implies.

What the paper does not show is the opening itself. A bound factor is not an open nucleosome, and the transition needs a time-resolved measurement.

For stem cell biology, the value is a structural basis for reprogramming. A factor that can enter closed chromatin is the first step of a conversion, and the structure shows what that step looks like.

The nucleosome's asymmetry is what the factor exploits. A nucleosome has two faces and a dyad, and a factor that binds one face at a defined position has a geometry that a sequence-specific recognition can use.

The histone's role is the second half. A factor's binding can displace a histone tail or change its contacts, and the structures show a tail that is repositioned rather than evicted.

The affinity is modest and that is the point. A pioneer binds weakly and transiently, which allows it to sample many sites and to open the ones that matter.

The comparison with the glucocorticoid receptor is instructive. That factor binds nucleosomes too, and a comparison of the two structures would show whether the mechanism is shared or convergent.

The chromatin's compaction matters. A factor that binds a single nucleosome has not yet encountered a fibre, and a fibre's folding is the harder obstacle.

The reprogramming use is the motivation. A cell that is converted to pluripotency needs its closed regions opened, and a pioneer's mechanism is the entry point of that process.
