---

Most inhibitory neurons in the cortex are born in a structure called the medial ganglionic eminence and then migrate into the cortex, and the human version of that structure produces a far greater variety of interneurons than the mouse version does. The difference is one of the clearest examples of how a small change in development can produce a large change in the adult brain, and its cellular basis was not known. Answering it requires looking at the structure while it is still producing cells. Fetal tissue is scarce, and every study of it is a negotiation with ethics committees and with the families who donate.

Jia and colleagues characterised the cellular composition of the human medial ganglionic eminence using single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics across a wide range of fetal developmental stages. The approach recovers both the identity of each cell and the position it occupied, which is what allows a progenitor to be placed in a layer of tissue. The work appeared in *Science* on 15 January, with a Perspective in the same issue.

One cell type stood out. A progenitor population in the subventricular zone of the structure appears to be responsible for maintaining the generation of interneurons and glial cells throughout human development, which is a job that the equivalent mouse population performs for a much shorter period. Persistence of production, rather than a larger initial stock, is the mechanism that the data support.

The authors also used computational methods to trace the trajectories of the neuronal populations that derive from the structure and to link specific cell types there to populations found in the cortex. That linkage is the part that connects a progenitor to an outcome, and it turns a census of cell types into a developmental pathway with a direction. Organoids that reproduce the structure would allow the population to be ablated and the consequence measured, and none yet reproduces it faithfully.

Its functional claim rests on correlation between transcriptomes, and correlation across development is weaker evidence than a lineage experiment. Human fetal tissue cannot be manipulated in the way a mouse can, so the field relies on inference from time series, and the inference would be strengthened by organoid systems that reproduce the structure and allow perturbation.

The medical motivation is not decorative. Interneuron populations are altered in epilepsy and in several neurodevelopmental conditions, and knowing which progenitor produces which population defines the cell type that a therapy would need to replace or protect. A map of the factory is a prerequisite for repairing its output.

What the paper establishes is that the human structure has a dedicated production line that keeps running, and that this is a plausible explanation for the diversity of the adult population. Whether it is the whole explanation is a question for the next decade of work. The same question applies to other progenitor populations that differ between species, and the methods now exist to ask it systematically.
