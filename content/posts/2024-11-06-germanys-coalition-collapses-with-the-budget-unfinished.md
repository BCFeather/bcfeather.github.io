Germany's three-party coalition ended on 6 November when the chancellor dismissed the finance minister and the smallest partner withdrew its ministers. The immediate dispute was over how to cover a gap in next year's budget without breaching a constitutional limit on new borrowing, and the disagreement had been building since a court ruled that money moved between pandemic funds was illegal. A coalition that spans three parties is a coalition that has to agree on a budget every year, and the budget is where the disagreements surface.

Debt brake is the rule at the centre of it. The constitution limits the structural deficit to a small fraction of output, with an escape clause for emergencies, and the finance minister insisted the clause could not be stretched for support to industry. The other partners wanted a looser reading, and a coalition that governs by arguing about a constitutional article was not going to survive a budget season. A court ruling that struck down a transfer of pandemic money is a ruling that removed the accounting trick the budget depended on.

Economy gave the argument its urgency. German industry has been losing competitiveness for several years, energy costs are above those of its competitors, and the automotive sector is cutting jobs while its Chinese market share falls. A government that cannot agree on a subsidy or a tax cut is a government that cannot answer the question its voters are asking. A car industry that is cutting jobs is an industry that has stopped waiting for a policy.

Political calendar moves next to an early election. A chancellor who has lost his majority governs with a minority until a confidence vote, and the opposition has an interest in the vote happening sooner than later. The campaign will be fought over the budget rule as much as over anything, which is unusual for a country that usually argues about policy rather than procedure. A confidence vote that the chancellor schedules is a vote he expects to lose, and losing it starts the clock.

Consequences for Europe matter outside Germany. The largest member state has been the anchor of the currency union's fiscal credibility, and a government in transition cannot agree to new common borrowing or to a bigger defence commitment. A Europe that is waiting for a German election is a Europe that is not making decisions. A defence commitment that needs money is a commitment that a caretaker government cannot sign.

What the collapse exposes is that the fiscal rule was written for a country that expected steady growth. An economy that has stopped growing turns a limit on borrowing into a limit on everything else, and the coalition discovered that a rule designed to protect credibility can also prevent a government from acting. A rule that was written in a different economy is a rule that will be rewritten by whoever wins the election.
