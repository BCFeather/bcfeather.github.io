description: "Adding magnesium to calcium aluminide drops the hardness from 4.9 to 4.1 gigapascals and the indentation modulus from 85.5 to 71.3, while opening slip systems the stoichiometric phase does not use."
---

Intermetallic Laves phases are hard, stiff and brittle at room temperature, which is why they appear in alloys as strengtheners and why their brittleness limits how much of them an alloy can carry. The C15 form of \(\mathrm{CaAl_2}\) is a convenient model: cubic, well ordered, and hard enough that deformation has to be studied at the scale of an indentation rather than in a tensile bar, where the sample would crack before it yielded.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 20 March 2024 measures how composition changes that behaviour. Two off-stoichiometric compositions were compared with the stoichiometric phase: one with 5.7 atomic per cent magnesium added, written \(\mathrm{Ca_{33}Al_{61}Mg_6}\), and one with 10.8 atomic per cent magnesium and 3.0 atomic per cent calcium added, written \(\mathrm{Ca_{36}Al_{53}Mg_{11}}\), against \(\mathrm{Ca_{33}Al_{67}}\) as the reference. The comparison is a substitution study in a single cubic structure, which keeps the crystal family fixed while the chemistry moves.

Nanoindentation on grains of several orientations produced the numbers. The reference phase gave 4.9 plus or minus 0.3 GPa and an indentation modulus of 85.5 plus or minus 4.0 GPa. The 5.7 per cent composition gave 4.6 plus or minus 0.2 GPa and 80.4 plus or minus 3.8 GPa, and the 10.8 per cent composition 4.1 plus or minus 0.3 GPa and 71.3 plus or minus 1.5 GPa. Both quantities fall as magnesium enters, and the intervals of the two extremes do not overlap.

Softening is only half the story, because the traces left on the indented surface say how the crystal gave way. Several distinct slip systems of the family written \(\{11n\}\) were active, and transmission electron microscopy confirmed the slip and crack planes identified from the pattern, which is what turns a hardness number into a statement about mechanism, and not one about a sample.

Atomistic simulation supplies the reason the systems differ. Energy barriers for the activated slip systems were computed and compared, which turns a list of observed planes into an argument about which of them a dislocation would prefer, why the magnesium-bearing compositions find more of them available, and how much resistance each has to overcome before it moves.

Room temperature is the whole of the mechanical result, and nanoindentation samples a small volume under a sharp tip. A bulk sample would average over grains, pores and second phases, the paper does not report fracture toughness, which is the property that decides whether a brittle intermetallic can be used at all, and three compositions are not a composition series.

What the comparison sets up is a lever. If magnesium buys plasticity at a known cost in stiffness, an alloy designer can trade the two deliberately, instead of discovering the trade in service. Room-temperature nanoindentation is a narrow window on a phase that usually serves hot, so the next experiment is whether the extra slip systems stay active at the higher temperatures where such compounds actually carry load.
