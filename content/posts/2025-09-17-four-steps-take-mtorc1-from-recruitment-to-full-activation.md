description: "Reconstitution on membranes and cryo-electron microscopy resolve how RHEB, RAGs and Ragulator bring mTORC1 to the lysosome and switch it on."
---

The kinase mTORC1 decides whether a cell grows, and it makes that decision by combining two signals that arrive by different routes. Growth factors act through the tuberous sclerosis complex on RHEB, a small GTPase anchored at the lysosome, while nutrients act through the RAG GTPases and their scaffold Ragulator. Direct binding of RHEB to the mTOR subunit allosterically activates the kinase, but how recruitment and activation fit together on a membrane was unresolved.

A paper in *Nature* rebuilds the system outside a cell. The authors reconstitute mTORC1 on lipid membranes with RHEB, the RAGs and Ragulator, and they image the assembled complex by cryo-electron microscopy. Both RAPTOR and mTOR turn out to touch the membrane directly, which is the observation the rest of the paper follows. The reconstitution is quantitative: each component is titrated, and the assembly is followed with labelled versions. The lipids are mixed to match the charge of the inner leaflet, and changing that charge shifts the recruitment step.

The structures support a four-step sequence. The RAG-Ragulator pair recruits mTORC1 to within about 100 angstroms of the membrane; RHEB then draws the complex to within about 40 angstroms; RAPTOR engages the membrane and the enzyme reaches an intermediate activity; mTOR engages the membrane and the active site reaches full activity. Each distance corresponds to a defined contact, so the sequence is read from the maps instead of from a kinetic ordering.

Full engagement is what aligns the catalytic residues. The paper shows that a partially engaged complex has a misaligned active site, so the last step is not a decoration on an already active enzyme but the event that makes catalysis efficient. That explains why two inputs are required for full activation when either alone produces only a fraction of the rate. A mutant that cannot touch the membrane keeps the intermediate activity, which is the control that separates the two contacts.

Each experiment is built to isolate one step. Membrane anchors are removed or mutated one at a time, and the distances are measured rather than inferred, so each transition is tied to a structural change instead of to a correlation between two signals. The reconstitution also allows the authors to leave out components and see what fails. The same strategy tests the RAG dependence, and removing those GTPases returns the system to the recruited but inactive state. The assay also asks whether the RAGs are needed after recruitment, and the answer is that they are not.

A reconstituted system is not a lysosome. TSC, DEPTOR, PRAS40 and the phosphatases that oppose the kinase are absent, and the membrane is a defined lipid mixture instead of the inner leaflet of a real organelle. The four steps are therefore a mechanism for the core machine, and their timing inside a cell remains to be measured. A lysosome has a protein density and a curvature that a supported bilayer does not reproduce, and either could shift the distances.
