The finance ministers of the group of seven agreed on Friday to a price cap on Russian crude, and they left the level of the cap to be decided later. The mechanism does not ban Russian oil; it bans the insurance, the shipping and the finance that make the oil deliverable unless it is sold below an agreed price. Since most of the world's tankers and most of its marine insurance are based in the countries that signed up, the argument is that the ban can bite without a formal embargo. The agreement was reached in a video call and published as a statement of principles.

An idea came from a problem the Europeans had created for themselves. An embargo on Russian oil agreed in the spring would remove about three million barrels a day from the market, and the price would rise for everybody, including the countries that imposed it. A cap tries to keep the oil flowing while cutting the revenue, which is the opposite of a boycott. Officials in Washington had argued for months that the two goals could be pursued at once, and the treasury secretary had toured the capitals to sell it.

Enforcement depends on the cooperation of countries that have not signed. India and China have bought Russian crude at a discount since the spring, and their refiners have no reason to respect a ceiling set in Brussels and Washington. The ships that carry the oil to those ports are insured in London, however, and the insurance is the part that can be withdrawn. Officials say the cap will be enforced through attestations and through the records that insurers keep, and that the shadow fleet of tankers bought by unknown owners is the loophole they are watching.

Level is the political question, and it has not been settled. A cap close to the market price would change nothing, and a cap far below it would remove the oil from the market, which is what the mechanism is meant to avoid. European officials had discussed a figure in the sixties, and the Russians said any cap would be a breach of contract. The Kremlin's spokesman said the countries that imposed it would lose the market to others, and he promised that Russian oil would find buyers.

Several questions are left open, including what happens to refined products, which the Europeans plan to embargo from February. Diesel is the product that matters most for Europe's trucks and tractors, and a cap on crude does nothing about it. Officials said a similar mechanism for refined products would follow, and that the details would be worked out with the shipping industry. The insurance market has asked for a transition period, and it has not been granted one yet.

What the cap will do, if it works, is take revenue from the Russian budget without taking barrels from the world market. What it may do as well is push the trade into channels that the group of seven cannot see, and create a discount that the Russian government absorbs by selling to a smaller number of buyers. Officials on both sides say the next month will decide it, and the markets have begun to price a winter in which the barrels move but the paperwork does not.
