description: "Classified American intelligence documents appeared on a chat server in early 2023 and became a public crisis in April, when a twenty-one-year-old airman was arrested and the Pentagon began an investigation into how a junior employee could remove material from a secure facility."
---

Documents began appearing on a Discord server used by a few dozen young men to talk about video games, and by the second week of April they had been reproduced in newspapers on three continents. On 13 April the Federal Bureau of Investigation arrested Jack Teixeira, a twenty-one-year-old member of the Massachusetts Air National Guard, at his home in North Dighton. He was charged under the Espionage Act, and the Pentagon announced a review of how a junior employee had access to material at that level, which in practice meant access to the system that produces it.

Material of this kind is never meant to be read outside a compartment. It includes assessments of Ukrainian air defences and of the timing of a counter-offensive, of the state of Russian and Ukrainian forces, of Israeli and South Korean intelligence, and of American surveillance of allied governments. Some of it appeared to be slide decks prepared for briefings, photographed rather than copied, which explains the creases and the desk edges visible in the images, and the quality of the photographs is what made the material so easy to authenticate.

Damage is measured in trust rather than in secrets. Several allied governments asked the Americans for an explanation, and South Korea said that it had raised the surveillance passages with Washington directly. Ukraine's government said publicly that the documents were being treated as Russian disinformation and privately that several of the numbers were accurate, which is the position that every government takes when it wants the leak to matter less than it does.

The Pentagon's problem is structural. About a million people hold security clearances in the United States, and the system that is supposed to prevent unauthorized disclosure relies on the assumption that people with clearances follow the rules. That assumption has failed repeatedly, from Chelsea Manning to Edward Snowden to this case, and each failure has produced a new set of procedures that the next person with a phone can work around. The review will ask whether the number of people cleared to see such material should fall, and whether a system built for paper can survive a camera.

The political reaction divided along the lines that the country has used since 2016. Supporters of the former president described the arrest as a case of one rule for the intelligence agencies and another for him, since he had been investigated for retaining documents at his club in Florida. The administration described the cases as different in kind, one involving a man who photographed secret slides and another involving a president who refused to return boxes.

By the end of the month the documents had stopped appearing, the chat server had been deleted, and the investigation had expanded to whether others had helped. What remains is a question that the agencies will be asked again: how many people need to see a document for a decision to be made, and whether the answer has grown faster than the ability to keep them from photographing it.
