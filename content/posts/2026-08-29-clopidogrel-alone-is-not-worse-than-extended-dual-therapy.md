After a drug-eluting stent, the question of how long to keep a patient on two antiplatelet drugs has been answered by trials that disagree, because the outcome that matters depends on which risk the patient carries. Extending dual therapy prevents stent thrombosis and myocardial infarction and causes bleeding. Shortening it does the reverse. A trial in patients selected for high ischemic risk asks whether one drug can hold the ischemic benefit while shedding the bleeding. The trial answers that question by defining net benefit explicitly and letting the reader weigh the parts.

The A-CLOSE trial, run in South Korea as an open-label noninferiority study, enrolled patients who had received a drug-eluting stent twelve months earlier and who had high-risk clinical or lesion characteristics. It randomized 3,203 patients, 1,601 to clopidogrel alone and 1,602 to clopidogrel plus aspirin, and published in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 29 August with a net adverse clinical event composite as the primary endpoint.

At 24 months the net composite, which combined death, myocardial infarction, stent thrombosis, stroke and clinically relevant bleeding, occurred in 80 patients on monotherapy, 5.0 per cent, and in 81 on dual therapy, 5.1 per cent. The risk difference was minus 0.1 percentage points with a 90 per cent interval from minus 1.3 to 1.2, and *P*=0.001 for noninferiority. A 90 per cent interval for a noninferiority claim is the conventional choice, and it is wider on the upper side than an investigator might like.

The components tell the real story, and they pull in opposite directions. The ischemic composite of death, myocardial infarction, stent thrombosis or stroke occurred in 60 patients on monotherapy against 26 on dual therapy, a hazard ratio of 2.33 with an interval from 1.47 to 3.69. Bleeding of BARC type 2, 3 or 5 occurred in 28 against 65, a hazard ratio of 0.43 with an interval from 0.27 to 0.67.

Noninferiority on a net endpoint is therefore a statement about arithmetic rather than about equivalence. The trial traded roughly 34 additional ischemic events for roughly 37 fewer bleeds in 3,203 patients, and whether that trade is acceptable depends on how a patient weights a stent thrombosis against a gastrointestinal bleed. The composite was designed to make the trade symmetric, and the components show it is not. Patients who had bled before were represented in the population, and the subgroup analysis in the paper is where a clinician should look first.

What the trial does not settle is the choice for a patient who has already bled on dual therapy or who has a lesion where thrombosis would be catastrophic. Those decisions need a subgroup analysis with more events than this trial produced, and the honest answer for now is that the two strategies are close enough on net benefit that the patient's history decides.
