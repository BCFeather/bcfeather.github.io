description: "An overloaded fishing boat carrying an estimated 750 people capsized in international waters off Pylos in southern Greece in the early hours of 14 June, and only 104 were rescued while 82 bodies were recovered."

An overcrowded fishing boat that had left Libya a few days earlier capsized in international waters about eighty kilometres off Pylos, in the southern Peloponnese, in the early hours of 14 June. Survivors and officials estimated that about seven hundred and fifty people were on board, including dozens of children who were hidden in the hold. The Hellenic coast guard rescued a hundred and four people and recovered eighty-two bodies, which leaves hundreds unaccounted for and makes the sinking the deadliest in modern Greek history.

The sequence of events is disputed and is the subject of several inquiries. Survivors have said the boat was unstable and that people had been transferred from smaller vessels as it waited, and that the coast guard's handling of the tow contributed to the capsize. The Greek authorities say the vessel was already listing and that the transfer they attempted was the only way to reach it. The Greek ombudsman's office and the European Union's border agency have both examined the response, and their reports have focused on how long the boat was observed before the rescue attempt.

Bound for Italy, the boat carried people from Syria, Pakistan, Egypt and elsewhere. The route from eastern Libya across the central Mediterranean is one of the two main channels through which people reach Europe without permission, and the boats that use it are increasingly small and unseaworthy. The survivors were taken to the port of Kalamata, where they were held, identified and, in several cases, charged with smuggling offences, and the Greek government has faced criticism for treating them as suspects rather than as witnesses.

Political consequences arrived quickly. The sinking revived an argument within the European Union about sea rescue and about the practice of returning boats to countries where the coast guard cannot guarantee that people will not be harmed. Greece has been accused by human rights organizations of carrying out pushbacks at the maritime border, which it denies, and the fact that this boat was observed for hours before sinking has been used to argue that the legal framework discourages timely rescue. The European Commission has asked for explanations and has received them.

Numbers are the hardest part to absorb. A hundred and four survivors from seven hundred and fifty people means that most of those on board died, and many of the families that will never learn what happened are in countries whose governments have no interest in investigating. The Mediterranean has been crossed by millions of people in the past decade and the crossings have become more dangerous as the routes have been closed. This accident is the worst of the modern era and it will be the subject of litigation and of a public argument, and neither will recover the people who went down with the boat.

Survivors will be held for weeks while the investigation continues, and the families of the missing will be asked for DNA samples. The boat itself was towed to a naval base, where it is being examined as evidence of an offence nobody has yet been charged with.
