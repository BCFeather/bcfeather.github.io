A polyelectrolyte brush is a carpet of charged chains anchored to a surface, and it earns its place in surface engineering by changing how a surface wets, slides and adsorbs. The chains repel one another along their length, their counterions screen the charge, and the solvent decides how much of the carpet stands up. What the carpet looks like at high grafting density has been the open question, because the two interactions inside it pull in different directions: the charges want the chains stretched apart, and the hydrophobic backbone wants them together.

Work reported in *Macromolecules* this year answers it with a continuous-space self-consistent field calculation. Competing electrostatic and hydrophobic forces are put into one mean field, and the calculation returns not a smooth profile but a series of microphase-segregated layers, polymer-rich and polymer-poor, stacked along the direction normal to the substrate. The field is solved repeatedly until the polymer density and the electrostatic potential agree with each other, which is what self-consistency means here.

Transitions between those morphologies are abrupt. A brush with one condensed layer becomes a brush with two, then three, and each step happens discontinuously, as does the final melting step in which the layered structure gives way to a fully swollen brush. There is no regime in which the layers thin out gradually and hand over to a uniform profile, and each additional layer appears at its own threshold in charge density or grafting density rather than at a threshold shared with its neighbours.

Which chains make up the layers is a separate finding. Each layer draws a different subpopulation of the whole set, and the picture therefore differs from the pearl-necklace collapse of a single chain in a poor solvent, where one molecule folds onto itself. Here separate chains, or separate stretches of chains, are segregated from their neighbours and stay that way, which matters for how a layered brush carries a load: the condensed layers and the depleted ones respond differently to a shear.

The prediction reaches experiment through reflectivity. Oscillations in a reflectivity spectrum carry the layer spacing as a period and the sharpness of the polymer-solvent boundary as an amplitude, so a measurement that resolves several periods would report both the number of layers and how crisp their edges are. A diffuse boundary damps the higher harmonics first, which is why the amplitude carries information the period does not.

An experimental anchor comes from a different material. The lamellar structure seen at the interface of a hydrated Nafion film matches the multi-layer morphology the calculation predicts, which gives the result a case in which the layered brush is not a theorist's convenience. The match is qualitative, since Nafion is a membrane rather than a grafted carpet, and the comparison rests on the spacing rather than on the chemistry.

Limits follow from the method. A mean-field theory sets fluctuations aside, the parameter ranges are chosen rather than scanned exhaustively, and the calculation says nothing about how long a real brush takes to reach a layered state. A reflectivity experiment on a brush of known grafting density would test the prediction directly, and brushes that keep surfaces clean, lubricate or bind proteins all depend on which of the two forces wins.
