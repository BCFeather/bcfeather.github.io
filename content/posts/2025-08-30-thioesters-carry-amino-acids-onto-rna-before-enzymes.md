Protein synthesis as cells do it needs two molecules that are hard to make without protein synthesis: a ribosome built of RNA and protein, and amino acids activated by attachment to adenosine. The origin of that system is the central problem of the field, and the standard answer is that RNA came first and the chemistry came later. A chemical route that aminoacylates RNA without enzymes would shorten the gap. Any route that closes the gap has to work in water, at neutral pH, without a protein holding the reacting ends together.

A paper in *Nature* on 27 August 2025 reports such a route. Its authors show that aminoacyl thioesters react with the 2',3'-diol at the end of an RNA chain, and that they do so in preference to the amine groups of the amino acid itself, which is the selectivity the biological machinery achieves with an enzyme. The reaction works for 14 amino acid side chains. The 14 side chains span small aliphatic residues and those with aromatic and charged groups, which is most of the chemical variety a peptide needs.

Selectivity is the part that needs explaining. A thioester carries an activated carbonyl, and an amine attacks it readily, so a mixture of amino acid and RNA would ordinarily give amide bonds to the amino acid instead of esters to the RNA. The authors show that duplex formation between the RNA and a template strands places the reacting ends together, and that the spatial arrangement favours the diol over the amine. The competing amine sits on the same molecule as the activated carbonyl, so the reaction is a contest between two groups held in close proximity.

Prebiotic plausibility is argued separately. Nitriles, N-carboxyanhydrides, amino acid anhydrides and aminoacyl adenylates, all of which have been proposed as prebiotic activated species, react with thiols to give the thioesters, and the thiols include coenzyme A and coenzyme M, which are themselves plausible products of a simple sulphur chemistry. Each of those species is an activated amino acid, and the observation is that all of them funnel through a thioester instead of reacting with RNA directly.

The last step is the one that suggests a path. Activating the amino acid as a thioacid instead of a thioester flips the selectivity, so that the amine reacts first and a peptide grows, and a neutral aqueous one-pot sequence produced peptidyl-RNA carrying several residues. That is a synthesis of the two classes of polymer in the same vessel. The one-pot sequence ran in water at neutral pH, the condition that makes a prebiotic claim credible, not a claim about a laboratory solvent.

What the paper does not show is a template that copies information. Aminoacylation directed by a duplex is a beginning, and translation needs a code, a reading mechanism and a product that can be selected. Reaction yields in water are modest, and the mixtures are more complicated than a cell. The yields are in the range a chemist calls promising and a cell would call impossible. Fidelity is the missing term, since a template that pairs loosely incorporates the wrong residue as readily as the right one.

The experiments that would matter are selection experiments: a pool of short RNA templates and a pool of thioesters, asked whether any pair reproduces with fidelity and whether the product helps its own template. That is the question the origin of translation turns on. The experiment would also need a selection pressure, because a chemistry that works once is not a chemistry that improves. That is the difference between a plausible route and an evolvable one.
