Fast times this season have restarted the usual argument. Several athletes have run personal bests on the same circuit, and the question in the commentary is whether the tracks are faster or the athletes are. The honest answer is that both are, and that the comparison to previous eras is complicated by a third factor that rarely gets mentioned: how the time is measured.

Track surfaces have changed a great deal. Synthetic tracks are now built with a stiff lower layer and a thin top layer whose stiffness and rebound are tuned, and the difference between a good track and an average one is measurable in hundredths over a hundred metres. The surface interacts with the spike plate: a stiffer track returns more energy to the athlete, and a shoe with a stiffer plate and a more responsive foam returns more still. Both have been engineered upward for two decades.

Shoes draw the most argument. Sprint spikes now include a rigid plate and a foam layer that stores and returns energy, and the governing body has set limits on stack height and on the number of plates. Within those limits, the designs differ, and a shoe that suits one athlete's mechanics may be slower for another. The records that fall in a season tend to fall across a range of athletes, which is a sign that the equipment is not the whole story.

Timing is the factor that decides whether a comparison is fair. Hand timing, used before the 1970s, rounds to a tenth of a second and favours the athlete by roughly 0.2 seconds. Fully automatic timing, with a camera at the finish and a start signal from the blocks, is accurate to a thousandth. A record ratified today is measured to a standard that did not exist when the older records were set, which means that a straight comparison across eras is never exact.

The wind is the other systematic. A tailwind above two metres per second disqualifies a time for record purposes, and the gauge is positioned at the track, not at the athlete. A race run with a legal but strong tailwind is faster than one run in still air, and the difference over a hundred metres is several hundredths. Records are therefore a distribution of weather as much as of physiology.

What has changed in the athletes is less discussed and probably real. Sprint training has become more specialized, the pool of competitors is larger, and the biomechanics of the start have been studied to a degree that was impossible before force plates and high-speed cameras. The best sprinters now reach top speed earlier and hold it longer, which is a coaching and physiological change.

So the fast times are a combination, and the argument about which factor dominates is not resolvable by watching races. It is resolvable by data: a track's stiffness can be measured, a shoe's energy return can be tested, and a timing system can be checked. The sport would benefit from publishing those numbers alongside the times, because a record is a claim about a performance, and a performance is only comparable if the conditions are known.
