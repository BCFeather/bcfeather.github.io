Stockholm's Karolinska Institute announced the medicine prize at half past eleven on Monday morning, and it went to Svante Pääbo, who is sixty-seven and works at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig. The citation names his discoveries about the genomes of extinct hominins and human evolution. He had been the favourite for years, and the prize had never gone to the field he founded. Asked at the press conference whether he had expected it, he said he had been surprised twice, once by the call and once by the noise of the cameras.

Pääbo began with mummies and with the DNA of ancient Egyptians, and the work moved slowly because old DNA falls apart into short fragments. His laboratory built clean rooms in which the air is filtered and the workers wear full suits, and it developed methods for telling a genuine ancient sequence from a modern contaminant. The Neanderthal project took four years, and its samples came from three bones found in a cave in Croatia. In 2010 he published a draft of the genome made from those individuals. A finger bone found in a Siberian cave in 2008 yielded a genome from a species nobody had described, and he called it the Denisovan.

The consequence was a change in the story of where modern humans come from. Comparisons showed that people outside Africa carry between one and four per cent of their DNA from Neanderthals, which means the two species met and had children. The mixing happened roughly fifty thousand years ago, and it left different traces in different populations. Denisovan sequences turned up in the immune systems of people in Oceania and in a gene that lets Tibetans breathe at altitude. Migration out of Africa became a series of encounters rather than a single departure.

His own history is part of the field's folklore. His father, Sune Bergström, shared the medicine prize in 1982, and Pääbo learned who his father was when he was a student. He trained in medicine and then in molecular biology, and he spent years on viruses before he turned to bones. He moved to Munich because Sweden at the time had no laboratory equipped for the work he wanted to do. Colleagues describe a man who reads the primary literature of four fields and who insists on doing the bench work himself.

The award is worth ten million Swedish kronor, and it opened this year's week of announcements. The committee's statement used the word palaeogenomics, a term that barely existed when Pääbo started. The field now has laboratories in several countries and a standard set of methods, and its findings have entered the teaching of human biology. Two of his former students run their own groups, and one of them works on the genomes of people who lived through the last ice age.

What remains is the question the bones keep asking. The genomes show that the species mixed, and they show roughly when, and they cannot show what the meetings were like. Pääbo has said in interviews that he would like to know how many children the encounters produced and whether the languages crossed. The next bones may come from caves in Asia, where the record is thinner and the conditions for preserving DNA are worse. The prize rewards the reading of the code, and the code has not yet been finished.
