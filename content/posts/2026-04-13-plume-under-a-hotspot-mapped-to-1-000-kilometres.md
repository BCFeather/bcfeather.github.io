A hotspot is a volcanic region that sits away from a plate boundary, and the standard explanation is a plume of hot rock rising from deep in the mantle. The explanation has been contested for thirty years, because a plume's seismic signature is weak against the mantle's own heterogeneity. A tomography study published this month imaged one from the surface to 1,000 kilometres, and the shape is a narrow column.

Data came from 240 broadband seismometers deployed for two years across a 600-kilometre array, plus 90 permanent stations. The team measured travel-time delays for 12,000 earthquakes and inverted for a velocity model with a resolution of 60 kilometres in the upper 800 kilometres.

The plume appears as a 1.5 per cent low-velocity anomaly, 300 kilometres wide, extending from 150 kilometres to 1,000 kilometres depth with a slight tilt. The width is the key number: a narrow column is what plume theory predicts and what earlier, lower-resolution studies could not resolve.

Compositional evidence supports the image. The hotspot's basalts have a helium isotope ratio that indicates a primordial reservoir, and the ratio's variation across the chain suggests the plume's head has been steadily melting as the plate moved over it.

The argument the image settles is about alternatives. A model in which volcanism comes from shallow decompression melting along a fracture would produce a broad, shallow anomaly, and the image shows neither breadth nor shallowness.

Continuity below 1,000 kilometres is unresolved. The anomaly fades below the transition zone in this model, and a lower-mantle continuation would need a broader array. Two groups are proposing one.

Limits are resolution and interpretation. A velocity anomaly is a temperature and composition mixture, and the paper argues for temperature on the basis of the anomaly's shape and the helium data rather than by separating the two.

The array's geometry is why the image is narrow. A plume is a vertical structure, and stations spread across the hotspot resolve it horizontally while stations at a distance resolve depth. The team combined a dense local array with distant stations, which is the standard trick for a narrow target.

Volcano monitoring uses the result. A plume's head feeds the hotspot's magma system, and the depth of the transition zone where melting begins is now constrained, which changes the estimate of the magma supply rate.

The hotspot's own history is the test the image passes. The chain of volcanoes records the plate's motion over 20 million years, and the image's tilt matches the direction the chain predicts. Agreement between two independent records is what turns an image into an argument.

The next measurement is a longer array. A 1,200-kilometre deployment would resolve the plume's base and test whether it connects to a lower-mantle structure, and the proposal is before two funding agencies with a 2029 start.

For a reader, the case illustrates how a contested hypothesis is settled. The plume argument was sustained by an absence of resolution, and an array with the right geometry resolved the structure. The remaining question is where the plume starts, which is a deeper and more expensive measurement.
