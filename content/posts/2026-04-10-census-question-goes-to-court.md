A census is the state's instrument for counting a population, and the questions it asks determine who is counted and who is missed. A proposed question has been challenged in court this month by a civil rights organization, which argues that it will reduce response rates among one group and therefore misallocate representation and funding.

The legal basis for the challenge is administrative procedure rather than the constitution. The agency must publish its question wording, test it, and justify it, and the challengers argue that the testing was inadequate for the group in question.

Testing data are the disputed ground. The agency ran a 30,000-household test and reported response rates of 68 per cent overall, and the challengers' expert reanalysis of the same data found a 52 per cent rate in one subgroup, which the agency's report presents as a footnote.

Mechanism is a known effect. A question that asks about a status a respondent fears disclosing depresses response, and the census has a long history of questions whose placement and wording were adjusted for that reason, including one about citizenship that was litigated and dropped.

Distributional consequence is the substantive issue. Census counts allocate seats and funding, and an undercount in one group shifts resources away from it. A response rate difference of 16 points in a subgroup of 3 per cent of the population shifts about 0.5 per cent of the total count.

The agency's defence is that the question serves a statutory purpose. A statute requires the data, and the agency argues that it has tested the wording and that the subgroup difference is within the range other questions produce.

Remedy available to the court is procedural. It can require further testing or a revised wording, and it cannot substitute its own question. A ruling is expected before the census's printing deadline.

The agency's testing protocol is the procedural question. Its standard requires cognitive interviews with 300 respondents per question, and the challengers argue the interviews for this question were conducted in two languages and skipped a group whose first language is neither.

Timing is the pressure. The census's printing contract has a 90-day lead, and a ruling after that date would force a supplemental questionnaire, which costs 40 million and is what the agency says it cannot afford.

The precedent is narrow on purpose. The court's earlier ruling allowed a question on citizenship and required the agency to explain its purpose, and this case turns on whether the explanation was written before or after the decision. That is a procedural question with a factual answer.

The statistical argument is about differential response. A question that some households answer and others skip changes the composition of the sample, and the agency's own analysis found a 3-point lower response among one group in testing. That is the harm the challengers quantify.

For a reader, the case is a reminder that a census is not a measurement of the population but a procedure that produces a number. The procedures are contestable, and the contest is about who is counted, which is the most political fact a statistic can carry.
