Chevron said on 23 October that it would buy Hess for about 53 billion dollars in shares, the second large deal in the American oil industry in two weeks after Exxon agreed to buy Pioneer for 60 billion. The deal was announced two weeks after Exxon's agreement for Pioneer, and the two together are the largest consolidation in the industry since the 1990s.

Guyana is the prize. Hess holds 30 per cent of the Stabroek block off the coast, where about 11 billion barrels of the oil have been found, and the production is expected to reach more than a million barrels a day by 2027. A field that is cheap to produce is a barrel that is profitable at any price. The block has been developed by a consortium with Exxon as the operator, and the production has risen from nothing in 2019 to about 400,000 barrels a day. The consortium includes Exxon and the China National Offshore Oil Corporation, and the partners have said the field will have four production vessels by 2027.

Shale is the base. Hess also owns about 400,000 acres in the Bakken field in North Dakota, and the output there is about 400,000 barrels a day. A shale asset is a production that can be turned on and off with the price. The Bakken production fell by about 5 per cent this year as the company cut the drilling, and the wells are among the most productive in the field.

Premium is modest. The offer values Hess at about 9 per cent above its price before the talks were reported, and the shareholders of Hess will own about 8 per cent of Chevron. A premium that is small is a deal that the buyer can defend as a merger of the equals. The offer is about 171 dollars a share, and the Hess shares closed at about 166 before the announcement, which the analysts called a full price. The Hess shareholders will receive 1.025 shares of Chevron for each of their own, and the deal is expected to close in the first half of next year.

Antitrust questions are smaller than for the Exxon deal. The two companies overlap in the Bakken, but the combined share there is about 10 per cent, and the lawyers expect a review rather than a challenge. A share that is small is a deal that closes. The Federal Trade Commission has been reviewing the Exxon deal for four months, and the lawyers say the Guyana asset is the part the regulators will not touch.

What the transaction shows is that the large oil companies are buying the reserves rather than drilling for them, and the reserves are what the shareholders reward. A buyer that pays in the shares is a buyer that uses the currency of its own valuation, and the valuation is what the market will test. The reserves are what the shareholders reward, and the reward is why the buyers pay in the shares rather than in the cash.
