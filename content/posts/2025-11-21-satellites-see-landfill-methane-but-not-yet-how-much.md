Methane is the second most important greenhouse gas and the one with the shortest lifetime, which makes it the cheapest lever for slowing warming within a decade. Landfills are among the largest anthropogenic sources, and they are also among the least measured: a site's emissions depend on how much waste was buried, how deep, how wet and how long ago, and the standard method is a model with coefficients rather than a measurement.

The survey reported in *Nature*, 13 November 2025 uses satellite observations to look at landfills worldwide and compares what the instruments see with what inventories assume. The result is a map of disagreement. Some sites emit far more than their reported figures, some far less, and the spread between methods is wide enough that a single global total carries little confidence.

Detection is easier than quantification. A plume of methane is visible to a spectrometer because the gas absorbs at a specific infrared wavelength, and a satellite passing over a site can see that a plume exists. Turning a plume into a mass per hour requires knowing the wind, the height of the plume and the concentration background, and each of those carries an error that propagates into the total.

The practical consequence is for reporting. National inventories are built from waste tonnage and default emission factors, and a country's number can be wrong by a large factor without anyone noticing. Satellites do not replace the inventory, but they can flag the sites where the inventory and the atmosphere disagree, which is where a measurement campaign should go first.

Landfills also differ from other methane sources in how they behave. A well, a pipeline or a compressor leaks from a point, and a plume is compact. A landfill leaks from an area, often through cover soil, and the emission is diffuse and variable in time as the waste decomposes. That geometry is what makes the satellite retrieval hard, and it is why the paper's uncertainty is a statement about method rather than about instruments.

What would reduce the uncertainty is repetition. A single overpass gives a snapshot; a year of overpasses gives a distribution, and the mean of a distribution is what an inventory needs. The task is less a better satellite than a better schedule, with revisit rates that match how the source varies.

The finding that matters politically is that the numbers are not settled. A country that claims a reduction cannot yet prove it from space, and one that is accused of underreporting cannot yet disprove it. Closing that gap is a precondition for any policy that treats methane as the fast lever it is.

Scale is part of the difficulty. A landfill is small relative to the pixel of a global survey instrument and large relative to the footprint of a targeted one, so the two classes of satellite answer different questions. Global instruments find the big plumes; targeting instruments quantify them, and the paper's comparison depends on combining both.
