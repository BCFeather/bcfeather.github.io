---

A reaction's rate is set by the barrier between reactants and products, and the usual way to cross a barrier is to raise the temperature. A mechanical force applied to a specific bond is another way, and it works on a different principle. In *Science* on 9 June 2023, that principle is demonstrated. The authors anchor a diene and a dienophile in a polymer and stretch the chain, and the force the chain transmits to the reacting groups accelerates the cycloaddition.

Selectivity is the design. A force applied to the wrong bond stiffens the wrong coordinate, and the anchoring point is chosen so the force pulls the two partners together along the reaction coordinate. Diels-Alder is well chosen for the test, because the reaction is concerted with a compact transition state, and the geometric change from reactant to transition state is exactly what a force can bias.

Measurement is single-molecule. A polymer's extension is controlled and events are counted, so the rate can be compared at several forces rather than inferred from a bulk average. Calibration is the technical crux, since a chain's tension comes from its extension through a model of elasticity and the model's error propagates into the rate, and two independent estimates are what make the number defensible.

Theory supplies the framework. A force tilts the energy landscape along one coordinate, and the tilt lowers a barrier in proportion to the distance between reactant and transition state along that coordinate. Heat is the natural control, because at a temperature where the thermal rate is measurable the force's contribution is a factor, and a factor growing with force is what the theory predicts.

Chemical consequence is a route to reactions heat cannot select. A force can favour one of two pathways differing in the direction of the geometry change, and that selectivity is unavailable to temperature. Bond selectivity makes it interpretable, since a force accelerating one reaction and suppressing its competitor has picked a coordinate, and a random effect would not show that pattern.

Scaffold sets the limitation. A polymer's force travels along its backbone, and a molecule that cannot be anchored at two points cannot be pulled. Preparative method stays unshown, because a single-molecule experiment is a proof of principle and a synthesis using force at scale needs a material in which many molecules are strained together.

Polymer chemistry gains a mechanism the field has exploited without measuring, since mechanochemical products have been observed for years and single-molecule rates put the mechanism on a quantitative footing. The practical horizon is a strained polymer reacting on demand, because a material storing mechanical energy as strain and releasing it as chemistry could heal or signal, and these rates are the input such a design needs. A different polymer with a different elasticity would separate the calibration's error from the chemistry's effect, and that control is the one a sceptical reader would want.
