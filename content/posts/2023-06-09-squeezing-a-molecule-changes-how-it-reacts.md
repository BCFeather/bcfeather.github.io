description: "Applying a directional force to the right part of a molecule accelerates a Diels-Alder reaction, according to work published in *Science* on 9 June 2023 that shows mechanical distortion can substitute for heat."
---

A reaction's rate is set by the barrier between reactants and products, and the usual way to cross a barrier is to raise the temperature. A mechanical force applied to a specific bond is another way, and it works on a different principle.

The demonstration in *Science*, 9 June 2023 uses that principle. The authors anchor a diene and a dienophile in a polymer and stretch the chain, and the force that the chain transmits to the reacting groups accelerates the cycloaddition.

The selectivity is the design. A force applied to the wrong bond stiffens the wrong coordinate, and the authors' anchoring point is chosen so that the force pulls the two partners together along the reaction coordinate.

The measurement is single-molecule. A polymer's extension is controlled, and the number of events is counted, so that the rate can be compared at several forces rather than inferred from a bulk average.

The theory is the framework. A force tilts the energy landscape along one coordinate, and the tilt lowers a barrier in proportion to the distance between reactant and transition state along that coordinate.

The Diels-Alder case is well chosen. The reaction is concerted and its transition state is compact, and the geometric change between reactant and transition state is exactly what a force can bias.

The chemical consequence is a route to reactions that heat cannot select. A force can favour one of two pathways that differ in the direction of the geometry change, which is a selectivity that temperature does not provide.

The limitation is the scaffold. A polymer's force is transmitted along its backbone, and a molecule that cannot be anchored at two points cannot be pulled.

What the paper does not show is a preparative method. A single-molecule experiment is a proof of principle, and a synthesis that uses force at scale needs a material in which many molecules are strained together.

For polymer chemistry, the value is a mechanism that the field has exploited without measuring. Mechanochemistry's products have been observed for years, and the paper's single-molecule rates put the mechanism on a quantitative footing.

The force's calibration is the technical crux. A chain's tension is inferred from its extension through a model of its elasticity, and the model's error propagates into the rate. The authors' combination of two independent estimates is what makes the number defensible.

The comparison with heat is the natural control. At a temperature where the thermal rate is measurable, the force's contribution is a factor, and a factor that grows with force is what the theory predicts.

The bond's selectivity is the interpretable part. A force that accelerates one reaction and suppresses its competitor is a force that has picked a coordinate, and a random effect would not show that pattern.

The practical horizon is a strained polymer that reacts on demand. A material that stores mechanical energy as strain and releases it as chemistry is a material that could heal or signal, and the paper's rates are the input to that design.
