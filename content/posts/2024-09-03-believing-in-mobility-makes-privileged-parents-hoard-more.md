description: "Across two studies of 2,557 American parents, high socioeconomic status combined with a belief that mobility is possible predicted stronger efforts to secure educational advantages for their children."
---


Societies that promise mobility describe it as a good in itself, and the promise is usually framed as one that everyone gains from. A paper in *PNAS* on 3 September 2024 finds that the promise has a second face, visible when the people who already hold advantages read it as a threat to their children's position. The gap between the promise and the outcome is where the interesting psychology sits, because a promise that everyone accepts can be used to justify an outcome that few accept.

The authors ran two studies with 2,557 American parents. The first was correlational: parents who believed that socioeconomic mobility was achievable were, if they were high in status, more inclined to secure valuable educational and economic resources for their own children. A correlational design can establish the association, and a second design is what the authors needed to show that the belief, rather than some other difference between families, was doing the work.

The second study manipulated the belief experimentally, telling parents that mobility was more or less likely, and the behaviour moved with the manipulation. That design turns a correlation into something closer to a causal claim. Manipulating a belief and then measuring a costly behaviour is a strong design, and the behaviour in question here is the allocation of money and time.

Opportunity hoarding is the term the paper uses is opportunity hoarding, and the behaviour it names is familiar from housing, from school admissions and from internships, where a family uses its resources to keep a scarce place within reach. Hoarding is a loaded word, and the paper uses it deliberately, since the behaviour is legal, common and rarely described in those terms by the people doing it.

Psychology is the interesting part. If a position is secure, there is no need to defend it; if a position is contestable, the belief that contest is fair makes defence feel legitimate, because the system is supposed to reward effort rather than inheritance.

American parents supply the sample, and the resources at stake are those a middle-class and upper-middle-class family can mobilize. Whether the effect appears in countries with different school systems, or among parents whose children face a different labour market, is untested. The two studies also differ in what they measure, since one asks about intentions and the other about behaviour under a manipulated belief.

Policy implications follow without being comfortable. A mobility narrative can raise support for the system while increasing the behaviour that closes it, and a policy that wants mobility to be real has to address the advantage that parents pass on rather than only the belief that they can. A policy that only widened access, without changing what parents can buy with their advantage, would leave the behaviour described here intact. The pairing of a competition narrative with a competitive advantage is the uncomfortable implication, and it is one that a mobility policy can either confront or ignore.
