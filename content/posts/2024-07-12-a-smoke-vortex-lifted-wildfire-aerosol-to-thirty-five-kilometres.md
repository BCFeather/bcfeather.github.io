Wildfire smoke usually stays in the troposphere, where rain can remove it within days. The Australian fires of 2019 and 2020 did something else, injecting material into the stratosphere, where aerosol persists for months and where chemistry that depends on particle surfaces becomes important. Satellites recorded the plume reaching heights that no smoke should have reached. The Australian episode stood out because the injection height broke every expectation set by earlier fires.

A paper in *Science Advances* on 12 July 2024 models that ascent and reproduces its trajectory. The plume travelled upward towards the middle stratosphere, to roughly 35 kilometres, at a speed and along a path that the authors attribute to a smoke-charged vortex, a structure the absorbing particles themselves induce and maintain. Reproducing the trajectory required resolving the plume's own radiative heating, not only the winds that carried it.

The mechanism is self-sustaining. Smoke absorbs sunlight, heats the air around it, and the warmed air rises; the rise draws in more aerosol, which absorbs more light, so a vortex forms and persists long enough to carry material across a scale height. Ordinary radiative transfer does not loft particles that far, which is why the observed altitude was a puzzle. Vortices of this kind are known from volcanic plumes, where sulfate particles play a comparable radiative role.

Once in the middle stratosphere, the smoke nearly doubled the southern hemispheric aerosol burden there. That redistribution changed where heterogeneous chemistry could happen, moving reactive surface area upward into a region where the background aerosol had been sparse. Aerosol at that altitude is not removed by precipitation, so the perturbation lasts for months rather than days.

Ozone responded in two directions. In the lower stratosphere the plume destroyed ozone, as smoke-driven chemistry does. Higher up, the extra aerosol surface area enhanced ozone production, and the authors estimate that this compensation offset up to 70 per cent of the loss below, so the column total moved far less than the profile suggested. Column measurements would therefore hide a redistribution that a profile measurement catches at once.

The caveat is that the result comes from a model constrained by observations rather than from a measurement of each term. Aerosol microphysics, the size distribution of the smoke particles, and the water content of the vortex all enter the calculation, and each carries uncertainty that propagates into the budget. Ensemble runs are the natural way to bracket that spread, and the paper reports a single configuration.

Warmer climate makes the mechanism more likely rather than less, since fire seasons lengthen and the plumes that reach the stratosphere become more frequent. If the vortex is a regular feature of extreme fires, then smoke has to be counted alongside volcanic sulfur as a driver of stratospheric aerosol, and the ozone accounting that follows has to include it. Whether such vortices become routine features of fire seasons decides how much weight they deserve in ozone accounting.
