The European Union agreed in the last week of August to build strategic stocks of a short list of critical materials, and to buy them through a joint mechanism rather than country by country. The list is narrow: rare earth elements, gallium, germanium, and a handful of processed metals that feed magnets, semiconductors and defence electronics. The volumes being discussed are measured in months of consumption rather than years, and the money attached is modest by the standards of the bloc's energy interventions. The design, though, is borrowed from an area where Europe has learned painful lessons.

Joint purchasing is what the EU built for gas during the energy crisis, and the logic transfers. Individual member states negotiating separately for a scarce commodity bid against each other and pay more; a single buyer with a stockpile can wait, can refuse a price, and can sign long-term contracts that individual countries could not finance. The commission's proposal also includes an obligation for larger consumers to report their stocks, which turns a set of national secrets into a shared picture, and a rule that lets the bloc release metal from the stockpile when prices spike.

The supply side is the difficult half. Europe has very little processing capacity for rare earths and almost none for gallium, which is mostly a by-product of alumina refining in a handful of countries. Building that capacity is slow, expensive and politically awkward, because refining produces waste and consumes large amounts of electricity, and the plants that do it are exactly the kind of industrial facility that European planning processes struggle to approve. The stockpile buys time; it does not create a supply chain.

The immediate effect is on contracts. Traders report that European buyers have begun asking for clauses that reference a benchmark price rather than a fixed one, and that some suppliers in Asia have started to treat European demand differently now that it arrives as a bloc. Nothing about the physical flow of metal has changed yet. What has changed is the expectation: a supplier who knows the buyer has a stockpile and a joint mandate knows that a price squeeze will be met with a drawdown rather than a panic.

Sceptics have a fair point, which is that a stockpile is a cushion, not a policy. If Europe's processing capacity stays where it is, the stockpile simply relocates the moment of scarcity, and the bloc will be buying insurance indefinitely. The commission's answer is that the stockpile is the first pillar of a wider package that includes faster permitting for refining, recycling targets for magnets and a fund for projects outside the union that could supply it. Those pillars are the ones that will take a decade to stand up.

The precedent matters beyond Europe. Japan, Korea and India have all built or expanded strategic reserves for similar materials in the past three years, and the United States has been funding domestic processing rather than stockpiling. If the European experiment works, the buyers of critical minerals will look less like customers and more like a cartel of consumers, which is a strange mirror image of the producers' arrangements that worried them in the first place.
