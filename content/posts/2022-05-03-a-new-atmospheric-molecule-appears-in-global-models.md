Atmospheric chemistry has a known inventory of odd oxygen, odd hydrogen and organic radicals, and the models that predict ozone and aerosol depend on the reactions among them. A molecule that had been omitted from that inventory has now been detected and measured. A molecule that has been invisible to instruments for decades is a reminder that the atmosphere is better characterized in its bulk than at its trace extremes.

The species is a hydrotrioxide, in which a carbon atom carries two oxygen atoms bonded to a third oxygen and a hydrogen, and it forms when a peroxy radical reacts with the hydroxyl radical. A study measured the reaction for several atmospherically relevant peroxy radicals, and appeared in *Science*, 26 May 2022.

Rate coefficients were close to the collision limit, which means that the reaction is as fast as physics allows and that every encounter produces a hydrotrioxide. That is the property that makes the omission consequential, because a fast sink for the hydroxyl radical changes the lifetime of the radical that drives most atmospheric oxidation. The reaction is a channel that competes with the reactions that the hydroxyl radical usually undergoes, and every per cent of the radical diverted is a per cent taken from the oxidation of methane and of every other species the radical touches. A rate at the collision limit means that the reaction is limited by how often the two radicals find each other, and in a polluted boundary layer that frequency is high.

For the degradation of isoprene, the most abundant biogenic volatile organic compound, global modelling predicts molar hydrotrioxide yields of up to 1 per cent, which corresponds to about 10 million tonnes formed each year. The lifetime of the molecule is minutes to hours.

A species with a short lifetime and a large production rate is a species that matters locally and hardly at all globally, and the modelling shows it acting as an intermediate that returns radicals to the pool rather than as a reservoir that removes them. A short-lived intermediate that returns radicals to the pool is a molecule that buffers the atmosphere against sudden changes in production and does so on a timescale that models treat as instantaneous.

The detection required a mass spectrometry method that could identify the weak signal of the trioxide group against the background of the chamber, and the assignment was confirmed by isotopic labelling. The measurement is the contribution, and the modelling follows from it. A chamber experiment is a controlled system, and a controlled system can establish a rate without demonstrating that the same reaction dominates in a forest or a city.

Whether hydrotrioxides affect ozone or aerosol formation at a scale that a policy would notice is the open question, and the authors propose it as the next target for the models. A newly identified molecule is not automatically an important one, and this one has to earn its place in the mechanism. A field that has settled its mechanism is a field that is vulnerable to a single new species, and the correction here is the kind that changes the numbers without changing the structure of the model.
