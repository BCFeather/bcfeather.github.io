---

A zeolite is a framework of silicon, aluminium and oxygen with pores of molecular width, and its function depends on where the aluminium sits and what occupies the pores. Conventional diffraction averages those details over a crystal.

The imaging in *Science*, 12 May 2023 resolves them. The authors use electron ptychography, a technique that reconstructs a specimen's phase from overlapping diffraction patterns, to map a zeolite's structure in three dimensions.

The principle is computational. A detector records the diffraction pattern from each position of a scanned beam, and an algorithm solves for the object that would produce all of them. The solution is a phase image with resolution below the microscope's aperture limit.

The result is that the framework is not uniform. Aluminium distribution varies from the crystal's core to its rim, and the pores' contents differ between regions, which a bulk measurement would report as one average composition.

The heterogeneity matters for catalysis. An acid site's strength depends on the aluminium's local environment, and a crystal with a gradient of aluminium has a gradient of activity.

The guest species are visible. Molecules adsorbed in the pores appear in the reconstruction, and their positions show which sites they occupy at a given loading.

The method's difficulty is the dose. A zeolite is beam-sensitive, and the electron dose that resolves atoms can also destroy the framework. The authors' low-dose scheme is what makes the measurement possible.

The comparison with tomography is instructive. A tilt series reconstructs a three-dimensional density, and ptychography reconstructs a phase with better resolution and fewer tilt angles.

The limitation is the crystal size. The technique works on thin specimens, and an industrial catalyst's particles are larger and more defective.

What the paper does not do is measure a working catalyst. An image is a state, and a catalyst's structure under reaction conditions is the next question.

For materials chemistry, the value is a check on a model. A structure determined by diffraction is an average, and an image that shows the variation is what tests whether the average was the right abstraction.

The aluminium gradient is a synthesis signature. A crystal grown quickly has a core that formed under different conditions from its rim, and the imaging reads that history. A synthesis that is tuned for uniformity is a synthesis that the technique can now verify.

The zeolite's industrial role is the motivation. Cracking and isomerization catalysts are zeolites, and a few per cent more active sites in the right place is a large change in a refinery's throughput.
The reconstruction is not a photograph. A phase image is a map of the electron wave's delay, and a heavy atom delays it more than a light one. The contrast is therefore a contrast in composition as well as in density, which is why aluminium and silicon can be told apart.
The scan's overlap is the algorithm's input. Adjacent probe positions share illumination, and the redundancy is what lets the solver converge. A scan with too little overlap produces an image that is consistent with the data and wrong about the object.
