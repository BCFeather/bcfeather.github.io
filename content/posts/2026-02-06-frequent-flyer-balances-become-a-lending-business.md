An airline's loyalty programme issues miles that can be redeemed for flights, and it sells those miles to banks that hand them to cardholders. The programme sold 4 billion dollars of future miles to a bank consortium this month, and the transaction is a secured borrowing dressed as a marketing partnership.

Accounting is the reason the structure exists. Miles sold in advance are cash received for a future obligation, and an airline that needs cash can sell more miles at a discount. The discount is set by the redemption rate, the share of miles that are eventually used, which the programme reports as 78 per cent.

Revenue recognition rules changed the incentive. Accounting standards require an airline to defer revenue on miles sold until they are redeemed, so a large advance sale improves cash without improving reported profit. That is why the deals are announced as partnerships rather than as financings.

Valuation is where analysts disagree. An outstanding mile is a liability at the cost of providing the seat, which for a domestic redemption is around 1.2 cents, against a sale price of 2.4. The gap is the programme's margin, and it depends on capacity that would otherwise fly empty.

Bank economics have shifted. A card issuer pays about 2 cents per mile and earns an interchange fee of 1.8 per cent on spending, so a cardholder who spends 30,000 dollars a year generates 540 dollars of interchange against 600 dollars of miles. The card is profitable on the annual fee and the interest, not on the miles.

Risk sits with the airline. A programme's liability grows with every mile sold, and the redemption rate can rise if award availability improves, which is what regulators in two markets have demanded. A higher redemption rate halves the margin on the miles sold.

Comparisons with hotels are instructive. Hotel programmes sell points at similar multiples and have faced the same regulatory pressure over availability guarantees, and two chains now publish an award calendar as a condition of their programme terms.

Regulatory attention is the risk in the structure. Two consumer agencies have asked whether miles sold in advance are a financial product, and an airline that treats them as marketing has no disclosure obligation. A finding that they are would bring the programme under rules designed for stored-value instruments.

The operational hedge is capacity. A programme's margin depends on seats that would fly empty, and an airline that fills its aircraft sells fewer award seats and faces a redemption backlog. Two carriers now publish an award calendar a year ahead to keep the redemption rate stable.

For a reader, the point is that loyalty schemes are financial instruments with a marketing surface. Miles are a currency issued by an airline, sold forward at a discount, and backed by seat capacity rather than by reserves. The credit quality of the programme is the airline's, which is why the consortium priced the deal against the carrier's rating.
