title: "Crystalline silicates shape the mid-infrared spectrum of disk surfaces"

description: "A model that tracks where silicate grains crystallize, mix vertically and drift outward finds that the ten micrometre feature is dominated by crystalline dust unless the inner disk is depleted of small crystals."
---

Silicate grains in a protoplanetary disk start out amorphous and become crystalline when they are heated above the annealing temperature, which happens in the hot inner region. The crystals then move, and mid-infrared spectra see whatever ends up on the disk surface, so the observed feature strengths depend on transport as much as on temperature. The annealing temperature is near 1,300 kelvins for the silicates of interest, so only the innermost astronomical units of a T Tauri disk qualify.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 1 May 2024 models that chain of events. The authors take a T Tauri disk, define the crystallization region from the crystallization and residence time scales, and compare radial mixing and radial drift to identify a region where vertical mixing keeps the surface coupled to the midplane. Residence time matters because a grain has to stay hot long enough to crystallize, which couples the crystallized region to the accretion rate.

The spatial distribution comes from DISKLAB, and the spectrum comes from MCMax. Together they allow the authors to ask which zone of the disk contributes to which feature, and the answer differs by grain size, since different sizes crystallize in different places. Surface crystals are well mixed with the midplane, and they spread outward by radial mixing while drifting inward, so crystalline dust appears at radii where annealing never occurred and the outer disk can show features its own temperature cannot produce.

Feature strengths respond to the assumed distribution. Varying the vertical and radial profiles changes the contrast between the crystalline bands and the amorphous continuum, so a spectrum is a probe of where the crystals are and not only of how many there are, and a profile that concentrates them near the surface brightens the bands against the continuum. Vertical mixing is what makes the surface spectrum report on the midplane instead of on a thin skin.

Modeled spectra agree with observations qualitatively, and one discrepancy is instructive. The modeled 10 micrometre feature is far too strongly dominated by crystalline dust, and only models with reduced crystallinity and a depletion of small crystals in the inner disk match the data, which differ from the standard assumption by a factor of two in the feature-to-continuum ratio.

The lesson is about what the observations mean. Mid-infrared light from the surface traces the radial distribution of small dust in the midplane, the inner disk dominates the shorter wavelengths and the outer disk the longer ones, and amorphization in the outer disk and sublimation near the star remove crystals, so a census is computed only after those losses are accounted for.

JWST is the instrument that will test this. The MIRI instrument covers the features with enough spectral resolution to separate the contributions, and the paper's framework is built to interpret those spectra instead of predicting a single number. The framework also predicts which apertures would separate the inner and outer contributions, a measurement the instrument can make.
