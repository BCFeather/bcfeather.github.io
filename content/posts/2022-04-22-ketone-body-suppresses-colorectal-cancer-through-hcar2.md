Ketogenic diets have been proposed as an adjunct in oncology for a century, and the evidence has been thin because a diet is hard to control and its effects are hard to attribute. The ketone bodies that the diet raises are molecules that can be studied directly. The diet raises ketones at the cost of a metabolic state that many patients cannot tolerate, and a molecule that reproduces the effect without the diet would be the more useful intervention.

A study in mice found that β-hydroxybutyrate, the main ketone body, reduced proliferation of colonic crypt cells and suppressed intestinal tumour growth. The effect ran through the surface receptor Hcar2 and the transcription factor Hopx. The work appeared in *Nature*, 27 April 2022. Hcar2 is also the receptor for butyrate, the short-chain fatty acid that the microbiota produces from fibre, and the two ligands converge on the same epithelial pathway.

Hcar2 is a G-protein coupled receptor that responds to butyrate and to β-hydroxybutyrate, and it is expressed on intestinal epithelial cells. The receptor is the reason the effect is local: a ketone body reaches the colon lumen and the epithelium, and the signalling it triggers changes the transcriptional programme of the cells that line the crypt. Hcar2 is the human orthologue of the mouse receptor, and the pathway is conserved, which is why the human organoid data are a reasonable extension rather than a separate story.

Hopx is the transcription factor that the pathway induces, and its induction is what links the receptor to the cell cycle. Organoid experiments showed that elevated β-hydroxybutyrate and active HOPX were associated with reduced epithelial proliferation, and single-cell sequencing of biopsies from patients with colorectal cancer reproduced the association.

Human data are correlational. A patient with a high ketone level may differ from one with a low level in diet, in insulin sensitivity and in the composition of the gut microbiota, and the observation that proliferation is lower in those patients is not a demonstration that the ketone caused it. Single-cell data from biopsies cannot establish causality, and the study presents the human findings as supporting evidence and not as a mechanism demonstrated in patients.

Dietary interventions are attractive because they are cheap and because a patient can adopt them without a prescription, and they are also difficult because adherence is low and the metabolic response varies. The paper supports the idea that a single metabolite can be delivered as a drug, which would remove the dietary variability.

A formulation is the next step that reaches the colon at a concentration the diet cannot sustain, and the authors point towards oral or systemic supplementation. Whether that produces the same suppression in a human tumour is the question that a trial would answer. Colorectal cancer prevention is the setting where a metabolite intervention is most plausible, because the epithelium turns over quickly and a modest reduction in proliferation accumulates over years.
