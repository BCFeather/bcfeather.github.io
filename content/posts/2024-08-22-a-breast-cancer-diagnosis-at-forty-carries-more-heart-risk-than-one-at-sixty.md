Survival after breast cancer has improved enough that cardiovascular disease now competes with the cancer itself as a cause of death. Whether the risk depends on the age at which the diagnosis was made had not been examined in a large cohort. Cardiovascular risk in this population has become a survivorship question rather than an acute one.

A paper in *eLife* on 22 August 2024 asks the question in the UK Biobank. The analysis covers 251,277 women with a mean age of 56.8 years, of whom 16,241 had breast cancer, and it follows myocardial infarction and heart failure over a median of 12.8 years. Diagnosis codes and dates at this scale are what no single-centre study could assemble over a comparable follow-up.

Propensity score matching puts the women with cancer against comparable women without it, and Cox proportional hazards models estimate the risk associated with age at diagnosis. Matching on age, body mass, smoking, diabetes and blood pressure removes the differences that would otherwise drive the comparison.

Each ten-year decrease in age at diagnosis raised the hazard of myocardial infarction by 36 per cent, with a confidence interval of 1.19 to 1.56, and the hazard of heart failure by 31 per cent, with an interval of 1.18 to 1.46. Both estimates are significant at the conventional threshold. Reported per decade, the hazard ratio is easy to translate into a schedule for surveillance. Two outcomes, infarction and failure, move together, which argues for a shared cardiac mechanism rather than two separate ones.

The direction is the interesting part. Younger women have fewer cardiovascular risk factors at baseline, so a higher relative hazard suggests that the treatment or the cancer itself does more damage when the heart is younger, or that the follow-up captures more years of exposure. If the treatment is equally cardiotoxic at any age, a younger woman has more years in which to accumulate the damage.

Anthracyclines and trastuzumab are the treatments most associated with cardiac injury, and the cohort predates some current protocols. The analysis cannot separate drug exposure from tumour biology, because the Biobank records diagnoses rather than regimens. Regimen-level data would be needed before the age effect could be attributed to a drug rather than to the tumour.

Implications for screening follow. A woman diagnosed in her forties may warrant cardiac surveillance into her sixties, and the current guidelines, which focus on age and on cumulative anthracycline dose, do not encode the age-at-diagnosis effect. What the study cannot show is causation, since observational cohorts are open to confounding by indication. It does show that the association is large enough to be worth a trial, and that the youngest survivors are the group to watch. A change to surveillance guidelines could follow from an association that no trial was designed to test. A woman diagnosed in her thirties would then be watched for thirty years, and the cost of that surveillance has not been estimated.
