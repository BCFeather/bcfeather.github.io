Rwanda began a national week of mourning on the seventh of April, thirty years after the killing began, with a ceremony at the memorial in Kigali where President Paul Kagame lit a flame that will burn for a hundred days, one for each day of the genocide. The commemoration opened with a march and with the naming of victims, and it was attended by several heads of state, including the president of France and the president of Israel.

History that the date marks began on the sixth of April 1994, when a plane carrying the presidents of Rwanda and of Burundi was shot down as it approached Kigali. Within hours, the presidential guard and militias began killing Tutsi civilians and moderate Hutu politicians, and the killing spread across the country over the following hundred days. About eight hundred thousand people were killed, according to the government's count, and most of them were Tutsi.

Failure of the international community is the part that foreign leaders addressed. The United Nations force in the country was reduced in the first days of the killing, and the Security Council did not describe the events as genocide until the middle of April. The American and French governments were criticized for their roles, and France in particular was accused of having trained and armed the government forces that took part in the killing.

Policy of the government since then has been built on the memory. Rwanda has prosecuted more than a hundred thousand people in local courts and has kept the history in the school curriculum and in an annual period in which public life stops. The government has also used the genocide as a justification for its tight control of the press and of political opposition, which critics say has turned commemoration into a instrument of rule.

Regional dispute that shadowed the ceremony concerned the responsibility of the government that now rules in Kigali. A report by a United Nations body and by researchers accused Rwanda's army of having killed Hutu refugees in Congo in the years after 1994, and Rwanda rejected the findings and said they were aimed at undermining the government. The president of Congo did not attend, and the two governments exchanged accusations in the weeks before the anniversary.

Consequence of the seventh of April is a country that has rebuilt itself and that has not settled its history. Rwanda's economy has grown at about seven per cent a year for two decades, and its capital is one of the cleanest and most orderly in Africa. Its politics remain closed, and the president has been in office since 2000.

What the anniversary established was that memory can be institutionalized and that the institutions which hold it are the same ones that hold power. The government also published a list of more than a thousand people who are still wanted for their part in the killing, and it said several of them had been traced to countries in Europe and in Africa. Diplomats said the anniversary had become an occasion for arguing about the past rather than for settling it.
