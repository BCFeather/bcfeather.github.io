---

Running from 27 February to 2 March at the Fira in Barcelona, the show drew about 88,000 visitors. That was short of the 109,000 of 2019 and well above the 20,000 of the first pandemic year. Exhibitors numbered 2,400, and the halls were full enough that the metro ran extra trains in the mornings. The organisers had expected 80,000 and said the final figure was higher because of late registrations.

Satellite connectivity was the theme that had not been on the agenda before. Chipmakers announced modems that could talk to low-orbit satellites, and two operators said they would offer messaging from space by the end of the year. The technology is limited to text for now, because the link budget does not support voice indoors. Several handset makers said they would ship the feature in the autumn. The satellite operators include one backed by a European consortium and one that launched its first batch of satellites in 2022. Regulators said the licensing for direct-to-device services would be settled country by country.

Private networks were the other centre of gravity. Industrial groups showed factories running their own radio systems on unlicensed spectrum, and equipment vendors sold the components as a package. The interest came from ports and mines rather than from offices, because those sites are large and wired networks are expensive to run. Regulators in three countries said they would release spectrum for the purpose within a year. The private networks ran on spectrum that in most countries is licensed to operators, which limits the market.

Open radio systems drew a crowd that was mostly engineers. The idea is to split the base station into software and hardware so that operators can buy from several suppliers, and the largest vendors have been slow to support it. A group of 50 operators signed a statement committing to buy open equipment for a third of new sites by 2025. Whether the equipment performs as well as the integrated systems remained the open question. Open radio equipment costs about 20 per cent less than integrated kit, according to one operator's estimate. The first large deployment was a rural network in Japan, and it covered 200 villages.

Chinese vendors had a smaller presence than in previous years, and European operators said they were reviewing their supplier lists. The debate about high-risk vendors had moved from politics into procurement, and the decisions were being taken site by site. One large operator said it would replace equipment in its core network but keep radio units for the time being. Two operators said they would publish their supplier lists for the first time this year.

Phones were the least interesting part of the show, as usual. The largest launches happened a week earlier at separate events, and the handsets on the stands were already on sale. What the show is for now is the network, and the network business is where the money and the argument are. Next year's dates were announced before the halls closed. The show's organisers said the 2024 edition would add a hall for computing and would run four days again.
