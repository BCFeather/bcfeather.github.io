Human brain organoids are clusters of cells that self-organise into something resembling cortex, and they have been useful precisely because human brain tissue is otherwise impossible to obtain at early developmental stages. Grown in a dish, they stop at a certain size because nutrients cannot reach the centre, and they lack the blood supply, immune cells and mechanical forces that shape a real brain. Transplanting them into an animal solves the supply problem and creates a competition problem, since the host's own neurons occupy the space and win the connections.

A team led by Sergiu Pașca at Stanford attacked the competition directly. The authors devised a genetic method to produce mice missing a cortical region, so that the grafted human tissue had somewhere to grow and something to do. Organoids implanted into these animals expanded, matured and formed functional neural networks, and the work was published in *Nature* on 17 September with open access.

What the grafts reproduce is the part of development that dishes cannot. The organoids developed cell types and patterns of organisation that appear later in human cortical development, and their activity became coordinated with the host brain instead of staying an isolated island of human cells. That coordination is the claim that matters, because it means the graft is participating in a circuit and not merely surviving in a rich medium.

Recording from human neurons inside a mouse is technically demanding, and the authors combine anatomical tracing with electrophysiology and imaging to make their case. The evidence is strongest for structural integration and for activity that follows the host's rhythms, and weaker for any statement about what the circuit computes. A network that fires together is not yet a network that thinks.

The model has an obvious use in disease research, since a human organoid carrying a patient's mutation can now be studied in an environment where it develops rather than degenerates. It also has an obvious hazard, which is that the boundary between a model of human development and an experiment on a human brain is set by ethics committees and not by the technology. Mice with human cortical tissue are not sentient in any sense that current frameworks recognise, and the frameworks should be revisited before the tissue becomes more complex.

Interpretation has to hold two things at once. The result is a real advance in the study of human neurodevelopment, and it is a mouse experiment, so every finding about human cells is a finding about human cells in a mouse brain. The field will need to compare what the graft does with what the same tissue does in human fetal specimens, which are scarce and ethically fraught.

The practical consequence is a better platform for testing compounds that act on human neurons during development, and a better platform for asking how psychiatric risk genes alter the shape of a cortex. Neither requires the animal to be more than a vessel, and the next round of work will be about making the vessel more faithful.
