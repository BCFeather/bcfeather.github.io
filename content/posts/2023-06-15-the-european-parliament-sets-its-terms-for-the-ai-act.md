description: "The European Parliament voted on 14 June 2023 by 499 to 28, with 93 abstentions, to adopt its negotiating position on the AI Act, backing bans on real-time biometric surveillance in public and on social scoring, and adding duties for providers of general-purpose models."
---

Members of the European Parliament adopted their negotiating position on the Artificial Intelligence Act on 14 June, with 499 votes in favour, 28 against and 93 abstentions. The margin was wide enough to end months of argument inside the chamber, and it moved the draft one step closer to the closed-door negotiations with member states that will decide its final shape. The act, proposed by the commission in April 2021, would be the first general law on machine learning anywhere, and its scope runs from a chatbot to a medical scanner.

The text that emerged from committee had been contested from both directions. Socialists and Greens wanted tighter limits on surveillance and on the use of algorithms in policing, while liberal and conservative groups warned that broad bans would push research and investment out of Europe. Industry federations circulated estimates of compliance costs, and civil society groups circulated evidence of systems already in use at borders and in welfare offices. The compromise that won the vote leans towards restriction on public uses and towards disclosure for the models that now sit underneath a large part of the software industry.

Real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces would be prohibited, with narrow exceptions for serious crime that require judicial authorization. The parliament also backed bans on social scoring by public authorities, on systems that exploit vulnerabilities of age or disability, and on biometric categorization that infers race, political opinion, religion or sexual orientation. Predictive policing tools based on profiling would be barred as well, and so would systems designed to manipulate behaviour in ways that cause harm.

For the first time in the legislative text, providers of general-purpose AI systems would carry obligations of their own. Those systems must disclose that content is machine generated, publish summaries of copyrighted training data, and let people object to being profiled. The heaviest duties fall on models judged to pose high risk, which would face documentation, testing and registration before entering the market.

Enforcement would run through a new European AI Office working with national supervisors, and fines could reach 7 per cent of global turnover for the most serious breaches, a level above the 4 per cent ceiling written into the data protection rules. Parliament also added a right for people to complain about an AI decision and to receive an explanation, a provision that member states have resisted because it implies obligations for public administrations.

The vote did not settle the argument. Industry groups said the bans on biometric systems would hamper security work, and digital rights organizations replied that exemptions written for one emergency tend to become permanent. Talks with the Council, which had not yet fixed its own position in June, are expected to run into the autumn, with the European elections of 2024 acting as a deadline and with several member states asking for delays in the obligations for smaller firms. What the parliament has established is a floor: any final text that removes the bans and the transparency duties will now need to explain why.
