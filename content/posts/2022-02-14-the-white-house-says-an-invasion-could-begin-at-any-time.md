description: "American officials warn that Russian forces around Ukraine are ready to attack, and the government in Kyiv says the warnings are damaging its economy."
---

American officials said in the second week of February that Russia had assembled enough troops around Ukraine to begin an attack at any time, and they put the number at more than 130,000 along the borders to the north, east and south. The national security adviser told reporters that an invasion could start before the Winter Olympics ended on 20 February, and that the intelligence was specific enough to describe how it might unfold. The State Department ordered the families of its diplomats out of Kyiv and then moved the embassy itself to Lviv, near the Polish border.

Troops moved to reassure the allies. About 3,000 American soldiers were sent to Poland, and more to Germany and Romania, and the British government sent instructors and anti-tank weapons to Ukraine. A telephone call between President Biden and President Putin on 12 February produced no agreement, and the Russian government said the American warnings were hysteria. Satellite photographs published by American companies showed field hospitals, fuel trucks and bridging equipment moving towards the frontier.

In Kyiv the reaction was more complicated than the warning implied. President Zelensky said publicly that his government saw no evidence of an imminent assault and that the constant talk of invasion was costing the country money, driving insurers away from its airspace and its ports. Officials complained that the Americans had described the attack in detail while declining to impose the sanctions that would deter it, and that the alliance had refused to say what it would do if the attack came. Some of the friction was political, and some of it came from a genuine difference in what each government could see.

The economic damage arrived before the soldiers did. Insurance for ships calling at Ukrainian ports became impossible to buy, and the cost of chartering a bulk carrier to carry grain rose by a third in a week. Foreign investors sold Ukrainian government bonds, the currency came under pressure, and the government in Kyiv said it had lost access to international borrowing. The army called up reservists for training, and the territorial defence units that had been formed after 2014 began to drill in public parks.

Russia kept the ambiguity going. It announced the end of exercises near the border and published pictures of trains leaving, while more equipment arrived at the same stations. The separatist republics in the east reported shelling along the line of contact that had barely moved in six years, and the observers of the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe recorded a sharp rise in ceasefire violations. On 21 February the Russian president recognized the two republics, and the question of whether there would be an invasion became a question of what kind.

History will record that the warnings were right and that they were heard as rhetoric. The government that received them had been told for eight years that an attack was possible, and it had learned that the sky did not fall. The soldiers on the border had heard the same reports, and they crossed it on 24 February.
