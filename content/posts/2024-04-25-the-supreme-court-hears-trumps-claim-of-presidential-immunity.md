description: "The Supreme Court heard arguments on 25 April on whether a former president can be prosecuted for acts taken in office, and the questions from the bench suggested that the justices would recognize some immunity without accepting the absolute version that Trump claimed."
---

Supreme Court heard about two and a half hours of argument on the twenty-fifth of April on whether Donald Trump can be prosecuted for conduct that took place while he was president, in a case brought by the special counsel who charged him with conspiring to overturn the election of 2020. The court took the criminality of the conduct as given for the purposes of the argument, and its answer would decide whether the trial could take place before the election in November.

Claim made by the defendant was absolute. His lawyers said a president cannot be prosecuted for any official act, and that the impeachment process is the only remedy that the constitution provides. They argued that without such protection, every president would face prosecution by his successor, and that the office would be weakened by the fear of it.

Questions from the bench were more practical than philosophical. Chief Justice John Roberts asked whether a president could be prosecuted for accepting a bribe in exchange for an appointment, and the lawyer for the government said he could. Several justices, including some in the majority, asked whether the case could be sent back to the trial court to determine which of the alleged acts were official and which were private, which is the outcome that most observers expected.

Argument from the government was that immunity of any kind would allow a president to commit crimes with impunity. The special counsel said the indictment described a private scheme to stay in power, conducted through private lawyers and through pressure on state officials, and he said the conduct fell outside the duties of the office. He also said the case had to proceed quickly because the election was in six months.

Consequence of the calendar was the part that mattered most. The trial judge had scheduled a trial for March and then postponed it while the immunity question was appealed, and the Supreme Court's decision, when it came on the first of July, returned the case to the district court for further findings. That meant no trial before the election, which is the outcome the defendant's strategy had been designed to produce.

Decision that followed was narrower than the absolute claim. The court held that a former president has immunity from prosecution for conduct within his core constitutional authority, and presumptive immunity for other official acts, and no immunity for unofficial acts. The practical result was a set of hearings in the autumn about which allegations fell into which category, and a case that would not be tried before the country voted.

What the argument of the twenty-fifth of April showed was that the court would define the boundary and that the definition would take longer than the election. The court had agreed in February to hear the case on an expedited schedule, and it did so without granting the defendant's request for a stay. Its decision, when it came, ran to more than a hundred pages and set out a framework that the trial court had to apply before the case could proceed.
