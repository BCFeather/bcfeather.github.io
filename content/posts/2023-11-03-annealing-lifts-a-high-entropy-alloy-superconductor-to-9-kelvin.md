High-entropy alloys mix several elements in near-equal proportions, and the resulting lattice disorder has proved friendly to superconductivity in a number of systems. Whether the disorder helps or hurts depends on how the material is made, and annealing is the cheapest knob available to a metallurgist. A eutectic alloy adds a second length scale, since two solid phases grow together and their boundaries scatter electrons as well as phonons.

Niobium, scandium, titanium and zirconium in equal measure form such an alloy, and its microstructure consists of a body-centred cubic phase and a hexagonal close-packed one. Samples were cast, then annealed at 800 and 1,000 degrees Celsius, with the lattice parameter of the cubic phase, the grain size of the eutectic structure, the critical temperature and the critical current density measured afterwards, according to a preprint posted on 1 November 2023.

The as-cast sample superconducted at 7.9 kelvins. Annealing at 800 degrees Celsius raised that to 9 kelvins, and annealing at 1,000 degrees Celsius brought it back to 8.7 kelvins, so the response is not monotonic in temperature. Both the lattice parameter and the grain size changed with the annealing temperature, which indicates that the heat treatment alters the microstructure and not only the chemistry of the alloy.

Lattice strain is the explanation the authors favour. Lower annealing temperatures leave residual strain in the cubic lattice, the measured parameter moves accordingly, and a strained lattice changes the electron-phonon coupling that sets the critical temperature. The density of states at the Fermi level was also considered, and it does not account for the pattern on its own. Strain relaxes as the annealing temperature rises, which is consistent with a maximum lying between the two extremes.

Measurements of the critical current density give the material a second figure of merit. In the as-cast state the self-field value at 2 kelvins exceeds \(10^{6}\) amperes per square centimetre, which is high enough for a bulk superconductor and places the alloy in the range where applications are discussed. Whether that current survives in an applied field is not reported, and the field dependence is what a magnet designer would ask for first.

Caveats follow from the design. One alloy and two annealing temperatures map a curve with three points, strain is inferred from lattice parameters rather than measured directly, and the density-of-states argument is left incomplete. Annealing time is a second variable that the study does not separate from temperature, although the two are coupled in every furnace. Other eutectic high-entropy alloys would test whether the maximum belongs to this composition or to the family.

The next experiments are straightforward. A series of annealing temperatures would locate the peak, a diffraction measurement under load would separate strain from composition, and transport in a field would show whether the current density is useful outside a laboratory. A material whose critical temperature can be tuned by heat treatment alone is easier to manufacture than one that needs a new composition for every improvement.
