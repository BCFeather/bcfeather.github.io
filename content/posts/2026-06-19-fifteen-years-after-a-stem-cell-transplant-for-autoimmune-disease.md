Autologous haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation has been used in multiple sclerosis and in other autoimmune diseases for two decades, on the reasoning that wiping out the immune repertoire and rebuilding it from the patient's own stem cells can reset a misdirected response. The procedure is risky, so it has been reserved for people who are failing conventional therapy. The procedure uses cyclophosphamide and anti-thymocyte globulin to ablate the immune system, then returns the patient's own stem cells, which have been harvested and frozen beforehand.

This condition damages the spinal cord and the optic nerve, which places it in the family of neurological autoimmune diseases that include neuromyelitis optica. A man and a woman who received the transplant have been in remission for more than fifteen years, according to the report in *Med* on 19 June. Both patients had failed conventional immunosuppression, which is the eligibility criterion that the transplant centres apply.

Fifteen years is a long follow-up for a procedure whose benefit is usually reported at two or five. Relapse in these diseases can be late, and a decade and a half without one is the kind of evidence that changes a risk-benefit calculation. The two patients were treated at a centre with experience in the procedure, and the report describes their neurological status at the last follow-up rather than at fixed intervals.

Two patients is not a trial. The report is a case series, and its value is in establishing that durable remission is possible rather than in estimating how often it occurs. The authors say a larger trial is warranted, which is the correct conclusion and also the expensive one. A trial would need a control arm of best medical therapy, and the diseases in this family are rare enough that a multicentre design is unavoidable. The report describes remission without maintenance therapy, which is the outcome that distinguishes a reset from suppression.

Mechanism remains an open question. Transplant resets the immune system, and whether the benefit comes from the reset itself or from the intensive immunosuppression that precedes it is unresolved, since both happen together. A trial that compared transplant against the conditioning alone would answer it and would be ethically difficult. Fertility, secondary autoimmune disease and infection are the late complications that a fifteen-year follow-up can speak to, and the report addresses them.

Where this leaves clinical practice is a narrow indication. For a patient with an aggressive neurological autoimmune disease who has failed several lines, a procedure with a real mortality risk and a fifteen-year remission on the other side of the balance is a genuine option. For anyone else it is not, and the report should not be read as widening that boundary. The report also provides a benchmark for informed consent, since a patient considering the procedure now has a fifteen-year outcome to weigh against its risks. The report's value is that it turns a theoretical benefit into a documented one, and that is what a trial proposal needs.
