The Hall effect is a transverse voltage in response to a longitudinal current and a magnetic field, and its quantum versions have been a testing ground for topology. A nonlinear Hall effect needs no magnetic field, and its origin has been a question.

The measurement in *Science*, 14 July 2023 answers it for one system. The authors observe a nonlinear Hall response in a topological antiferromagnetic heterostructure and attribute it to the quantum metric, a geometric quantity that describes how wavefunctions change across momentum space.

The distinction is subtle. A Berry curvature produces a Hall response that is linear in the field, and a quantum metric produces one that is quadratic in the current, and the two contributions can coexist.

The material is the platform. An antiferromagnet with a topological band structure breaks the symmetries that would forbid the response, and the heterostructure's interface adds the inversion asymmetry needed.

The antiferromagnet's advantage is the absence of a stray field. A ferromagnet's magnetization produces a field that complicates a transport measurement, and an antiferromagnet's does not.

The measurement is the test. The authors vary the current and the temperature, and the response's scaling distinguishes the quantum metric's contribution from a competing mechanism.

The theory is the framework. A wavefunction's quantum metric is the real part of the quantum geometric tensor, and its integral over occupied states enters the nonlinear conductivity.

The implication is a design principle. A material whose bands have a large quantum metric is a material with a strong nonlinear response, and the metric is computable before a material is made.

The limitation is the temperature. The effect is measured at low temperature, and a device would need it at room temperature.

What the paper does not show is a device. A response is a physical effect, and a rectifier or a detector built from it is the application that the result enables.

For condensed matter, the value is a geometric quantity made measurable. The Berry curvature has had a decade of attention, and the metric is its less-studied partner.

The symmetry analysis is the gate. A response of a given order requires a symmetry to be broken, and the authors' enumeration of the allowed terms is what tells them which measurement to make.

The heterostructure's interface is the ingredient. A single material with inversion symmetry forbids the effect, and a stack that breaks it permits one.

The scaling with current is the discriminator. A quadratic response grows as the square of the drive, and a linear one does not. The authors' current dependence is the cleanest evidence.

The disorder's effect is the caveat. A sample with domains averages the response, and a single-domain sample is what the authors' field-cooling produces.

The comparison with the Berry-curvature Hall effect is the useful frame. Both are geometric, and the difference is which part of the wavefunction's geometry does the work.

The material's magnetic order is the tunable knob. An antiferromagnet's order parameter can be reoriented, and a response that follows the order is one that a field can switch.
