The Hall effect is a transverse voltage in response to a longitudinal current and a magnetic field, and its quantum versions have been a testing ground for topology. A nonlinear Hall effect needs no magnetic field, and its origin has been a question. In *Science* on 14 July 2023, that question is answered for one system. The authors observe a nonlinear Hall response in a topological antiferromagnetic heterostructure and attribute it to the quantum metric, a geometric quantity describing how wavefunctions change across momentum space.

Subtlety lies in the distinction. Berry curvature produces a Hall response linear in the field, a quantum metric produces one quadratic in the current, and the two contributions can coexist. Scaling with current is the discriminator, because a quadratic response grows with the square of the drive while a linear one does not, and the current dependence is the cleanest evidence.

Material supplies the platform. An antiferromagnet with a topological band structure breaks the symmetries that would forbid the response, and the heterostructure's interface adds the inversion asymmetry needed. Absence of a stray field is the antiferromagnet's advantage, since a ferromagnet's magnetisation produces a field complicating transport, and an antiferromagnet's does not.

Symmetry analysis gates the effect. A response of a given order requires a symmetry to be broken, and an enumeration of allowed terms is what tells the authors which measurement to make. Interface is the ingredient, because a single material with inversion symmetry forbids the effect and a stack breaking it permits one. Measurement tests the claim. Current and temperature are varied, and the scaling separates the metric's contribution from a competing mechanism. Disorder is the caveat, since a sample with domains averages the response, and field-cooling produces a single domain.

Theory supplies the framework. A wavefunction's quantum metric is the real part of the quantum geometric tensor, and its integral over occupied states enters the nonlinear conductivity. Design principle follows, because a material whose bands have a large metric has a strong nonlinear response, and the metric is computable before the material is made.

Temperature sets the limitation, as the effect is measured cold and a device would need it at room temperature. A device is what the paper does not show, since a response is a physical effect and a rectifier or detector built from it is the application the result enables.

Condensed matter gains a geometric quantity made measurable, since Berry curvature has had a decade of attention and the metric is its less-studied partner. Magnetic order is the tunable knob, because an antiferromagnet's order parameter can be reoriented, and a response following the order is one a field can switch. A gate-tunable stack would let the authors vary the band filling and watch the response follow the metric, and that dependence is the test that would separate geometry from an artefact of one sample's bands. A material with a larger metric would make the effect easier to use, and a calculation across a family of antiferromagnets is how such a material would be found before one is grown.
