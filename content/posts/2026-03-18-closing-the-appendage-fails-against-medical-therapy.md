description: "In 912 patients with atrial fibrillation and a high risk of both stroke and bleeding, left atrial appendage closure did not meet noninferiority against physician-directed medical care over three years."
---

Left atrial appendage closure is an appealing idea with an awkward evidence base. The appendage is where clots form in atrial fibrillation, and sealing it off should reduce stroke without the bleeding that anticoagulation causes. Trials of the device against warfarin were designed to show noninferiority, and they did, in populations whose anticoagulation was often poorly controlled. Against a modern direct oral anticoagulant, the comparison has not been made.

This trial made it. A total of 912 patients were randomized, 446 to device closure and 442 to physician-directed best medical care, and the paper reports the results in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 18 March. The population was old and sick: mean age 77.9 years, 38.6 per cent women, a mean CHA\(_{2}\)DS\(_{2}\)-VASc score of 5.2 and a mean HAS-BLED score of 3.0, which is a group with a high risk of stroke and, separately, a high risk of bleeding.

The primary endpoint went the wrong way. A first event of stroke, systemic embolism, major bleeding or death occurred in 155 patients in the device group, an incidence of 16.8 per 100 patient-years, against 127 patients and 13.3 per 100 patient-years in the medical group. The difference in restricted mean survival time was minus 0.36 years, and the test for noninferiority returned a *P* value of 0.44, which means the trial did not establish equivalence.

Serious adverse events were common in both arms, 82.5 per cent against 77.4 per cent, which is what a three-year follow-up of an elderly population with atrial fibrillation produces. The excess in the device arm includes the procedural complications of implantation and the later consequences of a device that has to be managed, and it is the part of the trial that clinicians will weigh against the theoretical benefit.

Interpretation turns on what the medical arm received. Physician-directed best care in a trial like this includes anticoagulation, rate control and treatment of comorbidities, and it is a control that has improved since the device trials of the previous decade. A device that was noninferior to warfarin can be inferior to the care that replaced warfarin, and the trial is a demonstration of how a comparator's improvement can retire a technology.

The result will not end the practice. Device closure is used for patients who cannot take anticoagulation at all, and that population is not the population this trial randomized, since everyone enrolled had to be a candidate for medical therapy. For the patient with a history of intracranial haemorrhage and no acceptable anticoagulant, the trial answers a question that was not asked.

What it does answer is the question that guidelines were written around, and the answer is that closure is not equivalent to good medical care in a high-risk older population. A procedure that carries procedural risk, requires lifelong device management and produces more adverse events has to show a benefit against the alternative, and this trial did not.
