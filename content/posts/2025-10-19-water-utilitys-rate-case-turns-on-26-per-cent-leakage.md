A water utility's books have a line for water that never reaches a customer, and the line's size determines what a regulator will let the utility charge. The utility in this case reports 26 per cent unaccounted-for water and has asked for a 14 per cent rate rise to replace mains whose locations it is still mapping.

Measurement is the first obstacle and the harder one. Unaccounted-for water is production minus billed consumption, and it includes leakage, meter error and unbilled use. A utility that cannot separate the three cannot tell a regulator what a replacement program will achieve.

Acoustic sensors on 12 per cent of the mains found 340 breaks in a year, and the utility extrapolates to 2,800 across the network. The extrapolation is what the regulator has challenged.

Asset records are the deeper gap. A third of the network was installed before 1970 and its as-built drawings are on paper in a warehouse, and the utility's mapping project has digitized 60 per cent. A replacement program without a map is a program that digs up the wrong street.

Regulation treats leakage as an operating cost with a target. A utility that beats its target keeps the saving; one that misses it is penalized. That structure rewards a leak repair program and does not fund a main replacement, because the replacement's benefit is a lower future cost that the current rate base does not capture.

The utility describes its own mains as beyond repair, which is an argument about replacement rather than about repair. A cast-iron main from 1925 fails at a rate of 12 per 100 kilometres per year, against 2 for a ductile main, and the failures' cost includes a road closure and a boil-water notice.

Fairness enters through the neighbouring utility. That utility replaced its mains in the 1990s and charges 30 per cent more, and the customers in this city have paid less for 30 years and now face a larger bill at once.

Non-revenue water is measured as a difference between two meters, and both have errors. A treatment plant's outflow meter and a customer's billing meter each carry an accuracy class, and a utility that reports 26 per cent leakage is reporting a quantity whose uncertainty is a fifth of the number.

Repair economics decide the target. A utility's cheapest leak is a burst main under a road, and the cost of digging is 10 times the cost of the pipe. A program that replaces mains on a schedule spends its budget on pipes that are not leaking, and a program that repairs reacts to bursts that have already done damage.

Deferred costs behave this way: cheap to postpone and expensive to inherit. Leakage targets are cheap to meet and easy to measure; a replacement program is expensive, hard to measure, and the only thing that stops a network from failing. Regulators are built to prefer the first.
