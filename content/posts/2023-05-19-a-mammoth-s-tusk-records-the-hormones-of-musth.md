---

A tusk grows the way a tree grows, in layers, and each layer records the chemistry of the animal at the time it was deposited. A tusk is therefore a diary, and the entries are chemical.

In *Nature* on 18 May 2023, that diary is read for hormones. The authors sample dentin from a mammoth tusk and from modern elephant tusks, measure testosterone and its metabolites by liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry, and compare the profiles.

Periodic rises are the finding. The mammoth's record shows testosterone peaks resembling the musth cycles of living male elephants, a state of heightened aggression and reproductive readiness that recurs annually. Biology supplies the interpretation, since musth is a living elephant's adaptation to competition, and a mammoth showing the same cycle was an animal with the same social structure of males competing for seasonal access to females.

Technique is the innovation. A hormone is a labile molecule and a fossil's proteins are degraded, so the extraction and the mass spectrometry detect steroid residues in dentin thousands of years old. Growth supplies a cross-check, because a tusk's annual layers can be counted, and a hormone peak coinciding with a growth increment is dated by the animal's own calendar, which turns a chemical signal into a seasonal one.

Resolution sets the limit. A tusk's layers give a seasonal record at best, and a musth cycle of weeks falls below the sampling interval, so the pattern that emerges is annual rather than behavioural. Sex is the premise of the inference, since musth is a male phenomenon and a tusk's hormones can be read as musth only if the animal was male.

Taphonomy is the caveat. A tusk preserved in permafrost retains organic molecules and one buried in temperate soil does not, so applicability depends on where the fossil was found. Provenance matters as well, because a tusk with known stratigraphy and cold burial can be dated, while an isolated tusk with neither is a curiosity rather than a dataset. Contamination is the methodological caution, since dentin that has exchanged ions with groundwater carries a signal that is not the animal's, and comparing internal and external layers addresses it.

Behaviour stays beyond the evidence. A hormone profile is a physiological state, and the aggression musth produces is an inference from the living analogue. Growth offers a secondary finding, because the layers also record age and nutrition, and combining hormones with growth in one archive is what makes the specimen valuable. Palaeontology gains a route to physiology: bones have been read for diet and climate, and a tusk read for hormones extends the archive to the endocrine system. Whether the technique survives a temperate fossil is the durability question, since permafrost is the exception among mammoth sites rather than the rule. A tusk from a female would be the control the method lacks, since a female's testosterone profile should be flat and a male's should not.
