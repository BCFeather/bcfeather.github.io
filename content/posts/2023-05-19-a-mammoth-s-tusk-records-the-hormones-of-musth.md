title: "Mammoth's tusk records the hormones of musth"

description: "Testosterone measured in layers of a mammoth's tusk shows annual cycles like the musth of living elephants, according to a study published in *Nature* on 18 May 2023 that reads behaviour from fossil tissue."
---

A tusk grows the way a tree grows, in layers, and each layer records the chemistry of the animal at the time it was deposited. A tusk is therefore a diary, and the entries are chemical.

The study in *Nature*, 18 May 2023 reads that diary for hormones. The authors sample dentin from a mammoth tusk and from modern elephant tusks, measure testosterone and its metabolites by liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry, and compare the profiles.

The comparison is the finding. The mammoth's record shows periodic rises of testosterone that resemble the musth cycles of living male elephants, a state of heightened aggression and reproductive readiness that recurs annually.

The technique is the innovation. A hormone is a labile molecule, and a fossil's proteins are degraded. The authors' extraction and their mass spectrometry detect steroid residues in dentin that is thousands of years old.

The resolution is the limit. A tusk's layers give a seasonal record at best, and a musth cycle of weeks is below the sampling interval. The pattern that emerges is annual rather than behavioural.

The biology is the interpretation. Musth is a living elephant's adaptation to competition, and a mammoth that showed the same cycle was an animal with the same social structure: males competing for access to females in seasonal bouts.

The taphonomy is the caveat. A tusk that has been in permafrost preserves organic molecules, and one that has been in a temperate soil does not. The method's applicability depends on where the fossil was found.

What the paper cannot show is behaviour. A hormone profile is a physiological state, and the aggression that musth produces is an inference from the living analogue.

The secondary finding is about growth. The tusk's layers also record the animal's age and its nutrition, and the combination of hormones and growth in one archive is what makes the specimen valuable.

For palaeontology, the value is a route to physiology. Bones have been read for diet and for climate, and a tusk read for hormones extends the archive to the endocrine system.

The specimen's provenance matters. A tusk with a known stratigraphic context and a cold burial is a tusk whose record can be dated, and an isolated tusk with neither is a curiosity rather than a dataset.

The methodological caution is isotope contamination. A dentin sample that has exchanged ions with groundwater carries a signal that is not the animal's, and the authors' comparison of internal and external layers addresses that.
The growth record is a cross-check. A tusk's annual layers can be counted, and a hormone peak that coincides with a growth increment is a peak that the animal's own calendar dates. The alignment of the two records is what turns a chemical signal into a seasonal one.
The sex is the inference's premise. Musth is a male phenomenon, and a tusk's hormones can only be read as musth if the animal was male. The specimen's anatomy is the premise, and the paper states it.
