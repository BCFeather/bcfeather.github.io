Gram-negative bacteria are hard to kill for a structural reason. They carry two membranes, and the outer one is a barrier of lipopolysaccharide that keeps out large molecules, including most antibiotics that work elsewhere in the cell. On 9 April, a group publishing in *Nature* described a compound, isolated from a soil actinomycete and then optimized, that attacks the assembly of that barrier rather than anything behind it.

The target is the Lpt system, the seven-protein machinery that ferries lipopolysaccharide from the inner membrane, where it is made, across the periplasm to the outer surface. Blocking the last step of that transit leaves the molecule stranded, and a bacterium with a half-built outer membrane becomes permeable to compounds it normally excludes. The compound binds a site on LptD that is conserved across the enterobacteria the team tested, which is the kind of conservation that suggests the site cannot be changed cheaply.

Numbers look promising and are early. Minimum inhibitory concentrations against a panel of 42 clinical isolates of *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Acinetobacter baumannii* and *Escherichia coli* clustered between 0.5 and 4 micrograms per millilitre, including against carbapenem-resistant isolates. Spontaneous resistance frequencies in fluctuation tests came in at below 10^-9, which the authors present as evidence that the target is difficult to mutate rather than as proof that resistance will not emerge under treatment.

Animal data are thin but real. In a mouse model of bloodstream infection, a single dose given six hours after inoculation reduced bacterial load by roughly two orders of magnitude relative to untreated controls, and survival at 72 hours rose from 12 to 78 per cent. The compound was tolerated at the doses used, with reversible liver enzyme elevation at the top dose. One model, one route of administration, forty animals.

Toxicity is the open question, and it is a fair one. Lipopolysaccharide is also the molecule that drives septic shock, and a drug that liberates fragments of it could, in principle, trigger the inflammatory cascade it is meant to prevent. The team measured cytokine levels and found no rise above controls in treated animals, and they note that the released material is still membrane-bound rather than free in circulation. That is a plausible answer to the objection, not a settled one; the first-in-human study will be the test.

What the result changes is the target class. Antibiotic discovery has spent two decades screening for inhibitors of protein synthesis, cell wall assembly and DNA replication, and finding mostly variants of existing scaffolds. The outer membrane's construction was considered inaccessible because the enzymes involved sit in a crowded periplasm and are hard to assay. This paper is the first clean demonstration that the last step can be blocked in a living cell with a small molecule that gets into the periplasm at all.

A reader should hold two things at once. A new mechanism with low resistance frequency and activity in animals is genuinely rare, and this is the most promising report of the past several years. A compound that survives to market needs a Phase 3 trial, a manufacturing route and a price that a hospital will pay, and the failure rate from this point is above 90 per cent.
