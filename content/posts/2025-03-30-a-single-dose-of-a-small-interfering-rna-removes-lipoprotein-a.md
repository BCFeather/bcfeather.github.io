title: "Single dose of a small interfering RNA removes lipoprotein(a)"

description: "A single injection of lepodisiran lowered lipoprotein(a) by a time-averaged 93.9 percentage points over the following six months, according to a trial in *The New England Journal of Medicine*."
---

Lipoprotein(a) is a particle that carries cholesterol and is built around apolipoprotein(a), a protein whose gene came from a duplication and whose concentration is set almost entirely by inheritance. Levels vary by a factor of a thousand between people, and the variation is not explained by diet. Epidemiologists have known for decades that a high level predicts heart attack and stroke independently of LDL, and the absence of a drug that lowers it has been a standing gap in prevention.

Statins do not lower lipoprotein(a), and the fibrates and niacin that were tried either failed to reduce events or produced side effects that outweighed them. What changed the field is the technology of small interfering RNA, which silences a gene by destroying its messenger, and the same chemistry that silenced *PCSK9* has been applied to *LPA*, the gene that encodes apolipoprotein(a).

Molecule in the new trial is designed to last. A conventional siRNA effect decays in weeks, and the developers added a modification that extends the duration of silencing, which is what allows a dose every six months instead of every month. That interval is the difference between a drug that a patient will take and a drug that a patient will stop.

Trial gave a single 400 milligram dose to participants with elevated lipoprotein(a) and followed them for six months. The time-averaged reduction, measured against placebo over days 60 to 180, was 93.9 percentage points, which is close to the maximum that a silencing approach can achieve. The concentration fell within weeks and stayed low, and the effect had not fully worn off at the end of the follow-up.

A phase 2 trial measures a marker, and the outcome trial is the one that decides whether lowering lipoprotein(a) prevents events. The company has started one, and the design enrolls patients with established cardiovascular disease and a high level, which is the population in which the epidemiology predicts a benefit. The trial will take years, because events accumulate slowly in a population that is already treated with statins.

Safety profile was that of the class. Injection site reactions occurred more often than with placebo, liver enzymes did not rise, and there was no signal in platelet count or in the immune response. A drug that is given twice a year has an advantage in adherence and a disadvantage in reversibility, because a side effect that appears after an injection cannot be undone quickly.

What the trial shows is that a target that was previously untreatable is now treatable, and that the obstacle was the delivery of a nucleic acid to the liver and not the biology of the particle. Whether it should be treated is the question that the outcome trial answers, and the answer will decide the fate of a class that has three competing molecules and, as of this trial, no evidence that any of them prevents a heart attack in a population already treated with statins.
