Japan's core consumer prices, which exclude the fresh food, rose by 3.2 per cent over the year to May, down from 3.4 per cent in April. The headline was 3.2 per cent, and the measure that also strips the energy rose by 4.3, which is the highest since 1981. The figures were in line with the forecast. The measure has been above the 2 per cent target for seventeen months, and the peak was 4.2 per cent in January. The Tokyo figure for June, published a week later, showed the same pattern, with the core at about 3.2.

Energy did the work on the headline. The electricity fell by about 17 per cent over the year, and the gas by about 12, as the government's subsidies were paid through the utilities from January. The fuel rose by about 1 per cent, and the effect of the support is scheduled to be reviewed in the autumn. The subsidy is paid to the utilities and the refiners, and the government has set aside about 2 trillion yen for it this year. The wholesale electricity price has fallen by about 30 per cent since the winter, and the effect will show in the summer bills.

Food kept rising. The prices of the food rose by about 8 per cent, and the one of the eggs by about 18 as the bird flu cut the flocks. The restaurant prices rose by about 4, and the services outside the restaurants by about 2. The rice rose by about 3 per cent, and the vegetables fell by about 4 as the harvest arrived.

A narrower measure is the one the bank watches. A rise of 4.3 per cent means the pressure is broader than the food and the energy, and the services are the part that the wages feed. The bank has said it needs the core-core to settle near 2 per cent before it moves. The rents rose by about 2 per cent in the largest cities, and the one of the hotels by about 10 as the inbound travel recovered.

A weak currency has been the background. The currency fell to about 145 against the dollar in late June, the weakest since the autumn, and the import prices have been rising again. The pass-through to the consumer prices has been slower than in the past, and the bank says the effect will show with a lag. The import prices rose by about 2 per cent in May, and the one of the food and the energy by about 5.

This report keeps the bank in its corner. A core at 3.2 with the core-core at 4.3 is the split that the governor has described as the one that lets the bank wait, and the waiting is the policy. The bank meets in July, and the market expects the cap on the ten-year yield to be widened rather than removed.
