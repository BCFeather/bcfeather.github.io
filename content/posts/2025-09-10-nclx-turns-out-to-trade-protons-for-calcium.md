description: "Cryo-electron microscopy and reconstitution show that the mitochondrial calcium exporter runs on a proton gradient, not on sodium as its name implies."
---

Calcium leaves a mitochondrion through a protein called NCLX, and the protein has carried a sodium-calcium label since it was cloned. The label came from pharmacology: the transporter needs sodium in the assays, and inhibitors that block sodium-calcium exchange block it as well. Structural work over the past decade supported the assignment without resolving the mechanism, so the sentence that mitochondria export calcium by sodium exchange has been repeated in reviews ever since.

A paper in *Nature* reports cryo-electron microscopy structures of NCLX in several conformations and measures what the protein actually transports. The architecture shows a dimer, with each protomer built from two domains that open and close in alternation, which is the alternating-access arrangement that ion exchangers share. The structures resolve the calcium site and, unexpectedly, a pair of acidic residues that face the proton path. The dimer interface is extensive, and mutations that break it abolish transport without changing the fold of either protomer.

Functional assays decide the question. Reconstituted protein moves calcium against a proton gradient and does not require sodium, and the stoichiometry comes out at one calcium for two protons. The authors confirm the coupling in liposomes and then in a cellular context, where the exchanger draws on the proton gradient that the respiratory chain maintains across the inner membrane. In cells the export rate fell when the proton gradient was collapsed by an uncoupler, which is the test a sodium-dependent model fails.

The reassignment changes how the protein is modelled and how it is drugged. Sodium-calcium exchange in heart muscle is a long-standing drug target, and if NCLX is a different transporter, the pharmacology built on that assumption needs revisiting. The paper ties NCLX malfunction to heart failure and to neurodegenerative disease, and the mechanism now offers a route for molecules that act on the exchanger directly. Molecules designed against the old assignment would bind a site that this protein does not have.

Structural states also show where the ions bind. The acidic residues that coordinate protons sit at a different depth from the calcium site, and the protein alternates between inward- and outward-facing states through an intermediate that neither map captures cleanly. The cryo-electron microscopy maps leave density for lipids and for the dimer interface, which the functional work tests mutation by mutation. The intermediate is the state in which the two domains sit half-open, and the map for it came from a mutant that traps it.

Two limits are worth stating. The structures come from a detergent-solubilised protein, so the lipid environment of the inner membrane is absent, and the reconstitution cannot reproduce every regulator that a mitochondrion supplies. Whether proton coupling survives inside a living cell, with the accessory proteins of the inner membrane present, is the experiment the field will run next. The detergent is mild, but a detergent is not a membrane, and the lipid composition of the inner leaflet sets the free energy of the states.
