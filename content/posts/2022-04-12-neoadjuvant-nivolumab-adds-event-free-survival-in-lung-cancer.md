Adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery for non-small-cell lung cancer adds a few percentage points of survival, and neoadjuvant chemotherapy has been used for decades without a clear survival advantage over the adjuvant route. Immunotherapy changed the calculation, because a checkpoint inhibitor given while the tumour is still in place can recruit an immune response against antigens that surgery would remove. A tumour that is present at the time of vaccination carries its own antigens in the draining lymph nodes, and the immune response it generates can travel to micrometastases that imaging does not detect.

CheckMate 816 tested nivolumab plus platinum chemotherapy before resection against chemotherapy alone, in patients with stage IB to IIIA disease, with three cycles before surgery. The study randomized 358 patients and reported event-free survival and pathological complete response as dual primary endpoints. The paper appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 11 April 2022.

Pathological complete response, defined as no viable tumour in the resected lung or lymph nodes, occurred in 24.0 per cent of the nivolumab group and 2.2 per cent of the chemotherapy group. Median event-free survival was 31.6 months with nivolumab and 20.8 months with chemotherapy, a hazard ratio of 0.63. A pathological complete response rate of 24 per cent in a population that would otherwise receive chemotherapy and surgery is the largest improvement reported in this setting.

Surgery was performed in 83 per cent of the combination group and 75 per cent of the chemotherapy group, and the difference is part of the benefit rather than a side note, because a patient who reaches resection has a chance at cure that a patient who progresses before surgery does not.

The pathological response is a surrogate, and the trial was not powered to detect a difference in overall survival at this analysis. Event-free survival is the endpoint that regulators accepted for perioperative immunotherapy, and the overall survival data will come later. The trial was open-label, which matters for the assessment of adverse events and not for the pathological endpoint, because the pathologist who read the specimen was blinded.

Safety was consistent with the components: immune-related adverse events were more frequent with nivolumab, and 8.9 per cent of the combination group discontinued treatment because of adverse events. Neoadjuvant immunotherapy in a patient who may be cured by surgery alone is a trade-off that has to be explained rather than assumed.

This trial enrolled patients whose tumours were considered resectable by a multidisciplinary team, which is a selected group. For a thoracic surgeon, the result makes a preoperative checkpoint inhibitor plus chemotherapy the standard for stage IIIA disease, and the debate now concerns the patients at stage IB and the role of adjuvant immunotherapy afterwards. A patient with a stage IB tumour has a good prognosis after surgery alone, and adding two drugs to the preoperative regimen for a small absolute gain is the kind of decision that guidelines committees argue over.
