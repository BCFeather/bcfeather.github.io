Reptiles have been the neglected class in conservation, because the charisma that funds a panda does not extend to a skink and because many species are small, nocturnal and hard to survey. The absence of an assessment was read as an absence of a problem. An assessment requires a Red List category for each species, and the category is assigned by a workshop of specialists who review the data on range, population trend and threats.

A global assessment evaluated 10,196 species against the criteria of the International Union for Conservation of Nature, using the expertise of more than 900 specialists and a workshop process that took years. The results appeared in *Nature*, 27 April 2022. A threatened category requires a documented decline, a restricted range or a small population, and the workshop applies the same criteria that are used for birds and mammals.

The assessment found that at least 1,829 species, or 21.1 per cent, are threatened, which confirms an earlier extrapolation and represents 15.6 billion years of phylogenetic diversity. The figure is similar to the proportion in amphibians and mammals and lower than in cycads and conifers. A group that is diverse in deserts and poor in forests is a group whose risk is concentrated where people are, and the pattern is a statement about land use as much as about climate.

Threats are the same ones that affect other tetrapods: agriculture, logging, urban development and invasive species. Climate change is a threat whose magnitude for reptiles remains uncertain, because a ectotherm's response depends on microhabitat and on the availability of thermal refuge. An ectotherm that can retreat underground for the hottest hours is less exposed to a warming climate than a mammal that must forage, which is why the threat from climate change is uncertain for reptiles.

Forest reptiles are more threatened than those in arid regions, which contradicts the expectation that a group that is diverse in deserts would be most at risk there. The explanation is that forests are where the agricultural and logging pressure is concentrated, and the diversity in arid regions is a matter of adaptation rather than of safety. The surrogate finding is worth acting on because it means that a reserve designed for birds also protects the reptiles that live in the same forest, and the planning does not need a separate exercise.

Birds, mammals and amphibians turn out to be good surrogates for reptile conservation, which is a useful finding because it means that existing protected areas and existing programmes cover many reptiles by accident. The exception is the threatened species with the smallest ranges, which tend to be isolated from other threatened tetrapods and need targeted action.

Crocodiles and turtles need urgent species-specific work, and the rest can be protected by the habitat and trade measures that are already in place for other groups. The assessment converts a gap in knowledge into a list of priorities, which is what a conservation agency needs in order to act. The assessment covers 10,196 species against a described total of more than 11,000, and the remainder are data deficient, which is a group that a future assessment will have to handle.
