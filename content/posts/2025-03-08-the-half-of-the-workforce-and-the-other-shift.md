The eighth of March is marked with flowers, a discount in a department store and, in some workplaces, an afternoon off. Behind the gesture is a set of numbers about work, and the numbers are more interesting than the gesture. Women are just under half of the employed in the country, they are a larger share of the graduates of the universities than of the senior posts in the companies, and they do the majority of the unpaid work in the household.

Official statistics track the first two of those facts; the third they have only begun to track. Time-use surveys, which ask a sample of households to record what they did in fifteen-minute blocks, produce a figure that is consistent across countries and has barely moved in twenty years. Women spend roughly twice as much time on housework and care as men do, and the difference persists in households where both adults work full time. The unpaid work is not counted in the GDP and it is not counted in the pension, and both of those omissions follow the person for decades.

On the labour market, the facts are more encouraging and more complicated. The participation rate of women in the country is high by international standards, above the average of the rich economies, and it rose through the 1990s as manufacturing absorbed labour from the countryside. What has happened since is a change in the composition rather than the level, with more women in the service sector, in the professions, and in the civil service, and fewer in the factories that moved or automated.

The gap that the statistics show most clearly is at the top. Women are about a third of the managers in the private sector and a smaller share of the boards, and the difference is not explained by qualifications, which are on average higher for women among recent graduates. The explanations that the research supports are the interruption that childbirth imposes and the penalty that follows it, and the fact that promotion tracks reward uninterrupted tenure. A woman who takes six months out at thirty is, on the evidence, behind her cohort for the rest of her career.

What has changed in the last decade is the policy conversation about childcare. The supply of nursery places, the cost of them and the hours they keep are now treated as an infrastructure question rather than a family one, and the number of places has grown, unevenly, across provinces. The companies that have moved fastest are the ones competing for skilled staff, and the ones that have not are the ones where the staff are easier to replace.

Even now the day is a strange mixture of a celebration and a reminder. The flowers are sincere, the discount is a marketing decision, and the half-day is a concession that some employers make and others do not. What the eighth of March is actually good for is the annual publication of the numbers, which arrive in the same week in every newspaper, and a number that is published once a year is a number that somebody has to defend.
