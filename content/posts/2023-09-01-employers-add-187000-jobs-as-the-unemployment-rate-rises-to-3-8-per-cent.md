Employers added 187,000 jobs in August, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on 1 September, and the unemployment rate rose to 3.8 per cent from 3.5. The gain was below the pace of the previous year and close to the mark that keeps the workforce growing. Economists had expected about 170,000, and the report was the first since the strike by the actors and the walkout by the car workers began to distort the calendar.

Unemployment rising alongside hiring is the report's puzzle. The rate rose because the labour force grew by more than the number of jobs, which means people who had stayed out of work came back and had not yet been hired. A rate that rises for that reason is a rate that reflects supply. The participation rate rose to 62.8 per cent, and the share of the adults who are working or looking is now above its level before the pandemic.

Average hourly pay grew by 0.2 per cent on the month and 4.3 per cent over the year, the slowest annual pace since the middle of 2021. The wage is the number the Federal Reserve has watched most closely, and a slower one is a signal that the shortage is easing. The pay rise of 4.3 per cent is still above the pace that the Fed considers consistent with its target, and the productivity has not improved enough to absorb it. The share of the workers in the private services who received a pay rise has fallen for three quarters, which the surveys connect to the weaker hiring plans.

Sector detail tells the story. Health care added about 100,000 jobs and the restaurants and the hotels added more, while the transport and the warehousing lost jobs as the parcel volumes fell. An economy that adds care workers is an economy whose demand has shifted. The government added fewer jobs than in July, and the leisure sector hired at about half the pace of the spring as the summer season ended.

Revisions removed 110,000 jobs from the two previous months. The revisions have been negative for several months, and the economists read the pattern as evidence that the boom of the summer was weaker than it appeared. A number that is revised down is a number that was forecast too high. The three-month average is now about 150,000, which is roughly the pace that keeps the unemployment rate steady in a population that grows by that much.

Traders read the report as good news for the rates. Yields fell and the traders kept the odds of a November increase low, which is the reaction of a market that wants the tightening to end. A slower wage is a peak in sight. The next meeting is in September, and the futures market put the chance of an increase at about a quarter, down from a half before the report.
