European Union governments adopted a fifth package of sanctions against Russia on 8 April, ending imports of Russian coal, closing European ports to ships under the Russian flag and restricting exports of advanced technology. The package had been negotiated for a week, and the coal ban was its most visible element, since it was the first prohibition on Russian energy that the union had agreed to since the invasion began. The package was published in the official journal the following day, which is when its provisions took legal effect.

The coal measure covers about four billion euros of trade a year, a small share of the union's energy bill but a substantial share of the seaborne coal market, and it came with a transition period so that existing contracts could be wound down. Germany and Poland, the two largest consumers, had opposed an immediate cutoff, and the compromise language reflects their concern about replacing supplies and about the cost to utilities that had signed long-term deals. Coal was a modest share of the union's imports from Russia by value, but the ban mattered because it was the first energy commodity the union was willing to stop buying.

Port provisions went further than the coal clause. Vessels registered in Russia, or owned, chartered or operated by Russian interests, were barred from European ports except for defined purposes such as humanitarian deliveries, and Russian and Belarusian road hauliers lost access to the single market, which matters for goods that travel overland through Poland and the Baltics. Port states in the Mediterranean wanted the exception list to be narrower, and the final text added a ban on Russian vessels using European shipyards for repairs.

The export controls were the part that will be felt for years. The package restricted sales of quantum computers, advanced semiconductors, sensitive machinery and software to Russia, and it went beyond a pure export ban by prohibiting European firms from providing technical assistance, maintenance and training related to the listed goods. That turns a customs measure into an industrial one. European firms had supplied machine tools and testing equipment to Russian factories for decades, and the list was drafted to cover what could not be replaced quickly.

Its package also added names to the asset freeze list, barred transactions with four more Russian banks, banned deposits of more than a hundred thousand euros into accounts held by Russian nationals, extended the prohibition on high-value goods and suspended Russian participation in European public procurement. It was, in the commission's framing, designed to hit the industrial base that sustains the army rather than the population at large. The commission said the measures had been coordinated with the United States, Britain and other partners, which had adopted similar lists.

Oil was left out, and everyone knew why. Crude and gas were the two commodities on which Hungary, Slovakia, Austria and others depended, and a unanimous vote was required. The coal ban showed what was politically possible in April, and the arguments over oil that ran through the spring would test whether that ceiling could be raised without splitting the union.
