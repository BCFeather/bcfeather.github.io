Whether the built environment changes how much people move has been argued for decades, and the argument has been hard to settle because the studies were weak. Cohorts were small, activity was self-reported by people who knew what the question was, and most designs compared different people in different places, which cannot separate the street from the person who chose to live on it. The literature on the built environment has been long on plausible mechanisms and short on designs able to test them.

A paper in *Nature* on 13 August 2025 uses a natural experiment instead. Its authors took objectively measured step counts from smartphones belonging to 2,112,288 users in the United States, covering 248,266 days of activity, and matched them to 7,447 relocations between 1,609 cities. Each relocation compares a person with themselves before and after the move, which removes the self-selection problem that cross-sectional work could not remove: the same person, two addresses, one measurement device.

Walkability was scored by the usual composite of intersection density, destination mix and transit access. Moving from a city at the 25th percentile to one at the 75th added about 1,100 steps a day on average, and moving in the other direction removed a similar amount. The added activity was mostly moderate to vigorous, not gentle strolling, and the effect held across sex, age and body mass index, which is the pattern a public-health argument needs.

Duration is the part that answers the obvious objection. A change that fades in a few weeks would be a novelty effect, and the paper reports the increase persisting over three months after the move. It also reports tests against residential self-selection, since people who care about walking may choose walkable places for reasons that have nothing to do with the streets, and the within-person design is what makes those tests possible at all. Three months is long enough to rule out a novelty effect and short enough to miss a seasonal one.

The limits are those of phone data. Users of a step-counting app are not a random sample of the population, coverage varies by city and by phone, and the walkability score is a property of a metropolitan area, not of the street a person lives on, so a walkable block inside a car-dependent city is averaged away. Relocations also tend to arrive with job and household changes that the model cannot fully separate from the address.

What the study supports is a planning claim with a number attached: the layout of a city is one of the levers on population activity, and it works at the scale of a city and not of a single development. What it cannot show is whether the extra steps translate into less disease, which needs health outcomes and a longer window than three months, and which is the question a transport ministry will ask before it funds anything. A step count is a proxy for activity, and proxies are where policy arguments usually break.
