title: "A cell found only in human lungs rebuilds the alveoli"

description: "Human respiratory bronchioles contain a secretory cell that acts as a unidirectional progenitor for alveolar type 2 cells, a population absent in mice and transcriptionally altered in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease."
---

Mouse lungs have been the workhorse of airway biology, and the human lung is not a scaled version of them. The distal human airway branches interweave with the gas-exchange region to form respiratory bronchioles, a structure with no mouse counterpart, and the cells that maintain it have been uncharacterized because there was no animal model in which to study them. Comparative anatomy explains the gap: the mouse lung lacks respiratory bronchioles, so a cell population that lives there cannot be found in the animal that most airway research uses.

A study of human tissue identified a secretory population in the respiratory bronchioles that is distinct from the secretory cells of the larger proximal airways, and used organoid cultures to show that these cells act as progenitors for alveolar type 2 cells. The work appeared in *Nature*, 30 March 2022.

The progenitor relationship is unidirectional, which means that the secretory cells give rise to alveolar type 2 cells and not the reverse. Alveolar type 2 cells produce surfactant and act as the stem population of the alveolus, so a cell that feeds them from the airway is a new entry in the lineage diagram of the lung. Lineage tracing in organoids establishes the direction of the relationship, and tracing in human tissue is impossible, so the claim rests on the culture system.

Notch and Wnt signalling regulate the transition. Both pathways are familiar from other stem cell systems, and their involvement suggests that the airway niche controls the supply of alveolar cells rather than leaving it to the alveolus alone. Whether the signalling can be manipulated to expand the progenitor pool is the practical question for a therapy, and Notch and Wnt are both druggable pathways.

In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease the secretory cells were transcriptionally altered, and the alveolar type 2 cells in those lungs occupied abnormal states that were also seen in humans and ferrets exposed to smoke. The implication is that the progenitor population is part of the disease process rather than a bystander. Smoke exposure in ferrets reproduced the human transcriptional changes, which is the closest available test of the hypothesis that the progenitor is involved in disease.

Organoids are a model and not the tissue, and the study is descriptive in the sense that it identifies a population and its relationships without demonstrating regeneration in a living lung. The ferret experiments go some way towards that, since the ferret has a distal airway structure closer to the human one than the mouse does.

The clinical relevance is regenerative medicine for emphysema, where alveoli are destroyed and the lung has no approved way to rebuild them. A progenitor that sits in the airway and can be reached by an inhaled therapy is a more tractable target than one buried in the alveolar wall. An inhaled therapy would have to reach the respiratory bronchioles, which are deep in the lung, and delivering a molecule to that depth is a problem that has not been solved for any drug.
