Retraction is the part of science that rarely gets written up, and this one took four years and a public argument. On 26 June 2024 *Nature* withdrew a 2020 paper that had reported microbial DNA signatures distinguishing one cancer type from another, sometimes, the authors said, with accuracy approaching perfection. The journal's notice states that expert post-publication review confirmed that some findings are affected and that the conclusions are no longer supported. All authors agreed to the retraction, *Science* reported the same day.

The original work compared microbial DNA in blood and tissue from people with cancer against databases of known microbial genomes, and trained models to recognise tumour type from the microbial signal. It drew follow-up studies, a company called Micronoma founded by two of the authors, and a lung-cancer blood test that received a breakthrough-device designation from the Food and Drug Administration.

Criticism came from Britain, where researchers noticed that the paper's marker list for bladder cancer included bacteria associated with seaweed. Steven Salzberg of Johns Hopkins University joined them in a preprint, later published in *mBio*, which described major errors in the analysis. The central charge was that human DNA had not been filtered out properly, so that reads assigned to microbes could have come from the patient.

Money complicated the case. A retraction removes a paper, and a product built on it stays on the market, and *Science* reported that a National Cancer Institute researcher who had used the 2020 data set in a 2022 paper in *Nature Communications* was now deciding whether to redo the analysis or retract his own work.

The technical argument is narrower than the headlines suggested. A classifier that separates tumour types from microbial reads can be right about the separation and wrong about the cause, since the signal may be human sequence misassigned, contamination from reagents, or a genuine microbial trace whose origin is not the tumour. The retraction settles that the analysis as published cannot carry the claim. It does not settle whether any microbial signal exists.

One author's position is worth recording. Rob Knight of the University of California San Diego told *Science* that the retraction was warranted and that the team still believes the central claim, namely that the microbiome can separate tumour types, and intends to show it with better methods. That is the ordinary shape of these disputes: a specific analysis fails and a general claim survives in the authors' estimation until someone tests it again.

Post-publication review is what this episode marks out. Two outside critics, a preprint and a newspaper were enough to force a journal to commission a review that the original peer review had not, and the review took a year and a half to close. to force a journal to commission the review that the original peer review had not. The cost fell on the paper, and the tool that made the finding famous, a machine-learning classifier, was the same tool that made its errors hard to see.
