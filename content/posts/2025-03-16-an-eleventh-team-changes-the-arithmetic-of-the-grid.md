Formula One will have eleven teams in 2026, when a programme backed by General Motors and branded Cadillac joins the grid alongside ten existing entries. The expansion coincides with the largest change in the technical regulations since 2014: power units that produce half their output from the electrical component, fuel that is entirely sustainable, and aerodynamic rules that replace the current floor-and-wing package with active surfaces. The new entrant will build its own chassis and buy its power unit, which is the arrangement the regulations allow for a team that is not a manufacturer.

Commercial arithmetic is what makes an eleventh team difficult. The sport's Concorde Agreement divides prize money among the teams, and a new entrant takes a share without adding to the pool in its first years. The existing teams agreed to a dilution payment that the new entrant makes, spread over a decade, and that payment is the price of entry. A dilution payment of two hundred million dollars, spread over ten years, is the number that has been reported and not confirmed.

A cost cap is the mechanism that makes an eleventh team viable at all. A budget ceiling of roughly a hundred and thirty-five million dollars per year, with exceptions for driver salaries and for capital projects, means a new team does not have to outspend Ferrari to be competitive. A cap that is enforced by audits and by a penalty regime has changed the sport from a spending contest into a project management contest. A cap that excludes driver pay means a team can still spend fifty million on two drivers, and that exception is the largest remaining inequality.

The 2026 power units are the risk that every team is managing. A manufacturer that gets the electrical deployment wrong loses a second a lap for a season, and the regulations freeze the specification for years. The new entrant is buying a power unit from an existing manufacturer rather than building one, which removes that risk and adds a dependency. A manufacturer that builds its own power unit spends outside the cap on the development, which is why the engine rules are a separate negotiation.

Aerodynamics is where the new rules bite hardest. Active front and rear surfaces mean the car's downforce changes with speed under the control of the driver, and the simulation tools that teams rely on were built for a fixed geometry. A team that models the transition well gains more than a team that simply has more wind tunnel time. A team with less wind tunnel time than its rivals has to be better at choosing which concepts to test, and the choice is made by engineers rather than by money.

What the eleventh team changes is the market rather than the racing. Twenty-two cars, twenty-two drivers and one more sponsor inventory is a sport that has grown its product while its revenue per team stays flat. The newcomers will be judged on whether they can score points in three years, and the incumbents will be judged on whether they let them. A grid of twenty-two cars is also a grid of twenty-two drivers, and the seats that open are the ones at the back.
