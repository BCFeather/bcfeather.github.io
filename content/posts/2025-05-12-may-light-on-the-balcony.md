The light changes in the middle of May. For a fortnight it has been bright and flat, the kind that makes photographs look overexposed and shadows look like smudges, and then one morning the angle is different and the balcony is divided into strips: sun on the railing, shade on the tiles, a bright band across the wall where the window frame cuts the beam. It lasts about three weeks, this angle, and then the sun climbs too high and the balcony becomes a place to dry things rather than to sit.

The plants notice before I do. A basil on the sill that had been leggy and pale turns a deeper green within a week, and the tomatoes that were sulking in the flat light start to set fruit. A neighbour who grows orchids says the same thing every year, that the plants know the season before the calendar does, and that the trick is to move them at the right moment rather than to feed them more. She has a theory that the light in May is stronger in the ultraviolet than the light in August, which is not true, but the plants behave as though it were.

City crews are doing their spring cleaning. In the compound below, the management company has hired a crew to trim the plane trees, and the branches that come down are stacked on the pavement in bundles that children climb on until someone shouts. The smell of cut wood goes through the courtyard for two days, which is one of the few smells that a city shares with a village. The crew works from a truck with a chipper on the back, and the chips are left in a heap for anyone who wants them, which is how the courtyard's rose beds get their mulch.

Evening lasts longer now. At seven the sky is still blue, and the balcony is warm enough to sit on without a jacket. People who have not spoken to each other all winter begin to talk across the gap between buildings, which is a distance of perhaps fifteen metres and, in the cold months, an unbridgeable one. A man on the fourth floor has started practicing the trumpet again, badly, and the woman opposite has taken to commenting on his progress, which is a form of friendship that only the warm evenings allow.

There is a specific pleasure in the third week of May, before the heat arrives. The air is warm but not heavy, the mosquitoes have not yet discovered the balcony, and the city's noise carries further in the evening than it does in July, when humidity dampens everything. It is the last fortnight in which sitting outside is a choice rather than an endurance test.

By the end of the month the tiles will be too hot to walk on in the afternoon and the basil will need water twice a day. That is fine. A season is best appreciated in the ten days when it is still becoming itself, and the balcony, which faces west and collects the afternoon, is a good place to watch the change happen.
