The fourth global coral bleaching event was declared in April 2024, and the declaration was a statement about geography and not about a single reef. Bleaching had been reported in the Pacific, the Indian Ocean, the Red Sea, the Caribbean and the Great Barrier Reef within the same season, which is the pattern that earns the word global, and the months that followed added the Gulf of Mexico and parts of the western Pacific to the list. The declaration came from a monitoring programme that had been issuing alerts for two decades, and its wording was careful: an event is global when reports arrive from several basins inside twelve months.

The mechanism is a partnership breaking down. Corals build their skeletons with algae of the genus Symbiodinium living inside their tissue, and the algae photosynthesise and pass most of the carbon to the coral in exchange for shelter and nutrients. Warm the water a few degrees above the summer maximum, and the algae's photosystem is stressed, producing reactive oxygen that damages the coral's cells; the coral responds by expelling the algae, and the white skeleton that remains is the colour of the limestone underneath. The expulsion is a process that runs over days and not in a single act, and a colony can take its algae back if the water cools in time, which is why a pale reef and a white reef are in different states. The distinction is one that the monitors try to keep, because a reef that has bleached and recovered reads very differently from one that has bleached and died, and the two look much the same from a boat.

Temperature is the trigger that the records can see. Satellites measure the sea surface, and the index used to describe stress is the number of degree-heating weeks, which accumulates the amount by which the water exceeds the local bleaching threshold. The 2023 and 2024 heatwaves pushed that index past the thresholds of many reefs at once, and the El Niño that began in 2023 spread the warm water across ocean basins instead of confining it to one. The threshold itself is set for each reef from its own temperature record, so the same absolute reading can be stressful in one place and ordinary in another, and a map of stress is a map of anomalies. The record behind those thresholds is shorter than the lifespan of the corals themselves, which means the baseline is a recent average and not a natural one.

Recovery depends on the species. A bleached coral is not a dead coral, and a colony that regains its algae within a few weeks usually survives; one that stays white for a month loses tissue and dies. Fast-growing branching corals bleach and die quickly, massive Porites bleaches late and recovers slowly, and the mix of species on a reef after an event is therefore different from the mix before it, which is the part that matters for the reef's structure. Reefs that bleached in 2016, again in 2020 and again in 2024 have had no interval long enough to rebuild, and the gaps between events are now shorter than the time a colony needs to recover. A branching coral that dies is gone within weeks, and the space it leaves is colonised by algae instead of by another coral.

Limits of the measurement are known to the people who use it. A satellite sees the temperature of the surface, not the coral, and a survey sees a transect, not a reef system; the two are combined into an estimate that is honest but coarse. Reports of bleaching come from divers, from rangers and from researchers, unevenly distributed across the tropics, and the reefs with no observers are the ones the maps fill in by inference. On the Great Barrier Reef the surveys are close to a census, while in parts of the western Indian Ocean the estimate rests on a handful of sites, and the difference does not always show in the headline figure. The programme separates observed bleaching from modelled bleaching in its reports, and the second number is always the larger.

What would help is not in doubt, and it is not a single measure. Local stress, from runoff, from fishing and from anchoring, reduces the temperature a reef can take, so removing it buys time. Coral nurseries and assisted breeding can put heat-tolerant genotypes back on a reef, at a scale of hectares rather than of coastlines, and the honest description of that work is that it is a stopgap while the water keeps warming. The distance between what is being done and what the temperature requires is the uncomfortable part, and the modelling papers tend to say so without softening it. Reefs that have bleached least are often the ones with the strongest local management, which is a reason to keep the local work going even when the global number decides the outcome.
