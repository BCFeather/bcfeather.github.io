description: "A new model ships almost every week now, and each one arrives with a benchmark table that is obsolete by the following Friday. The cadence itself has become the product, which is a strange thing for a technology to sell."
---

Somebody released a model this week. Somebody released a model last week as well, and the week before that, and the chances are good that another will be released before this piece is read by anyone. The pattern is now stable enough to describe. A short announcement, a table of benchmark scores, a pricing page, a claim about one capability that is said to be state of the art, and a wave of commentary that lasts about seventy-two hours before the next announcement arrives.

The compression is the thing to notice. Two years ago a model release was an event with a date, and people rearranged their week around it. Now the releases arrive in clusters, and a smaller lab will often move its launch a day in either direction to avoid being buried. The same dynamic existed in the phone business, where manufacturers avoid the week of a major launch, and it produced the same result, which is a calendar of announcements that the audience learns to skim.

Benchmarks have degraded as a consequence. A score is meaningful when everyone runs the same test on the same data under the same conditions. In a market where a new entrant can choose its own evaluation suite, the number in the announcement is closer to a marketing asset than to a measurement, and readers have learned to discount it. The serious evaluations now come from third parties, take weeks to publish, and are frequently overtaken before they appear.

The effects are not evenly distributed. A large company with an evaluation team can absorb a migration every quarter. A two-person studio cannot, and it ends up locked to a model it chose eighteen months ago, paying for stability with quality. What the cadence does to the people who use these systems is more interesting than what it does to the vendors. A team that builds a product on top of a model has to decide, every quarter, whether to migrate. Migrating costs engineering time, breaks prompts that were tuned to the old behaviour, and occasionally breaks a feature that nobody realized depended on a quirk. Not migrating has a cost too, and it accumulates silently as the old model falls behind on quality.

There is a version of the industry in which the releases slow down because the models converge. That has not happened yet. Each generation still improves on something specific, and the improvements are large enough to justify the churn. What has happened is that the improvements have become narrower, which makes the announcements louder in compensation.

For anyone outside the industry, the practical advice is to ignore the weekly calendar entirely and check the state of the art twice a year. The differences that matter for a real task do not change between Tuesday and Thursday, and the energy spent following each launch is energy not spent on the task. The vendors are selling a cadence. The buyer needs a tool that works in six months, and those two things have very little to do with each other.
