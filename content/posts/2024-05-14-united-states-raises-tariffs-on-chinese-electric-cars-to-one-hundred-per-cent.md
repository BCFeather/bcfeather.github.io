United States raised tariffs on Chinese electric cars to 100 per cent on 14 May, and added or increased duties on batteries, solar cells, semiconductors and critical minerals. The measures cover a few billion dollars of imports, which is small in the trade data and large in the politics. The announcement came after a review that had been running for a year, and it kept most of the duties that the previous administration had imposed while raising the ones on the newest industries.

Arithmetic of the tariff is straightforward. A car that sells for twenty thousand dollars abroad becomes one that costs forty thousand at the border, which is above the price of most American models, and an importer who cannot sell at that price stops importing. A duty that is high enough is a ban that is written as a tax. The rate is high enough that no importer would pay it, which is why the officials described the measure as a protection of the domestic supply chain rather than as a source of revenue.

Volumes were already small. Very few Chinese cars were sold in the United States, and the tariffs are aimed at the future rather than at the present, because the manufacturers have been building plants in other markets and would eventually have reached this one. A tariff that arrives before the trade is a tariff that is a threat rather than a remedy. The manufacturers have been building plants in Europe and in South-East Asia rather than in the United States, which means the duty closes a route that the industry had planned to use.

Supply chain is the more consequential part. Batteries and the minerals that go into them are inputs for American manufacturers as well as for Chinese ones, and a duty on those raises the cost of the cars that the policy is meant to protect. A tariff on a component is a tariff on the industry that buys it. The battery is the component that matters most, and the duty on it raises the cost of the cars that the American plants are meant to build with imported cells.

Response from the other side has been measured so far. The government has said it will take the case to the trade organization and has opened an inquiry into the imports of American products, which is the route it has used before. A dispute that is litigated is a dispute that takes years to resolve. The case at the trade organization will take years, and the precedent that the duties set will be cited by the countries that want to protect their own industries in the meantime.

What the announcement shows is that the tariff has become the ordinary instrument of industrial policy, and the countries that use it are now choosing which industries they are willing to make more expensive. Industrial policy has become the ordinary business of governments, and the question for the firms is which market they can serve at the price that the tariffs now imply.
