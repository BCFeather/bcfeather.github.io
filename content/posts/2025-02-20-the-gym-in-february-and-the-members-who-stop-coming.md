January's first wave of new members thins out in the second half of February, and the gym industry has built a pricing model that survives the thinning. The crowd at seven in the evening in the first week of January is a memory by the third week of February, and the equipment that was queued for is available again.

Attrition is not a surprise to the operators. The data that a chain collects shows a curve, a large share of the new members attend four times in the first month, twice in the second, and then not at all. The members who cancel are a minority, and the members who keep paying and stop attending are the ones the business depends on.

Pricing has adapted to that. The annual membership that is paid in full is the product that the operator prefers, the monthly membership is the product the customer prefers, and the two have been converging on a middle ground that the chains call a season pass. The gym that sells a month at a time has to keep the member coming back, and the gym that sells a year does not.

Class-based studios have a different model and a different problem. A studio that sells a pack of ten sessions has a member who has paid and a calendar that has to be filled, and the no-show rate is the number that decides the profitability. The studios that charge a small fee for a late cancellation are the ones that keep the room full.

Gym design has followed the economics. A gym with a large floor and a small number of machines is a gym that expects few people, and a gym with a small floor and a large number of machines is a gym that expects a crowd at six and an empty room at eleven. The layout is a forecast about the attendance, and the forecast is made when the lease is signed.

A February visitor notices the change and interprets it as a decline. The room that was full in January is comfortable in February, and the comfortable room is the room that the operator planned for. The member who keeps coming is the one the gym wants and the one the gym has priced for.

What the month shows is that a subscription business is a forecast about behaviour rather than a sale of a service. The gym sells a year of access and it knows how much of the year will be used, and the price is set on the average rather than on the keen member. The February room is the average, and the average is what the accounts assume. A business that sold only to the keen member would charge less and earn more from each of them, and the industry has chosen the other arrangement because the keen members are a small and expensive group to find, and the indifferent ones are the many who pay in advance and forget the card in a drawer.
