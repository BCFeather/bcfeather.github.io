One vial that holds against eighteen African elapid venoms is a wider panel than any serum has been asked to cover, and breadth is the claim a recombinant product has to meet before potency matters. Cobras, mambas and rinkhals differ in which neurotoxins and cytotoxins dominate their venom, so a mixture that works across the three genera answers a question about coverage rather than about dose. A conventional product is raised against one or a few species, and a patient bitten by something outside that list is treated with whatever the clinic has on the shelf.

Timothy Jenkins, Anne Ljungars and Andreas Laustsen at the Technical University of Denmark, with collaborators including the Instituto de Biotecnología at the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México and the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine, report an alternative in *Nature*, 20 November 2025. They immunized an alpaca and a llama with venoms from 18 African elapid species, built a phage-display library from the immune response, and selected nanobodies, the VHH fragments of camelid antibodies, that bind the key toxin families. A small mixture of those nanobodies made a recombinant antivenom.

In mice, the mixture prevented death from 17 of the 18 venoms and reduced the skin necrosis caused by several of the most damaging ones. Against the commercial Inoserp PAN-AFRICA, it performed better at preventing death and at limiting necrosis across the tested species. That breadth is the point: a product made from a handful of engineered proteins, and not from plasma, covered a panel that was deliberately wider than what a conventional serum is expected to handle.

Coverage is not uniform. The serum was only partly effective against the venoms of the green mamba and the black mamba, and those two gaps argue against describing it as a universal antivenom. A product that misses two medically important species is still a useful product, but it is not the single vial that the field has been hoping for.

Production is also more controllable than conventional serum manufacture. Each nanobody is a single protein encoded by a sequence, so a mixture can be designed, manufactured in cells, and adjusted when a gap appears. A nanobody that fails against one venom can be swapped in without raising another animal, and there is no plasma-derived fraction to provoke an adverse reaction.

The evidence is preclinical. The study is in mice and sheep, with a small number of animals per venom, and neutralization in a rodent does not establish clinical efficacy or a human dose. Tissue damage in mice is not the same outcome as tissue salvage in a bitten limb, and no clinical trial has started. Manufacturing cost at the scale a rural health service would require has not been demonstrated either.

A phase I study in people, paired with a clinical trial in bitten patients, is what would turn the claim from breadth into a treatment. Until those data exist, the conclusion is narrower: a nanobody mixture can neutralize most of a deliberately broad panel of African elapid venoms in mice, and it leaves two mambas uncovered.
