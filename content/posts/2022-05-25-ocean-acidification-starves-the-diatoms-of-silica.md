Diatoms build their shells from silicic acid, and the supply of that acid in the surface ocean is maintained by the dissolution of silica in sinking particles. A change in the dissolution rate is therefore a change in the nutrient supply of an entire functional group. A nutrient whose supply depends on a dissolution reaction is a nutrient whose supply depends on the chemistry of the water it sinks through, and that chemistry is changing.

Five in situ mesocosm experiments were run with natural plankton communities in different biomes, and the elemental ratio of silicon to nitrogen in sinking biogenic matter was measured under present and projected conditions. The paper appeared in *Nature*, 25 May 2022. Five mesocosms in different biomes were needed because the effect could otherwise be an artefact of one community or one temperature regime, and the consistency across them is what makes the ratio robust.

Acidification increased the Si:N ratio by 17 per cent, with a spread of 6 per cent, under the carbon dioxide level projected for 2100. The shift appears to be caused by slower chemical dissolution of silica at lower pH, which leaves more silicon in the sinking flux and less in the surface layer. Slower dissolution leaves more silicon in the sinking flux and less in the surface layer, and the surface layer is the only place where a diatom can build a shell.

Sediment trap data from the global ocean confirm that pH exerts a widespread influence on Si:N in the water column, which turns a mesocosm observation into a global relationship. A relationship derived from mesocosms is a relationship that holds in a bag, and the sediment traps test it across the whole ocean before it is allowed into a global model.

Earth-system model simulations propagate the relationship: the reduced silica dissolution lowers the silicic acid available in the surface ocean and triggers a global decline of diatoms by 13 to 26 per cent by 2200. That projection reverses the conclusion of earlier experiments, which had found that acidification favours diatoms. A reversal of that size matters because diatoms carry a large share of ocean primary production and of the biological carbon pump, and a decline shifts the community towards other phytoplankton with different stoichiometry. A decline of a quarter in a functional group that carries a third of ocean primary production is a change in the biological pump, and the pump is what moves carbon into the deep ocean.

The lesson is about the scale at which a mechanism is tested. A bottle experiment can show that a species grows faster at low pH, and only a model that includes the silica cycle can show that the same species starves because its nutrient is being exported instead of recycled.
