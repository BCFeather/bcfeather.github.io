Between 2021 and 2023 the Antarctic ice sheet lost mass more slowly, and for a period East Antarctica gained more than West Antarctica lost. The observation was awkward because it looked like good news in a system where every other indicator points the other way, and no explanation had survived scrutiny. A paper in *Nature* on 19 August offers a mechanism that connects the anomaly to the ocean thousands of miles north.

Qinghua Ding and colleagues combined observations with model experiments and traced the gain to surface temperatures in the tropical warm pool, the region between the western Pacific and the eastern Indian Ocean where surface water regularly exceeds 28 degrees Celsius. That region had been unusually warm for three consecutive years, compared with the two preceding decades, and the persistence rather than the peak is what the mechanism depends on. Warm pool anomalies are themselves linked to El Nino cycles, so the mechanism couples two climate modes that are usually studied separately.

Warm pool heating generates a Rossby wave train, a series of alternating high and low pressure anomalies that propagates poleward. The train terminated in a high-pressure anomaly over East Antarctica, which increased precipitation, and precipitation over an ice sheet adds mass as snowfall. The chain from tropical sea surface temperature to Antarctic snowfall is four steps long and each step is measurable.

Ice cores provided the test that separates a trend from an oscillation. Records from East Antarctica suggest the pattern recurs roughly once every ten years, which makes the 2021 to 2023 pause a recurring mode of variability rather than a change in the direction of the system. Jonathan Wille, writing the accompanying News and Views piece, makes the point directly: tropical variability has to enter any account of Antarctica's future. Ice cores date the snowfall, which is what turns a plausible chain into a recurrence interval with a number attached.

The caveat is scale. Three years is short for an ice sheet, and a snowfall anomaly that adds mass in the interior does not offset the dynamic losses at the margins, where warm ocean water thins floating shelves and accelerates glaciers. A pause in total mass loss is compatible with continued retreat at the coast, and the two processes run on different clocks. Snowfall that falls on the interior is also the component that satellites measure least precisely, which is why the ice core record carries weight.

Whether the mechanism strengthens under warming is the open question, and it cuts both ways. A warmer tropical ocean could produce the wave train more often, and a warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, so snowfall might increase. The same warming also melts more at the surface and at the base of shelves, and the net effect is the kind of question that ice sheet models are built to answer badly. A model that reproduces the wave train should also reproduce the snowfall pattern in the historical record, and that test is the one to watch.
