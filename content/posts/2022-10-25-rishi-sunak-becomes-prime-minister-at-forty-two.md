Rishi Sunak became prime minister on Tuesday, after an audience with the King at Buckingham Palace that lasted about half an hour. He is forty-two, which makes him the youngest holder of the office since 1812, and the first of South Asian descent. He promised a government of integrity, professionalism and accountability, three words that were read as a comment on his predecessor. He entered Downing Street in the afternoon and began appointing ministers within the hour.

A contest that lasted three days put him there, and it involved no vote of the party membership. He passed the threshold of a hundred nominations on Monday afternoon, and Penny Mordaunt withdrew when it became clear that she could not reach it. Members who had voted for Liz Truss in the summer had no say in it, and several of them said so publicly. The party chose a leader it had rejected in September, and the country got a prime minister it had never voted for.

He was born in Southampton to a father who was a general practitioner and a mother who ran a pharmacy, and he went to Winchester and then to Oxford. He studied at Stanford on a scholarship, worked for Goldman Sachs and for two hedge funds, and married Akshata Murty, whose father founded Infosys. He entered parliament in 2015 and became chancellor in February 2020, four weeks before the pandemic closed the country.

His record at the Treasury is the furlough scheme, which paid the wages of eleven million people, and a set of support programmes for businesses that cost about seventy billion pounds. He also ran a discount scheme for restaurant meals that public health experts attacked at the time. He resigned in July and spent the summer arguing that the tax cuts promised by his rival could not be afforded, which is exactly what happened. His wife's tax arrangements had been a political problem in the spring, and she gave up her non-domiciled status in April.

His cabinet was assembled in a day and includes Jeremy Hunt at the Treasury, James Cleverly at the Foreign Office and Suella Braverman back at the Home Office. Braverman had resigned a week earlier over a security breach and was reappointed, which drew criticism from within the party. Ben Wallace stayed at Defence and Michael Gove returned to the department for levelling up. The chancellor is due to present a fiscal plan at the end of the month, and the prime minister said nothing would be settled before it.

The economy he inherits has inflation above ten per cent, mortgage rates at their highest in twenty years and a budget gap that the forecasters put at about forty billion pounds. He used the phrase profound economic crisis in his first speech, and he said the decisions ahead would be difficult. His party trails the opposition by about thirty points in the polls, and the next election is two years away. What he has to do first is persuade his own benches that the summer's argument is finished.
