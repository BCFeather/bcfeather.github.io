---

Dormant eggs are a library. A lake's sediment holds decades of resting eggs produced by the zooplankton that lived in it, and each layer corresponds to a year. Hatching them gives a living sample of a past population.

The study in *PNAS*, 7 February 2023 uses that library. Matthew Wersebe and Lawrence Weider hatch Daphnia from sediments of a lake whose salinity rose, and compare the resurrected animals and their genomes with the modern population.

The finding is rapid adaptation. The older genotypes differ from the modern ones at loci associated with salt tolerance, and the resurrected animals from the recent layers tolerate higher salinity than those from the older layers. The change is measurable within the sediment record.

The mechanism is selection on standing variation. A population of Daphnia carries many genotypes, and a change in salinity shifts the frequencies rather than waiting for a new mutation. Selection on a trait that already varies is fast, and the record shows it.

The resurrection method's power is the control. A modern sample compared with a historical one isolates change over time, and the sediment's chronology provides the dates. A laboratory evolution experiment can do something similar and cannot reproduce the field's history.

The limitation is the sample size. Hatching enough animals for a genomic comparison is laborious, and a few sediment layers are not a continuous record. The trend is visible and the resolution is coarse.

The ecological stake is the keystone role. Daphnia graze algae and are eaten by fish, so a population that shifts its salt tolerance changes the food web's base. A lake that becomes saltier is a lake whose zooplankton either adapt or collapse.

The genomic analysis is the second line of evidence. Loci under selection are identified by comparing allele frequencies across layers, and the candidates are genes that a salt-tolerance mechanism would use.

What the study cannot establish is the cost. A genotype that tolerates salt may be worse at something else, and a population that adapts to salinity may lose fitness in the conditions it left. The trade-off is where the ecological consequence lies.

For conservation, the result is a note of caution and of hope. Rapid adaptation is possible when variation exists, and it is not a licence to assume that every species will manage a change of the same size.

The sediment chronology is the backbone. Each layer is dated by the same methods that lake cores use, and a hatched animal can be assigned to a decade. Without the chronology, a difference between layers is a difference without a date.
The trait measurement is the phenotype. Salt tolerance is assayed in a common garden, where all genotypes experience the same conditions, so that a difference reflects genetics rather than the environment the animals were raised in.

The lake's own history is the context. Salinity rose with agricultural runoff or with the climate, and the timing of the rise is what the sediment layers date. The population's response and the environmental change can therefore be aligned year by year.
