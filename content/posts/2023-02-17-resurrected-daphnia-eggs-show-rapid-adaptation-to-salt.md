description: "Dormant *Daphnia* eggs hatched from lake sediments show that a population adapted to rising salinity within decades, a rate of evolution that the fossil record rarely captures."

Dormant eggs are a library. A lake's sediment holds decades of resting eggs laid by the zooplankton that lived in it, each layer corresponding to a year, and hatching them gives a living sample of a past population.

In *PNAS* on 7 February 2023, that library is used. Matthew Wersebe and Lawrence Weider hatch *Daphnia* from the sediments of a lake whose salinity rose, and compare the resurrected animals and their genomes with the modern population.

Adaptation is rapid in this record. Older genotypes differ from modern ones at loci associated with salt tolerance, and resurrected animals from the recent layers tolerate higher salinity than those from older ones, a change that is measurable within the sediment sequence. The mechanism is selection on standing variation, because a population carries many genotypes and a change in salinity shifts their frequencies rather than waiting for a new mutation.

Because each layer is dated by the methods that lake cores use, a hatched animal can be assigned to a decade, and without that chronology a difference between layers would be a difference without a date; the lake's own salinity history, rising with agricultural runoff or with the climate, can therefore be aligned with the population's response year by year. Phenotype is assayed in a common garden, where all genotypes meet the same conditions, so a difference reflects genetics rather than the environment the animals were raised in, and loci under selection are found by comparing allele frequencies across layers.

Power comes from the control. A modern sample compared with a historical one isolates change over time, and a laboratory evolution experiment can do something similar but cannot reproduce the field's history. Sample size is the limit, since hatching enough animals for a genomic comparison is laborious and a few sediment layers are not a continuous record, leaving a visible trend at coarse resolution.

Keystone ecology is the stake, because *Daphnia* graze algae and are eaten by fish, so a population that shifts its salt tolerance changes the base of the food web, and a lake that becomes saltier holds zooplankton that either adapt or collapse. Cost stays unmeasured, since a genotype that tolerates salt may be worse at something else and a population adapting to salinity may lose fitness in the conditions it left, which is where the ecological consequence lies. Conservation gets a note of caution and of hope: rapid adaptation is possible where variation exists, and its possibility is not a licence for assuming that every species will manage a change of the same size.

Which genes carry the adaptation is the other half, since a shift in allele frequencies near a candidate is not the same as a change in the protein's function, and an assay of the candidate across layers would close that gap. The same archives exist in many lakes, and a comparison across them would show whether rapid adaptation is the rule under this kind of stress or an accident of one basin.
