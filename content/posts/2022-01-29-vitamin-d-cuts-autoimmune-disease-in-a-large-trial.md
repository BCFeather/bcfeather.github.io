description: "Five years of vitamin D supplementation reduced confirmed autoimmune disease by 22 per cent among 25,871 older adults, while omega 3 fatty acids did not reach significance."
---

Autoimmune disease accumulates slowly and unevenly, which is why prevention trials are expensive and rare. A drug that halves the incidence of rheumatoid arthritis would need tens of thousands of participants followed for years to show the effect, and a vitamin that might shift incidence by a fifth needs the same. Most claims about vitamin D and immunity have therefore rested on laboratory markers rather than on disease. A null result from a trial of that size would also have been informative, which is why the question waited for a cohort already assembled for other purposes.

The VITAL trial was designed for cancer and cardiovascular endpoints, and its factorial structure left room for an autoimmune question. Between 2011 and 2014 it enrolled 25,871 people, 12,786 men aged 50 or older and 13,085 women aged 55 or older, and assigned them to 2,000 international units of vitamin D daily or placebo, and to 1,000 milligrams of marine omega 3 fatty acids daily or placebo. The analysis appeared in *The BMJ*, 26 January 2022.

Over a median follow-up of 5.3 years, confirmed autoimmune disease occurred in 123 participants taking vitamin D and 155 taking placebo, a hazard ratio of 0.78 with a 95 per cent confidence interval from 0.61 to 0.99 and a *P* value of 0.05. Omega 3 fatty acids produced 130 cases against 148, a hazard ratio of 0.85 with an interval from 0.67 to 1.08 and a *P* value of 0.19.

The factorial design allows a comparison that a single-agent trial cannot make. Against the double-placebo arm, which recorded 88 cases, vitamin D alone recorded 60 with a hazard ratio of 0.68, the combination recorded 63 with 0.69, and omega 3 alone recorded 67 with 0.74. The two supplements did not multiply, and the omega-3 effect alone did not reach significance. The pattern suggests that vitamin D carries most of the effect, although the arms were not powered for a formal interaction test.

Why vitamin D might work is plausible without being settled. Receptors for the active hormone sit on dendritic cells and on T lymphocytes, and vitamin D shifts the balance of helper T-cell subsets towards a less inflammatory profile in culture. A five-year trial measures disease onset rather than immune mechanism, so the result supports a clinical effect without identifying the pathway.

Several limits qualify the finding. The *P* value sits at the boundary, the confidence interval nearly touches unity, and the trial population was older, mostly white and free of autoimmune disease at entry, which limits generalization. Participants self-reported diagnoses, and although records confirmed them, mild disease may have been missed. The dose was modest by the standards of some supplementation regimes.

Whether the effect persists after stopping, and whether a higher dose does more, are separate trials. For a clinician the result is a rare piece of evidence that a cheap supplement changes a hard endpoint, and for a researcher it is a hint that the immune effects measured in culture do translate into disease.
