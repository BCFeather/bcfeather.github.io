---

Employers in the United States added 175,000 jobs in April, the labour department reported on the third of May, well below the 240,000 that economists had expected and roughly half the pace of the previous month. Unemployment rate rose to 3.9 per cent from 3.8, and average hourly earnings were up 3.9 per cent on the year, the slowest rate since 2021. Report was the first clear evidence that the labour market was cooling after a year in which hiring had repeatedly beaten forecasts.

Composition of the gain was narrow. Health care and social assistance added about ninety thousand jobs between them, and transportation and warehousing recovered from a weak March. Construction added a small number, and manufacturing was flat. Temporary help services, which are usually the first to weaken when demand slows, shed jobs for a third month, and the number of people working part time for economic reasons rose.

Household survey told a weaker story than the establishment survey. Employment measured by households fell by about twenty-five thousand, and the labour force was roughly unchanged, which is why the unemployment rate rose even as the number of jobs in the payroll survey increased. Participation among prime-age workers slipped from its recent high, and the number of people who had been unemployed for more than six months edged up, which are the kinds of movements that appear before a slowdown becomes visible in the headline numbers.

Wage figures were the part of the report that markets cared about. Annual growth of 3.9 per cent is close to the rate that economists think is consistent with two per cent inflation, given the pace of productivity growth, and the slowdown from 4.1 per cent was read as evidence that the labour market is rebalancing without a jump in unemployment. Federal Reserve officials had said they wanted to see wages and prices decelerate together, and April provided one half of that.

Market reaction was immediate and large. Yields on two-year Treasury notes fell by more than ten basis points, and traders moved to price two cuts this year rather than one. Dollar weakened, and equities rose on the argument that a softer labour market gives the central bank the room it said it needed. Several economists revised their forecasts for the first cut from September to July, and the probability of a reduction before the election moved above fifty per cent.

Economists also pointed to the hurricane season and to the strike at Boeing, both of which will distort the May and June figures, and said the next two reports will matter more than the one published on the third of May.

Deeper question raised by the report is whether it marks the start of a trend or a single month of noise. Hiring has beaten forecasts in most months since 2022, and the establishment survey has been revised down repeatedly, which suggests the underlying pace is slower than the headlines. April figure fits that pattern. It also fits the picture of a labour market that is cooling gradually as higher rates work through the economy, which is what the central bank has been trying to produce without tipping the country into recession.
