The tournament that opens in Mexico City on 20 June is the first World Cup with forty-eight teams, and the format has been argued about since it was approved. Twelve groups of four, the top two from each group advancing, plus the eight best third-placed teams, gives a round of thirty-two and a knockout bracket that is three rounds longer than the old one. The arithmetic is tidy on paper. On the pitch it produces a set of incentives that the previous format did not have.

Group stage feels the first effect. With four teams in a group and three matches each, the third match is often dead for one side and decisive for another, and the possibility of advancing as a third-placed team changes the calculus for everyone. A team that has lost its first match can still advance with a win and a favourable result elsewhere, which means the final round has more matches with something at stake than the old format did, and fewer dead rubbers.

Coaches will complain about the second effect. A team that needs only a draw to be certain of advancing has an incentive to play for one, and the third-placed route makes a draw more valuable than it used to be, because a single point can be enough to be among the best third-placed sides. The tournament has seen this before in formats that admit third-placed teams, most notably in the European Championship, where the final group matches have produced some conspicuously cautious football.

Bracket geometry produces the third effect. A team that finishes second in its group may find itself with an easier path than a team that wins its group, because of how the twelve winners and eight third-placed teams are distributed across the round of thirty-two. That is a structural feature of any bracket that is not a pure power-of-two tree, and it produces the familiar calculating about which side of the draw a team would prefer. The organizers have published the allocation, and the calculations are already being done in coaching offices.

Access is the sporting argument for expansion. Forty-eight teams means more confederations are represented, more countries get a World Cup appearance, and the money that flows from qualification is spread wider. The sporting argument against it is about quality: the gap between the strongest and weakest qualifiers is larger, and some group matches will be uncompetitive. Both are true, and the format is a compromise between them rather than a resolution.

Scheduling decided the details. A forty-eight-team tournament needs more matches, more stadiums and more days, and the calendar is already crowded with club competitions. The compromise was to keep the group stage at three matches per team and to expand the knockout round, which adds matches without extending the tournament by as much as a larger group stage would. That is an administrative solution, and it is the reason the format looks the way it does.

What to watch, then, is not the opening match but the third round of group games and the bracket. The format will be judged on whether the final group matches are competitive, whether the third-placed calculations produce the absurdity of teams playing for a specific scoreline elsewhere, and whether the extra round adds drama or merely adds matches. The tournament will answer those questions in July, and the answers will decide whether the format survives.
