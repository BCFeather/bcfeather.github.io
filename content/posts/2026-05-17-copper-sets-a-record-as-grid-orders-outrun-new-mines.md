Copper for three-month delivery touched 11,940 dollars a tonne on the London Metal Exchange on 13 May, a record, before closing the week a little below that peak. The move capped a rise of about thirty per cent in twelve months and pulled other industrial metals up with it. Inventories in warehouses registered with the exchange fell to the lowest level for the time of year since 2005, which turned a price story into a physical one: there are not many tonnes left in the sheds.

The demand side has changed shape. For most of the past two decades, Chinese construction was the variable that set the copper price, and a slowdown in apartment building was expected to be bearish. What has replaced it is the grid: transmission lines, substations, transformers and the cables that connect wind and solar farms to the network use far more copper per unit of capacity than a coal plant does, and every country that has decided to electrify its heat and transport has discovered that the wires are the binding constraint. Data centres add a smaller but faster-growing draw, since the busbars, cooling and back-up systems of a large campus contain thousands of tonnes.

Supply has not responded, and the reason is geological rather than financial. The average grade of ore in the world's large mines has fallen for twenty years, which means more rock must be moved for each tonne of metal, and the mines that are still rich are in places where permits take a decade. The biggest producers reported lower output last year than the year before, and the pipeline of projects that could come online before 2030 is short. Recycling has grown, and it supplies about a third of demand, and its growth is limited by the scrap that exists rather than by the capacity to process it.

There is a demand-side counterweight that the bulls tend to skip. High prices invite substitution, and aluminium has already replaced copper in some medium-voltage cable and in automotive wiring where weight matters more than conductivity. Thieves also respond to price: theft of copper from substations and railway signalling has risen sharply in several countries, which is a signal that the metal has become valuable enough to steal and a cost that utilities now budget for. Neither substitution nor theft can absorb a shortage of this size, and both reduce demand at the margin.

The macro consequence is familiar and unwelcome. Copper is the metal most exposed to the physical side of the energy transition, and its price is now a tax on every grid project that a government has promised. Utilities that budgeted for eight thousand dollars a tonne are discovering that the transformers they ordered are repriced at delivery, and some have begun to stretch their construction schedules rather than pay. A record copper price is, in that sense, an honest measurement of how much the electrification of everything actually costs, and the bill is arriving before the wires do.
