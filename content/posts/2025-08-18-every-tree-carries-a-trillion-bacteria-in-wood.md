Microbiome surveys have covered soil, leaf surfaces, roots and animal guts, and left one large habitat out of the count: the wood of living trees. Reaching it means drilling a core out of a trunk, and what comes up is low in oxygen, poor in nutrients and slow to change. Most of what was known about wood communities therefore came from dead logs, where decay has already reorganized the population. Whether a healthy tree carried a community of its own, and whether the living outer wood differed from the darker core, was an open question.

Arnold and colleagues answered it with cores. Their paper, in *Nature* on 6 August 2025, sampled 16 tree species from 11 genera, treated heartwood and sapwood as separate compartments, counted bacteria by 16S quantitative PCR and sequenced bacterial 16S and fungal ITS amplicons from the same cores. Source-tracking then compared each wood community with the soil gathered around the tree it came from, the test that separates a resident population from an invasion. Those genera are spread across continents, so the survey covers more than one forest.

The number the paper turns on is an order of magnitude. Scaling the qPCR counts, on the assumption of four copies of the 16S gene per cell, puts roughly \(10^{12}\) bacteria in the wood of a single tree; per gram, counts vary between species and between tissues. Communities in heartwood and sapwood were distinct within the same trunk, and neither closely resembled the soil, the leaves or the roots of the tree that held it, which is the part of the result that source-tracking was built to check.

Partitioning is where the biology sits. Sapwood conducts water and exchanges gases with the outside, so its community can plausibly be fed from the soil. Heartwood is sealed, old and nearly without oxygen, and its assemblage is built around archaea and anaerobic bacteria, groups that do their chemistry in the absence of air. That the two tissues hold different communities, consistently across species, says the trunk is two habitats divided by a wall, and the wall is the transition between living wood and dead.

Limits sit in the design. Sixteen species from 11 genera makes a wide survey and a shallow one, with 2 to 38 cores per species; amplicon sequencing returns relative abundances, so composition is easier to defend than absolute counts; and the trillion rests on the copy-number assumption above. Nothing in the paper shows that these communities help a tree or harm it. The holobiont framing is an argument about what a plant is, and it is not yet a measurement of what the bacteria inside it do.

Activity is what would settle function: metagenome-assembled genomes from heartwood, cultures of the anaerobes, and gas flux measured from intact trunks across a year. Sampling at several heights and in different seasons would show whether the community is stable or rebuilt with each ring, and comparing drought-stressed trees with watered ones would test whether the interior shifts before the canopy does. Until then the wood microbiome is a habitat with a measured size and an unmeasured function.
