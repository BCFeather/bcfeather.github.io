The solar term that falls on the fifth of March is called jingzhe, which translates roughly as the awakening of the dormant insects, and it marks the point in the agricultural year when the soil is worked for the first time. The name is a piece of phenology, a calendar built on what a person could see in a field, and the calendar has been in continuous use for two thousand years because what a person could see in a field was, for most of that time, the most reliable signal available.

The system it belongs to divides the year into twenty-four terms of about fifteen days, and the terms are defined by the position of the sun rather than by the weather. lichun is the beginning of spring, chunfen is the equinox and xiazhi is the solstice, and the sequence is regular enough that a farmer without an almanac could keep it by counting. The terms were then attached to instructions, when to sow, when to transplant, when to harvest, and those instructions were local, adjusted by every county to its own altitude and soil.

What awakens is not what a modern reader imagines. The insects that the name refers to are the ones that overwinter as larvae or adults in the soil and the leaf litter, and the signal that wakes them is temperature rather than date. A warm spell in late February will bring them out two weeks before the term that names them, and a cold March will keep them down for a fortnight after. The calendar names an average, and the average is a poor guide in any particular year.

What has changed is the average itself. The observations that phenologists collect, the dates of the first blossom, the first frog, the first leaf, have moved earlier across the northern hemisphere by an amount that varies by region and by species but is measurable everywhere. The apricot in the north of China now flowers about a week earlier than it did in the 1980s, and the term that names the flowering has not moved with it, because the terms are tied to the sun and the sun keeps its schedule.

Farming, which the calendar governs, has also changed in a way that the calendar cannot express. The ploughing that jingzhe announces is now done by a machine that a cooperative owns and hires out by the hectare, the seed is bought rather than saved, the irrigation is scheduled by a phone app that reads the soil moisture from a probe, and the decisions that the old calendar timed are made by a county agronomist with a spreadsheet. The terms survive as a vocabulary, in the names of dishes and festivals and the small talk of a spring afternoon, and the work they once timed is organized by other means.

What the term still does, and this is the part that has not changed, is give the year a shape that a person can hold. Twenty-four names in a fixed order, each with a thing to look for, is a way of noticing the passage of a year that a date on a screen does not provide. The insects wake, the blossom comes, the rain arrives, and the noticing is its own reward, even for a person who will never plough anything.
