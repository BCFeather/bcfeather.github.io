In 2022, mpox spread through countries where it had never been endemic, and clinicians had a drug and no evidence. Tecovirimat was approved for smallpox on the strength of animal models, was available under an expanded-access protocol, and was offered to patients who were in pain and frightened. The alternative was to withhold a plausible treatment until a trial finished, and the trial needed those patients to enrol.

The trial finished this week and the answer is that tecovirimat did not shorten clinical resolution, did not reduce pain and did not accelerate viral clearance. Resolution by day 29 was 83 per cent on the drug against 84 per cent on placebo, across 412 randomised participants. The drug was safe, and safety was never the concern.

Reading this as a mistake would be unkind and inaccurate. The patients who received the drug under access protocols were not harmed, and the trial that produced the answer was possible because a clinical infrastructure existed to run it. The alternative histories are worse: a pandemic with no treatment offered, no trial organised, and no answer five years later.

Awkward is the incentive structure. Expanded access gives a patient a drug and gives a manufacturer a market, and both of those make a randomised trial harder to complete. The field managed it in mpox, and the same pattern in other outbreaks has produced trials that never finished and drugs that stayed in use for a decade without evidence.

So the lesson is procedural and dull. Plan the trial before the outbreak, fund it in advance, and make access conditional on enrolment where that is ethical. Those are administrative decisions that no one makes during a crisis, and every crisis demonstrates the cost of not making them earlier. Scientific lesson concerns plausibility. Tecovirimat blocks a protein that the virus needs to leave a cell, the mechanism is clean, the drug is well tolerated, and none of that translated into a clinical benefit. A mechanism explains how a drug could work, and only a trial establishes that it does, and the gap between those two sentences is where most of clinical research lives. The two lessons are administrative and the second is scientific, and a field that learns only one of them repeats the failure.

None of this settles what to do for clade I mpox, which kills more people and affects children. The trial enrolled a population with a mostly self-limiting illness, and the patients who most need a drug were largely outside the comparison. The honest position is that the question is open for severe disease and closed for mild disease, and a single trial has produced both.

Uncomfortable conclusion: good clinical practice and good evidence can pull in opposite directions for a few months, and the only way to shorten those months is to have the trial ready before the disease arrives. A severe case needs a different trial, and the population that would supply it is small, scattered and often outside the health systems that run trials.
