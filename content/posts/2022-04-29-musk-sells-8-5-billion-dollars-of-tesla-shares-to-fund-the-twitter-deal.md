Elon Musk sold about 8.5 billion dollars of Tesla stock on 26 and 28 April, according to filings with the securities regulator published on 29 April, raising part of the equity he had committed to the Twitter purchase. He disposed of roughly 9.65 million shares, about 4.5 per cent of the company, at prices between 872 and 999 dollars, and the sales were disclosed after the Twitter board had accepted his offer. The filings were made under a rule requiring disclosure within two business days, and they landed the day after the agreement was announced.

The timing made the sales awkward. Musk had told the Twitter board that he had committed 21 billion dollars of equity, and the market learned a week later that most of it had come from selling the shares of the company he runs, at prices below the 1,005 dollars the stock had reached before the agreement. Tesla investors had been told nothing in advance, and the stock fell about 12 per cent in the following sessions. The shares were held in a trust, and the filing disclosed the transactions without stating their purpose.

That sale also contradicted something he had said in public. During the negotiations he had argued that Tesla's shares were undervalued and that he had no intention of selling, and he described the disposal as a one-off that would be followed by no further sales. Within a fortnight he would pledge again that he had no plans to sell, and the pledge held until the autumn, when he sold another 7 billion dollars of stock after the deal closed. He had made a similar pledge in November 2021, when he sold about a tenth of the company to cover a tax bill.

Financing structure explains the awkwardness. Musk's equity commitment was larger than his liquid assets, and the collateral behind his margin loans was Tesla stock, so the more he pledged, the more of the company he had to sell to keep the covenant intact. The banks that lent him the money had insisted on a cushion of unpledged shares, and that cushion could only come from the same holding.

Tesla's own position was stronger than the share price suggested that week. The company had reported record quarterly profit days earlier, on the strength of price increases and a production recovery in Shanghai, and deliveries were running at more than a million vehicles a year. The stock fell because its largest shareholder had become a forced seller, which is a governance fact rather than an operating one. The company's market value was still above a trillion dollars, which made the sale small against the holding and large against the float.

For Twitter the sale was the first piece of evidence that the financing behind the offer was real but improvised. For Tesla holders, the lesson was that the chief executive's attention and capital were now committed to a different company, and that they would be asked to approve a compensation package and a share structure designed partly to fund it. Whether the board's independence survives that arrangement is the question the Delaware case would eventually have to answer.
