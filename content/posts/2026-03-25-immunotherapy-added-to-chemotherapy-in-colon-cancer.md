Colon cancer that lacks mismatch repair is the subtype that responds to immunotherapy, and the evidence for that came from metastatic disease, where checkpoint inhibitors produce durable responses in a minority of patients. Whether the same biology helps in the adjuvant setting, where the tumour has been removed and the goal is to prevent recurrence, was the question the ATOMIC trial was built to answer.

ATOMIC randomised 355 patients to atezolizumab plus modified FOLFOX6 and 357 to modified FOLFOX6 alone, all with stage III mismatch repair-deficient colon cancer. The median age was 64, 55.1 per cent were women, and 53.9 per cent had T4 or N2 disease, which is the high-risk end of stage III. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 25 March, funded by the National Cancer Institute and Genentech.

Disease-free survival at three years was 86.3 per cent with atezolizumab and 76.2 per cent with chemotherapy alone, a hazard ratio for recurrence or death of 0.50 with a confidence interval from 0.35 to 0.73 and a *P* value below 0.001. A ten-point absolute improvement at three years in a disease that recurs early is a large effect, and the confidence interval excludes anything modest.

Toxicity cost is real and stated plainly. Grade 3 or 4 adverse events occurred in 84.1 per cent of patients receiving atezolizumab against 71.9 per cent receiving chemotherapy alone, which is a twelve-point increase in serious toxicity for a treatment given with curative intent, to patients who have just recovered from surgery and expect to return to work. Adjuvant therapy is offered to people who feel well after surgery, and a regimen that makes four in five patients seriously unwell is a regimen that patients abandon.

Why the combination works is the interesting biology. Mismatch repair deficiency produces frameshift mutations that generate neoantigens, and chemotherapy releases tumour antigen while destroying the cells that would present it. Giving the checkpoint inhibitor together with the chemotherapy, and not after it, exposes the immune system to antigen at the moment when the tumour burden is being reduced.

Follow-up is the limitation, and it is the limitation that every adjuvant trial lives with until the survival curve matures. Disease-free survival is a surrogate, and a three-year horizon in colon cancer captures most recurrences without capturing all of them. Overall survival data will arrive later, and the history of adjuvant oncology includes several interventions that improved disease-free survival and did not extend life.

The practical question is who gets it, and the answer will be shaped as much by price and by local reimbursement as by the hazard ratio. Every patient in this trial had the same subtype, and the subtype is about fifteen per cent of stage III colon cancer, which makes the eligible population large enough to matter and small enough that the cost of a checkpoint inhibitor in the adjuvant setting will be a budget conversation rather than a footnote.
