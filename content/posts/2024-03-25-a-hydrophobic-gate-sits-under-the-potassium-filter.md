Voltage-gated potassium channels open when the membrane potential changes, and many of them then close again while the voltage is still held, a behaviour called C-type inactivation. The closing is not the pore gate that responds to voltage: it happens at the selectivity filter, the narrow stretch that decides which ions pass, and it runs on a slower clock than the opening that preceded it.

Structures of the filter in its conductive state are well known, and the inactivated state has been the missing picture, since a filter that changes shape has to be caught in that shape before anything can be said about it. A high-resolution structure of a strongly inactivating triple mutant, kv1.2-kv2.1-3m, showed a filter dilated at its outer end, and that conformation was read as the non-conductive state that electrophysiology had been looking for.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 24 March 2024 tests the reading, and finds it wanting. Molecular dynamics simulations and electrophysiology together show that the dilated filter of that mutant still conducts, so a filter in that conformation cannot be the thing that blocks current in the functional experiments. The block must therefore lie somewhere the structure had not put it.

Where it lies is the paper's claim. A second change is required, involving the isoleucine residues at position 398 on the pore-lining segment S6, and it is those residues, and not the outer mouth of the filter, that shut the pore. The mutant that produced the dilated structure therefore lost conduction for a reason the images could not show, and naming that reason is what the paper sets out to do.

The geometry explains the name. The four I398 side chains, one from each subunit, form a hydrophobic gate immediately beneath the selectivity filter, and the gate closes and opens with the state of the channel rather than with the voltage sensor alone. That is what a state-dependent block means once it is drawn in atomic detail, and it explains why the block survives a voltage that keeps the pore gate open.

One construct carries the argument. The simulations describe a channel the electrophysiology can test, and both agree that the dilated filter conducts, but a structure of the gate in its closed state at high resolution is still missing, and a simulation running for microseconds cannot cover the seconds over which inactivation is observed. The gate also has to open again as the membrane repolarizes, and nothing here follows that half of the cycle.

Resolving the closed gate would settle the geometry, and the pharmacology is the nearer prize: a compound that holds the gate shut, or one that keeps it open, would test the model in a way that neither simulation nor electrophysiology manages on its own. A structure would also show whether the gate is the same in other Kv channels that inactivate, which is the question a drug programme would ask first.
