NVIDIA reported revenue of 26.04 billion dollars for the quarter to the end of April, up 262 per cent from a year earlier, and net income of 14.88 billion. The data-centre business reached 22.6 billion dollars, and the company said it expected about 28 billion in the current quarter. The quarter was the third in a row in which revenue more than doubled from a year earlier, and the company said the supply of the newest systems would remain short through the year.

Guidance is the number the market trades on. A company that is supply-constrained rather than demand-constrained gives guidance that reflects how many chips it can obtain, and the company said the next quarter would be limited by the packaging capacity it has contracted. A forecast that is a capacity plan is a forecast that the suppliers can read. The company has been pre-paying its suppliers to secure capacity, and the deposits it holds are a measure of how far ahead it has booked the factories that make its chips.

Margins are extraordinary. Gross margin reached 78 per cent, which is the level of a software business rather than of a hardware one, and it reflects a product that has no substitute at the scale the customers need. A margin that is that wide is an invitation to every competitor and to every customer that can design its own chip. The customers have been buying whole systems rather than cards, and the networking equipment that connects them has become a larger part of the revenue than it was a year ago.

Split is a cosmetic change with a real effect. Ten-for-one lowers the price of a share to a level that smaller investors can buy, and the company said the change was intended to make the stock accessible to employees. A split changes nothing about the business and everything about who can own a piece of it. The split will take effect in June, and the company's employees are paid largely in shares, which means the change is also a decision about retention.

Customer concentration is the risk the results conceal. A handful of cloud operators account for a large share of the revenue, and their capital budgets are set once a year by committees that are now weighing whether to build their own accelerators. A customer that can become a competitor is a customer that the supplier has to keep satisfied. The largest customers have been developing their own accelerators for internal use, and the company's answer is that its software platform is the part that is hardest to replace.

What the quarter shows is that a company can become the centre of an industry within two years, and the constraint on it is now the number of machines that the world can build rather than the number of buyers. A supplier that is the bottleneck of an entire industry has pricing power that no competition policy has yet tested, and the scrutiny will arrive before the capacity does.
