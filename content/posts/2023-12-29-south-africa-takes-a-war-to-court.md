South Africa filed an application against Israel at the International Court of Justice on 29 December, alleging that the military campaign in Gaza amounts to genocide and asking the court for provisional measures, including an order to suspend the operation. The application runs to 84 pages and relies on the Genocide Convention of 1948, to which both states are parties, and on statements by Israeli officials that the applicants characterize as evidence of intent. Hearings were scheduled for 11 and 12 January, an unusually rapid timetable for a court that usually takes years.

The Convention gives the court a low threshold for interim relief: the applicant must show that the rights claimed are plausible and that there is urgency. South Africa argued that the scale of the killing, the destruction of hospitals, the displacement of most of the population and the restriction of food and water met the definition, and that the statements of ministers about a human animals and about flattening the strip supplied the intent that a genocide finding requires. Israel's answer, filed in January, was that the war is a lawful response to an attack and that the statements were rhetoric rather than policy.

Israel does not accept the court's authority in the way that most respondents do, and its government said before the hearing that it would not comply with an order it considered political. The United States called the case meritless, which is a position it has taken on other ICJ matters involving its allies. The court's orders are binding in law and unenforceable in practice, and the mechanism that gives them weight is the Security Council, where a veto awaits any attempt to enforce one against Israel.

The timing of the filing was political on both sides. South Africa has been the most vocal critic of the campaign among states that lie outside the region, and its governing party has a domestic audience that has followed the Palestinian question for decades. Israel's campaign against the case began within hours, with a foreign ministry statement, a briefing to journalists and a legal team assembled from its own justice ministry and from barristers in London, because a finding at the provisional stage would shape how the war is judged even without enforcement.

Third states filed declarations of intervention in the following weeks, and the list became a map of the argument: Nicaragua, Colombia, Mexico, Libya, Pakistan, Turkey and others on one side, Germany, Britain and Canada supporting Israel's position in statements rather than filings. The court allowed the interventions but limited them, and the judges made clear that the case would be decided on the law and the record rather than on the number of governments that had chosen a side.

Written after the Holocaust, the Genocide Convention has been invoked rarely, in the cases against Serbia, Bosnia and Myanmar, each of which took years and each of which ended without an order that changed the conduct of the war. What South Africa's application changes is the vocabulary. A campaign that had been argued about in terms of proportionality and humanitarian law is now argued about in terms of the crime the twentieth century promised to prevent, which is a heavier charge and a harder one to answer in public.
