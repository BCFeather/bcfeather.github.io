Joe Biden completed his first year in office on 20 January with an approval rating in the low forties, a signed infrastructure law and an economic agenda that had stopped moving in the Senate. He marked the anniversary with a televised session from the White House rather than with a rally, and the questions he took were mostly about inflation and about the two senators who had blocked his spending bill. His approval stood at about 42 per cent in the polling averages, and the number had been falling since the summer.

The record is more substantial than the commentary suggests. Congress passed a 1.2 trillion dollar infrastructure package in November with Republican votes, the vaccination campaign had reached most adults by the summer, and the child tax credit expansion in the spring cut child poverty sharply for a year. The American Rescue Plan, signed in March, had also put money into households at the moment when supply chains were least able to absorb the spending. The credit expired at the end of the year, and the poverty figures fell back as it lapsed.

The failures were legislative and they were predictable. The Build Back Better bill, which bundled climate spending, childcare and health subsidies, needed every Democratic vote in a chamber split fifty-fifty, and one senator from West Virginia said in December that he could not support it in its current form. Two voting-rights bills failed on 19 January when the Senate could not change its own filibuster rule, which the president had gone to the chamber to urge.

Inflation supplied the frame for the year. The December report had put consumer prices 7 per cent higher than a year earlier, and the White House's argument that the increase was temporary had run out of time. Petrol prices, grocery bills and rents were the numbers voters used to judge the administration, and the president's legislative achievements were difficult to feel at a filling station.

Foreign policy gave the administration its clearest success and its clearest warning. NATO had held together through the Afghan withdrawal and the diplomatic confrontation with Russia, and the intelligence community had begun to publish its assessments of Russian troop movements in public. That transparency would matter in February, and in January it was still a story about credibility. The administration's public warnings were unusual, and their purpose was to make the case for sanctions before any troops moved.

A first year is usually judged by what it legislated and what it promised. Biden's year produced one large bipartisan law, a stalled second one, and a public argument about prices that he did not win; the second year would be fought on the same ground, with fewer legislative vehicles and a harder economic backdrop. The midterms would be fought on prices, and the president's legislative record did not travel well through a grocery aisle. The White House's answer was to talk about the deficit and about jobs, and neither subject moved the voters who had made the year difficult.
