Antiretroviral therapy works when it is taken, and the people it fails are usually people who cannot take it reliably. Housing instability, mental illness, stigma and a chaotic daily routine are the reasons that a drug with a genetic explanation for failure is unusual and a drug with a social explanation is common. A treatment that removes the daily decision is therefore a treatment aimed at the mechanism.

The LATITUDE study approached the problem in two steps. Step 1 gave participants with adherence challenges a conditional cash incentive and oral therapy, and those who achieved suppression moved into step 2, which randomized 306 people to long-acting cabotegravir plus rilpivirine every two months or to standard care with oral therapy. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 18 February.

Step 2 stopped early, which changes how the estimate should be read. An independent analysis at a median follow-up of 48 weeks found regimen failure in 22.8 per cent of the injected group against 41.2 per cent of the standard-care group, a difference of minus 18.4 percentage points with a confidence interval from minus 32.4 to minus 4.3. When a trial stops for benefit, the estimate is usually robust and the follow-up is usually short, and both are true here.

Design deserves attention because it is unusual. The conditional incentive in step 1 is an intervention that some will find paternalistic and that the investigators defend as a way to give people a fair chance at suppressing before randomization. Excluding the incentive from step 2 means the comparison isolates the injection, which is the part a health system would pay for.

Implementation will decide whether the result reaches the people it describes. Long-acting cabotegravir and rilpivirine require two injections at a clinic every two months, and a missed appointment leaves a patient with subtherapeutic drug levels and a risk of resistance. The advantage over pills is not that injections are easier to take but that a clinic can see whether they were taken, and that requires a clinic that can keep the appointment.

Cost and access will determine reach, as they do for every long-acting formulation in HIV. Long-acting regimens are expensive, and the populations that need them most are the populations least served by the clinics that can administer them. A programme that funds the drug without funding the visits will produce virological failure, and the failure will be reported as resistance, which is a word that ends programmes.

The result also reframes adherence as a clinical problem with a clinical solution. For twenty years the field has treated missed doses as a behavioural failure to be counselled, and this trial suggests that changing the delivery route changes the outcome. That is a smaller claim than a cure and a larger claim than a leaflet, and it points at a delivery problem that has been misdescribed as a behavioural one.
