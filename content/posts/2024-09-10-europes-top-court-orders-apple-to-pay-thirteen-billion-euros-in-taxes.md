Judges at the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled on 10 September that Ireland had granted illegal state aid to Apple and that the company owed thirteen billion euros in back taxes, overturning a lower court that had annulled the finding four years earlier. On the same morning the court rejected Google's appeal against a fine over its shopping service. The judgment ended a case that had run for a decade and had been decided in the company's favour by the general court in 2020, which is the appeal that the court has now reversed.

Their reasoning is about the arm's length principle. Two Irish subsidiaries were allowed to allocate most of their profit to a head office that had no employees and no premises, and the commission argued that a company which cannot show that its transfer pricing reflects the market has been given an advantage. A tax arrangement that exists only on paper is an arrangement that a court can take apart. The commission's calculation rested on a comparison with the profit that a similar company would have earned if it had negotiated with an independent party, and the subsidiaries could produce no such comparison.

Money goes to Ireland, not to the commission. The state that gave the advantage is the state that collects the arrears, which is an unusual outcome for a case about competition law and a welcome one for a government that had argued against the ruling. A fine paid to the plaintiff is a fine that changes the politics. The government had set aside the money in an escrow account years earlier, and it said after the ruling that the funds would be released to the exchequer.

Ireland's position has already changed. The country had resisted the case for a decade and then signed up to the global minimum tax that makes the old arrangement impossible, because a rate that applies everywhere removes the advantage of a rate that applies in one place. A country that loses its specialty is a country that has to compete on something else. The corporate rate in Ireland is now fifteen per cent for large firms, which is the rate that the global agreement requires and a rate that no longer depends on a special arrangement.

Precedents are the larger effect. The ruling ends a decade of litigation about whether a member state can be ordered to collect tax it chose not to collect, and it settles that the commission can act when the arrangement is selective. A doctrine that is settled is a doctrine that the next case will apply. Companies that had similar structures have been reviewing them since the judgment, because the reasoning applies to any arrangement that cannot be defended with a contract.

What the two judgments show is that the court has become the place where the rules about digital markets are decided, which is a role that legislatures have been slow to take. A company that loses twice in a morning is a company that will plan its structure around the court's reasoning. A company that loses twice in a morning is a company that will plan its structure around the court's reasoning rather than around the advice of its tax lawyers.
