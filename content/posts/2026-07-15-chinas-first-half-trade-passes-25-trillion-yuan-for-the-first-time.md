Customs data published on 15 July put China's trade in goods above 25 trillion yuan in the first half of the year, the first time the six-month figure has crossed that line, with exports doing most of the work. The surplus widened from a year earlier because imports grew more slowly than shipments abroad, and officials attributed the pattern to prices as much as to volumes. Economists had expected a strong number and were still surprised by the size of the gap. Officials said they expect the second half to grow more slowly because so many orders were pulled forward.

Composition explains why the export side held up. Machinery, electrical equipment, ships and electric vehicles accounted for most of the increase, and the share of trade with ASEAN and with the European Union rose while the share with the United States fell. Chinese exporters have also been selling more to countries that assemble goods for the American market, which means some of the trade has been rerouted rather than lost. Shipments of ships and of containers rose fastest in value terms, reflecting orders foreign buyers placed two years ago.

Weakness on the import side is the part officials glossed over. Imports of commodities were flat in volume and lower in value because prices for iron ore and for oil had fallen, and imports of consumer goods grew slowly. A trade surplus that widens because the domestic economy is soft is a different signal from one that widens because exports are booming, and the first-half figure contains both. Retail sales grew more slowly than output and the savings rate stayed high, which is why imports of consumer goods lagged.

Tariff calendar shaped the timing. American tariffs against sixty trading partners were due to take effect on 24 July, and importers on both sides brought orders forward into June and early July. Customs officials said they could see the effect in the monthly numbers, and they warned that the third quarter would look weaker as a result. Customs offices in Guangdong and Zhejiang reported queues of containers in the last week of June.

Prices tell a story the volumes hide. Export unit values have been falling for a year, particularly for electric vehicles and for solar equipment, and Chinese manufacturers have been absorbing costs to hold market share abroad as they cut prices at home. A larger volume of goods sold for less money is how a deflationary export machine looks in the trade statistics. Producer prices have been falling for months, and the gap between the price of exports and the price of imports has widened.

Trade figures of this kind are read as evidence in a political argument, and both sides will use this one. Beijing can point to a record half-year, and its partners can point to a surplus that keeps widening while their own factories idle. The number that matters for the second half will be the import figure, because an economy that buys more from the world is one that its trading partners can live with. The finance ministry has said it will publish support for smaller exporters in the autumn.
