---Rebels led by Hayat Tahrir al-Sham entered the city of Aleppo on the twenty-ninth of November and by the following morning controlled most of it, including the citadel and the central districts that government forces had held since 2016. Advance began three days earlier in the countryside west of the city, where the rebels broke through government lines and captured dozens of villages. It was the largest change of territorial control in Syria since the war's front lines froze in 2020.

Speed of the collapse surprised everyone, including the rebels. Government forces withdrew from neighbourhoods without organizing a defence, and videos posted by residents showed soldiers abandoning checkpoints and vehicles. Rebel fighters moved through the city in convoy and met little resistance. Military analysts said the offensive exploited the redeployment of government troops and their allied militias to other fronts, and that the government's forces had not been resupplied at the rate they had been in earlier years.

The Response from the government was to promise a counterattack and to ask its allies for help. Syrian army said it had withdrawn to regroup and that it would restore control, and Russian aircraft struck rebel positions and the city's outskirts through the weekend, according to rescue workers and monitoring groups. Iranian-backed militias that had held positions near Aleppo were reported to have pulled back towards Hama, which left the government's defensive line thinner than it had been at any point in the war.

Regional context explains why the offensive came now. Hezbollah, the most effective of the government's allies, had been fighting Israel for more than a year and had agreed a ceasefire on the twenty-seventh of November. Russia had moved air defence and other assets away from Syria, and its attention was fixed on Ukraine. Turkey, which supports parts of the opposition, had spent the year signalling that it wanted an arrangement on the border. All three shifts removed cover from the government at the same time.

Humanitarian effect was immediate. Thousands of people left Aleppo towards the north and towards government-held areas, and the main road out of the city was jammed within hours. Hospitals in the rebel-held areas reported shortages of blood and fuel, and aid agencies suspended cross-border operations while the front moved. Universities and schools closed, and the citadel, which had been restored as a visitor site, was reported to have been damaged by shelling.

The Consequence for the wider war is the part that will be decided in the coming weeks. Government has promised to retake the city, and its allies have an interest in preventing a permanent loss. Rebels have said they do not intend to advance beyond Aleppo and have called for a political settlement, which is a position they have taken before. What has changed is that the front lines that everyone had treated as permanent are moving again, and that the diplomatic framework built around them, including the ceasefire agreements of 2020 and the talks in Astana, now rests on a map that no longer exists.
