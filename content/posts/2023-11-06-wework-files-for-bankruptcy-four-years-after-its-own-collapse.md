WeWork filed for Chapter 11 protection in New Jersey on 6 November, with its Canadian subsidiary following the same route, and the company said it would use the process to renegotiate a lease book that had become the central problem of its business. The filing listed liabilities of roughly eighteen billion dollars against assets of about fifteen. The shared-office company had spent four years trying to recover from a collapse that began in 2019, when a planned listing exposed losses and a founder whose behaviour alarmed investors.

The core mismatch was structural. WeWork signed long leases with landlords, often for fifteen years, and sold short-term access to desks, sometimes by the month. When the economy was growing and occupancy was high, the spread paid for the overhead and the expansion. When occupancy fell, the company carried the rent anyway, and the pandemic emptied its buildings in the middle of its biggest expansion. By 2023 roughly half of its leases were losing money at current occupancy.

The history explains how the company arrived at that position. It was valued at forty-seven billion dollars in a private round in 2019, and its prospectus that year disclosed losses, related-party transactions and a plan to sell its own currency. The listing was withdrawn, the valuation collapsed, and SoftBank, which had invested more than ten billion dollars, took control and installed a new chief executive. The new team closed locations, cut staff and renegotiated leases, and the business did improve at the operating level while the debt remained.

Chapter 11 gives a company room to walk away from contracts it can no longer afford, and the case will be watched by retail landlords who spent a decade signing leases with co-working tenants on the strength of their growth. Some of the largest landlords in New York and London are creditors in the case, and a few have already written down the value of their exposure. The company said it intended to keep operating while it restructures and to reject leases in buildings it no longer wants.

One wider question is whether the model itself works. Flexible office space has real customers, from freelancers to companies that want overflow desks without signing a lease, and several competitors have grown by renting rather than owning the space. The difference between WeWork and those rivals was ambition and leverage: the company wanted to be a global brand with a property portfolio, and it borrowed against a business that could never carry that weight if demand turned.

Adam Neumann, the founder, has watched the process from outside, and his own attempt to buy the company back was reported and then abandoned. Employees faced a restructuring in which the company said most would keep their jobs at first, with cuts to follow in buildings it handed back. The office market, meanwhile, is still absorbing the shift to hybrid work, and the bankruptcy will leave a set of desirable buildings looking for tenants at a moment when few are signing long leases.
