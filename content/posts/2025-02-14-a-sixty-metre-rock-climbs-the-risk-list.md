description: "Asteroid 2024 YR4 reached a three per cent chance of hitting Earth in 2032 before new observations lowered it again, a rare exercise in the machinery that planetary defence built for exactly this case."
---

Discovery came on 27 December 2024 by the ATLAS survey in Hawaii, two days before it passed close to Earth, and the orbit that the discovery provided was good enough to show that a similar pass was due in December 2032. A body of forty to ninety metres, with a most likely diameter near sixty, is large enough to damage a city and small enough that the catalogue had never contained it.

Probability climbed as the observations accumulated. The impact corridor on 22 December 2032 crossed the eastern Pacific, the Atlantic, Africa and South Asia, and the chance rose from about one per cent to 2 per cent and then, on 18 February, to 3.1 per cent. That is the highest probability ever recorded for an object above one per cent in this size class, and it triggered the notification procedures that the international asteroid warning network exists to run.

Rise in probability was not a change in the rock's path. It was a narrowing of the uncertainty, and a narrowing band that still contains Earth raises the fraction of the probability that falls on the planet. A prediction with a wide error bar spreads the chance across a corridor several thousand kilometres wide; a prediction with a narrow one concentrates it, and the concentration is what a probability of impact measures.

Instruments that resolved the question were a combination of new optical observations from ground telescopes and, for the shape and the rotation, radar and thermal measurements. A body this small is faint and fast, and the window in which it is observable closed in the spring as the object moved away from Earth's vicinity; the next favourable geometry comes in 2028, which is why the 2032 question had to be settled with data taken in a few weeks or left open for three years.

By 20 February the probability had fallen to 0.28 per cent, and by late February it was below one in ten thousand, which is the threshold at which the object comes off the risk list. The drop followed the same logic as the rise, with a wider set of observations shrinking the error ellipse until it no longer touched Earth. The Moon remained a small target at about one per cent for a while before it too was excluded.

The episode is the first real test of the warning system for an object in the range that a deflection mission could handle. A kinetic impactor launched in 2028 could reach such a body with years to spare, and the DART mission demonstrated the technique on a moonlet of Didymos in 2022. A decision to launch would have had to be taken on a probability that was still rising, and the criteria for that decision are the part of planetary defence that has never been exercised.

What the object leaves behind is a better characterized rock and a public that has seen the machinery work. Astronomers will observe it in 2028, the orbit will be known well enough to retire the question for good, and the exercise has produced a documented case of a risk that rose, peaked and fell within eight weeks, which is what the procedures were written for.
