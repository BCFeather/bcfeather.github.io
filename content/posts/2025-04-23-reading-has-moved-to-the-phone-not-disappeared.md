description: "Book sales have held up while the time people spend reading has shifted to a screen, and the two numbers describe different industries."
---

World Book Day is a promotion that assumes people want to read more and need an occasion, and the evidence suggests that the demand was never the problem. Print sales in most large markets have been stable or rising for a decade, and the number of people who read a book in a given year has stayed roughly flat. What has changed is where the reading happens and what competes with it. A household that buys two books a year is a household that the industry counts as a reader, and the count is generous.

The phone absorbed the commute. A reader who once carried a paperback now carries a device with a hundred apps, and the minutes that used to go to a chapter now go to a feed. The substitution is not total: audiobooks and e-books have grown, and a reader who listens on a walk is reading in a form that the statistics did not exist to count. A feed is designed to be interruptible, and a chapter is not, which is the real difference between the two formats.

Bookshops have recovered in a specific niche. The stores that survived the discount era are the ones that became places rather than shops, with events, cafes and a curation that a website cannot replicate. A chain that competes on price against an online retailer loses, and a store that competes on discovery wins a customer who was not looking for a specific title. A store that hosts a reading group on a Tuesday evening is a store that has built a habit rather than a transaction.

Publishers face a margin problem that has little to do with reading. A bestseller sells at a discount that leaves the publisher with a thin margin, and a midlist title sells few copies at full price. The industry survives on the former and complains about the latter, and the advances that fund the midlist come from the bestseller. A bestseller that sells a million copies at a discount earns less per copy than one that sells a hundred thousand at full price.

Censorship and copyright are the policy questions that shape the catalogue. A market with a rigorous review process produces fewer translations, and a market with weak copyright enforcement produces fewer originals. Both constraints act on the supply of books rather than on the demand for them. A translation that is licensed cheaply is a book that a small press can afford to publish, and the small presses are the ones that find the next market.

What the day is actually for is the library. A library lends for free, runs children's programmes and provides the only reading material that some households have. A country that funds libraries is a country that has decided that reading is infrastructure, and the decision is cheap relative to almost any other cultural subsidy. A library that closes on a Sunday is a library that has decided which readers it serves.
