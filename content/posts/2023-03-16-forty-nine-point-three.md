description: "On 16 March 2023 the French government used article 49.3 of the constitution to pass its pension reform without a vote in the National Assembly, raising the retirement age to sixty-four."
---

Shortly after the assembly had failed to finish its debate, the prime minister stood up and used the article that allows a government to pass a bill without a vote. Article 49.3 had been used dozens of times since 1958 and had become a routine tool for budget legislation, and it had never been used on a reform of this size. Elisabeth Borne told the chamber that the government was taking responsibility, which is the phrase that the article allows a prime minister to say. The chamber had been debating the text for a fortnight and had not reached the article that raises the retirement age, and the government had said it would not wait.

The reform raised the legal retirement age from sixty-two to sixty-four, phased over eight years, and increased the number of years of contributions required for a full pension to forty-three. It also ended several special regimes, including the one for the Paris transport authority, and it introduced a minimum pension for people who had worked a full career. The government's argument was that the system would be in deficit within a decade without it, and the council that audits the accounts had published numbers that supported the claim.

Calculations had been made earlier, in the winter, when the government still believed it could win a vote. The government had spent the winter negotiating with the opposition party that had once been its parliamentary partner, and the negotiation had failed in the same week that the bill reached the floor. The vote count in the assembly showed that a majority would vote against, and the article was used because the alternative was a defeat rather than because the debate had run out of time.

Since January the unions had been preparing for the moment. Refineries stopped, railways ran a skeleton service, and the bins in Paris went uncollected for weeks, which produced images that the international press used for the rest of the year. The strikes were strongest in the sectors where retirement age mattered most, and the turnout in the demonstrations peaked at more than a million people on a single day.

The article gives the opposition a way of answering, which is a censure motion, and two were filed within the hour. The first came from the centrist group that had been the government's partner, and the second from the far right, and both were debated on 20 March. The government's survival depended on the votes of a few dozen members who had said they would never support the reform but who also did not want an election. The motion needed two hundred and eighty-seven votes, and the government's own arithmetic had put its ceiling at two hundred and eighty.

What the decision produced was a law and a legitimacy problem. The bill was adopted because the constitution says a bill is adopted if no censure motion passes, and the public understood the sentence as a technicality. The government had the votes of the parliament it did not have, and the argument that followed was about whether that was the same thing as consent.
