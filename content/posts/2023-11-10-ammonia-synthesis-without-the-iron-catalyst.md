description: "Ammonia can be made from nitrogen and water at room temperature if a lithium intermediate carries the nitrogen, and the electrode chemistry that does it is the part that decides whether it works."
---

Ammonia is made by the Haber process in plants that consume about one per cent of the world's energy, and the process runs at a few hundred degrees and a few hundred atmospheres. A route that works at room temperature would need a different way of breaking the nitrogen-nitrogen bond, since the triple bond is the reason the industrial process runs hot. The route that has been developed breaks it with an alkali metal instead of with an iron surface.

The mechanism proceeds in steps. Lithium metal, or an lithium alloy, reacts with nitrogen to form lithium nitride, and the nitride reacts with protons to give ammonia and lithium ions. The nitrogen is activated by the metal and not by a transition metal, and the bond that is broken is the same bond that the Haber catalyst breaks, with a different reagent doing the breaking. The cycle closes when the lithium ions are reduced back to metal at an electrode.

The electrochemistry is where the difficulty lies. A cell that reduces lithium at one electrode and oxidises hydrogen at the other has a voltage that depends on the solvent and on the membrane, and the membrane has to conduct lithium ions while blocking the ammonia that is produced. A membrane that leaks allows the ammonia to reach the anode and react there, which loses the product and the efficiency at the same time.

Proton supply is the second problem. The protons have to come from water or from hydrogen, and the source decides the overpotential. A cell that uses water as the proton source splits water at the anode, which is the same reaction that an electrolyser performs, and the energy cost of the whole route is the sum of that step and the nitrogen step. The comparison with the Haber process has to include the electrolyser, and that comparison is where the numbers are least favourable. A cell that runs on hydrogen instead avoids the water-splitting half but pays for the hydrogen upstream, and the two routes have to be compared with their balance of plant included.

Rate is set by the dissolution of nitrogen into the electrolyte and by the transport of lithium to the surface. A cell that runs at a high current depletes the nitrogen at the electrode and the reaction stops, and the current density that has been demonstrated is a few milliamperes per square centimetre, which is three orders of magnitude below the current of an industrial electrolyser. The gap is not a detail of the catalyst but a limit of the transport.

What has been shown is that the metal route works and that the chemistry is thermodynamically possible at room temperature. Whether it can be made to run at a rate that a plant would accept is a question about membranes, about solvents that survive the lithium, and about a cell design that keeps the nitrogen dissolved. Those are engineering questions with a chemistry answer, and the answer has not been given yet.
