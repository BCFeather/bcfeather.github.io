Polling stations opened at seven in the morning on 1 November and closed at ten at night, and by then the ritual had become entirely familiar to the people taking part. Israelis had been asked to choose a parliament four times since April 2019, and each count had produced a Knesset that could not agree on a government. This time the campaign was short, the weather was mild, and the roads into Jerusalem filled early with party volunteers holding placards at the junctions. Cars passed them with horns sounding, and the pavement outside the schools where the booths were set up was covered with the paper slips that had come with the party leaflets. Turnout was high, which is what happens when the voters believe that a single seat will decide the result.

Counting took four days to settle, because the last seats depend on ballots cast by soldiers, prisoners, hospital patients and diplomats abroad. When the central election committee published its final figures, Likud held thirty-two seats, Yesh Atid twenty-four, Religious Zionism fourteen, National Unity twelve, Shas eleven and United Torah Judaism seven. Meretz and Balad both fell below the threshold of three and a quarter per cent, and the votes cast for them were redistributed among the parties that had survived the cut. Those two failures were worth more than a seat each, and they moved the balance of the house by a margin nobody had predicted.

One detail decided the outcome. The parties willing to sit with Netanyahu came to sixty-four seats in a house of one hundred and twenty, a majority of one, and a combination that no rival could match. His opponents, from Yesh Atid through the remains of the left and the Arab parties, could assemble rather less, and several of them had already refused to sit with one another. The arithmetic was narrow enough that a single defection in the new house would break it, and every faction in the bloc knew that when the agreements were signed.

Size of the Religious Zionism list surprised people who had watched its leaders for years. Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich had spent the campaign arguing that the previous government was too soft on security and too willing to bargain, and fourteen seats made them the third largest force in the house. A party that had been a sect on the fringe now held the balance of the coalition, and both men had begun to speak openly about the ministries they wanted. Ben-Gvir asked for the police, and Smotrich asked for the treasury, and neither request was treated as a joke.

Yair Lapid conceded on the second evening, and said that he wished the incoming prime minister well for the sake of the country. His government had lasted a year, a rotating arrangement that came apart over a budget vote and a law about national service, and it had been the most diverse coalition in the country's history. Its collapse returned Israel to the arithmetic that had trapped it since 2019, when the same blocs began refusing to sit together in the same room. He told his office to prepare for a transfer of power.

What followed was procedure. The president was to consult each party, then hand a mandate to the candidate with the best chance of assembling sixty-one seats, and Netanyahu was expected to receive it with four weeks, extendable by two, to sign the agreements. His trial on corruption charges continues, and the court had refused to accept a plea bargain of the kind that would have ended it. His partners want the police ministry and the finance ministry, and the coalition that emerges will be the most right-wing the country has known.
