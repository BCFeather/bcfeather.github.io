description: "A study of multispecies biofilms finds that the spatial architecture of clonal cooperation breaks down under predation, which changes the ecology of the community and the way its members compete."
---





A biofilm is a community with a structure. Bacteria attach to a surface, secrete a matrix and divide in place, so the descendants of a founder stay together, and that spatial arrangement is what makes cooperation possible, because a cell that pays a cost can benefit its relatives.

In *PNAS* on 7 February 2023, what happens when the arrangement is disturbed is examined. The authors build multispecies biofilms, map their spatial structure as clones grow, introduce a predator that consumes bacteria, and observe the resulting ecology.

Breakdown is the result. Predation disrupts the clonal architecture, mixing cells that were relatives with cells that were not, and the cooperation that depends on relatedness weakens, so the community becomes less productive and more heterogeneous. Geometry supplies the mechanism, since a predator that moves through a biofilm opens channels and displaces clusters, and a displaced cluster loses the local density that lets secreted products be shared.

Because a biofilm's properties, such as its resistance to antibiotics, are usually attributed to the matrix or to the cells' physiology, the spatial structure is a third factor, and this work shows it can be altered from outside. Signals are another route, since cells coordinate through diffusible quorum-sensing molecules whose concentration depends on local density, and breaking the clusters pushes the signals below threshold, which is how architecture becomes behaviour.

Design gives the result its strength. A defined community, a defined predator and a spatial readout let the authors separate the effect of predation from the effect of competition, which a natural sample would confound. The predator's own ecology is the other half, because a predator feeding on a structured community reaches prey that an unstructured one would hide, and its growth is a function of the architecture it destroys. The matrix belongs to the story as well, since extracellular polymers hold the community together and slow a predator's movement, and a matrix degraded by the predator's enzymes no longer protects.

Evolution is what the paper does not measure, because a community under predation selects for cells that resist being eaten, and resistance may change the architecture in turn. Species number is the limit, since a three-species biofilm models a community of hundreds, and the principles may scale while the parameters differ. Medicine draws the implication: a biofilm in a wound or on a catheter is a structured community, and a treatment that disrupts its architecture may leave it more vulnerable rather than less, which is the opposite of what antibiotics alone achieve. The ecological lesson generalizes, because competition, cooperation and predation are usually modelled between populations, and here geometry sets their strength, which makes a community's shape a parameter of its ecology.

Treatment design is where the implication lands, because a therapy that disrupts architecture and a therapy that kills cells are different tools, and combining them needs a schedule this experiment has not tested. Predation is a normal condition in a real community, and a model that isolates it shows how much of a biofilm's behaviour depends on staying put.
