Drylands are usually mapped as a single category, and the trees within them are invisible to a satellite whose pixel is larger than a canopy. An inventory that counts individual trees changes the carbon accounting for a fifth of the land surface.

In *Nature* on 2 March 2023, one such inventory is produced. Compton Tucker and colleagues apply machine learning to high-resolution imagery covering nearly ten million square kilometres of sub-Saharan Africa, detect individual tree crowns, and count close to ten billion trees.

Carbon follows from the count. Each detected crown is assigned a wood, foliage and root mass, and the totals differ by rainfall zone, at about 51 kilograms of carbon per tree in the driest regions rising to about 98 kilograms where rainfall is greatest. Zoning is the analytical choice, because a tree's size and density depend on water, and an average over the whole region would misrepresent both ends.

Because field plots measure trees accurately over hectares and a survey that covers a continent at tree resolution is the only way to reconcile plot and map, scale is the method's contribution, and the model is trained on the plots before being applied to the imagery. Detection rests on shape and shadow, since a dryland tree casts a shadow longer than its crown at most sun angles, and that pair is what a model learns to recognize while grass and bare soil produce neither.

Policy gains a baseline, because dryland restoration is a commitment in many African countries and in international finance, and a baseline is what makes a gain measurable, whereas a programme that plants trees without a census cannot report its effect. Carbon markets get a correction, since woody biomass in drylands has been estimated from coarse land cover and a count of individual trees revises it in both directions depending on the zone. Release is part of the result as well, as wood mass, foliage mass, root mass and carbon stock for each tree are published, which lets a country, a project or a critic recompute the totals from the same detections rather than trusting a summary.

Resolution and model error set the limit. A canopy that overlaps another is one detection or two, a shrub counted as a tree inflates the total, and the paper reports error rates along with the regions where they are largest.

Change is what the inventory cannot show, because a single epoch is a snapshot and the trend that matters for carbon is a difference between epochs, so the database is designed to be repeated. A tree outside a forest is not counted by forest policy, and for the countries involved an inventory that counts it is an inventory that gives it standing. A repeated census would also expose loss, and loss detection is where an inventory becomes an early warning rather than an accounting entry. Standing is what the inventory grants, since a resource that can be counted can be argued for in a budget, and a resource that cannot be counted is one that a ministry cannot defend.
