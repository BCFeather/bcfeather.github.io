World water day falls on the twenty-second of March, and the theme this year is the glacier. The choice is unusual for an observance that usually concerns taps and pipes, and it is the right one, because the glaciers of the high mountains are the reservoirs that fill the rivers of Asia in the dry months, and the reservoirs are being drawn down.

What happens is simple; what follows is not. A glacier accumulates snow in the winter and releases meltwater in the summer, and a river fed by a glacier carries water in July when the monsoon has not arrived or has failed. The volume that a glacier can store depends on its mass, and the mass of most glaciers in the region has been falling for forty years. The first effect of the loss is more water, because a shrinking glacier melts faster than a stable one, and the second effect is less water, because a smaller glacier has less to give.

Science has been trying to pin down the timetable of the two effects. The inventories that satellites and field teams have built show a consistent pattern, with the small glaciers in the eastern ranges losing mass fastest and the large ones in the west losing more slowly. The projections put the peak of the meltwater in the middle of this century for many basins, and the decline after it, and the uncertainty in the date is large enough that a water authority cannot plan on it alone.

What a water authority can plan on is the variability, which has already changed. A river that was fed by a predictable melt now has a season in which the flow depends on whether the previous winter was snowy, and the reservoirs that were designed for the old pattern are being operated with rules that are revised every few years. The engineering of a dam assumes a hydrology, and the hydrology has moved.

The demand side has moved too. The cities of the plain have grown, the irrigation districts have expanded, and the water that the glacier supplies is now claimed by more users than the glacier was ever asked to serve. A cubic metre of meltwater that reaches a city is a cubic metre that does not reach a farm, and the allocation between them is a political question that the hydrology makes urgent.

What the day is good for is the reminder that water is a stock and a flow at the same time. The stock in the glacier took thousands of years to accumulate and the flow through the river is what the year's farmers and cities use. A society can manage the flow with dams and canals and prices, and it cannot manufacture the stock, which is why the ice at the head of the river is the part of the water system that no engineering can replace, and why the measurement of it has become a standing item in the budgets of the agencies that study it.
