Consumer confidence fell to 103.2 in September from a revised 108.7 in August, the Conference Board said on 26 September. The expectations component fell to 73.3, while the measure of the present situation was little changed at 147.1. The figure was below the forecast of about 105, and the index has now fallen in two of the last three months after a summer of improvement.

Fuel is the visible cause. The national average price of petrol rose by about 25 cents over the month, and the respondents mentioned the prices at the pump more often than any other subject, which is the pattern of a mood that follows the weekly sign. A tank that costs more is a household that feels poorer by the day. The energy prices are about 10 per cent higher than in June, and the economists expect them to fall again as the winter blend reaches the stations. The survey's buying plans for the vehicles fell to their lowest since the start of the pandemic, and the plans for the appliances followed.

Borrowing costs are the structural cause. The mortgage rate is near 7.5 per cent, the highest in more than twenty years, and the credit card rate has passed 20, which is the reason the buying plans for the houses and the cars fell. A loan that is expensive is a purchase that is postponed. The average rate on a thirty-year mortgage is the highest since 2000, and the applications for the loans are at their lowest since 1995.

Present conditions held up because jobs are still there. The share of the respondents who said that jobs are plentiful rose again, and the unemployment rate has stayed below four per cent, which is the floor under the index. A pay packet that arrives is a mood that does not collapse. The respondents were more positive about the availability of the jobs than at any time this year, and the gap between the present and the expectations is the widest since 2022.

Expectations are the number that matters for the forecast. The measure has fallen below the level that the economists associate with a recession in four of the last five months, and the index is now about 15 points below its level before the pandemic. An expectation that sags is a spending that follows with a lag. The saving rate has fallen to about 3.5 per cent, and the households have been borrowing on the credit cards to keep the spending up.

What the month shows is that the household is comfortable with the job and unhappy with the price, and the second is what the survey has been recording since the summer. A consumer who is confident about the job and frightened by the price is a consumer who keeps the spending and cuts the saving, and the cut has limits.
