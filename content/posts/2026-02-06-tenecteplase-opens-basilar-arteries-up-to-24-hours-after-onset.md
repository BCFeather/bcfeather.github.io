Basilar artery occlusion is the stroke that kills fastest, because the artery supplies the brainstem, and roughly nine in ten patients are left dead or disabled if the vessel does not open. Thrombectomy is the established treatment, and it depends on reaching a centre with a neurointerventional team inside a window measured in hours. Most of the world's population does not live within that window, which makes a drug that can be given anywhere a different kind of solution.

TRACE-5 was designed to test intravenous tenecteplase against standard medical treatment in patients with subtotal or complete basilar occlusion within 24 hours of onset, including those who woke with symptoms or whose onset was unwitnessed. Tenecteplase was given at 0.25 milligrams per kilogram to a maximum of 25 milligrams, and endovascular treatment was permitted in both arms. The trial was multicentre, prospective, randomized, open-label with blinded endpoint assessment, and designed as a superiority phase 3 study. It appeared in *The Lancet* on 6 February.

The result favoured thrombolysis. Tenecteplase reduced disability at the primary assessment without a significant increase in symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage or death, which is the safety finding that the late window most needed. The investigators present the trial as the first evidence that basilar occlusion can be treated with a lytic drug up to 24 hours after onset, which is a claim about a specific artery rather than about stroke in general.

A 24-hour window is the striking element and the one that requires explanation. Brainstem tissue tolerates ischaemia differently from cortex, and the collateral supply through the posterior circulation is variable enough that some patients retain viable tissue far longer than the anterior circulation would allow. Selection on imaging and not on the clock is the principle that makes late treatment defensible.

Generalizability is the practical question. TRACE-5 recruited in China and in Australia, with a protocol that specified imaging selection, and a hospital that can obtain that imaging is a hospital that can also transfer a patient for thrombectomy. The trial's benefit is largest for the patient who presents late at a centre without intervention, and that patient is exactly the one the trial's protocol is least able to select.

Comparator design deserves attention, because it decides what the trial has shown. Standard medical treatment permitted alteplase within 4.5 hours, so some patients in the control arm received a lytic drug, which makes the comparison a test of tenecteplase against the best available medical care and not against nothing. That design strengthens the result and complicates its interpretation.

Whether the finding changes guidelines will depend on a second trial and on how the imaging selection is standardized. A single positive trial in an open-label design with a subjective primary endpoint has moved stroke practice before, and it has also produced reversals. The direction of the result, though, is what the field expected from the anatomy.
