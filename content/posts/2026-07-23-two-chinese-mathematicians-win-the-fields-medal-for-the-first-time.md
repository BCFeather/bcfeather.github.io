The Fields Medal was awarded to Deng Yu and Wang Hong at the opening ceremony of the International Congress of Mathematicians in Philadelphia on 23 July, the first time the prize has gone to mathematicians who are Chinese nationals. The other two laureates were John Pardon of the United States and Jacob Tsimerman of Canada. The medal is given every four years to up to four mathematicians under forty, and it is the closest thing the discipline has to a Nobel. The congress runs from 23 to 26 July and brought several thousand mathematicians to the Pennsylvania Convention Center.

Deng was cited for work on partial differential equations, in a line of research that asks how solutions behave over time and whether they stay smooth. The problems in that field connect to fluid dynamics and to the kinetic theory of gases, and progress usually comes from estimates that hold across a whole class of equations rather than from a single example. His results have been described by colleagues as settling questions that had been open for decades. The citation committee reads a body of work rather than a single paper, and it weighs influence on the field as much as any one result.

Wang was cited for work in harmonic analysis and for a proof, with a co-author, of the three-dimensional Kakeya conjecture. The conjecture asks how small a set can be while containing a unit line segment in every direction, and it had resisted attack since the early twentieth century. Her proof combined ideas from combinatorics and from geometry, and it changed what analysts expect from the tools they use. Her proof appeared in a paper that other analysts have been checking line by line since it was posted.

Pardon was recognized for work in symplectic geometry and on counting holomorphic curves, a subject that began with questions about the shortest paths in curved space and became a machinery for computing invariants. Tsimerman was recognized for work on unlikely intersections and on the arithmetic of Shimura varieties, which connects geometry to number theory. Both had been named as likely laureates in the years before the congress. All four prizes were announced in the same ceremony, and the laureates appeared on stage together.

Reaction in China was immediate. Shing-Tung Yau, himself a Fields laureate and now a professor at Tsinghua, published a note congratulating the two and arguing that Chinese mathematics had reached a point where its best work could be done at home. Universities posted announcements within hours, and the congress's programme listed sessions in which both laureates were to speak later in the week. Chinese universities have been building doctoral programmes in pure mathematics for two decades, and the first cohorts now hold chairs.

Prizes lag the work, and this one reflects a generation that entered the field when China was expanding its universities. The question the medal raises is whether the pipeline that produced two laureates in a single cycle will keep producing them, and the answer depends on institutions rather than on a single cohort. The medal also changes what the laureates do next, because the years after it are spent on committees as much as on research.
