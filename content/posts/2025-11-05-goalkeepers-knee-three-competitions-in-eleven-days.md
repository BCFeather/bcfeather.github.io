A goalkeeper played three competitions in eleven days and tore a ligament in the third, and the injury has become an argument about the calendar. The medical literature has a number for this, and the number is not about the total matches a season holds.

Recovery interval between matches is the measure that matters. A study of 2,400 injuries across five leagues found that a match played fewer than 96 hours after a previous one carries 1.6 times the injury rate of a match played after five days, and the effect is largest for players over 30.

Travel is part of the interval. A flight of four hours with a cabin pressure equivalent to 2,000 metres costs about half a day of recovery, and a competition that spans a continent adds that to every fixture.

Three matches in eleven days is the pattern, and the goalkeeper's case fits it. Three matches in eleven days, two of them after flights of three and five hours, and an anterior cruciate ligament rupture in a landing after a save. A goalkeeper's exposure to that mechanism is higher than a midfielder's because the position involves repeated jumping.

The counterargument is squad rotation. A club with 25 players can rotate, and the study's data show that teams with the largest squads have the lowest injury rates per match played. The constraint is that a goalkeeper cannot rotate.

Broadcast scheduling sets the fixtures. A competition's contract specifies kick-off windows, and a team in three competitions has three contracts to satisfy. No medical standard can override a contract, which is why the study's authors address their recommendation to the competition organizers.

Another sport already runs the instrument this case needs. A basketball league limits a player's back-to-back games and reports a 12 per cent injury reduction since the rule. The mechanism is identical and the regulatory instrument is available.

A goalkeeper's load is not measured in distance. A match involves 90 minutes of standing, diving and restarting, and the tissue that fails is the ligament rather than the muscle. A load model built for outfield players understates the position's risk.

Calendar design is the regulator's lever. A competition's fixture list sets the minimum interval, and a governing body that wants a three-day minimum must move matches to other dates. That is a decision about revenue and not about physiology.

A club's incentive is to play the best goalkeeper and a competition's incentive is to fill its dates, and the player's interest is the one that neither party's contract protects. A medical department's recommendation is advice rather than a veto, and the case is the reason some federations have begun to require it in writing.

Fixture congestion is a measurable exposure rather than a matter of opinion. The number to argue about is the interval, and the interval is set by a contract that no one at the training ground can change.
