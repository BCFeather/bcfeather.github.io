*KRAS* was called undruggable for four decades, and the covalent inhibitors that finally bound the G12C mutant proved the label wrong without solving the problem. In lung cancer the drugs shrink tumours in roughly a third of patients and the responses last less than a year, and in colorectal cancer the same drugs do much less, because the tumour rewires its signalling within hours of the drug arriving. Combinations that block the escape route are the obvious next move. Colorectal tumours also carry a much higher mutational burden than lung tumours, which gives them more routes around a single blockade.

Ifebemtinib inhibits a kinase that sits on that escape route, and garsorasib is a *KRAS* G12C inhibitor already developed in China. A multicentre randomised phase 1b/2 trial tested the pair in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer whose tumours carried the mutation and who had already received chemotherapy. The results appeared in *The Lancet Oncology* in its September issue, volume 27, at page 1133. The trial enrolled patients who had received at least one prior line, and the primary endpoint was response rate in the randomised portion.

Activity was real and the statistics were not. The combination produced responses at a rate that the authors describe as promising, with a safety profile consistent with what each drug does on its own, and the randomised comparison against garsorasib alone did not reach significance for the improvement in response rate. That pattern, a signal that fails a formal test, is common in early-phase work and it leaves the field with an unresolved question. A response rate that does not clear its prespecified threshold in a randomised comparison is a negative result for that endpoint, whatever the biological rationale.

Colorectal cancer has been the graveyard of *KRAS* inhibition, and the reasons are partly anatomical. The colon epithelium expresses high levels of the receptor that drives the feedback loop, so a single inhibitor is defeated quickly, and any combination has to be tolerable enough to give continuously. A phase 1b/2 trial with a few dozen patients per arm cannot establish either durability or survival. Tolerability at the doses used was the constraint the designers had to solve, and the paper reports dose reductions as the main consequence, with few permanent discontinuations.

The safety profile is the part worth keeping. Both drugs carry gastrointestinal and hepatic effects, and the combination did not produce a rate of serious events that would stop development. That is a low bar and a necessary one, since a regimen for third-line disease has to be gentler than the disease it treats. Hepatic enzymes rose in a proportion of patients and recovered on dose interruption, which is the pattern that lets a regimen continue rather than stop.

The next step is a larger randomised trial with progression-free survival as its endpoint, and the design will need to specify how the combination is sequenced against standard care. For now the paper records a hypothesis that survived early testing, which is more than most attempts at this target have managed. A trial that combines two experimental agents in a small population is a bet on biology, and the bet is that the escape route matters more than the cost of adding a second drug.
