description: "A study in *Nature* reports that the brain of the budgerigar contains a region organized like the language areas of the human cortex, which makes the bird a better model for speech than the songbirds that researchers usually use."
---

Budgerigars are small Australian parrots that learn to imitate sounds, including human speech, and they do it with a brain that is organized on a plan different from the mammalian one. Mammals have a cortex with layered columns, and birds have a pallium with nuclei, and the two structures evolved separately from a common ancestor that had neither.

A paper in *Nature* maps the vocal region of the budgerigar brain and finds that it contains a pathway that is functionally like the human one: a motor area that produces the sound, an auditory area that analyses it and a set of connections between the two that allow a bird to compare what it hears with what it says. The comparison is what a parrot does when it imitates, and a brain that supports it has to have the loop that carries the heard sound back to the motor plan, which is the loop that the mapping found.

Human parallel is anatomical as well as functional. In the human brain the language network is lateralized, with the left hemisphere doing most of the work, and the budgerigar's vocal pathway shows a similar asymmetry. The authors argue that the convergence is not an accident of naming, because the same computational problem, which is to learn a sequence of sounds and to produce it, produces the same architecture in two lineages separated by 300 million years.

Advantage over songbirds is the repertoire. A songbird learns a fixed song with a small number of syllables, and a budgerigar learns to imitate an open-ended set of sounds, which is closer to what human language requires. A model that can imitate a new word on request is a model in which the learning can be studied with stimuli that a songbird cannot produce.

What the study offers clinicians is a model for disorders of speech. A region that is organized like the human one, in an animal that is cheap to keep and that learns quickly, gives a laboratory a way to test a hypothesis about how a language network fails. The authors do not claim that a bird's brain is a human's, and they do claim that the circuit is comparable.

Limit is the cortex. A bird has no layered cortex, and the pallial nuclei that do the work are not homologous to the cortical areas that do it in a human. The functional similarity is real, and the developmental route to it is different, which makes the bird a model of the computation rather than of the tissue.

What the paper shows is that a language area is not a unique solution that requires a cortex. The same circuit has been assembled twice, in lineages that separated before the dinosaurs, and the parrot version is the one that a laboratory can watch learn a word, which makes it the more useful of the two for anyone who wants to study how a sound becomes a symbol.
