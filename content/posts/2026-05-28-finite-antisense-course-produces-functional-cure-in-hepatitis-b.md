description: "Two phase 3 trials in The New England Journal of Medicine report that bepirovirsen, given for a fixed course, produced a functional cure in about one in five patients with chronic hepatitis B."
---

Chronic hepatitis B is managed rather than cured. Nucleotide analogues suppress the virus indefinitely and rarely clear the surface antigen, so patients stay on treatment for decades and remain at risk of cirrhosis and liver cancer. A functional cure, meaning sustained loss of hepatitis B surface antigen with undetectable viral DNA, has been the field's target for a generation. The trials also matter for what they say about the standard of care, since a functional cure rate of zero on placebo is a reminder that decades of antiviral therapy have not changed the underlying reservoir.

Bepirovirsen is an antisense oligonucleotide that reduces viral RNA, including transcripts produced from DNA integrated into the host genome, which is the reservoir that nucleoside analogues cannot touch. The two trials reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 28 May gave the drug for a finite course and then stopped, which is the design that separates a cure from another chronic therapy.

B-Well 1 produced a functional cure at week 72 in 127 of 650 patients, 20 per cent, against none of 328 on placebo. B-Well 2 produced 106 of 570, 19 per cent, against none of 286. The placebo arms are the striking part of that comparison, because they show what the existing standard achieves when added to the same background therapy, which is nothing. Bepirovirsen is given as a finite course rather than as maintenance, which is why the week 72 time point matters: the drug has been withdrawn and the answer is still visible.

Safety was the price. Pooled adverse events occurred in 91 per cent of treated patients and 73 per cent of controls, serious adverse events in 7 and 4 per cent, and grade 3 or worse events in 16 and 3 per cent. Alanine aminotransferase rises were the commonest grade 3 event, in 6 per cent, which is what happens when an antiviral works hard enough to provoke an immune response.

One in five is not a cure for everybody, and the trials will be read for who the responders were. Baseline surface antigen levels and the state of the viral reservoir are the usual predictors, and a drug that works in a fifth of patients is a drug that needs a companion test rather than a blanket prescription. Cost will decide how the drug is deployed first, since a finite course priced per cure is cheaper than lifelong antiviral therapy in most health systems.

The clinical significance is nonetheless large. Patients who clear the surface antigen can stop antiviral therapy, and the risk of liver cancer falls, though it does not disappear. A finite course also changes the economics and the adherence problem, and it gives the field a benchmark against which the next generation of siRNA, capsid assembly modulators and therapeutic vaccines can be measured. The follow-up will also show whether the cure holds, since surface antigen can return and the trials are still reporting.
