American employers added 272,000 jobs in May, well above the 180,000 that forecasters expected, while the unemployment rate rose to 4.0 per cent from 3.9. The two surveys moved in opposite directions, which is the puzzle at the centre of the report. The gap is the largest of the year, and the economists who follow the surveys say it is usually resolved by the payroll figure being revised down rather than by the household figure being revised up.

Household survey is the weaker one. It showed employment falling and the labour force shrinking, which is why the unemployment rate rose even as the payroll count beat expectations. A payroll survey counts jobs and a household survey counts people, and the gap between them has widened all year. Participation for prime-age workers fell slightly, which is the detail that explains the rise in the unemployment rate, because fewer people were looking for work.

Wages rose 0.4 per cent on the month and 4.1 per cent on the year, faster than the previous reading, which is the detail that made the market push back the expected date of the first cut. A wage number that accelerates is a number that the committee cannot ignore. The health care sector has added jobs every month for three years, and government hiring has been supported by the spending bills passed in the previous administration.

Composition was narrow. Health care and government added a large share of the jobs, and the private service sector hired at a slower pace than in the winter. A total that depends on two sectors is a total that the next month can revise away. The two-year yield rose by fifteen basis points, a large move for a single report, and the futures market pushed the expected first cut from September to November.

Reaction was a sell-off in bonds. Yields rose, the probability of a September cut fell, and the shares that had led the rally gave back part of their gain, which is the response of a market that had positioned for a weaker number. A report that is strong is a report that costs the bond market money. The strength of the report also complicates the political argument, because the government wants to claim the growth and the central bank wants to see it slow.

What the month shows is that the two surveys can disagree for months without either being wrong, and the committee has said it will weight the trend rather than any single print. A labour market that adds a quarter of a million jobs while the unemployment rate rises is a market where the measurement is doing the work, and the revisions will decide which survey was right. The report was the last before the committee met, and it was the reason the meeting in June was described as a close call even though the vote was unanimous.
