description: "California now has enough batteries to shift a large share of its midday solar surplus into the evening, and the duck curve has begun to flatten."
---

California's grid operator publishes a chart every day that shows the net load it has to serve, and the shape of that chart has been the subject of a decade of planning. Solar output peaks at midday and collapses at sunset, and the net load that remains after solar is subtracted dips in the afternoon and then rises steeply as the sun goes down. The curve looks like a duck, and the duck's neck is the ramp that the system has to meet with something other than sunlight. A chart that planners once used to describe a problem is now the chart they use to measure the fix.

Batteries have changed the shape. Installed storage capacity in the state has grown from a few hundred megawatts in 2019 to more than ten thousand, and most of it is lithium iron phosphate in containers at substations. A battery that charges at midday and discharges between five and nine in the evening moves energy across six hours, which is exactly the length of the ramp. A battery that is paid for capacity rather than for energy is a battery that is available when the system needs it and idle when it does not.

The economics of that move depend on the spread. A battery earns revenue from the difference between the price it pays to charge and the price it receives to discharge, and the midday price has fallen towards zero as solar has grown. A spread that is too narrow leaves the battery earning only from capacity payments, and those payments have been the reason the projects were financed. A project that earns most of its revenue from a single evening peak is a project that is exposed to the weather in that hour.

Negative prices are the signal that the system has more solar than it can use. When the midday price goes below zero, a generator is paying to produce, and a battery that is paid to charge turns the problem into revenue. Curtailment of solar in the state has risen as capacity has grown, and curtailment is the alternative that the batteries are meant to reduce. A curtailment payment is a payment to a generator to stop producing, and the cost is passed to ratepayers.

The duck's neck is still there. The evening ramp is met by batteries, by gas plants and by imports, and a hot evening with low wind can still strain the system. The difference is that the strain now arrives later and lasts for a shorter period, which changes which plants are needed and how they are paid. A gas plant that runs for three hours a day is a plant whose fixed costs are spread over few hours, and the price it charges reflects that.

What comes next is the seasonal problem. A battery moves energy across hours, and a grid that produces most of its solar in spring and needs most of its power in summer needs storage across months. The technologies that could do that are expensive and unproven at scale, and the duck curve is a small problem next to the season. A seasonal store would need to hold a fifth of annual demand, and no battery chemistry does that at a price a utility will pay.
