Forty men in New Haven answered a newspaper advertisement in 1961 that offered four dollars for an hour of research on memory and learning, and the study they joined was published two years later in the *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology*. Stanley Milgram told each of them to give an electric shock to another man when he answered a question wrongly, and the shocks were to rise by fifteen volts with each error. The machine was a prop, the learner was an actor, and the men who obeyed went on to four hundred and fifty volts.

Twenty-six of the forty, which is sixty-five per cent, continued to the highest level the dial allowed, and they did so after the learner had stopped answering and had begun to pound on the wall. Variations that Milgram ran afterwards are the more interesting part. The rate fell when the learner was in the same room, and it fell further when the experimenter gave his orders by telephone. Distance between the man who gives the order and the man who carries it out is the variable the study isolates.

The paper teaches the difference between a result and an explanation. The discussion offers several interpretations, from the tendency to obey a legitimate authority to the gradual shift in the meaning of the act, and none of them is tested. The design lets a reader see the prompts, which were scripted and delivered in a fixed order, and the responses the actor was told to make. What the paper does not report is how the subjects understood the situation, and that question is what later work took up.

Ethical questions arrived later and they have not been settled. Subjects were deceived, many left the laboratory in distress, and Milgram reported that some were angry once the deception was revealed. The American Psychological Association tightened its rules in the following decade, and the study became a standard case in the teaching of research ethics. Defenders say the distress was the point, and critics say the point was bought at a cost the subjects never agreed to pay.

Perry's archive work changed how the results are read. In 2012 Gina Perry went through Milgram's tapes and papers and found that many subjects had suspected the machine, and that the actor had sometimes told them what to do. Transcripts show men asking whether the shocks were real, and the answers kept the experiment running. Published rates therefore describe a situation more ambiguous than the paper suggests, which makes the result harder to state.

Replication and the closing question belong together. A Polish team ran a version of the experiment in 2017 with a lower maximum and found that a majority still continued, and a group in the United States ran one with a virtual learner and found similar rates. What the study shows is a limit of the experimental method the field still lives with: a laboratory can isolate one variable and it cannot reproduce the meaning a subject gives to the situation. Read the paper for its design, its numbers and the questions it leaves open, and read the archive for the answers the subjects gave when they thought nobody was counting.
