Federal agents searched the former president's residence in Palm Beach on Monday, and the operation lasted most of the day. The search was carried out under a warrant signed by a magistrate, and the agents were seen carrying boxes out of the building. The former president called the search a political persecution in a statement that was issued while the agents were still inside. The Justice Department did not comment, and the news came from the former president's own office.

The papers in question were the ones that left the White House in January 2021. The National Archives had asked for them for more than a year, and it sent a referral to the Justice Department in February after it recovered fifteen boxes from the club. The former president's lawyers had said that the material had been returned and that the rest was personal. The warrant was later revealed to cover three criminal statutes, including one about the handling of national defence information.

The documents that the search recovered were described in the inventory that was unsealed on Friday. It listed eleven sets of classified material, some of them marked for the highest levels of protection, along with photographs and personal items. The inventory did not describe the content of the papers, and the affidavit that supported the warrant was kept sealed for another week. Lawyers for the former president asked for a special master to review the material, and the judge later granted the request.

At the same time, the political reaction was immediate and it divided along the lines that everything divides on. Republican officeholders called the search an abuse of power, and some of them spoke of defunding the bureau. The Democrats said the law should apply to everyone and that the former president had invited the search by refusing to return the papers. The attorney general, who had been appointed by the former president and had been criticized by the Democrats for it, appeared on television to defend the decision without discussing the evidence.

Meanwhile, the legal question is narrower than the political one. The statutes that the warrant cited require the documents to have been wilfully retained and to have been kept in an unauthorized place, and the former president's defence is that he had the authority to declassify the material. Several lawyers who have worked on these cases said the argument is weak for documents that were generated by other agencies and that carry their own markings. The case is now before a grand jury, and the grand jury will decide whether there is a charge.

What the search does is put a former president inside a criminal investigation for the first time. It also puts the institution that is investigating him inside a political campaign in which he is a candidate. Both of those facts will be tested in the courts and at the ballot box, and neither will be settled quickly. The papers are now in a government facility, and the argument about who owns them has become an argument about who decides.
