Thermal insulation for a furnace, a turbine or a re-entry vehicle has to survive the temperature it insulates against, and the ceramics that conduct heat least are usually the ones that crack when the heat changes quickly. An aerogel has been built to do both. A material that insulates at high temperature has to be porous and has to be strong, and the two requirements have usually been met by different materials.

The material is a zircon nanofibrous aerogel with a zig-zag architecture and a hypocrystalline structure, made by electrospinning fibres and assembling them into a porous network. The paper appeared in *Nature*, 29 June 2022. Electrospinning produces fibres with a controlled diameter, and the assembly of those fibres into a zig-zag network is what gives the aerogel its recoverable deformation.

Its Poisson ratio is 3.3 times ten to the minus four and its thermal expansion coefficient 1.2 times ten to the minus seven per degree Celsius, both effectively zero. Those two numbers are what allows the material to survive a thermal shock without the internal stress that would fracture a conventional ceramic. A near-zero expansion coefficient is a property of a crystal structure as much as of an architecture, and a hypocrystalline material combines ordered domains with a disordered matrix that accommodates strain.

Strength degradation was less than 1 per cent after sharp thermal shocks, and the working temperature reached 1,300 degrees Celsius. At 1,000 degrees Celsius the thermal conductivity was 104 milliwatts per metre per kelvin, among the lowest reported for a ceramic aerogel. A strength loss of less than 1 per cent after a thermal shock is the number that a service part would need, and the working temperature of 1,300 degrees Celsius is the range of a kiln or a turbine.

Radiation is the heat-transfer channel that dominates at high temperature, and the design traps residual carbon species inside the zircon fibres to absorb and scatter the infrared. The zig-zag architecture is the mechanical trick, because a fibre network that folds rather than stretches can accommodate deformation without a large change in volume, and the near-zero Poisson ratio follows from that geometry. A near-zero Poisson ratio means that a sample stretches hardly at all when it is pulled, and that property is what prevents the crack that a thermal gradient would otherwise start.
