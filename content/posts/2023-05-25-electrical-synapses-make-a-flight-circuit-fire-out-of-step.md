Central pattern generators produce rhythmic movement, and the textbook version of a coupled oscillator network is one in which the units synchronize. A network whose units deliberately desynchronize is a different solution to the same problem.

The circuit in *Nature*, 24 May 2023 is that solution. The authors study the flight control network of Drosophila, find that its motoneurons are connected by electrical synapses, and show that the connections produce activity splayed out in time rather than synchronized.

The counterintuitive result is the paper's core. An electrical synapse is a direct connection that tends to equalize voltage, and equalization usually means synchrony. Here the coupling is weak and the neurons' excitability differs, so that the connection produces a phase lag rather than a lockstep.

The mechanism is a combination of weak coupling and specific dynamics. A weak electrical connection shifts a neuron's phase without forcing it, and a neuron near a threshold responds to a small shift with a large change in timing.

The function is the wingbeat. A flight muscle needs a sequence of activations in a fixed order, and a network that produces a sequence from unpatterned input is a network that stabilizes the beat against noise.

The evidence is an experiment-theory combination. Electrophysiology, optogenetic perturbation, genetics and a mathematical model each contribute, and the model's prediction is what makes the mechanism a mechanism rather than a description.

The general lesson is about network motifs. A field that associates electrical coupling with synchrony has a repertoire that is smaller than the biology, and the paper adds a motif.

The limitation is the species. Drosophila's flight apparatus is a special case, and whether the motif appears in other rhythmic circuits is a comparative question.

What the paper does not measure is the behavioural consequence of perturbing the coupling. A fly whose wings beat out of sequence would be a fly that cannot hover, and the behavioural assay is the test of the model's function.

For neuroscience, the value is a principle. A rhythm can be built from a sequence rather than from a unison, and the sequence is what a muscle needs.

The model's fit is its argument. A single origin fails to reproduce the diversity of ancient genomes, and a weakly structured stem reproduces it without positing a migration that the fossil record does not support.

The date matters for archaeology. A split around 120,000 years ago is consistent with fossils of that age, and a model that fits both the genes and the stones is stronger than one that fits only the genes.
The developmental question is how the coupling is built. A gap junction is a channel between cells, and its conductance depends on the connexin that forms it. The authors' genetic identification of the connexin is what makes the coupling a defined component.
The comparison with the vertebrate heartbeat is a useful contrast. A heartbeat is a synchronized rhythm, and a wingbeat is a sequenced one. The same physics of coupled oscillators produces both, and the difference is in the coupling's strength.
