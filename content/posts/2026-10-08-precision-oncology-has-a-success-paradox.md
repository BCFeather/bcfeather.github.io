A commission published in *The Lancet Oncology* in October set out to measure how far precision oncology has travelled and found a distribution problem at the end of the road. Genomic testing and matched therapy remain sharply unequal, both between countries and within them, and the commission describes a success paradox: as the field advances, its cost and complexity grow faster than the benefit it delivers to a population. The document is the most comprehensive assessment of the field's reach that has been assembled, and it was written by a group with no interest in arguing that sequencing should stop.

The evidence base is skewed in a way that is easy to state and hard to fix. Genomic research and clinical trials concentrate in high-income regions and enrol populations of mostly European ancestry, so the variants that get studied are the variants those populations carry. A test developed on that base travels badly, and the commission treats representation as an infrastructure problem rather than a matter of etiquette. A test whose evidence base excludes a population is a test with an unmeasured failure mode.

Clinical benefit is heterogeneous even where testing is available. Some strategies give a patient more options, and among those who receive the matched drug only a minority obtain a benefit that a clinician would call meaningful. That is not a failure of the drugs, which work in the subsets they were designed for; it is a failure of the arithmetic by which a health system decides what to pay for, because the numerator is measured in responses and the denominator in patients tested.

The commission's answer is a tiered, value-based framework for choosing which tests and therapies to implement first, paired with a readiness assessment tool for health systems, a competency framework for the staff who have to interpret the results, and principles for responsible data sharing. Ten core recommendations follow, and the emphasis throughout is on sequencing rather than on aspiration.

Sequencing is the practical contribution. A country that cannot sequence a tumour does not need a lecture about the promise of multi-omic profiling; it needs a short list of tests that change treatment and a plan for the staff who will run them. The framework gives that list a method, which is more than the field has had, and it lets a ministry say no to a test without saying no to the field, which is the decision most ministries actually face.

Any commission carries its own limits. Recommendations are not implementation, the readiness tool is a self-assessment rather than an audit, and the value thresholds will be contested by companies whose products sit below them. What the document does establish is that the equity question is now an efficiency question: a system that tests the wrong people and treats the few who respond is paying for complexity rather than for outcomes, and the bill arrives before the benefit does.
