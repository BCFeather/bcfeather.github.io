An earthquake of magnitude 7.4 struck off Hualien on 3 April, the strongest in twenty-five years, and the chipmakers evacuated their plants and stopped some of the most delicate processes. Production resumed within days, and the industry counted the wafers that were lost rather than the ones that were delayed. The count was smaller than the industry feared, and the insurers that cover the plants reported claims in the millions rather than the billions that a fire would have produced.

Physics is the reason for the stoppage. A furnace that grows a crystal and a machine that prints circuits both need to run without vibration, and a technician has to inspect every batch that was in the line when the shaking began. A process measured in nanometres cannot tolerate a tremor measured in centimetres. The machines that print the most advanced circuits are installed in fewer than a dozen buildings worldwide, and each of them takes months to calibrate after it is moved.

Redundancy is the mitigation. The largest manufacturer runs several sites on the island and has been building capacity in other countries, and the customers who design chips now hold inventory that they did not hold five years ago. A stock of finished wafers is an insurance policy that the customer pays for. The customers who design chips have learned to hold several weeks of inventory since the shortage of 2021, and that buffer is what absorbed the interruption without a visible shortage at retail.

Concentration remains the exposure. The island produces the overwhelming majority of the most advanced chips, and no other region can replace that output within a year, because the equipment is scarce and the engineers who can run it are scarcer. A supply chain with one node is a supply chain with one point of failure. The new plants in the United States and in Japan will add capacity at older process nodes first, which means the most advanced work will stay where it is for several years yet.

Insurance costs are now part of the business. The companies buy parametric cover for earthquake risk, and the premiums have risen as the value of the equipment in a single cleanroom has grown past what any local insurer can underwrite. A policy that is expensive is a policy that signals the size of the exposure. The premiums are paid by the manufacturer and recovered in the price of a wafer, which means part of the cost of the risk is now visible in the price of every chip that comes off the island.

What the episode shows is that a natural hazard is also an industrial policy question, and the answer that the industry has chosen is to build plants in more places rather than to make the plants stronger. Diversifying a supply chain is a decade-long project, and the episode is the kind of reminder that makes the spending easier to defend in a boardroom.
