Staging tells a surgeon how large a liver tumour is and whether it has invaded a vessel, and it says very little about which patient will relapse within a year. Two tumours of the same size, resected by the same operation, behave differently, and the difference sits in the tissue around them. The immune cells in that tissue are the obvious candidate, and conventional pathology reads them on a single slice, in two dimensions, without knowing where each cell sits.

A study published in *Nature* on 24 April 2025 took the spatial question seriously. Cheng Sun and colleagues built an imaging pipeline that labels immune cells in sections of hepatocellular carcinoma, the most common form of liver cancer, and used artificial intelligence to score the tissue by the arrangement of those cells rather than by their number. They focused on natural killer cells, which kill tumour cells without needing to recognise a specific antigen.

Five genes carried the signal. The team identified a set of genes expressed by natural killer cells, *SPON2*, *ZFP36L2*, *ZFP36*, *VIM* and *HLA-DRB1*, whose spatial pattern in the tumour microenvironment was associated with better outcomes after surgery. They combined that pattern into a score, the tumour immune microenvironment spatial score, and tested it against clinical staging and against the established predictors.

The score's numbers are the reason to take it seriously. It predicted recurrence with an accuracy of about 82 per cent and a specificity of 86 per cent, and it outperformed staging systems and clinical factors in the cohorts tested. More telling is the ablation: when the same five genes were scored without their spatial information, the predictive value disappeared. A marker that needs its position is a marker that measures architecture, and architecture is what a pathologist cannot see on a single slice.

Mechanism followed the statistics. The authors used genetically modified mice and three-dimensional bioprinted tumour models built from HepG2 liver cancer cells to show that *SPON2*-positive natural killer cells restrain tumour growth, which makes the spatial pattern a cause and not a correlate. Natural killer cells at the invasive edge behave differently from those trapped in the tumour core, and a score that weights position is measuring which population dominates.

The caveats are those of a retrospective imaging study. The cohorts come from a small number of hospitals, the staining and scanning were standardized within the study and would have to be standardized across laboratories, and the artificial intelligence has to be trained on data that a regulator can inspect. A score that needs a slide scanner, a staining protocol and a model file is a diagnostic that has to be deployed, not a test that a clinic can pick up.

What would settle it is a prospective trial in which the score is computed before the operation and patients are stratified by it. If a high score identifies a group that can safely skip adjuvant treatment, the score has clinical value; if it only reorders patients within an existing risk band, it is an expensive version of staging. The paper makes the case for the trial, and the trial has not been run.
