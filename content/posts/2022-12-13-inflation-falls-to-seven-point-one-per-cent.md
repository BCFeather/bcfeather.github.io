Figures from the labour department were published at half past eight in the morning, and the markets had finished pricing them before nine. Consumer prices rose seven point one per cent over the year to November, down from seven point seven per cent in October, and by one tenth of one per cent during the month itself. The monthly figure was the smallest since December of the previous year, and it was small enough to be described as flat in the first paragraphs of the wire copy. Traders had expected something closer to half a per cent, and the gap between the forecast and the number set the tone for the day.

Core prices, which exclude food and energy, rose six per cent over the year and four tenths of one per cent during the month. That number mattered more to the officials who set interest rates, because it strips out the items that swing about and leaves the ones that reflect wages, rents and services. Core inflation had been the stubborn part of the problem all year, and in November it finally moved down. Economists had been waiting for that turn since the spring, and several of them had predicted it for each of the previous three months.

Shelter was the largest single contribution, as it had been for months. Rents and the owners' equivalent of rent rise slowly and with a lag, because leases are signed once a year, and the measure that appears in the index reflects deals agreed months earlier. Economists had been predicting that this component would turn, and the turn had arrived later than the models expected. In November it rose again, and it will keep the index elevated well into the following year.

Energy prices fell during the month, and so did the price of used cars and of some goods that had been scarce during the pandemic. Airline fares dropped by more than a tenth, which the department attributed to the end of the summer season. Food prices kept rising, and they were the item that households noticed most. Grocery bills had become the subject of ordinary conversation in a way that the index never is.

Reaction was immediate and almost joyful. Stock indices rose through the morning, the dollar weakened, and the yield on the ten-year Treasury fell below three and a half per cent for the first time in months. Traders moved their expectations for the next rate decision within the hour, and several of them wrote that the peak had arrived. The chief economist of one large bank said on television that the report was the best inflation number since the pandemic began.

Caution came from the same economists who had welcomed the figure. One month does not make a trend, and the index had produced encouraging months before that were revised upwards later. The Federal Reserve was due to meet the next day, and its officials had spent weeks saying that they wanted to see a series of such reports. A single month of good news does not change a policy that has been built on the fear of repeating the inflation of the 1970s.
