The aridity index compares water supplied by rain with water demanded by the atmosphere, usually as the ratio of precipitation to potential evapotranspiration. A region below one is classed as dry, and the classification is used to place deserts, to set agricultural expectations and to sort climates into types. What the ratio leaves out is water that arrives sideways: rivers running from wet mountains into dry basins, and groundwater moving beneath the surface into the same lowlands.

A paper in *Nature* on 6 August 2025 puts that flow back in. Its authors ran a global hydrology model at 30 arc-second grids to estimate monthly lateral subsidy by rivers and groundwater, then computed two indices: the conventional ratio of precipitation to potential evapotranspiration, and a second one adding the subsidy to the numerator before dividing. The second index carries a subscript for topography, because what it adds is a property of terrain, not of climate.

The difference is large where terrain converges. Land with the topographic index at or above one covers 33 per cent more area than land meeting the conventional threshold, and the gap widens in arid and seasonally arid climates, which is where the subsidy matters most. Lowlands that receive water from mountains are classed as humid under the new measure and dry under the old one, and those lowlands are where much of the population in those climates lives, which makes the choice of index a question about people and not about maps.

Timing changes as well. Water arrives at a lowland after the rain that fed it has fallen, and it arrives through channels and aquifers that spread the delivery over months, so the subsidized index peaks later and varies less across the year than precipitation does. The authors show the phase lag and the damping separately, and both follow from the routing and not from any change in rainfall. A basin that fills in June from snow that fell in March is the case the revised index is built for.

Observations where they exist provided the check. Monthly evapotranspiration was compared with 103 long-term eddy-flux towers, and river discharge with 34 large gauges, with the mismatch reported by continent instead of averaged into a single score. Groundwater subsidy is the harder term to validate, since it is modelled and not measured, and the grid resolution smooths channels narrower than 30 arc-seconds, so an oasis fed by a small channel may be invisible.

Where the index would change decisions is in water accounting for cities and irrigation schemes downstream of wet mountains, and in the classification of drylands that are not dry. A planning rule based on local rainfall alone underestimates supply in exactly those places, while a rule based on the subsidized index has to attribute the water to its source, which is a political question as much as a hydrological one. That attribution is what a downstream water right formalizes, and it is where hydrology meets law.
