Japanese consumer prices excluding fresh food rose 2.8 per cent in the year to August, the statistics bureau said on 22 September, down from 3.1 per cent. The measure that also strips out the energy fell to 4.3 per cent from 4.4. The figure was slightly below the forecast of 2.9 per cent and the second monthly decline, and the headline measure including fresh food was 3.2 per cent.

Energy subsidies explain much of the fall. The government has been paying part of the household electricity and gas bills since the start of the year, and the effect has kept the energy line negative. A subsidy that expires is an inflation that returns. The subsidy is due to expire at the end of the year, and the analysts expect the measure to rise again in the first quarter unless it is extended.

Food inflation is slowing but still high. The food prices excluding the fresh items rose by about seven per cent, and the restaurants raised their prices as the labour shortage in the sector persisted. A wage in the kitchens is a price on the menu. The rice prices have risen by about a tenth after the poor harvest, and the government has released some of its stock to the market to ease the pressure. The restaurants have raised the prices by about 5 per cent on the year, and the survey of the employment shows the hotels are short of about 100,000 workers.

Services are the sticky part of the basket. The measure of the services rose by about 2 per cent, and the tourism-related categories were the strongest as the visitors returned. A service price that follows the visitor is a price that the weak yen brought. The number of foreign visitors in August was about 85 per cent of the level of 2019, and the hotel prices in the main cities are above their pre-pandemic levels.

Policy was held on the same morning. The governor said the bank would not exit the negative rates until it had evidence that the wage growth would be sustained, and he repeated that the process would be gradual. A bank that waits for the wages is a bank that has already decided the direction. The bank also said it would keep buying the bonds to hold the yield around zero, and it raised its forecast for the inflation of this year to 2.5 per cent.

What the month shows is that the inflation has peaked and the target is in sight, and the timing of the exit is now a question about the spring wage talks. The spring wage talks are the event the bank has said it will watch, and the unions have already signalled that they will ask for about five per cent. The first round of the talks will be reported in March, and the bank meets two days later, which is the calendar the market is trading.
