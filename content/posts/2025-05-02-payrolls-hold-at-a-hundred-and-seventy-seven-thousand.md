A payroll number is a survey with a sampling error, a birth-death adjustment and a revision schedule, and the April report published on 2 May showed all three at work. Employers added 177,000 jobs against a consensus near 133,000, and the unemployment rate stayed at 4.2 per cent. The headline was strong enough to knock rate-cut expectations back by a meeting, and futures moved within an hour of the release.

The revisions took part of it away. The estimate for March was cut to 185,000 from 228,000, and February to 102,000 from 151,000, so the two months together lost 58,000 jobs and the three-month average settled at 155,000. That average is still above the roughly 100,000 per month that the labour force needs to absorb new entrants, and it is below the pace of last year.

Wages cooled. Average hourly earnings rose 0.2 per cent on the month and 3.8 per cent on the year, the slowest monthly gain since August 2023, with average weekly hours at 34.3. A labour market that adds jobs without accelerating pay is the soft-landing case, and it is the case that lets a central bank wait while it watches what tariffs do to prices.

Sector detail carried a tariff story. Health care added 51,000 jobs, transportation and warehousing 29,000 against a twelve-month average of 12,000, and federal employment fell 9,000. Warehousing hiring at double its trend is what inventory building looks like in a payroll report: firms that pulled orders forward needed people to move the goods, and the jobs arrived with the containers.

Manufacturing lost about a thousand jobs, and the survey evidence from purchasing managers had shown layoffs in the same month. The sector that tariffs are supposed to help is the sector that is shedding workers first, because a tariff raises the cost of its inputs before it raises the price of its output, and a factory that cannot see its input costs cannot plan a second shift.

Participation held at 62.6 per cent, matching the highs of the previous September, and the number of unemployed people changed little at 7.2 million. A rate that has stayed between 4.0 and 4.2 per cent for a year is a rate that is not moving, and a rate that is not moving gives no signal about the next quarter. The level of 7.2 million unemployed is about one in twenty of the labour force, which is a normal number and not a warning.

What would settle the reading is the next two reports. Firms that front-ran tariffs will unwind the inventory, and the warehousing jobs will go with it, probably in the same month; firms that face higher input costs will decide in the third quarter whether to hire or to wait. The April number describes a labour market before either decision was made, which is why it reads as a photograph of a moment and not as a forecast of the year.
