A transformer changes voltage by inducing current across a pair of coils wound around a laminated iron core, and the core is most of its weight. A 2-megawatt solid-state transformer completed a year of service in a distribution substation this month, converting 10 kilovolts to 400 volts through power electronics at 20 kilohertz, with no iron core at all.

The topology is familiar from other power converters. Alternating current is rectified to direct current, switched at high frequency through silicon-carbide devices, and transformed by a much smaller high-frequency magnetic component before being inverted back. The high frequency is what shrinks the magnetics by two orders of magnitude.

Size is the visible gain. The unit occupies 0.9 cubic metres against 3.1 for the iron-core transformer it replaced, and weighs 800 kilograms against 4,200. In a substation where land is scarce, that difference is a real estate argument as much as an engineering one.

Function is the bigger gain. A solid-state transformer can regulate voltage on either side, correct power factor, and limit fault current in microseconds, which a passive transformer cannot do at all. The substation's protection scheme was rewritten to use those capabilities.

Efficiency is the trade. A conventional transformer runs at 98.5 per cent; the solid-state unit runs at 97.1 at full load and falls to 94 at 20 per cent load, because switching losses do not scale down. A distribution substation spends much of its time at part load, which is where the comparison looks worst.

Reliability data is one year old and thin. The unit has not failed, and its silicon-carbide modules have a predicted wear-out life of 25 years at the measured junction temperature. A passive transformer's life is 40 years with no maintenance, so the comparison is between replacement and repair.

Cost remains the barrier. The unit cost 340,000 dollars against 90,000 for the conventional equivalent, and the operators justify it with the voltage regulation and the fault current limiting rather than with efficiency. A second unit is on order for a substation with a high penetration of rooftop solar.

Grid codes are the gate that will decide adoption. A converter connected to a distribution network must ride through a voltage dip for 150 milliseconds and must not inject harmonics above a limit, and a solid-state transformer meets both with controls rather than with physics. Two utilities have written those requirements into their interconnection standards this year.

Service life is the unknown that insurers care about. The electrolytic capacitors in the intermediate direct-current link are the components with the shortest life, at 15 years against the transformer's 40, and a design that replaces them with film capacitors costs 20 per cent more and removes that limit.

For a reader, the interesting variable is the semiconductor. Silicon-carbide devices at 10 kilovolts and 20 kilohertz became available at reasonable cost about five years ago, and the transformer is the first application where their advantages compound. The technology will spread where space and control matter more than efficiency, which describes a distribution network with distributed generation.
