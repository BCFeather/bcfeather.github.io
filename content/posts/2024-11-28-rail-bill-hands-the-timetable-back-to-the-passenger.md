A bill that would take passenger rail franchises into public ownership as their contracts expire completed its passage through the Commons this month, and the first operator is expected to transfer next year. The policy reverses a privatization that began in the mid-1990s and has since produced a system in which the state already pays most of the cost. A bill that completes its stages is a bill that still needs a budget and a board.

Franchise model failed on a specific measure. A contract that specifies a timetable and a subsidy is a contract that a private operator can run profitably by cutting costs that passengers notice, and the failures came one after another as passenger numbers grew faster than the infrastructure. A operator that cannot add trains because the track is full is an operator that cannot meet its own contract. A timetable that is specified in a contract is a timetable that a operator meets by cutting the things a passenger values. A franchise that hands back a route has usually already lost the passengers it was paid to attract.

Ownership does not add track. The constraint on capacity is the signaling and the platforms, and a national operator inherits the same constraint. The promised improvements depend on projects that are already behind schedule, and a change of ownership does not shorten a construction programme. A platform that is full at nine in the morning is a platform that no ownership change can lengthen. A signal that fails on a wet morning delays a timetable that has no slack built into it.

Fares question is the one the public will judge. A nationalized operator can set fares by policy rather than by contract, and a government that holds fares down has to fund the difference. A railway that is funded by subsidy is a railway whose service level is a budget decision, and budgets change every year. A fare that is set below cost is a fare that the treasury pays for, and the treasury has other claims. A passenger who buys a season ticket is a passenger whose loyalty is priced on reliability.

Staff question is the quieter one. Franchises employ people on contracts that differ between operators, and a single employer will eventually harmonize them. A harmonization that raises the lowest pay is a cost, and a harmonization that lowers the highest is a dispute. A pay harmonization that raises the lowest grade is a cost that a department has to absorb. A driver who trains for six months is a driver who can be poached by a freight operator.

What the bill establishes is that the state is willing to own the risk it already pays for. Whether that produces a better railway depends on decisions about track, fares and staffing that the ownership change only makes possible. A railway that the state owns is a railway whose failures are the state's to explain.
