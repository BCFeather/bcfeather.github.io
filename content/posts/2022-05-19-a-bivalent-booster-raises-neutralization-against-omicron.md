A vaccine that matches the circulating variant is the obvious response to immune escape, and the obvious complication is that the variant will have moved by the time the product is released. The compromise is a bivalent vaccine that carries two spikes. A virus that escapes the antibody response of the first vaccine is a virus that will escape the second unless the second is designed against it, and the bivalent design is the compromise between specificity and breadth.

Two chimeric spike designs were tested as boosters in animals and in a small human immunogenicity study: a prototype-Beta dimer and a Delta-Omicron dimer, each built as a single molecule that presents two RBDs. The paper appeared in *Cell*, 27 April 2022.

A prototype-Beta booster raised neutralizing titres against Omicron above those produced by the ancestral booster alone, and the Delta-Omicron version produced the broadest response across the variants tested. The comparison is between boosters in previously vaccinated animals, which is the setting in which a booster is actually used. A titre against Omicron that is higher than the titre produced by the ancestral booster is the endpoint that the programme uses, and it is a surrogate for the outcome that the programme cares about.

A bivalent immunogen works because the immune system sees two versions of the same epitope and produces a repertoire that covers both, and the design of the dimer matters because the two RBDs are presented on one molecule rather than as a mixture of two proteins. A single molecule that presents two RBDs is more constrained than a mixture, because the two domains have to fold together without distorting either epitope.

Human data are limited to immunogenicity, with no efficacy endpoint, and the number of participants is small. A titre is a surrogate, and the relationship between titre and protection against Omicron is the assumption on which the entire booster programme rests. A surrogate endpoint that has been validated in one variant is a surrogate that may not transfer to the next, and the immunogenicity programme has to assume a continuity that the virus has not honoured.

A Delta-Omicron design is the one that anticipates the future, because it includes the variant that was current and the one that had just emerged. A vaccine that is designed against the variant of last year is a vaccine that is always behind. A design that includes the variant that is current and the one that has just emerged is a design that is making a bet about the future, and the bet is that the two will be related.

The result supported the decision to deploy bivalent boosters in the autumn of 2022, and it also illustrates the limit of the approach: a bivalent vaccine covers two variants, and the virus has more than two. An annual update of the vaccine is the practical consequence, and it is the same model that influenza vaccination has used for decades.
