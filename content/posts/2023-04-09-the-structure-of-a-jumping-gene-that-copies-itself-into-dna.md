Transposons are DNA sequences that move, and the mechanisms they use are a catalogue of molecular tricks. One class, the non-LTR retrotransposons, copies its RNA back into DNA directly at the site where it will insert, without a free DNA end to prime the synthesis.

The structure in *Science*, 6 April 2023 shows the trick. The authors determine the cryo-electron microscopy structure of R2, a retrotransposon that inserts into the ribosomal DNA of insects, captured with its target and its template, and the arrangement reveals how the element primes reverse transcription at the insertion site.

The mechanism is target-primed. The element's encoded protein nicks the target DNA, and the nick's exposed end serves as the primer for copying the element's RNA. A free 3' hydroxyl is the chemistry's requirement, and the element makes one where it wants to insert.

The structural detail is the achievement. A protein that binds RNA, binds DNA and catalyses both a nick and a polymerization is a machine with several active sites, and the structure shows how they are arranged around the target.

The ribosomal DNA target is the element's habitat. R2 inserts into a specific sequence in the ribosomal RNA genes, and the specificity is what keeps the element from destroying the genome. A structure that shows the recognition explains the targeting.

The evolutionary consequence is the genome's composition. Retrotransposons make up a large fraction of many eukaryotic genomes, and their insertion mechanisms are the reason. A structure of one is a window on how genomes acquire sequence.

The comparison with retroviruses is informative. A retrovirus integrates with an integrase and a reverse transcriptase, and a non-LTR element does the same chemistry with a single protein. The two solutions are related and distinct.

The limitation is the state captured. A structure is a snapshot of one conformation, and the reaction's steps are inferred from the arrangement. The kinetic evidence that orders the steps is complementary.

What the structure does not show is the element's life cycle in a cell. An insertion that happens in the germline is inherited and one that happens in the soma is not, and the tissue specificity is a separate question.

For genome biology, the value is a mechanism at atomic resolution. A mobile element's insertion is a source of mutation and of novelty, and a structure of the machinery makes the process a target for study and, in principle, for control.

The protein's domains are the map. A restriction-like endonuclease domain makes the nick, a reverse transcriptase domain copies the RNA, and a nucleic-acid-binding region holds the target. The structure's value is the arrangement of the three.

The RNA's structure is part of the mechanism. The template has to be positioned so that its 3' end is at the polymerase's active site, and the cryo-EM map resolves the RNA's path through the complex.

Artificial versions of the element are a biotechnology prospect. A retrotransposon that can be retargeted is a tool for inserting sequence at a chosen site, and the structure is the information that engineering needs.
