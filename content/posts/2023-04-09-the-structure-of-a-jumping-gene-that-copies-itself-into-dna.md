Copying their RNA back into DNA at the site where it will land, non-LTR retrotransposons insert without a free DNA end to prime the synthesis, and the mechanisms they use form a catalogue of molecular tricks. R2, one of the family, chooses the ribosomal DNA of insects as its target.

In *Science* on 6 April 2023, the machinery is resolved. The authors determined the cryo-electron microscopy structure of R2 caught with its target and its template, and the arrangement reveals how the element primes reverse transcription at the insertion site.

Target-primed copying is what the structure explains. The element's encoded protein nicks the target DNA, and the exposed end of the nick serves as the primer for copying the element's RNA; a free 3-prime hydroxyl is the chemistry's requirement, and the element makes one exactly where it wants to insert. That is why the mechanism carries the name: the primer is not a separate molecule but the cut end of the genome itself. Holding RNA, holding DNA and catalysing both a nick and a polymerization makes the protein a machine with several active sites, and the map shows how they are arranged around the target.

Because the element inserts into a specific sequence of the ribosomal RNA genes, recognition keeps it from wrecking the genome, and it is also a safety mechanism, since an element that cut elsewhere would take the host's genes with it. The domains divide the labour: a restriction-like endonuclease makes the nick, a reverse transcriptase copies the RNA, and a nucleic-acid-binding region holds the target. The RNA's path is resolved as well, positioned so that its 3-prime end reaches the polymerase's active site.

Comparison with retroviruses sharpens the picture. A retrovirus integrates with an integrase and a reverse transcriptase, while a non-LTR element does the same chemistry with a single protein, and the two solutions are related yet distinct: one protein where the retrovirus needs two. The order of steps is inferred rather than seen, since a structure freezes one conformation and the kinetics supply the sequence. The life cycle in a cell stays outside the picture, where a germline insertion is inherited and a somatic one is not.

Retrotransposons fill large fractions of many eukaryotic genomes, so a mechanism at atomic resolution explains how genomes acquire sequence. Mutation and novelty arrive through the same reaction, which is why the machinery draws interest beyond its own family. Engineering follows the same information: an element that can be retargeted is a tool for inserting sequence at a chosen site, and the structure is what such a tool needs.

Reaction conditions matter for the retargeting claim, because an element that copies at one site in one cell type may prefer another elsewhere, and the determinants of that preference are only partly visible in a single structure. Host factors that the structure leaves out may explain the difference, and their identification is the work a static picture invites.
