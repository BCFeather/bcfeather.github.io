The gate of the primary school opens at twenty to seven. The first class is at half past eight. Between those two times, several hundred children sit on the low wall of the neighbouring housing compound, eat breakfast from plastic bags, and wait. The compound's security guards have given up moving them on, and the school has installed a bench along the wall, which is the closest thing to an admission that the arrangement is permanent.

A queue forms because two schedules do not match. Parents in manufacturing and retail shifts start work at eight or earlier, and the school day is built around a teaching timetable rather than around the working day. The gap is filled by grandparents, by paid pickup services, and increasingly by the school itself, which opens early because the alternative is children waiting on the street. The duty roster rotates among the teachers, and the ones who live nearby volunteer because they would be at school anyway.

Schools call it after-school care extended into the morning, and it costs money. A teacher on duty before seven is paid an allowance; the canteen opens early for those who arrive without breakfast. In some districts the local government pays, in others the parents do, and the difference shows up as a fee of a few hundred yuan a term on the invoice.

The children are fine with it, mostly. The early arrivals play on the concrete, do homework on their knees, and by half past seven the noise is at a level that no classroom could contain. Teachers say the ones who arrive earliest are often the ones who live furthest away, which is not surprising: the bus that serves the outer estates arrives at the school gate at half past six. A child who takes that bus has been awake since five. The school does not know how many of the early arrivals come from those estates, because it does not ask, but the duty teacher recognises most of the faces.

Arrangements like this illustrate a large problem in miniature. Chinese cities have extended the school day at both ends to accommodate working parents, and each extension costs money and staff time that is not in the education budget. The alternative, a shorter school day that matches the teaching timetable, is politically impossible, because it would push the childcare problem back onto households. Employers have shown no interest in moving their start times, and the school has no authority over them.

Nobody has measured whether the early gate improves outcomes. It clearly improves the logistics, and the parents who use it are grateful in the way people are grateful for a bus that runs on time. The children, asked what they do in the forty minutes, mostly say they wait, and one of them said it is the best part of the day because nothing is expected of them yet.
