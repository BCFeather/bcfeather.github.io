The economics prize went on Monday to Ben Bernanke, Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig for work on how banks work and why they fail. Bernanke, sixty-eight, chaired the Federal Reserve from 2006 to 2014 and now works at the Brookings Institution. Diamond, sixty-eight, teaches at Chicago, and Dybvig, sixty-seven, at Washington University in St Louis. The committee said the three had shown why banks are needed, how they become vulnerable and what can be done about it.

Diamond and Dybvig published their model in 1983, and it describes a bank as an institution that borrows short and lends long. Depositors can ask for their money at any time, and the loans cannot be called in as quickly, which means a rumour alone can empty a bank that holds sound assets. Their answer was deposit insurance, which removes the reason to run ahead of the person in the queue. That argument became the basis of the guarantee schemes that most countries now run, and it is also the reason regulators worry about banks that fund themselves in markets rather than with deposits.

Bernanke's contribution was historical, and it began as doctoral work on the Depression. He showed that bank failures in the early 1930s did more than follow the contraction: they caused it, by destroying the credit relationships on which small firms depended. The paper was published in 1983, the same year as the other two, and it gave the Fed a framework it used in 2008. It also changed how economists read the record of the 1930s, which had been treated as a story about money and prices rather than about institutions.

All three have been close to policy for two decades. Bernanke cut rates to zero, started the bond-buying programmes and lent to investment banks through facilities designed for commercial ones. Diamond and Dybvig advised central banks and wrote about the design of guarantees, and their work was cited throughout the debates over the rescues of 2008. None of them has been a household name outside the profession, which the committee said was part of the point.

Short and technical, the committee's statement makes one argument plainly. A bank that holds long assets against short liabilities is always exposed to a run, and the state has to decide in advance how it will answer one. Deposit insurance protects small savers, and it also changes the incentives of the people who take the risk on the other side. The design of that trade-off is what the prize is for, and the committee noted that the model still describes the runs of the digital age.

Bernanke said in a telephone call that he was glad the committee had rewarded a field that people find dull until the moment it matters. Diamond described the prize as recognition for a model that had survived forty years of argument and several crises. Dybvig noted that the three had never written a paper together. The money is ten million kronor, and the ceremony in December will be the first since the pandemic with a full hall.
