The opening match kicked off at seven in the evening, in a stadium built for the tournament in the desert north of Doha. Qatar had spent twelve years and a great deal of money to bring the competition to the Gulf, and the stadium held fewer people than had wanted tickets. The stands were full of white thobes and maroon scarves, and the noise at the start was the loudest of the whole competition. A short ceremony before kick-off featured a singer and a dance troupe, and the television feed cut away to the emir in his box. The pitch had been grown in a nursery and flown in, and the grass looked greener than anything outside the stadium walls.

Ecuador scored after sixteen minutes, and the goal was disallowed for offside after a review that took a long minute. The same striker scored again at the quarter hour of the second period, and this time the flag stayed down. Enner Valencia added a second from a header, and the host team finished the match with a single shot on target and a goalkeeper who had kept the score from being worse. The Ecuadorian bench celebrated the first win of the tournament as if it had won the competition.

Result was a first in the history of the competition. No host country had lost its opening match, and the record stretched back to the first tournament in 1930. Qatar had prepared for years, with a national academy, a league of imported players and a coach appointed in 2017, and the gap between that preparation and the level of a South American qualifier was visible from the first ten minutes. Passes went astray under pressure, and the defence was pulled apart by simple movement. The midfield that had been assembled from naturalized players could hold the ball for a minute at a time, and then lose it in a single tackle.

Stands emptied slowly, and the streets outside were quieter than the tournament's officials had hoped. Reporters who had spent weeks writing about labour conditions and air conditioning found themselves writing about a defence that could not cope with a through ball. A handful of the home fans left at half time, and the rest stayed to applaud a team that had at least kept trying. The coach said afterwards that the occasion had been too big for his players, and that the second match would be different.

Questions about alcohol, armbands and labour had been waiting for the competition since the draw. Alcohol was restricted to certain zones, the rainbow armband was banned and then partly unbanned, and the treatment of migrant workers had been documented by human rights groups for a decade. Those questions followed the football into every press conference, and they will outlast the final whistle.

By midnight the group table had Ecuador on three points and Qatar on none, and the next host match was four days away. Sixteen years of planning had produced a team that could not hold the ball, and a country that had built seven stadiums to watch it try.
