The textbook picture of the last glacial maximum has cold surface water sinking to fill the deep ocean with water close to freezing, and that picture underpins the way ocean circulation is reconstructed in climate models. The evidence behind it comes from proxies whose calibration has improved slowly, and each improvement has narrowed the temperature difference between the ice age and today. A new set of measurements narrows it further.

An international team measured the isotopic and trace-element composition of benthic foraminifera from sediment cores in the north-west Atlantic, which is the region where deep water forms today and where the ice-age circulation's fate has been most debated. The proxies were calibrated against a modern dataset instead of against a theoretical relationship, which reduces the uncertainty that comes from assuming how a shell records temperature.

Water below 1.5 kilometres was about 0 to 2 degrees Celsius at the peak of the last ice age, roughly 19,000 to 23,000 years before present. Compared with the modern ocean it was colder by only \(1.8\pm0.5\) degrees Celsius, and the oxygen isotope composition of the water itself was higher by \(0.3\pm0.1\) per mille, which points to a deep ocean that was saltier as well as colder. The work appeared in *Nature* on 21 January, with a correction published on 27 January.

Both numbers matter. A difference of less than two degrees is small enough that deep convection did not require the extreme cooling that some reconstructions implied, and a modest increase in salinity is what would allow water to sink despite being only marginally colder than today. The implication is that the ice-age Atlantic still had a northern source of deep water, which many models had reduced to a shadow of its present strength. A saltier deep ocean is a deep ocean that can sink from a high latitude without cooling to the freezing point, and that is the physical link the authors draw.

The paleoclimate significance is about the circulation's response to freshwater. If deep water formed in the ice-age North Atlantic, then the freshwater from melting ice sheets did not shut it down completely, and the abrupt climate events recorded in Greenland ice are read differently if the circulation was weakening instead of switching off. That is a large interpretive shift from a small measurement.

Uncertainty in proxy calibration is the persistent issue. Foraminiferal chemistry responds to temperature, to salinity and to the carbonate chemistry of the water, and separating those influences requires assumptions that differ between groups. The authors test their conclusion against several calibrations and report a result that survives those tests, which is more than most paleoclimate studies can claim.

A paper does not overturn the ice age. It replaces a caricature with a number, and the caricature had been doing duty in textbooks and in model boundary conditions for thirty years. That kind of correction is slow, unglamorous and consequential. The correction published six days later changed a figure rather than a conclusion, which is worth noting when the result is cited in synthesis papers.
