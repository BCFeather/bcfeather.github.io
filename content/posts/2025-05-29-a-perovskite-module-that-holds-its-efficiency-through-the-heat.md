Perovskite solar cells have been the most efficient laboratory curiosity in photovoltaics for a decade and the least durable product, a combination that has kept them out of factories while their record efficiency climbed. The efficiency record has climbed above 26 per cent for small cells, and the commercial question has always been whether a module can hold that number through the heat and humidity of a rooftop in summer, where a panel sits at 60 degrees for hours and cycles every night.

A paper in *Science* on 29 May 2025 described a module that holds it. The authors used an ionic electron shuttle that improved molecular packing in the transport layer and produced a roughly threefold increase in interfacial toughness, which is the property that fails first when a film is cycled between hot and cool, because two layers with different expansion coefficients peel apart at the interface before either one degrades.

Measured numbers are module numbers. Minimodules based on 6 square centimetre subcells reached power conversion efficiencies of about 23 per cent, and lost less than 9 per cent of that after 2,200 hours of operation at 55 degrees Celsius, which is an accelerated version of a rooftop summer.

Toughness is the right variable to attack. Perovskite films degrade at boundaries and interfaces before they degrade in the bulk, and a transport layer that delaminates under thermal cycling is what turns a 25 per cent cell into a 15 per cent module. Improving adhesion by a factor of three buys time, and time at 55 degrees is what the standard test measures, so the result is a statement about a mechanism and not only about a number.

Silicon is the commercial comparison. Silicon modules at 22 to 23 per cent efficiency come with a 25-year warranty, and a perovskite module needs to approach that warranty before a manufacturer will build a line. Two thousand two hundred hours is about three months of accelerated life, which is a good result and not a warranty, and a rooftop in Arizona will spend more than that at 55 degrees in a single summer.

Stability test also is not the field. Damp heat at 85 per cent humidity and 85 degrees is the standard a module must survive, and the paper's condition is dry heat at 55, which is a different failure mode. A pass at one and a fail at the other is the usual trajectory of this field, and the humidity is what attacks the contacts and not the absorber, which is why the interfacial work in this paper is the relevant kind.

What would settle the technology is a full-size module, a damp-heat test, and a cost per watt that includes the encapsulation. The efficiency is now high enough that the remaining problems are engineering problems, which is the best news the field has had and the least interesting news for a journal.
