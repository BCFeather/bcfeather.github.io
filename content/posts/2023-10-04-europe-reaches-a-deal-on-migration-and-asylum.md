description: "Negotiators for the European Parliament and the member states agreed on 4 October 2023 to a five-part migration and asylum package, including screening rules at the border and a solidarity pool of 30,000 relocations a year."
---

After nearly a decade of failed attempts, the European Parliament and the Council reached a political agreement on the core of a migration and asylum package on 4 October. The package covers five instruments: a screening regulation for arrivals at the external border, a revised fingerprint database, a common asylum procedure, a solidarity mechanism for sharing responsibility, and a crisis regulation for sudden surges. Each had been blocked separately by different coalitions of governments and lawmakers. The five texts had been negotiated in parallel for four years, and each carried a compromise that another government disliked.

The solidarity mechanism is the part that took longest. Member states will owe an annual contribution measured as 30,000 relocations, and a country that refuses to take people can pay twenty thousand euros per place instead, which works out to a fund of roughly six hundred million euros a year. Frontline states such as Italy, Greece and Spain had argued that the previous voluntary system left them carrying the burden alone, and the countries of central Europe had argued that any mandatory quota would be imposed on them without consent. The formula replaced a voluntary scheme that relocated only a fraction of the people it promised.

A screening regulation sets a maximum of seven days to register and assess new arrivals at the border, with detention-like conditions for people judged to pose a security risk. Human rights groups said the rules would allow families with children to be held at the frontier, and the agencies that work in reception centers warned that the seven-day clock assumes staff and facilities that many frontline states lack.

A crisis regulation allows states to extend registration deadlines and to apply looser standards when arrivals are exceptional, and it was the clause that the European Parliament resisted most. Lawmakers argued that a crisis label would let governments suspend safeguards for months, and the final text limits how long the derogations can run and requires a formal decision at European level.

Implementation is left for the next Parliament and Commission. The package has to be formally adopted before the European elections in June, and then transposed into national law, with the Commission aiming for 2026. Officials acknowledge that the reception capacity, the databases and the case-processing staff needed for the timetable do not exist in several member states, and that money for them will compete with other budget lines. Officials in several capitals have said privately that the timetable will slip if the money arrives late.

Supporters claim more for the deal than it delivers, and its critics expected less than it contains. It replaces a patchwork of national rules with a single procedure and a predictable sharing formula, and it keeps the external bargain in place: agreements with countries of origin and transit, and payments for border management. Whether that combination reduces irregular arrivals depends on governments whose cooperation the union cannot enforce. What the package does not change is the number of people who arrive, which depends on conditions far from Brussels.
