Salesforce reported revenue of 9.13 billion dollars for the quarter to the end of April, up 11 per cent, and cut its forecast for the year, sending the shares down by about a fifth on 30 May. It was the sharpest one-day fall the company has recorded. The company also said its margin would be lower than it had promised, because the acquisitions it has made are less profitable than the software it sells.

Guidance is the reason. The company said that customers were signing deals later in the quarter and that the deals were smaller, and it described the pattern as a buying environment rather than as a problem with its products. A customer who delays a purchase is a customer who is waiting to see whether the new tools work. The sales cycle for the larger contracts has lengthened from months to two quarters, and the buyers are now asking for the approval of a committee rather than of a single executive.

Artificial intelligence products are the pitch and not yet the revenue. The company has been adding assistants to its applications and charging for them, and the customers who have bought them are running pilots rather than rolling them out. A pilot that is not renewed is a pilot that the buyer treats as research. The customers who have bought the pilots report that the tools save time on administration and that the savings are hard to measure against the price of the subscription.

Valuation is what changed. The shares had been valued as a growth company for a decade, and a forecast that grows in single digits makes that valuation harder to defend, which is why the fall was larger than the revision. A multiple that depends on growth is a multiple that a downgrade can remove. The company has been buying back shares to support the price, and the repurchase is smaller relative to the market value than it was two years ago.

Rest of the sector moved with it. Other software companies that sell to businesses fell on the same day, because the market read the guidance as a statement about corporate budgets rather than about one firm. A read-across is the mechanism by which one company's warning becomes an industry's problem. The buyers of software have become more careful since the rate rises, and the budgets that were approved in the winter are being spent on hardware and on security rather than on new applications.

What the quarter shows is that a new technology reaches the revenue line more slowly than it reaches the conference stage, and the companies that sell it are valued on the gap between the two. A company that sells a promise is a company whose value depends on the customers believing that the promise arrives, and the quarter showed how quickly that belief can move. The customers who have renewed their contracts have accepted a price increase, and the company said the retention rate was unchanged.
