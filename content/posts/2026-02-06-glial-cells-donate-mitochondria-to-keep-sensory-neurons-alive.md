A sensory neuron that runs from the dorsal root ganglion to the tip of a toe is one of the longest cells in the body, and its most distant part is a metre from the nucleus that supplies it. Mitochondria are made in the cell body and transported along the axon, and a neuron whose transport slows will starve its own endings of energy long before the cell body shows any sign of damage. That fragility explains a great deal about peripheral neuropathy, including why the symptoms begin in the feet and why they progress slowly and irreversibly.

Xu and colleagues describe a mechanism that supplements the transport. Satellite glial cells wrap the cell bodies of dorsal root ganglion neurons, and the new work shows that they form tunnelling nanotubes and transfer their own mitochondria into the neurons they enclose. The transfer depends on glial myosin 10, a motor protein that builds the nanotube, and the study appeared in *Nature*, with a research highlight in *Nature Neuroscience* on 6 February. A neuron cannot import an organelle from the bloodstream the way a liver cell can absorb a metabolite, so the last metre of the cell depends on transport that fails quietly.

The diabetic part is what makes the finding clinically interesting. In co-cultures of satellite glia and neurons derived from people with diabetes, the transfer process was disrupted, which suggests that the glial supply line fails in the condition that produces the most common peripheral neuropathy. That is a mechanism for a disease that has been described as metabolic for decades.

Replacement worked in mice. Adoptive transfer of satellite glial cells or of mitochondria from healthy human donors alleviated pain and improved neuronal health in a mouse model of diabetic neuropathy, which is a therapeutic result obtained by supplying a missing component rather than by blocking a pathway. For a condition with no disease-modifying treatment, a supply model is worth testing.

Interpretation needs care. Mouse models of diabetic neuropathy reproduce the metabolic state imperfectly, and pain behaviour in a mouse is a proxy for a human symptom that is difficult to measure in either species. The co-culture experiment establishes that the phenomenon is disrupted in human cells and does not establish that it is the mechanism of human neuropathy.

A therapeutic route is not obvious, and the paper is honest about the distance. A treatment that delivers glia or mitochondria to ganglia would need to reach structures that sit inside the spinal column, and systemic delivery of mitochondria has been attempted for other indications with limited success. The paper's contribution is a target and not yet a therapy.

What it establishes is a new kind of glial support, one that transfers a whole organelle and not a metabolite. Astrocytes have been shown to pass mitochondria to neurons after stroke, and the picture now includes a second glial population doing something similar in the peripheral nervous system, which suggests that donation is a general strategy.
