The Perseid shower peaks in the middle of August, and for a few nights the sky produces a meteor every minute or two for anyone who is willing to lie on the ground in the dark and wait. The event is predictable to within a day, which is a remarkable thing for a phenomenon that consists of grains of dust burning at a hundred kilometres above the surface.

The dust comes from a comet on a long, slow orbit that brings it close to the sun once every hundred and thirty-three years. Each pass leaves a trail of debris along the orbit, and the earth crosses that trail every August. The grains hit the atmosphere at about a hundred kilometres a second relative to the planet, and they vaporize in less than a second at a temperature that makes the air around them glow. Nothing reaches the ground except occasionally a fragment large enough to survive, which is rare and well documented.

The peak this year falls close to a date when the moon is bright, which is bad news for anyone who has been looking forward to it. A bright moon washes out the faint trails, the count drops by three quarters, and the difference between a good year and a poor one is decided by an object that has nothing to do with the comet.

The practical advice is always the same and always ignored. Get away from the city, because a street light is worth more damage than a cloud. Lie flat, because the neck cannot hold an angle for an hour. Look at a wide patch of sky rather than at the radiant point, because the meteors appear everywhere. Give it half an hour for the eyes to adapt, and do not use a phone, because the screen resets the adaptation in about a second.

What the shower provides, more than the meteors, is an occasion. A family that would not otherwise leave the city drives forty minutes to a field, and a group of friends lie on a blanket and talk for three hours. The astronomy is the excuse and the occasion is the point, and the same is true of every comet and eclipse that draws a crowd.

The science that comes out of it is modest and steady. Amateur networks count meteors and report them, the counts feed models of the dust trail, and the models allow a prediction of which years will be strong. The predictions have been reliable for two decades, which is one of the quieter successes of observational astronomy.

The shower will be over by the twenty-fourth, and the newspapers will not mention it again until next year, and the comet will return in 2126, which is a date nobody reading this will see, and the arithmetic is the reason people stand in a field at two in the morning for a light that is already gone by the time the eye registers it.
