About thirty-three thousand machinists at Boeing walked out on 13 September after rejecting a contract that offered a twenty-five per cent wage increase over four years, and the assembly lines that build the company's two narrowbody models stopped within days. The strike was the first at the planemaker since 2008. The union had recommended acceptance, and the members rejected it anyway, which is the rarest outcome in a vote that a leadership has endorsed.

Vote was overwhelming. Ninety-six per cent of those who voted rejected the offer, which is a margin that tells the leadership that the proposal was understood as inadequate rather than as a starting point. A membership that votes like that is a membership that has been saving for a stoppage. The contract was the first since the pandemic and the first since a door panel blew out of a 737 in January, and the members had watched the company's safety record in the news.

Grievance is about more than pay. The workers lost a defined-benefit pension in an earlier contract, and the cost of housing in the Seattle area has risen faster than the wages that the company offered, so a rise that looks generous against the national average looks small against the local one. A wage claim is a housing claim in a company town. The offer also cut the annual bonus into the wage rise, and a bonus that is paid in shares is worth less to a worker than a rise in the hourly rate.

Company's position is weak. Boeing had already slowed production after a door panel failure in January, and its cash flow was negative for the quarter before the strike. A stoppage that costs the company money each day is a stoppage that comes at the worst moment in a decade. The company has about ten billion dollars of debt maturing before the end of next year, and a strike that stops deliveries makes the refinancing harder. The regulator had already limited production of the 737 until the company could show that its processes were under control.

Supply chain is exposed again. The smaller firms that make parts for the assembly lines are paid by the piece, and they had already been told to slow down after the January incident. A supplier that has been idle for a year is a supplier that cannot easily restart. A supplier that has laid off staff cannot rehire them in a week, and the restart of the lines will therefore be slower than the stop was.

What the strike shows is that a company can lose the confidence of its customers, its regulator and its workforce within a single year. The contract that ends it will be judged not by the wage line but by whether the workers believe the next offer will be different. The company said it would furlough salaried staff and freeze hiring, which is the standard response of a planemaker that cannot deliver aircraft.
