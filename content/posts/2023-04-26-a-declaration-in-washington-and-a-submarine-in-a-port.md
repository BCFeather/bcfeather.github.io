The two presidents met in Washington on 26 April, on the seventieth anniversary of the alliance, and signed a document that commits the United States to consult South Korea before using nuclear weapons in a conflict on the peninsula. The Washington Declaration creates a Nuclear Consultative Group, which will meet at assistant secretary level and will discuss planning, intelligence sharing and exercises. It also states that a ballistic missile submarine will make a port call in South Korea, the first since 1981. The boat would be an Ohio-class vessel carrying ballistic missiles, and its arrival would be the most visible deployment of American nuclear force on the peninsula in four decades.

The declaration was written to answer a question that had become urgent in Seoul. Polls have shown that a large majority of South Koreans, and a majority of younger voters, would prefer the country to build its own nuclear weapons rather than rely on an American guarantee. The government had floated the idea publicly in January, and the declaration is designed to give the appearance of shared control without transferring any.

Wording is careful about what was conceded. The submarine will visit and will not be deployed, and the group will advise rather than decide, which leaves the president of the United States with the authority he had before. South Korean officials described the arrangement as the closest the alliance has come to nuclear sharing, and American officials described it as a consultation mechanism, and both descriptions appear in the document's margins. The difference matters in Seoul, where the government has to tell voters that it has gained something, and in Washington, where no administration wants to suggest that the decision has been shared.

North Korea's response came within days. The foreign ministry said the declaration formalized a plan for a preemptive nuclear strike and warned that the visit would raise tensions to a level not seen since 2017. The regime tested a solid-fuel intercontinental missile in the following weeks, and it has since described the peninsula as a zone in which the use of nuclear weapons is a settled question.

Ordinary alliance business was on the agenda as well. The two sides agreed on extended deterrence, on cyber cooperation, on a chip export framework and on the supply chains that the previous year's American legislation had disturbed. South Korean companies had been anxious about the subsidies and the conditions attached to them, and the communique included language that allowed them to keep their plants in China while expanding at home. The two governments also agreed to coordinate on the export controls imposed on advanced chips, which South Korean manufacturers had complained about for a year.

What the declaration did was postpone a decision rather than settle one. South Korea will not build a bomb while the guarantee appears to hold, and the guarantee now depends on a consultative body whose recommendations a future American president may ignore. The alliance has an arrangement that works as long as both governments want it to, which is the condition that has held since 1953 and that the declaration makes slightly more explicit.
