description: "Images from the James Webb telescope keep finding galaxies that are brighter and more massive than the models allowed for at their age."
---

Astronomers closed the telescope's second year of science operations with a catalogue of galaxies that formed earlier and brighter than the standard model predicted. The objects that caused the most argument are small red points at high redshift, and their existence has forced theorists to reconsider how quickly a galaxy can assemble. A year of observations is a year in which the instruments were calibrated and the survey strategy was adjusted.

Redshift is the measurement that dates them. Light from a distant galaxy is stretched by the expansion of the universe, and the amount of stretch gives the time since the light left. A galaxy at a redshift above ten is a galaxy seen within a few hundred million years of the beginning, and the models that describe the first stars expected very little structure by then. A redshift is a number that depends on a spectrum, and a spectrum takes hours of telescope time per object.

Brightness is the puzzle. A galaxy that is bright at that distance has to be forming stars at a high rate or has to contain a large mass of stars, and both are difficult so early. A population of massive early galaxies is a population that either formed stars more efficiently than expected or contains objects that are not stars at all. A galaxy that is bright is a galaxy that a survey finds easily, and the easily found objects are the ones that create the anomaly.

Little red dots are the candidate for the second explanation. Their light has a shape that is consistent with a black hole accreting matter rather than with a galaxy of stars, and if that is right, the early universe contained black holes that grew faster than the models allow. A black hole that is heavy at high redshift is a black hole that formed from a massive seed. A black hole that grows from a heavy seed is a black hole that formed in a collapsing cloud rather than from a star.

Observations are also a test of the telescope. An instrument that was designed to see the first galaxies is an instrument whose calibration has to be perfect, and the teams that publish these results have spent years on the photometry. A claim about the early universe is a claim that rests on a measurement of brightness. A photometric measurement is a measurement that has to be corrected for dust, for the instrument and for the expansion.

What the year establishes is that the models are being revised by data rather than by theory. A simulation that cannot produce the galaxies that the telescope sees is a simulation with a missing ingredient, and the next generation of models will be written around what the images show. A model that is revised by data is a model that will be tested again by the next survey.
