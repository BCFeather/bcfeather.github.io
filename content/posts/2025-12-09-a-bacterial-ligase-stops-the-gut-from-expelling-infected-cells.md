The intestinal epithelium has an eviction policy. A cell that detects an infection triggers pyroptosis, dies in a way that alerts the tissue, and is pushed out of the monolayer into the lumen, where it is carried away with the contents of the gut. It is an effective defence: the pathogen loses its host cell and its position.

The study in *Nature*, 22 October 2025 describes how one pathogen defeats it. Giovanni Luchetti, Marin Miner, Vishva Dixit and colleagues work with enteropathogenic bacteria that inject a ubiquitin ligase, NleL, into the cells they colonise. The enzyme blocks pyroptosis, and it also prevents the extrusion that would otherwise remove the infected cell.

Extrusion is a mechanical process as much as an immune one. Cells are pulled outwards by the contraction of their neighbours, driven by the kinase ROCK, and the monolayer closes behind them. Interfering with that contraction leaves the infected cell in place, and the paper traces the block to the bacterial enzyme.

The consequence is that the infection keeps a base. A pathogen that stayed in the lumen would be washed away; one that holds a patch of epithelium can multiply and shed progeny into the gut for as long as the patch survives. The ligase is therefore a colonisation factor, and its activity is the difference between a transient exposure and an established infection.

Both functions in one enzyme is the striking part. Pyroptosis and extrusion are separate defences, and a bacterium that had to defeat only one of them would be easier to stop. The paper's argument is that the two are coupled, so that blocking one without the other would not suffice, and the enzyme's dual activity is the evidence.

The target of the ligase is a host protein that the group identifies, and the identification opens a therapeutic question: a small molecule that inhibits NleL would restore the epithelium's ability to evict, and it would do so without touching the host's own machinery. Whether such a molecule is tractable depends on the enzyme's structure, which the paper provides in part.

What remains is the variation between strains. Enteropathogenic bacteria come in several lineages, and not all carry the same effectors; a strategy that works for one may be absent in another. The comparative picture would show whether the evasion mechanism is conserved or a local adaptation.

For the field, the result places the epithelium's mechanics inside its immunology. A defence that depends on a cell being physically removed is a defence that a pathogen can defeat by stopping the removal, and recognizing that changes what a screen for virulence factors should look for.

Strain diversity is where the translation begins. The effectors that enter a host cell differ between pathotypes, and a ligase that one lineage carries may be absent from another that causes the same disease. A therapy aimed at NleL would be a therapy for the strains that carry it.

The assay that would settle the mechanism is a direct measurement of where the infected cell sits after the conflict between the two defences has played out. The paper's imaging gets close to that, and a time series through a full infection would close it.
