---

Nike reported revenue of 12.6 billion dollars for the quarter to the end of May, down 2 per cent from a year earlier, and said sales would fall by a tenth in the current quarter. The shares fell by about 20 per cent on 28 June. The forecast for the year was cut by more than the analysts expected, and the company withdrew the target for the margin that it had given at its investor day. The margin was lower than the forecast and the company said the discounting would continue into the autumn.

Direct selling is the problem. The company has been selling more through its own stores and its app, and that business fell faster than the wholesale one, which is the opposite of the plan that the management set out four years ago. A channel that is supposed to grow and does not is a strategy that has to be rewritten. The wholesale partners had been cut back during the pandemic, and they have not restored the orders at the level the company needs to keep the factories full.

China is the second problem. Sales there fell by a tenth, and the local brands have taken share with lower prices, which the company has answered with discounting rather than with new products. A discount is a price that the customer remembers. The running shoes that the company launched this year have sold well, and the basketball line has not, which is the difference between a product problem and a market problem.

Inventory is the third. The company has been clearing the shoes that it ordered before the demand slowed, and the markdowns have compressed the margin, which it expects to continue for two more quarters. A stock that is cleared at a discount is a profit that is paid in advance. The company has said it will reduce the supply of the older models and take the write-down in the next quarter, which is the accounting of a decision already made.

A new chief executive arrives with the problems. He came from a rival and has said the company had lost its focus on sport, and the market has given him a year to show that the products can be sold at full price. A turnaround is a promise with a date. The shares are now worth a third less than they were in the spring, and the dividend yield has risen to a level that the board did not plan. The shares of the retailers that sell the brand fell as well, because the inventory they hold loses value when the maker marks it down.

What the quarter shows is that a brand can lose pricing power faster than it can rebuild the products that justify it, and the decline is now visible in the channel that the company chose. A company that is cutting its outlook while its rival grows is a company that has to explain the difference, and the explanation will be the first task of the new management.
