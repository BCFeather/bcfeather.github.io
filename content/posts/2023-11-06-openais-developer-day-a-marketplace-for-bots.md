OpenAI held its first developer conference in San Francisco on 6 November, and the announcements ran for less than an hour in a converted warehouse with a crowd of engineers. The centrepiece was GPT-4 Turbo, a version of the model with a context window of 128,000 tokens, roughly the length of a long book, and a price cut that the company put at several times lower per token than the previous model. The knowledge cutoff moved forward, and the model gained the ability to call functions and to see images at higher resolution.

The second product was a builder for custom chatbots, which the company called GPTs. A user could describe a task in plain language, attach files, and publish a link to a bot that behaved according to those instructions, with a storefront for discovering what others had made. The point was to move the work of building assistants away from engineers. Company staff demonstrated a bot that read a spreadsheet and answered questions about it, and another that suggested recipes from photographs of a fridge.

A third piece was an assistants interface for developers, with tools for file handling, memory inside a conversation, and code execution. The company described it as a way to turn the API from a call-and-response service into something closer to an agent that can work through a task in steps. It also said that DALL·E 3 and the newer speech model were available through the same platform, and that the transcription service had been extended to more languages.

Usage framed the event. Altman said ChatGPT had about 100 million weekly users, a figure that had not been published before and that put the product in the same league as the largest consumer internet services. Enterprise customers were cited as the fastest-growing segment, with more than a million people paying for seats inside organizations, and the company said it would begin selling team subscriptions more widely.

There were omissions as well. The company gave no date for the model that its rivals had spent the year speculating about, beyond saying that a newer family was in internal testing, and it was vague on when image generation would reach the general public. Moderation and safety tools were mentioned briefly, with a promise of a system that would let developers add their own filters on top of the platform's defaults. Reporters asked about the cost of running the service and about the terms of the Microsoft partnership, and got no figure on either.

A clear strategy sat behind the day. OpenAI had spent two years selling access to a model, and it was now selling a place to build things, which is a harder product to leave once a team has invested in it. The bot storefront borrows the shape of an app store, and the assistants interface borrows the shape of a cloud platform, and both are bets that the market will standardize on one vendor's primitives rather than on open weights that anyone can copy.
