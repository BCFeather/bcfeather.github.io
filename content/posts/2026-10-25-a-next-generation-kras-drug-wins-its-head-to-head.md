Drugs that hit KRAS G12C changed what was thought possible and then disappointed in the clinic. A phase 3 trial reported in *Nature Medicine* in October and presented at the ESMO congress in Madrid tests the second generation against the first, and the second generation wins clearly: median progression-free survival was 9.8 months with divarasib against 5.7 months with sotorasib or adagrasib, and the survival curve separates early rather than at the tail.

KRAS was called undruggable for forty years, and the switch that made it druggable was a chemical one: a covalent bond to a cysteine that exists only in the G12C mutant and only when the protein is in one of its states. That constraint explains both the achievement and the limit. The drugs work, and they work for a while, because a tumour finds ways around a single covalent bond.

Krascendo 1 is a global, randomised, open-label trial in 338 patients with locally advanced or metastatic KRAS G12C non-small-cell lung cancer who had already received treatment. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival as assessed by blinded independent review, and divarasib cut the risk of progression or death by 42 per cent, with a hazard ratio of 0.58 and a confidence interval from 0.44 to 0.76. Blinded review matters in an open-label trial, because a drug that causes fewer side effects is a drug whose scans are read with more optimism.

Overall survival also moved, by almost six months, and the response rate improved. A survival benefit in a second-line lung cancer population is the kind of result that changes a label rather than a guideline. Safety stayed consistent with earlier data, which is the phrasing companies use when nothing new appeared, and in this class the side effects are gastrointestinal and hepatic rather than dramatic.

The comparison is between a drug designed to be more selective and more potent and drugs designed five years earlier, so part of the gain is the ordinary progress of medicinal chemistry. The interesting part is the size: a doubling of progression-free survival against an active comparator is rare in oncology, and it suggests that the earlier agents were limited by their pharmacology as much as by the biology of resistance, which is a more encouraging conclusion than the alternative.

Questions remain about sequencing. If divarasib becomes the standard in the second line, the trials that put KRAS inhibitors after it have not been run, and the resistance mechanisms that emerge on a better drug will differ from those seen on a weaker one. Cost and access will also decide how quickly the result reaches patients, since the first-generation drugs took years to be priced for routine use. A better drug that few patients can afford changes a trial result into a market question. The trial will not be repeated, so its numbers stand as the comparison between the two generations of this class.
