British consumer prices rose 6.7 per cent in the year to August, the Office for National Statistics said on 20 September, down from 6.8 per cent and well below the 7 per cent that the economists had expected. The core measure fell to 6.2 per cent. The figure was the lowest since February last year and was below every forecast in the survey, and the pound fell about half a per cent within minutes.

Food did most of the work. The food inflation fell to about 13 per cent from 14.9, and the milk, the eggs and the vegetables came down as the wholesale prices eased. A grocery price that stops rising is the line that the households notice first. The bread and the cereals rose by about 14 per cent over the year, and the oils and the fats by about 20, both slower than in the spring.

Air fares dropped by about 20 per cent on the month. The summer routes were priced lower than a year earlier, and the hotels in the holiday towns cut their rates as the bookings softened. A holiday that is cheaper is a services measure that falls. The air fares fell after the summer peak, and the package holidays were about 12 per cent cheaper than a year earlier as the operators cut the capacity. The hotel prices in the coastal towns fell by about 4 per cent, and the occupancy was lower than the operators had hoped for August.

Services are the number the committee watches. The measure slowed to 6.8 per cent from 7.4, and the Bank of England has said it needs to see it near 5 before it can think about cutting. A service that falls slowly is a rate that stays high. The private sector wage growth has been about 8 per cent, and the committee has said the services will not fall to the target until it slows to about 5.

Markets reacted sharply. The pound fell to its lowest in six months and the traders cut the odds of a November increase to about a third, because the report removed the case for one. A surprise that is downward is a currency that sells off. The traders now price the first cut for the middle of next year, and the vote on the next decision is expected to be closer than the last. The next decision is in November, and the economists are split on whether the committee will raise the rate once more to be sure.

What the report shows is that the disinflation has reached the shops and the holidays, and the wages are the part that has not yet arrived at the target. The wages are the last part of the inflation, and the settlements in the autumn round will show whether the fall can continue.
