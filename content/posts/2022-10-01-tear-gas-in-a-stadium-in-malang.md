The match at the Kanjuruhan stadium in Malang had ended minutes earlier, and Arema had lost at home to Persebaya Surabaya for the first time in twenty-three years. The fixture had been moved to the evening because of the heat, and the stands behind both goals were fuller than they had been all season. Supporters climbed over the fencing at the north end and ran onto the pitch. Police in riot gear followed them and fired tear gas into the stands. The crowd turned towards the exits, and the exits were where most of the dead were found.

By midnight the count stood at one hundred and twenty-five dead and more than three hundred injured, and officials warned that the toll would rise. Hospital staff in Malang said most of the casualties had been crushed or suffocated at the gates, and that children were among them. Witnesses described people falling in the stairwells and being walked over by the crowd behind them. Videos posted from the ground showed officers in helmets swinging batons at men who were trying to lift the injured. Two policemen were reported killed as well, and the club said it had lost supporters, stewards and staff.

The stadium was built in 2004 and holds about forty-two thousand people, and it was full. Officials said the north stand had been sold beyond its capacity, and that several gates at that end were closed or half closed when the gas came over. Fire officers said the gas had been fired into a confined space with one usable exit, which turns a crowd into a funnel. Police have long used tear gas at football matches in Indonesia, and the practice had been condemned by the football association before this season.

FIFA's stadium regulations prohibit tear gas and firearms inside grounds, on the argument that a crowd which cannot see the way out will run into it. Those rules bind the national association, and the association's own safety code repeats them. The code also asks for a steward for every hundred seats, and the stewards at Kanjuruhan were hired by the club rather than by the police. Whether the rules were broken on the night is a question the government will have to answer. The top league has been suspended while it does.

President Joko Widodo ordered the police and the football association to investigate, and said the stadium would stay closed until they finished. He asked for the injured to be treated at the government's expense and for the families of the dead to be visited. The national police chief said he had sent a team to Malang and had asked for the closed-circuit footage from every gate. The police said they had opened a criminal inquiry into the use of the gas, and that the officers on duty would be questioned. The association postponed the rest of the season's fixtures without saying when they would resume.

Malang is a football town, and the rivalry with Persebaya is older than the stadium. Supporters had travelled from Surabaya, and the two sets of fans had been kept apart for the match. What happened afterwards belongs to a longer record of disasters at Indonesian grounds, and the question the survivors asked on the night was why the gates at their end had been shut. Nobody in authority had answered it by dawn, and the families were still waiting outside the hospitals. The club's president said the club would pay for the funerals.
