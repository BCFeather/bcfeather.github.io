The Senate voted 53 to 47 on 7 April to confirm Ketanji Brown Jackson as an associate justice of the Supreme Court, making her the first Black woman to sit on the court in its 231-year history. Three Republicans, Susan Collins of Maine, Lisa Murkowski of Alaska and Mitt Romney of Utah, joined all fifty Democrats, the vote was tied at 50-50 for a moment as the clerk worked through the roll, and Vice President Kamala Harris presided as the tally was recorded. The gallery was full, and the clerk had to call for silence twice before the result could be read.

A short and unusually combative process produced that margin. President Biden announced the nomination at the end of February, hearings ran for four days in March with twenty-two senators questioning her for close to twenty-four hours, and the Judiciary Committee deadlocked 11 to 11 before a discharge motion carried the nomination to the floor. Democrats held the majority and needed no Republican vote to confirm her, which makes the three defections a question of tone rather than of arithmetic.

Republican objections centred on her sentencing in child pornography cases, which they said fell below federal guidelines, and on her refusal to name a judicial philosophy in the terms they proposed. She answered that she applies the law to the facts before her, and she declined to take positions on questions such as court expansion, which she said fall outside a judge's role. Senators also pressed her on her work as a defence lawyer for detainees at Guantanamo Bay, which she described as part of a lawyer's duty rather than an endorsement of the policy.

Her record includes a period as an assistant public defender, service on the district court in Washington and membership of the Sentencing Commission, a combination no previous justice has brought. She joins a court whose conservative majority is intact, and she will not change the balance of decisions, because she replaces the retiring Stephen Breyer, who announced at the end of January that he would step down when the term ends in the summer. She will be the first justice to have worked as a federal public defender, and the first on the Sentencing Commission since Breyer himself.

The historical weight sits beside a structural fact that is easy to lose. A confirmation that changes no vote on the court is a symbolic appointment, and the symbolism is real: two Black justices will serve together for the first time, four women will sit at once, and the bench will look more like the country it rules. It is also the first confirmation since Clarence Thomas in 1991 that a Democratic president has won with bipartisan support in a polarized chamber.

Symbols matter to an institution, and the next term will test how much. She will hear cases on admissions at Harvard and the University of North Carolina, and will step aside from the Harvard one. The seat is the same seat Breyer held for twenty-eight years, and the arithmetic stays at six to three. Her chambers will be staffed by clerks she has worked with on the district court, and the new term opens in October.
