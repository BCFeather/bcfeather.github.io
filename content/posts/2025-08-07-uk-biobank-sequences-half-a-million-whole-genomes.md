Genome-wide association studies have run on genotyping arrays for two decades, and exome sequencing added the coding fraction of the genome a decade ago. Neither reads the whole thing. Arrays interrogate a fixed set of common variants chosen before the study begins, exomes cover about one per cent of the sequence, and both leave structural variants, deep intronic sites and most regulatory sequence outside the measurement. The distance between what an array covers and what a genome contains is where most of the missing-heritability argument has lived.

A paper in *Nature* on 6 August 2025 reports the obvious next step. Its authors sequenced whole genomes from 490,640 UK Biobank participants, then joined that sequence to the questionnaire, imaging and hospital records the cohort already carries. The point of the exercise is not the count on its own but what a complete sequence can be associated with, and the cohort design, with its half million people and its long follow-up, is what makes the count worth having.

The findings run in two directions. Within ancestries, the authors report associations that earlier array work had missed, and across ancestries they found strong or new signals in participants of African and Asian descent, groups that array studies have always underpowered because the panels were built from European samples. Structural variants and variation in untranslated regions could be genotyped accurately for the first time, and comparison with the cohort's exome data showed where the wider sequence strengthened an existing result and where it produced a new one.

The technical gain sits in the variant classes. A deletion of a few thousand bases, an inversion, or a tandem repeat expansion is invisible to an array and awkward for an exome, and each can remove a gene, fuse two, or change how a gene is expressed without touching its coding sequence. Sequencing also puts non-coding sites on the same footing as coding ones, which matters because most trait-associated variants from arrays land outside genes, in enhancers and promoters whose logic is still poorly mapped.

Limits are structural rather than incidental. Most participants are of European ancestry, so the cross-ancestry claims rest on subsets that are large by the standards of the field and small by the standards of a genome-wide scan. A cohort recruited from one country carries its own health and environmental profile, sequencing depth determines which structural variants are callable, and a rare variant seen once in half a million people cannot carry an association on its own.

What would show the resource earning its cost is prospective use: returning actionable findings to participants, replication of the new signals in other biobanks, and evidence that a sequence changes a diagnosis or a treatment for someone in the cohort. Those tests sit outside the paper, and they are the ones that decide whether whole genomes become the standard input for genetics rather than a richer appendix to the array. A resource described in a paper remains a promise until someone uses it for something.
