ExxonMobil said on 11 October that it would buy Pioneer Natural Resources in an all-stock transaction valued at $59.5 billion, or $253 per share, with an enterprise value close to $64.5 billion once Pioneer's net debt is included. The deal is the company's largest since it merged with Mobil in 1999, and it makes Exxon the dominant producer in the Permian basin of west Texas and New Mexico, where the combined output would run to about 1.3 million barrels of oil equivalent a day. It is the largest transaction in the industry since the merger wave of the late 1990s, and it makes the combined company the biggest acreage holder in the basin.

Inventory rather than price explains the strategic logic. Shale wells decline quickly, and a producer that wants to hold output steady has to keep drilling, which means it needs undrilled acreage. Pioneer's position includes large blocks of low-cost acreage in the Midland basin, and Exxon has said the combination would give it fifteen years of drilling inventory at current rates, a figure that matters to investors who judge shale companies on how long their inventory lasts.

The price implies a premium of about 18 per cent to Pioneer's closing level before reports of the talks appeared, and Exxon has promised cost savings of around $2 billion a year within two years. Analysts have questioned whether the savings are achievable without cutting the drilling that generates the production, and whether the deal can clear antitrust review in a sector that has consolidated rapidly over the past two years.

Consolidation has been the pattern. Chevron agreed to buy PDC Energy and then Hess, ConocoPhillips bought ConocoPhillips' Permian rival Concho several years earlier, and Occidental took over Anadarko. Each deal narrowed the field of independent shale producers, and the Federal Trade Commission has been examining whether the combinations reduce competition in local markets for drilling services, land and labour. The commission has also asked the service companies that drill and complete the wells for documents, which is where local competition matters most.

Exxon's longer argument about oil demand fits the deal as well. The company has projected that oil and gas will remain central to the energy system through 2050, a view that puts it at odds with the scenarios used by the International Energy Agency, and it has committed $17 billion a year to low-carbon projects while spending far more on fossil production. Buying Pioneer increases the share of the portfolio that depends on prices holding up.

Pioneer's chief executive, Scott Sheffield, has said he will retire at the end of the year, and the transaction is expected to close in the first half of 2024. For the basin, the change is that the largest operator will be an integrated company with a global balance sheet rather than a pure shale producer. That matters for how quickly the wells get drilled and for how much of the profit stays in Texas. The company will keep Pioneer's Midland offices, and folding the two organizations together will take most of next year.
