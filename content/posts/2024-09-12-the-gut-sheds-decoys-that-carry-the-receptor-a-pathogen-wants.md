Enterotoxigenic *Escherichia coli* cause hundreds of millions of diarrhoeal illnesses each year, and the infection is usually self-limiting. A pathogen that clears on its own has often been cleared by something the host does, and finding that mechanism is a route to treatment. The bacterium colonizes the small intestine and secretes toxins that drive fluid secretion, and the diarrhoea is the toxin's effect rather than the immune response.

A paper in *PNAS* on 12 September 2024 identifies one. The heat-labile toxin that the bacterium secretes raises the expression of carcinoembryonic cell adhesion molecules on extracellular vesicles shed by intestinal epithelium, and the vesicles then act as decoys. Identifying a host mechanism is attractive because the target does not mutate, so a treatment built on it would not lose potency as the bacterium evolves.

The toxin's other job is known. It induces CEACAMs on the cell surface, where the bacterium exploits them as receptors to deliver more toxin, which is a positive feedback that favours the pathogen. That feedback explains why the receptor appears on the surface at all, and it also explains why the decoy response is available, since the same molecule is being produced in greater quantity.

Vesicles reverse the sign. CEACAM-laden vesicles bind the bacterium and carry the receptor away from the cell, so the interaction that would deliver toxin instead ends in clearance, and the vesicles increase in abundance during human infection, which suggests the response is real rather than theoretical. A vesicle is a membrane-bound package that a cell sheds, and the surface it carries is a sample of the receptors the cell is expressing at that moment.

Scavenging is the mechanism the authors demonstrate, and it fits a broader picture in which shed membrane vesicles act as a buffer between a mucosal surface and its microbes. The host is not only a barrier but a producer of decoys. The idea has precedent in other mucosal infections, where shed receptors bind a pathogen before it reaches a cell, and the paper supplies the specific case.

The finding does not show that vesicles are sufficient for clearance, since the immune response and the wash of fluid through the gut also contribute. Nor does it establish whether the effect can be strengthened deliberately. A trial would have to establish whether the response is protective in a natural infection, and observational data from patients would be the place to start.

Clinically, the appeal is that the target is a host molecule rather than a bacterial one. A treatment that increased vesicle release, or that supplied CEACAM-bearing vesicles, would be less exposed to resistance than an antibiotic, since the pathogen is not being killed directly. A decoy-based approach also has a long history in bacterial toxin biology, where soluble receptor analogues are used to absorb a toxin before it binds. Whether the decoy response can be boosted without disturbing the rest of mucosal immunity is the question a therapy would have to answer.
