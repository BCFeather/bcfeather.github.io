With a fadeaway jumper in the third quarter against the Oklahoma City Thunder, LeBron James passed Kareem Abdul-Jabbar as the leading scorer in the history of the National Basketball Association. The basket took him to 38,388 points, one more than the total Abdul-Jabbar had left in 1989 and which had stood as the record for thirty-eight years. Abdul-Jabbar was sitting courtside, and the game was stopped for a ceremony while the crowd stood.

Everything about the room had been prepared for it. The league had scheduled the game on national television, the Lakers had sent out a warning that the record could fall that night, and the price of a ticket in the lower bowl had risen accordingly. James reached the mark in his twentieth season, at the age of thirty-eight, which is the part of the record that his contemporaries found most implausible.

Durability, rather than a single skill, is what the total records. James had never led the league in scoring for a season, and his career average of about 27 points was lower than several players who never came close to the total. What he had done instead was play at an elite level for two decades, missing fewer than ten games in most seasons, in a sport that had become faster and more demanding on joints.

The comparison with Abdul-Jabbar was awkward in the way that comparisons across eras always are. The older record was set without a three-point line for most of its run, in a game with more possessions and less spacing, and Abdul-Jabbar scored his points with a shot that no one else has been able to copy. James scored his with a body that no one else has been able to maintain, and the argument about which is the greater feat ran for a week without a conclusion. Abdul-Jabbar had said in interviews that he expected the record to fall and that he was glad it would fall to someone who had spoken about the same pressures.

The business of the night was as organized as the record. The Lakers marketed the game as a milestone event, the league's partners produced commemorative goods before the ball went in, and the ball itself was taken out of play and authenticated. A record that had been approached for a month became, within an hour, a set of products and a highlight that would be replayed for years. The league's merchandising operation had prepared shirts and prints before the game, which is how a record becomes a product within an hour of happening.

A second axis for the argument arrived with the scoring record, and it made the case that longevity at the top is its own kind of greatness. He had been compared to Michael Jordan for two decades, and the comparison had usually turned on championships, where he was behind. The scoring record gave the argument a second axis, and it made the case that longevity at the top is its own kind of greatness, which is a claim that basketball had been slow to accept.
