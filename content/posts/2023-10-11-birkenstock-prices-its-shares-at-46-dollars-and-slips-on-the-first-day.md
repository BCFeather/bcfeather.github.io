Birkenstock priced its shares at 46 dollars on 10 October, at the top of the range it had marketed, and the stock opened at 41 dollars and closed at about 40 on its first day of trading. The offer sold about 32 million shares and raised about 1.5 billion dollars, and the company's owners sold part of their holding in the same transaction. The shares recovered to about 42 dollars by the end of the week, which the dealers read as a sign that the selling was technical rather than fundamental.

Valuation was the argument. The company was valued at about 8.6 billion dollars on the offer, which is more than 20 times its earnings, and the investors compared it with the footwear firms that trade at half that multiple. A multiple that is high is a multiple that has to be justified by the growth. The revenue was about 1.2 billion euros last year, and the profit about 190 million, which puts the offer at about 40 times the earnings on the European accounts. The company's sales have grown by about 20 per cent a year since 2019, and the margins are about 30 per cent at the operating level, which is high for the footwear.

Brand is the asset. Birkenstock has sold the same cork footbed since the 1770s, and the sales have grown by about 20 per cent a year as the sandals became a fashion item in the pandemic. A product that does not change is a product that does not need the discounts. About a third of the sales are now made through the company's own shops and website, up from a fifth five years ago, which is the margin that the investors pay for. The sandals are made in Germany, and about 70 per cent of the production is still in the factory in the Rhineland that the family has run for six generations.

Debt is the reason for the listing. The company was bought by L Catterton, the private equity firm backed by LVMH, in 2021, and the proceeds will be used to pay down the borrowings that the buyout loaded on it. A listing that repays the debt is a listing that the owner chose rather than the company. The borrowings were about 1.4 billion euros after the buyout, and the interest is the reason the company reported a loss in the first half of this year. The owners sold about 10 per cent of the shares in the offer, and the family retains about 15 per cent of the voting rights.

Investors have turned selective. The listings of the summer were priced at discounts and several were withdrawn, and the investors have been demanding the profits rather than the story. A window that is narrow is a window that only the profitable can pass. The listings of Arm and Instacart in September also traded below their offer prices, and the bankers have said the pipeline for the winter is thin. The equity markets have been volatile since the summer, and the investors have been rotating into the cash rather than the new issues.

What the day shows is that a famous brand can still raise the money, and the discount on the first trade is the price of the borrowings that the buyout left behind. A brand that sells the same shoe for 250 years is a brand that has survived the fashion, and the fashion is what the public market is pricing. A public listing exposes the brand to the quarterly numbers, and the numbers are what the family business had avoided for six generations.
