Faults release accumulated stress in two ways. A fast rupture radiates seismic waves in seconds, and a slow slip event creeps over days or months without radiating. The two are observed on the same faults and influence each other, but how a creeping patch changes the dynamics of the locked region beside it has been hard to establish from field data alone, because the relevant depths are unobserved, the record is short and the two phenomena overlap in time.

Laboratory experiments supply the missing control. A preprint posted on 29 December 2023 reports faults built to include a patch of granular material along part of the interface, which creeps where the rest of the fault sticks, and the response of the whole interface to shear is measured while the normal load is varied.

A granular patch acts as a nucleation center. Fast ruptures begin there at a lower shear resistance than they would on a uniform fault, so the heterogeneity seeds the event the way a scratch seeds a crack in glass. The frequency of fast events rises accordingly, since less stress has to accumulate before the next one starts, and the interval between events shortens in proportion to the reduction in resistance.

The normal load controls how far the slow region spreads. With a light load the creeping zone stays close to the granular patch, and with a heavier load it extends beyond the material that caused it, which shows that composition alone does not define a slow-slip region. Stress state does, and the boundary between creeping and locked behaviour moves with it.

Framing the problem as fracture mechanics, not as friction alone, is the paper's main move. A creeping patch is a pre-existing crack that concentrates stress at its tip, and the tip propagates when the stress intensity reaches a threshold that the surrounding material sets, so the failure criterion is local and the size of the patch enters through the stress field it produces. That language explains why the experiments produce a lower nucleation barrier and not a uniformly weaker fault, since the barrier sits at the tip and the rest of the interface is unchanged.

Limits are those of a scaled experiment. The fault is small, the granular material stands in for a natural gouge, and the loading is imposed rather than tectonic, so the experiment isolates a mechanism and says nothing about a particular setting. Rates and stresses are many orders of magnitude away from a real fault, so the result transfers as a mechanism and not as a number.

What the experiments supply is a target for monitoring and a mechanism to look for. If slow slip near a locked patch is a precursor in the laboratory, then geodesy that resolves creeping regions at depth could identify where nucleation is cheapest, and hazard estimates that treat a fault as uniform would be systematically wrong about where events begin and how often they repeat.
