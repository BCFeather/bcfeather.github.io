President Volodymyr Zelenskyy presented a plan for ending the war to the Ukrainian parliament on the sixteenth of October, in a speech that set out five points and referred to three annexes that were not made public. First point was an invitation to join NATO, which he said would show Russia that its geopolitical calculations had failed, and he accepted that membership itself would come only after the fighting ended. He described the plan as a programme for implementation rather than an offer to negotiate, and said it was written to be carried out within months.

Remaining points covered weapons, deterrence, economics and security. Second point asked for the removal of restrictions on long-range strikes and for continued operations inside Russian territory. Third proposed a non-nuclear strategic deterrent on Ukrainian soil, which officials have described as a package of conventional missiles that could reach targets deep in Russia. Fourth pointed to the country's reserves of minerals and its capacity to supply allies. Fifth described a role for Ukrainian forces in the security of Europe after the war, which would replace some of the American troops stationed on the continent.

Presentation was timed to influence three audiences. Plan was briefed to the American president, to Congress and to European leaders in the weeks before the speech, and Zelenskyy said he would present it at a summit of European Union leaders the following day. He also said that if the plan was supported, the war could end next year, a formulation that puts a timetable on a settlement without describing the terms.

Reception abroad was polite and noncommittal. The White House said it had discussed the plan with Ukrainian officials and that it was reviewing the elements, and NATO's secretary general said the alliance's position on membership had not changed, which is that it will happen eventually but not while the war continues. Germany said it would not send the Taurus cruise missiles it has refused to supply since the spring. Several governments noted privately that the plan's most consequential requests, on NATO and on long-range weapons, are the two things their voters are least willing to concede.

Kremlin dismissed it before the speech had finished. Spokesman said the plan was a set of demands that would achieve nothing and that Ukraine would be made to accept reality. Russian officials have consistently argued that any settlement must begin with the recognition of the territorial changes of 2022, and the plan contains nothing that approaches that. Ukrainian officials have said in turn that any ceasefire that leaves Russia in control of occupied land would only give it time to rearm.

Political function of the plan at home is as important as its content. Ukrainian forces have been withdrawing slowly in the east for months, the energy system has been damaged by repeated strikes, and the country faces a winter with a diminished grid capacity. Public support for a long war has been falling in surveys, and the plan offers a version of victory that does not require a military breakthrough. Whether it can be delivered depends on decisions in Washington and Berlin, which the speech could influence but not decide.
