title: "Phosphorus ribbons turn magnetic at room temperature"
date: "2025-03-12T20:40:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "*Nature* reports on 12 March that single-atom-thick ribbons of black phosphorus behave as magnets at room temperature and as semiconductors, a combination that no other material has offered at that scale."
---

Phosphorene is the phosphorus analogue of graphene, a sheet one atom thick that was predicted in 2014 and produced in small quantities soon afterwards. The ribbons that the London group isolated in 2019 are a few nanometres wide and many micrometres long, and they form when black phosphorus is exfoliated in a liquid that breaks the crystal along its grain boundaries instead of between its layers.

Most two-dimensional materials lose their magnetic order when they are thinned, because the ordering depends on the interaction between layers. The ribbons keep it. In films made from the ribbons, the researchers measured a magnetization that persists at room temperature and that behaves like the magnetism of iron or nickel, with a coercive field and a remanence that a magnetometer can see without cooling the sample.

Semiconducting behaviour comes from the width. A ribbon a few nanometres wide has a band gap that depends on how wide it is, which is a property that the group had already measured, and the gap is large enough that the material can be used as a channel in a transistor and not as a conductor. A material that is magnetic and semiconducting in the same sheet is one in which a spin can be injected electrically and read electrically.

Device implications are the reason the result is interesting. A transistor that uses spin rather than charge to store a bit is a device that switches at a lower energy, because flipping a spin costs less than moving a charge across a gate oxide. The field has pursued that idea with dilute magnetic semiconductors for two decades, and the materials have required temperatures near absolute zero or have lost their order at the temperature of a working chip.

The measurements are on films and not on single ribbons, which is the limit of the claim. A film contains many ribbons in contact with each other, and whether an individual ribbon is magnetic, or whether the magnetism arises at the junctions between them, is a question that the current data do not settle. The authors argue that the anisotropy of the magnetization points to the ribbon and not to the contacts, and the argument is plausible and not yet decisive.

Stability is the second question. Phosphorus oxidizes in air, and a material that decomposes in a week is a material that no fabrication line will use. The group reports that the films survived for months under a protective layer, which is the condition that a device would require, and the encapsulation is a part of the result that a reader should not skip.

What the paper opens is a materials problem that has been stuck. A magnetic semiconductor that works at room temperature and can be made in solution is a platform, and the next steps are the ones that any platform needs: a transistor, a memory cell and a measurement of how long the spin survives.
