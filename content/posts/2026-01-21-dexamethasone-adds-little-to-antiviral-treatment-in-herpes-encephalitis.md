title: "Dexamethasone adds little to antiviral treatment in herpes encephalitis"
date: "2026-01-21T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A 94-patient trial across 53 NHS hospitals found no overall improvement in verbal memory at 26 weeks when dexamethasone was added to aciclovir for herpes simplex encephalitis, though earlier treatment looked better."
---

Herpes simplex encephalitis is a rare infection with a case fatality that fell from about seventy per cent to roughly twenty once aciclovir became standard, and survivors are often left with memory deficits that are severe enough to end careers and relationships. The inflammation that the virus provokes is part of the damage, which is why clinicians have added corticosteroids on physiological grounds for decades without a trial large enough to settle the question.

DexEnceph randomized 94 patients with herpes simplex encephalitis across 53 hospitals in the English National Health Service to dexamethasone plus aciclovir or aciclovir alone. The primary outcome was verbal memory at 26 weeks, chosen because it is the deficit that patients and families notice most and because it is measurable with a validated test. The trial appeared in *The Lancet Neurology* on 21 January and was funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research.

No overall difference appeared. Patients given dexamethasone performed no better on the memory measure than those given aciclovir alone, and the confidence interval around the estimate spans both a meaningful benefit and a meaningful harm. In a trial of this size that is an honest answer to the question and an unhelpful one for a clinician standing at a bedside.

Timing produced the most interesting signal. Patients treated early in the course of the illness appeared to do better on dexamethasone, which is consistent with the argument that the damage from inflammation is done in the first days and cannot be undone later. That analysis is a subgroup observation in an underpowered trial and has to be treated as a hypothesis, and it is the kind of hypothesis that shapes the next trial's design.

Safety was acceptable, with no excess of serious adverse events that would rule out the approach. A steroid in a patient with an infection always carries the theoretical risk of worsening it, and the trial's contribution is that this risk did not appear in a population of this size, which will reassure clinicians who already use dexamethasone. The trial's safety database is therefore a contribution in itself, and it is the part that a clinician can quote when obtaining consent.

The design constraint is the recruitment rate. Herpes encephalitis is rare, the window for enrolment is short and consent has to be obtained from families under stress, and 94 patients over several years is what a well-organized national network can achieve. A definitive trial would need international recruitment and an endpoint agreed in advance. Funding came from a public research programme rather than from a manufacturer, which is increasingly unusual for a trial of a repurposed drug.

Where this leaves practice is in the usual place for a neutral trial with a suggestive subgroup. Clinicians who use dexamethasone will continue to, and they will start it early, and clinicians who do not will point to the absence of an overall effect. The question will be settled by a larger trial that randomizes within the first day of presentation, and that trial does not yet exist.
