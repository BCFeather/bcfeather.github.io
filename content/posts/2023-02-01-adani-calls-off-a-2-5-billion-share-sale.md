Subscriptions passed the number of shares on offer by the time the sale closed on 31 January, and the group withdrew it the next day. Adani Enterprises had marketed 2.5 billion dollars of new shares at 3,112 rupees each, a discount to the market price at the time. The company said the volatility made the settlement impractical, and it returned the money to investors. Retail bids were the largest share of the book for the first time in any of the group's offers.

Withdrawing a sale after the book closed was unusual rather than illegal. Under Indian rules a company may cancel an offer before allotment if the board decides it is not in its interest, and the group said it had taken legal advice. Investors who had bid through the anchor book, including several Indian funds, were told the money would be returned within a week. The board met on the morning of 1 February, and the decision was announced before the market opened.

Behind the decision was a short seller's report published on 24 January, which alleged accounting fraud and stock manipulation across the group. The group denied the allegations and called them an attack on the country. Its shares fell 25 per cent over the following week, and the dollar bonds issued by group companies traded at distressed levels. The group's shares had fallen 20 per cent on the day the report was published, and the bond market moved faster.

Retail investors bidding in small lots filled most of the book on the last day. Foreign institutional investors stayed away, and the anchor book was filled by domestic funds and insurers. Analysts said the subscription was a statement of domestic support rather than of confidence in the numbers. Domestic institutions were told the cancellation was procedural, and none of them publicly objected.

Banks that had lent to the group began asking for additional collateral on loans backed by shares, according to two people with knowledge of the arrangements. The lenders included state banks and a French institution, and all of them declined to comment. The group's net debt was about 24 billion dollars at the last published accounts, and its ports and power plants generate most of the cash. The group's ports handle about a quarter of the country's container traffic, which is why lenders were watching.

Afterwards the group changed how it raises money. It cancelled plans for a hydrogen plant in the spring and slowed the timetable for its airport projects. Its founder told a television interviewer in February that the group would reduce leverage and repay debt ahead of schedule, and the shares recovered about half their losses by April. The founder had built the group from a trading house in the 1980s, and the ports business began in 1998. Its shares ended the year lower than they began it, though above the level reached in the week after the report.
