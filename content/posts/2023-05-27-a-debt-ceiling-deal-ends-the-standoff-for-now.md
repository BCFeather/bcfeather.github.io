President Joe Biden and the Republican speaker of the House, Kevin McCarthy, reached an agreement on the evening of 27 May to raise the federal debt ceiling, two days after the treasury had warned that it would run out of room to pay its bills. The deal suspends the limit until January 2025, which is after the next election, and pairs the suspension with caps on domestic discretionary spending for two years, a small clawback of unused pandemic funds, and a modest expansion of work requirements for some benefit programmes.

The mechanics of the thing had been an argument between two numbers. Republicans had passed a bill in April that cut spending by trillions over a decade and reversed parts of the president's climate and tax legislation; the White House had insisted on a clean increase. The final text sits between them: a freeze in nominal terms, which over two years becomes a cut in real terms, and a defence budget that grows more than the rest of the discretionary total, which is what allowed the deal to pass the House with a majority of Republicans.

Deadline pressure made it possible. The treasury's so-called extraordinary measures were exhausted, and a technical default would have stopped payments to pensioners, contractors and holders of government bonds, roiled the money markets and pushed borrowing costs up for everyone in the economy. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that a default would cost hundreds of thousands of jobs, and the rating agencies had begun to put the country on notice, which is something they have done before and which markets treat as a signal about governance rather than about solvency.

The politics were hardest inside the parties. The speaker negotiated with a president of the other party while a faction of his own conference, which had objected to his election in January and had extracted rules that give it leverage, promised to vote against any deal that looked like surrender. The president faced the anger of progressives who had watched him give away spending that they had spent two years legislating. Both of them signed the agreement and then spent the weekend counting the votes, and the House passed it on 31 May with 314 votes to 117.

Ratings and debt are what remain. The country's debt stands at about thirty-one trillion dollars, and the two years of caps will not change the trajectory, which is driven by the cost of health care and pensions for an ageing population that neither party wants to touch. The suspension has moved the argument past the election, which is what the White House wanted, and it has settled nothing about the deficit, which is what the ratings agencies will say.

For the rest of the world, the lesson is about the reliability of the dollar system. A country that periodically threatens to default on its own obligations, and that resolves the threat with a last-minute deal, has shown that its bargains are real and that its political system can be made to work. It has also shown that the arrangement recurs every two years, and that the cost of the uncertainty is paid by the borrowers whose debt is denominated in the currency that the argument is about.
