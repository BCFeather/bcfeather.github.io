Hydrogen storage, battery electrodes and metal hydride sensors all work by pushing atoms into a host lattice and letting them out again, and the process is rarely uniform. The incoming atoms form a phase that is richer in hydrogen than the host, the boundary between the two phases moves, and the strain at that boundary controls how fast the front travels. Palladium is the model system, because it absorbs hydrogen at room temperature and the two phases differ by a few per cent in lattice constant, so the strain is small enough to see.

The textbook picture of that front is a shrinking core, in which the new phase forms a shell at the surface and eats inward with a boundary parallel to the surface. A different mechanism, an intercalation wave, runs as a band that sweeps through the crystal, and which one operates depends on the strain energy and on the mobility of the host atoms.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 3 April 2024 watches the process directly. The authors use liquid-phase transmission electron microscopy to follow hydrogen entering and leaving palladium nanocubes, and they obtain atomic-resolution images of the front as it moves. The liquid cell keeps the particle wet and admits hydrogen through a sealed membrane, which is what makes the observation possible at ambient pressure.

The wave wins at room temperature. For cubes from about 60 nanometres down to about 10 nanometres, the authors find intercalation waves rather than shrinking cores, which extends the mechanism to sizes where the surface-to-volume ratio would be expected to favour a shell. At 10 nanometres a cube holds a few thousand unit cells, and the front shows up as a contrast difference across a handful of atomic columns.

As the front advances, the boundary changes shape. It starts out non-planar and inclined to the cube faces, and it evolves towards orientations aligned with the {100} planes, which is the configuration that minimizes the lattice mismatch between the hydrogen-poor and hydrogen-rich phases. An inclined boundary cuts through the lattice, and the alignment that follows costs less elastic energy than the orientation it replaces.

Simulation agrees with the observation. Kinetic Monte Carlo calculations reproduce the same sequence of boundary shapes, and they identify the pathway as the one that minimizes the strain at the phase boundary, which is the physical reason the front ends up on a low-index plane.

What the experiment does not yet give is a rate law. The images are snapshots of a process that runs in seconds to minutes, and a model that predicted the front speed from the particle size and the temperature would need time-resolved data at the same resolution, which is the measurement the technique is moving towards. What the images do supply is the shape of the front, and a front that aligns with a low-index plane can be built into a continuum model as a boundary condition.
