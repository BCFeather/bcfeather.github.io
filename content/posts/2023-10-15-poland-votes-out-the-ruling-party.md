---

The Law and Justice party won the largest share of the vote in Poland's election on 15 October, taking 35.4 per cent and 194 of the 460 seats in the Sejm, and it lost the ability to govern. The Civic Coalition led by Donald Tusk took 30.7 per cent and 157 seats, the Third Way alliance 14.4 per cent and 65 seats, and the Left 8.6 per cent and 26 seats, which gives the three opposition groupings 248 seats and a working majority. Turnout reached 74.4 per cent, the highest in a Polish parliamentary election since the return of competitive voting in 1989.

Both sides fought the campaign over the country's direction rather than its management. Law and Justice had spent eight years reshaping the judiciary, the public broadcasters and the state companies, and it framed the vote as a defence of sovereignty against the European Union and against what it described as German influence. The opposition campaigned on restoring judicial independence, unlocking money from the union's recovery fund, and ending the culture war over abortion and minority rights that had defined the previous term.

The money is the immediate issue. About 35 billion euros of cohesion and recovery funds have been withheld while the Commission pursues the rule-of-law case, and Warsaw's relations with Brussels have been frozen since 2021. A government led by Tusk would be expected to legislate on the judiciary and to accept the milestones the Commission set, which could release the funds within months, and the first payments would arrive into a budget that has run large deficits.

Mechanics are slower than the arithmetic. The president, who is aligned with Law and Justice, has to nominate a prime minister, and he has said he will give the incumbent party the first attempt, which opens a path of weeks in which Law and Justice could try to peel away a handful of MPs. The three opposition parties have signed an agreement and say they will hold, and the question is whether the parliamentary arithmetic survives the president's discretion.

That result also changes the argument inside the European Union. Hungary and Poland had together formed a bloc that resisted the rule-of-law conditionality, and a change of government in Warsaw leaves Budapest more isolated in the debates over the budget and over migration. Leaders in Brussels have been careful not to welcome the outcome too loudly, because the new government has to be formed before it can be tested.

For voters the decisive themes were prices and the role of the church in public life, and the exit polls found that younger voters and women in cities moved towards the opposition in large numbers. The conservative countryside turned out too, and the election produced a clear majority rather than a coalition puzzle. Eight years of one-party government ended at the ballot box, and the institutions it rebuilt will be the first thing the next government reopens. The new coalition will have to hold together three parties whose programmes overlap on the courts and diverge on almost everything else.
