Insect diversification is often attributed to the rise of flowering plants, and the attribution is tested by families old enough to have seen both that rise and a continental break-up. A paper in *eLife* on 12 December 2024 examines one such family. Two processes with different signatures is a favourable situation for inference, since a coincidence can be assigned to one process more easily than to a mixture. Host records from museum collections would widen the sample, and widening is the cheapest improvement available. Dates good enough to resolve events are the fragile part of the method, and a fossil calibration that is wrong shifts every coincidence in the tree. A dated tree is a hypothesis about timing, and timing is what either explanation has to respect.

Belidae is a weevil family of modest size whose lineages are distributed across the fragments of Gondwana. A distribution matching a break-up permits a biogeographic test that a distribution matching host plants does not. A family distributed across fragments is a family whose history is partly a history of the fragments, and that history is what the tree recovers. A mixed answer is more honest than a single cause, and honesty is what a biogeographic claim needs in a family this old.

Phylogenetic reconstruction and dating were combined with host records. Dates locate the splits and host records say which plants each lineage uses, so the two explanations can be compared event by event. Event-by-event comparison is the method, and the method requires dates good enough to resolve the events, which is the fragile part. Dates constrain where a shift can be placed, and a constraint is what separates a testable story from a plausible one.

Some splits coincide with the separation of landmasses and others with host shifts. The mixed pattern is the result, and it argues that neither process explains the family on its own. A mixed pattern is a less satisfying answer than a single cause and a more honest one, since the family probably experienced both. Museum records are an underused resource, and underuse is why distributional questions move slowly in taxonomy.

Host shifts are frequent in weevils, which makes the plant explanation attractive and also hard to falsify. A shift long after a continental separation cannot be attributed to the separation. A shift that can be placed anywhere is a shift that explains nothing, and the paper's dates are what constrain where a shift can be placed.

The contribution is a dated tree with hosts mapped onto it, which is the object either explanation has to fit. Uneven sampling is structural in southern hemisphere work, and the structural bias is the kind of limitation a reader should hold in view.

Sampling of southern hemisphere taxa is the limitation, and a family spread across fragments is sampled unevenly. A reusable tree is the deliverable, and a deliverable that others extend is how taxonomy makes progress.
