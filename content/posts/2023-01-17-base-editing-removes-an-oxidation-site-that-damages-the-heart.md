Calcium-calmodulin kinase II is a signalling enzyme with a switch that is not supposed to exist. In the heart, its delta isoform is activated by calcium, and it is also activated when two methionine residues in its regulatory domain are oxidized. The second route is a damage pathway, and it is the one that runs during a heart attack.

The study in *Science*, 13 January 2023 targets that pathway at the genome. Simon Lebek and colleagues use CRISPR base editing to change the codons for the two methionines, so that the protein can no longer be oxidized, while leaving its calcium regulation intact.

Base editing is the right tool because the change is a point substitution. A cytosine base editor converts a C to a U, and with the right protospacer it can turn an ATG codon into one that encodes a different residue, or a synonymous codon that shifts the site. The paper's editing is targeted at the two positions that matter, and the rest of the kinase is untouched.

The demonstration runs in mice subjected to ischaemia-reperfusion, and in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes. In both, the edited kinase reduces the damage that follows the restoration of blood flow, and the diastolic calcium handling that deteriorates after reperfusion is preserved.

The mechanism is that oxidation locks the kinase in an active state that outlasts the calcium signal. A persistent kinase phosphorylates targets that include the ryanodine receptor and the contractile machinery, and the result is a heart that cannot relax. Removing the oxidation site removes the persistence.

Specificity is the safety question. Base editors can edit off-target cytosines, and a permanent genomic change in a patient's heart cells has to be free of unintended edits before it can be considered. The paper reports off-target analysis, and the standard for a therapy would be stricter than the standard for an experiment.

Delivery is the other obstacle. Editing a heart means reaching enough cardiomyocytes to matter, and adeno-associated vectors have limited cargo and finite duration. The study's approach is a proof of principle in systems where delivery is easy, and the clinical version would need a vector that a heart can tolerate.

The alternative is pharmacological. A small molecule that blocks oxidation of the kinase would not need a genomic edit, and the difficulty is selectivity, because the same methionine chemistry appears in many proteins. The genetic route avoids that problem by construction, and it trades selectivity for permanence.

What remains unmeasured is the long-term cost. A kinase that cannot be oxidized may lose a protective function, since oxidation also happens at low levels in healthy tissue, and the paper's time course is short. A year of follow-up in an animal model would show whether the edit is a repair or a trade.
