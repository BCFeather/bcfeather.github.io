The Devonian period is when land changed from a flat, wet surface into something with structure. Plants had been on land for tens of millions of years, but they were small, and their roots were shallow. Then, in a span of perhaps twenty million years, roots deepened, soils thickened, rivers began to meander, and the atmosphere's carbon dioxide fell. The evidence for that transition is mostly in rocks that record the shape of river channels and the chemistry of ancient soils. A quarry in south China has now produced something rarer: a fossil forest floor with the root systems still in place.

The site preserves a soil surface from roughly 390 million years ago, with the stumps and root traces of early trees spread across an area of a few hundred square metres. The roots are the interesting part. They branch downward for more than a metre, which means the plants were exploring a volume of soil rather than holding a thin skin of it, and the traces show the characteristic pattern of a root system that grew into soft ground and was later cast in place by sediment.

The dating comes from spores and from the stratigraphy. The assemblage places the site in the Middle Devonian, close to the period when the first forests appear in the record elsewhere, and the species present are a mix of early tree forms and smaller plants that grew between them. The authors argue that the site represents a coastal forest on a muddy plain, periodically flooded, with the soil developing between floods.

The significance is about feedback. A root system a metre deep does several things at once: it binds soil, which slows erosion; it draws water from below the surface, which changes how the ground dries; it delivers organic carbon into the soil, where it can be stored; and it accelerates the weathering of bedrock, which consumes carbon dioxide and releases nutrients. The last of those is the one with global consequences, because chemical weathering of silicate rocks is the main long-term sink for atmospheric carbon dioxide, and it is a process that roots speed up by a large factor.

The claim that the authors make is modest and worth stating precisely. They do not argue that this forest caused the Devonian decline in carbon dioxide, which is recorded in the chemistry of marine sediments over tens of millions of years. They argue that the site shows the kind of rooting depth and soil development that would have made such a decline possible, at a date earlier than some models assumed. That is a contribution to a chronology rather than a causal claim.

The wider point is about how much of the history of life is recorded in the ground rather than in the fossils. A forest is an event in the atmosphere and the rivers before it is an event in the fossil record, and the rock that preserves the roots is the place where those two records meet. Quarries like this one are rarely beautiful. They are, occasionally, the reason a chapter in a textbook gets rewritten.
