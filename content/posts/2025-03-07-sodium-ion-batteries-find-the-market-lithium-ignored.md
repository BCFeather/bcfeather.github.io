description: "A chemistry that gave up energy density in exchange for cheap materials has settled into grid storage and small vehicles, where density is not the constraint."
---

Sodium-ion batteries were a laboratory curiosity for a decade and have been a commercial product for about two years, and the case for them has changed shape. The original pitch was that sodium is abundant and cheap while lithium is scarce and expensive, and that pitch lost force when lithium prices fell by eighty per cent from their 2022 peak. What survived is a different argument about temperature, safety and cycle life. A cell chemistry that was priced as a hedge against lithium has had to find a reason to exist at a lithium price of one hundred dollars a kilogram.

Engineering trade is clear. A sodium-ion cell stores less energy per kilogram than a lithium iron phosphate cell, and it stores it at a voltage that is lower and flatter. What it gains is the ability to operate at low temperatures, a lower risk of thermal runaway, and the option to use aluminium instead of copper for the current collector, which removes a metal that is both expensive and supply-constrained. The aluminium collector is the detail that matters at scale, because copper foil is a cost that a large pack cannot avoid.

Grid storage is the first volume market. A container of batteries that sits beside a substation does not care about weight, and it cares a great deal about cost per cycle and about safety. Sodium-ion cells are being deployed in projects in China for exactly that use, and the chemistry's flat voltage curve suits a system that is managed by a power converter rather than by a motor. A storage project is financed on the number of cycles it can deliver before capacity falls below eighty per cent, and that number is the chemistry's strength.

Small vehicles are the second market. A two-wheeled electric scooter in a city needs twenty kilometres of range and a battery that does not catch fire in a stairwell, and several manufacturers now sell sodium-ion versions at a price below the lithium equivalent. The energy density penalty is acceptable because the vehicle is light and the trip is short. A scooter that charges overnight in a courtyard needs a charger that is cheap, and the lower voltage of a sodium pack makes the electronics simpler.

Cold weather is the third. A sodium-ion pack retains most of its capacity at minus twenty degrees, where a lithium pack loses a large fraction, and that property is worth more than density in northern China and in Canada. Fleets that operate in winter have begun to test the chemistry for that reason alone.

Whether sodium-ion follows lithium down the cost curve is the strategic question. The materials are cheap, the manufacturing is the same as lithium-ion, and the scale is still small. If the chemistry improves at the rate that lithium iron phosphate did after 2020, it will take share in storage and in entry-level transport, and it will never take share in a long-range car. That is a large market and a bounded one. A chemistry that wins storage and entry-level transport has found two markets that are larger than the one it was designed for.
