Parker Solar Probe flew within 6.1 million kilometres of the Sun's surface on the twenty-fourth of December, closer than any spacecraft has come to a star, and moved at about 430,000 miles an hour, roughly 690,000 kilometres an hour, as it crossed the outer atmosphere. Contact was lost during the pass, as designed, because the heat shield faces the Sun and the antenna points away from Earth. Mission team at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory regained the signal on the evening of the twenty-sixth, and NASA confirmed the spacecraft was healthy the following day.

Design of the probe is built around that passage. Carbon-composite shield on the sunward side is about eleven centimetres thick and is designed to keep the instruments behind it at temperatures near room level while the outside reaches 1,400 degrees Celsius, and the radiators that dump heat have to work while the spacecraft is inside the corona. Solar arrays are retracted and cooled, and the whole spacecraft is oriented so that the shield, and nothing else, faces the Sun. Any failure of that attitude would destroy it within seconds.

Scientific purpose is to answer a question that has resisted modelling for decades: why the corona, which is further from the core than the visible surface, is hundreds of times hotter, and how the solar wind is accelerated to the speeds measured at Earth. Earlier passes produced measurements of switchbacks, sharp folds in the magnetic field that appear to carry energy outward, and of small jets of plasma near the surface that may feed the wind. Data from this pass, the closest yet, will show whether those features persist at the boundary where the corona begins.

Pass was the first of three at this distance, the last of which is planned for 2025, and each one takes the spacecraft through a region that no instrument has visited. Speed record set during the pass exceeds the previous mark held by the probe itself, and it is a consequence of orbital mechanics rather than propulsion: repeated passes by Venus have shrunk the orbit until the probe's perihelion falls inside the corona. Mission launched in 2018 and has spent six years tightening that spiral.

Engineering limits are now the constraint. At the temperatures and speeds of these passes, the instruments must be protected and the spacecraft must be able to survive a failure that lasts minutes, and the team has said that the thermal margin is smaller than at any earlier point in the mission. Telemetry confirming the state of the heat shield is expected in early January, when the spacecraft is far enough from the Sun to transmit at full rate.

What the mission will leave behind is a set of measurements rather than a single discovery. Data from the pass will be released to researchers through the mission archive, and the results will feed models of space weather that are used to forecast disturbances affecting satellites, power grids and radio communication. Star that the probe has now touched is the one that governs those disturbances, and the point of the exercise is to know enough about it to predict what it will do next.
