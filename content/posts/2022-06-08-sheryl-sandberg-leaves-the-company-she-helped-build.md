Sheryl Sandberg announced on 1 June that she would step down as chief operating officer of Meta in the autumn, after fourteen years in the job. She joined Facebook in 2008, when the company had fewer than a thousand employees and revenue of about 270 million dollars a year, and she leaves it with more than seventy thousand staff and revenue of nearly 118 billion dollars last year. Her successor is Javier Olivan, who has run the part of the business that manages growth and product strategy. The announcement came in a post on the platform itself, and it was followed within minutes by a message from the founder calling her a partner.

What she built was the commercial side of a social network. Advertising accounted for almost all of the company's revenue, and the system that sold it was assembled under her: self-service tools for small businesses, a sales force for brands, measurement products that told advertisers what their money had bought, and a policy apparatus that negotiated with regulators in every market where the company operated. Analysts who covered the company for a decade describe the ad machine as her achievement rather than the founder's.

Her public profile extended beyond the company. The book she published in 2013, *Lean In*, argued that women should treat ambition as a duty and that the obstacles in their way were partly internal, and it sold millions of copies and started an organization that still runs circles in offices. The argument drew criticism from those who thought it placed the burden of structural change on individual women, and she revised parts of it in later editions.

Timing gave the announcement a second meaning. Meta was in the middle of its slowest year since it became public: revenue growth had stalled, Apple's restrictions on tracking had made advertising less precise, and younger users were moving to other applications. Shares had fallen by more than half since the autumn, and the company had told managers to identify low performers, which in the language of the industry means preparing to cut.

The role she leaves behind is narrower than the one she held. Meta's reorganization puts growth, advertising and partnerships under different executives, and the operating committee that she chaired will report to the founder directly. Some of the functions she managed, including policy and communications, will move to other parts of the company, which is what usually happens when a long-serving executive departs. Her departure leaves a smaller executive team at a moment when the company is spending heavily on a hardware project that has produced little revenue.

A departure is a way of dating an era. Sandberg was the last of the executives who joined Facebook in its early years and stayed through its transformation into one of the largest advertising businesses in the world, and her leaving marks the point where the company's founding generation has almost entirely handed over. What replaces that generation is a set of managers who inherited a machine rather than built one.
