Pyroclastic density currents are the deadliest thing a volcano does. They are mixtures of hot gas and rock fragments that travel along the ground at speeds no one can outrun, and the quantity that matters for evacuation is run-out distance. Forecasts of run-out rest on a rheology, a rule for how the mixture resists flow, and that rule has been largely assumed rather than measured.

A paper in *Nature Communications* on 9 May 2024 measures it. The authors built a laboratory apparatus that shears real gas-pyroclast mixtures at the concentrations and strain rates found in the dense to intermediate part of a pumice-rich current, and recorded how the material responded.

Response here is decidedly non-Newtonian. Three regimes appear. At low stress the mixture carries a yield strength and stops, which is where deposition begins. At intermediate stress it shear-thins, so that faster patches resist less than slow ones and the flow organises into channels with locally higher velocity. At high stress it shear-thickens, so that rapid shearing raises resistance, the mixture decouples, and ash can rise into a co-current plume above the ground-hugging part.

Condensing the three regimes into one map is the paper's second contribution. into a single diagram in dimensionless stress and strain rate. The diagram is the practical product, since it lets a modeller place a current on the map and see which behaviour dominates, and it allows a current to cross from one regime to another as it travels and loses energy.

Previous work in the field had two options, a Coulomb friction model that treats the current as a sliding block, and a suspension model that treats it as a dilute turbulent flow. Neither covers the concentrated, particle-rich core that does the damage, and the new measurements sit exactly there. What the paper adds is a constitutive law for that core, with the coefficients that a depth-averaged model needs to be fitted against deposits.

Hazard modelling implications are direct. A model that treats a pyroclastic current as a Newtonian fluid with one viscosity will misplace the deposit front in both directions, too far in one regime and too short in another. Channel formation also explains the surges that have been observed in real currents, where a lobe advances faster than the bulk and overtakes it. One consequence is that the deposit record becomes readable. Channels and levees in an ancient ignimbrite imply shear thinning at the time of emplacement, and a co-current ash layer implies that the flow passed through shear thickening.

The limits are scale and complexity. A laboratory channel is metres long, a natural current is kilometres long, and the apparatus cannot reproduce the fragmentation and air entrainment that happen at the front of a real flow. The authors present the regime diagram as a framework for field calibration rather than a finished forecast. Even so, refitting hazard models to a measured rheology is a step that has been missing, and the three regimes give the modellers something to fit.
