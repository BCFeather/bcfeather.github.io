title: "Stalled ribosomes leave a protein that damages the brain"

description: "When ribosome-associated quality control fails, the protein TTC3 accumulates and cognitive function declines, according to a study published in *PNAS* on 21 March 2023 that links a basic quality-control pathway to neurological disease."
---

A ribosome that stalls on a messenger RNA leaves a half-made protein, and a cell has a pathway that removes it. The ribosome-associated quality control machinery splits the stalled ribosome and tags the incomplete chain for degradation, and the pathway's failure is a source of protein aggregation.

The study in *PNAS*, 21 March 2023 traces that failure to a phenotype. The authors show that dysregulation of the quality-control pathway leads to overaccumulation of TTC3, a protein that the pathway would normally keep in check, and that the accumulation is accompanied by cognitive impairment.

The mechanism is an interaction. TTC3 is an E3 ligase, and its substrates include proteins that the quality-control pathway depends on, so that accumulation of TTC3 feeds back on the machinery that should restrain it. The loop is what turns a modest defect into a persistent one.

The model is the evidence. The authors use cell systems and animal models, and the behavioural readouts in the animals are what connect a molecular accumulation to a cognitive outcome.

The disease relevance is the point. Several neurodegenerative conditions involve stalled translation and aggregated protein, and a pathway that is common to them is a target that a single therapy could address.

The therapeutic direction is to lower TTC3. If accumulation is the cause, then reducing the protein's level is the intervention, and the paper's genetic manipulations test whether that is sufficient.

The caveat is the model's distance from a patient. A mouse with an engineered defect is not a person with a sporadic disease, and the human evidence that TTC3 accumulates in patients is the bridge that the paper's discussion has to build.

The measurement of cognition is coarse. A behavioural battery in a mouse measures performance and not the subjective decline that a patient reports, and the translation between the two is approximate.

What the paper does not establish is the threshold. A protein that accumulates slightly may be harmless, and the level at which function begins to decline is a number that a therapy would need.

For neurobiology, the result connects two fields. Ribosome quality control is a cell-biology topic and cognition is a clinical one, and the paper's chain of evidence runs from a stalled ribosome to a behavioural deficit.

The broader lesson is about quality control as a disease axis. A cell that cannot dispose of its mistakes accumulates them, and the accumulation is a mechanism that is separate from the mistake's cause.

The pathway's components are several. A splitting factor releases the ribosome's subunits, and a tagging factor marks the nascent chain. A defect in either step produces a different intermediate, and the paper distinguishes them.

The aggregation is not the only route. An incomplete chain can be degraded, can be sequestered, or can interfere with a complex that it resembles. The paper's data suggest the third, which is why the effect is a gain of function rather than a loss.
The cell's response to the accumulation is a second variable. A protein that rises may be cleared by a chaperone, and the balance between production and clearance is what sets the steady state that a therapy would target.
