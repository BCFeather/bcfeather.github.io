description: "Two climate model suites run under the same scenario but with different Atlantic overturning states show that a weakened circulation reduces end-of-century Arctic warming by about 2 degrees Celsius, mostly through sea ice and ocean heat uptake."
---

Arctic amplification is the observation that the far north warms several times faster than the global mean, and the reasons usually offered include albedo loss as sea ice retreats, changed atmospheric transport and altered ocean heat uptake. A paper in *PNAS* on 16 September 2024 isolates the contribution of the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation to that list. The list of mechanisms has grown faster than the models that quantify their separate contributions, which is why a controlled comparison is useful.

The design is a comparison between two suites of simulations. Both follow the same climate change scenario, and the only difference is the state of the overturning, which is weakened in one suite and held steady in the other, so that the two can be subtracted. Subtraction is the whole argument, and it only works if the two suites differ in the circulation and in nothing else that matters.

A weakened circulation reduces annual mean Arctic warming by about 2 °C by the end of the century. That reduction is large enough to matter for impacts, and it runs against the intuitive expectation that a circulation which carries heat northward must warm the north when it slows. Because the two runs share a scenario, the difference is attributable to the circulation rather than to the emissions trajectory they follow.

Surface albedo is the first contributor. A weaker overturning leaves more sea ice in place, so less dark ocean is exposed in summer, and the feedback that would otherwise amplify warming is correspondingly weaker. Ice that survives the summer keeps the surface bright through the following winter, so the effect persists beyond the season in which it is created.

Ocean heat uptake is the second contributor. The circulation transports heat into the high latitudes, and its slowdown changes where that heat is stored, so the surface warming that the atmosphere feels is reduced even though the total heat in the system has not fallen. Heat that is taken up at depth is unavailable to warm the air, and the pattern of where it is stored is what the circulation controls.

Attribution of this kind is what model intercomparisons are for, and the paper's value is that it separates a mechanism from the ensemble spread rather than inferring it from a single run. An ensemble of runs is needed because a single pair of simulations can differ for reasons unrelated to the mechanism under study.

The result does not mean that a slowdown is good news. A weaker overturning is associated with shifts in precipitation, with sea level rise along the western Atlantic seaboard, and with a colder North Atlantic, and the Arctic effect is one term among several. Model resolution is a limitation, since the overturning is parametrized in the runs described and its response to freshwater forcing is sensitive to that choice. Observations of the circulation are also short relative to the timescales involved. The Arctic term is also small compared with the regional effects that the same circulation change produces elsewhere, which is why the framing matters.
