Staphylococcus aureus resists penicillins by two routes, and both are switched on by the same sensor. The bacterium carries a membrane protein, BlaR1, that detects the drug and transmits the signal inward, and a repressor, BlaI, that sits on the DNA until the signal arrives.

The structures in *Nature*, 4 January 2023 show that transmission. J. Andrew Alexander, Liam Worrall, Natalie Strynadka and colleagues solved cryo-electron microscopy structures of BlaR1 in states that correspond to the resting and the signalling conformations, and the difference is a change that activates a protease.

The sensor's mechanism is acylation. A beta-lactam binds a serine in the sensor's extracellular domain and forms a covalent adduct, the same chemistry that inhibits the transpeptidases the drug targets. The adduct is not chemistry for nothing: it is the event that switches the protein on.

Activation leads to cleavage. The cytoplasmic domain of BlaR1 becomes an active protease, and it cuts BlaI, which releases the repressor from the operators that control blaZ and mecA. The beta-lactamase is then made, and the alternative transpeptidase, which the drug cannot bind, is made too.

The structures place the two domains in relation to each other, and that relation is the puzzle the paper solves. A sensor that spans the membrane has to couple an extracellular acylation to an intracellular cleavage, and the conformational change the structures reveal is the coupling.

The resistance that follows is broad-spectrum. BlaZ hydrolyses penicillins, and MecA is a transpeptidase with a low affinity for nearly every beta-lactam, which is why methicillin-resistant strains resist the whole class. A signal that induces both is a signal that arms the cell twice.

The therapeutic implication is that the sensor is a target. A molecule that blocks activation without blocking the active site would leave the bacterium unable to respond to the drug, and the structures provide the surfaces such a molecule would have to bind.

The resistance to that idea is evolutionary. A sensor that can be inhibited is a sensor under selection, and the bacterium has already evolved variants of BlaR1 that respond to different drugs. The structures make the design possible, and they do not make it easy.

What the paper does not show is the kinetics of the switch. Acylation is slow, and the time between the drug arriving and the resistance appearing determines whether a dose can win. The structural states are the framework, and the rates are the next measurement.

The work also clarifies why the inducible system exists. Constitutive resistance costs the cell, because BlaZ and MecA are expensive and because the transpeptidase is less efficient than the native one. Inducibility keeps the cost down until the drug is present.

The sensor also illustrates a general principle of bacterial signalling. A membrane protein with a covalent modification as its input is a switch that cannot be reset until the modification is turned over, and the lifetime of the acyl-enzyme is a built-in delay that shapes the response.
