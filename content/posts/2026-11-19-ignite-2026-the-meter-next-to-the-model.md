Microsoft opened its annual Ignite conference in San Francisco on 17 November with a keynote arranged around three words, human ambition, intelligence and trust, and with one product claim underneath them: Copilot as the operating system for work. The announcements with the most operational detail sat lower in the stack, in the parts that let a company see what its agents did, price what they cost and restrict what they may touch.

The Copilot surface now has four named forms. Home merges chat and cowork. Code is the build-it-yourself form, promised within weeks. Autopilot is an agent the company describes as running continuously across the places a team works. Copilot Workspaces adds a shared room where people and agents pursue one goal with common context, and Office inside Copilot is generally available. Work IQ sits beneath all of it as the index of people, documents, meetings and projects. The company also added plugins for business applications, starting with Dynamics 365, and said Salesforce content would come into Copilot.

Model news was organised by task. Microsoft-Decision-1.1 is generally available with multimodal input, and the company says more than four thousand customers use it for routing, labelling, content filtering and personalisation. Code 2 and Code 2 Flash went generally available on GitHub Copilot and in Microsoft Foundry, pitched at cost per task in production work. Cyber 2 Flash, a security model, is being integrated with Perception. GPT 6.2 and Meta's newest models arrive in Foundry, and MAI Licensed Weights will give trusted customers access to Microsoft's own model weights.

Instrumentation drew the clearest line under the week. Agent 365 extends identity, security and management rules to agents, and the company counts more than fifty million agents registered there. Agent 365 Business Analytics, announced at the keynote, is described as an ERP system for AI usage, connecting spend to completed work. Microsoft Perception, a security system staffed by red, blue and green agent teams running playbooks, is generally available. The Microsoft AI Transparency Initiative adds a standards track, with work inside the NIST agent standards effort and forty organisations signed up to define independent verification of a model's actions.

Hardware carried its own claims. Cobalt 200 virtual machines are generally available, described as the highest-performing general-purpose instances Azure has shipped. Maia 300, the next accelerator, is claimed at 40 per cent more tokens per watt than leading GPU-based systems, and Azure Boost and the integrated hardware security module move infrastructure and key-security functions onto purpose-built silicon. Those figures are vendor figures, published before independent measurement.

Four things will settle the week. Whether Business Analytics can attribute spend to an outcome rather than to a seat. Whether licensed weights reach customers outside the Frontier programme. Whether the transparency work produces a test anyone can run against a model they did not build. Whether Code 2 holds up on repositories it has not seen. The keynote's three words describe an intention; the products announced underneath them are the ones that will be measured.
