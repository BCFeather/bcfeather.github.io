title: "Dog behaviour owes more to ancestry than to breed"
date: "2022-04-29T09:55:00+08:00"

description: "Genomes and owner surveys from 27,541 dogs found that breed explains 9 per cent of behavioural variation, that 11 loci are associated with behaviour, and that breed stereotypes align only weakly with ancestral function."
---

Breed stereotypes are the basis of housing regulations, insurance premiums and the choice of a family pet, and they rest on the assumption that a breed carries a behavioural type. The assumption has never been tested against genomes at scale. A breed is a closed breeding population selected for appearance, and the assumption that behaviour followed the appearance is a claim about correlated selection that genetics can test.

A study sequenced 27,541 dogs from 138 breeds alongside owner-completed behavioural surveys, and asked how much of the variation in behaviour is explained by breed. The results appeared in *Science*, 29 April 2022. A survey completed by an owner is a report about the dog as the owner sees it, and the method is standard in behavioural genetics because no laboratory test can measure a pet's daily behaviour at scale.

Breed explained about 9 per cent of the variation in individual behaviour, and several behavioural traits are heritable with heritability above 25 per cent. The two numbers are not contradictory: behaviour is partly genetic and only a small part of that genetic variation is organized by breed. The heritability estimates are computed from the genomic relationship matrix, which separates the genetic from the environmental contribution without requiring pedigrees.

Genome-wide association identified 11 loci significantly associated with behaviour, and the loci are not unusually differentiated between breeds. That is the quantitative version of the argument: selection for appearance has not carried behaviour along with it in the way that breed clubs assume. A locus associated with behaviour and not differentiated between breeds is a locus that responds to selection inside a breed and not to the selection that created the breed.

Breed propensities align weakly with ancestral function, so a livestock guardian breed is somewhat more likely to guard and a retriever is somewhat more likely to retrieve, but the individual dog is a poor predictor of its breed's reputation. Modern breeds are distinguished primarily by aesthetic traits. A breeder who selects for a coat colour is not selecting for a temperament, and if the two are not genetically correlated the breed will drift in behaviour as it is refined in appearance.

Admixture patterns in mixed-breed dogs show the same structure, which supports the authors' proposal that the behaviours perceived as characteristic of modern breeds derive from thousands of years of polygenic adaptation that predates breed formation. A dog that is a mixture of breeds inherits the polygenic background of the population and not the aesthetic traits of one club, which is why a mixed-breed animal is a poor test of a breed stereotype.

A policy implication is that a breed-specific ban or a breed-specific insurance category is a weak instrument, because the variation within a breed is larger than the difference between breeds. A behaviour assessment of an individual dog is the better predictor, and the study gives that claim the data it has lacked. The finding also bears on the question of dangerous breeds, where a bite statistic that is aggregated by breed reflects both the population of dogs and the population of owners.
