Alexander Lukashenko, the president of Belarus, confirmed on 27 June that Yevgeny Prigozhin had arrived in the country, three days after the Wagner group's abortive march towards Moscow ended under an arrangement that Lukashenko said he had brokered. Russian officials said the criminal case against Prigozhin had been closed, and the defence ministry announced that Wagner's heavy equipment would be handed over to the regular army and that its fighters would be offered contracts, which is the language of absorption rather than of pardon.

The terms of the arrangement were never made public in full and remain disputed. Lukashenko said he had offered Wagner the use of an abandoned military base in Belarus if the group wanted it, and he described the conversation as one in which two men who had known each other for years spoke plainly. The Kremlin's spokesman said the agreement included a guarantee that Prigozhin could leave for Belarus, and that the criminal case had been dropped as part of the deal. Neither government has explained what happens to the thousands of fighters who followed him.

Dismantling matters most for the war in Ukraine. Wagner had been the most effective Russian formation in the battle for Bakhmut, and its withdrawal to camps in the occupied east and its disarmament remove a unit that the army had relied on. The fighters were offered contracts with the ministry of defence, which is what Prigozhin had refused to sign before the mutiny, and those who decline will be expected to return home. The units in Africa, where Wagner has operations in Mali, Libya, the Central African Republic and Sudan, were not mentioned.

Political consequences for Putin are harder to read. He described the mutiny as treason and said the organizers had betrayed the country, and he then allowed the man who led it to leave with his life and with his business intact. That combination is a signal to everyone in the system that the rules are applied unevenly, and it sits badly with a leader whose authority has rested on the appearance of control. The closing of the case without a charge is a stronger signal than any of the public statements, because it tells the security services that the decision was political.

For Belarus, the arrival is an opportunity and a risk. Lukashenko has been dependent on Russian support since the protests of 2020, and hosting a man who nearly took Moscow gives him a card that he did not have before. It also puts a private army with combat experience on his territory, which is a thing that no small state wants when it is already sharing a border with a war. The base that was offered has not been used since the cold war, and whether Wagner ends up there is the question that the next weeks will answer.

Lukashenko's offered base has not been used since the cold war, and satellite images have shown vehicles arriving there. Whether Wagner reconstitutes itself in Belarus, in Africa or not at all is the question the next months will answer, and Kyiv will read the answer as closely as Moscow.
