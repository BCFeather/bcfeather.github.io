---

The eukaryotic cell is distinguished by compartments and by a cytoskeleton, and the genes for both are found in archaea that have never been cultured. Asgard archaea carry eukaryotic signature proteins, including actin homologues, and the question has been what those genes build.

The organism in *Nature*, 12 January 2023 is one of them. Thiago Rodrigues-Oliveira, Florian Wollweber, Christa Schleper and colleagues established a culture of Candidatus Lokiarchaeum ossiferum, a member of the Asgard phylum, and imaged its cells by electron microscopy.

The images show protrusions. The cell is not a simple rod or coccus; it extends membrane-bound processes, and the processes contain filaments. The filaments are actin-like, and the paper's labelling identifies the archaeal actin among them.

The cytoskeleton is the finding that connects the genes to a structure. An actin homologue that assembles into filaments inside a cell, and that shapes its protrusions, is the ancestral version of the machinery that eukaryotes use for cell shape, division and motility.

The culture is the technical achievement. Asgard archaea grow slowly and at low abundance, and the organism was enriched from marine sediment and maintained with a partner or with defined substrates. A two-week doubling time is a constraint on every experiment that follows.

The interpretation is about the path to eukaryotes. A host that already had actin filaments and membrane protrusions would have been primed for the engulfment that produced mitochondria, and the paper's cell is a plausible ancestor rather than a proven one.

The caution is the cultivation. An organism grown in enrichment may express genes that it would not express in its sediment, and the structures seen in one culture are not necessarily the structures of the wild cell.

What the paper does not establish is the mechanism of protrusion formation. An actin filament that pushes a membrane requires nucleation, elongation and a coupling to the bilayer, and the archaeal versions of those proteins are not all identified in the genome.

The next experiments are genetic. A culture that can be manipulated would let the actin gene be deleted, and the protrusions' disappearance would be the test that the filament is the cause. Such tools are being developed for Asgard archaea, and they are not yet standard.

For the origin-of-eukaryotes field, the implication is that complexity is not a eukaryotic invention. An archaeon with a cytoskeleton and internal membranes is a cell that had already crossed a threshold, and the eukaryotic innovations built on it.

The membrane itself is evidence of another capacity. A cell that extends protrusions has to bend its membrane and keep it curved, and the archaeal lipids differ from bacterial ones, so the mechanics of that bending is a question the paper's images raise without answering.

Comparison with eukaryotic actin is the test of ancestry. The archaeal filaments are thinner and their assembly is less well understood, and the sequence similarity is enough to identify the family but not enough to predict the behaviour. The paper's images supply the behaviour.
For the debate about the eukaryotic host, a cytoskeleton is the argument in favour of a cell that could engulf. The counterargument, that phagocytosis requires more than actin, is not answered by the images, and the field will argue over which came first.
