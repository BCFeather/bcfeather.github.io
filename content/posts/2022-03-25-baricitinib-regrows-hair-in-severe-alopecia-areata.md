Alopecia areata is an autoimmune attack on the hair follicle that can strip the scalp, the eyebrows and the eyelashes, and it has had no approved treatment. Patients have been offered corticosteroids, contact sensitizers and reassurance, and the disease has been treated as a cosmetic problem by a system that funds drugs for diseases with mortality. The disease also carries a psychiatric burden that is proportional to the visible loss, and a treatment that regrows hair is a treatment for that burden as well.

The immunology pointed to the Janus kinase pathway, because the cytokines that drive the attack signal through JAK1 and JAK2, and small series of patients on JAK inhibitors for other conditions regrew hair. Two phase 3 trials tested baricitinib, an oral inhibitor of both kinases, in adults with a Severity of Alopecia Tool score of 50 or more. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 26 March 2022.

BRAVE-AA1 enrolled 654 patients and BRAVE-AA2 enrolled 546, randomized in a 3:2:2 ratio to 4 milligrams, 2 milligrams or placebo. The primary outcome was a score of 20 or less at week 36, meaning that 80 per cent of the scalp had recovered. The endpoint is a threshold rather than a continuous measure, and a patient who moves from a score of 90 to 25 has regrown most of their hair without meeting it, which is why the response rate understates the benefit.

In BRAVE-AA1 the estimated proportion reaching that threshold was 38.8 per cent with 4 milligrams, 22.8 per cent with 2 milligrams and 6.2 per cent with placebo, and in BRAVE-AA2 the figures were 35.9, 19.4 and 3.3 per cent. The difference between the 4-milligram dose and placebo was 32.6 percentage points in the first trial.

A response rate below 40 per cent means that most patients did not regrow enough hair to meet the endpoint, and the trial does not report which baseline features predicted response. Patients with long-standing disease and those with a shorter history were pooled, and the duration of the current episode is the variable that clinicians use to set expectations. Long-standing disease is often resistant because the follicle has entered a prolonged resting phase, and the trials enrolled both recent and chronic cases without stratifying the analysis by duration.

Safety is the constraint on a drug given to people who are not otherwise ill. Baricitinib carries warnings about serious infection, thrombosis and malignancy, and the trials were too short to measure the risks that matter over the years a patient would take it.

The result changes the conversation for a patient with severe disease and no options, and it does not make the drug a first-line treatment. Dermatologists will need a way to select patients, a monitoring protocol, and a view on whether the benefit persists after treatment stops. A patient who responds must stay on the drug to keep the hair, and the cost of lifelong treatment for a condition that is not life-threatening is the question that payers will ask.
