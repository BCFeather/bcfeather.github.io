Belém's first week carried the negotiations through their opening rounds, and by the end of it the shape of the gathering had become readable. Technical sessions went through the draft texts paragraph by paragraph, ministers holding the finance portfolios met in smaller rooms, and delegations that had arrived with different mandates began to test where the others would move. First weeks rarely decide anything on their own; what they do is set the terms that the second week will have to settle.

Work on the texts is where the summit's substance sits. Officials and experts tightened language, adjusted mechanisms, and turned the drafts prepared over the year into something close to their final form. None of it makes a headline, and all of it fixes what the closing days can agree to. A text that still carries an open bracket when the ministers return is a text that has already failed, however warmly the week was described in the corridors.

Finance is the question the gathering keeps returning to, and the week did not move it far. Ministers met, positions were exchanged, and the distance between what has been promised and what has been paid out was measured in the language of the sessions rather than in the closing statements. The gap is not a technicality of accounting. It decides whether the texts the officials have been polishing are a set of commitments or a set of intentions, and every delegation in the room knows which of the two its capital will accept.

Coalitions moved through the week with the energy that a deadline produces. The vulnerable countries organized early, issuing statements and coordinating positions so that their numbers would be heard as one voice rather than as forty. The larger economies responded with the calibrations that their own politics require, offering procedure where money was asked for, and money where procedure was asked for. That choreography is the negotiation itself, and it will decide the shape of the final document far more than any single drafting session.

The room is also shaped by who is not in it. The largest economy has stayed away from the table this year, and the institutions that carry the finance have to work out how much they can promise without its contribution. Institutional capacity is being tested in the same week as the texts, and the test is quieter than the plenary speeches but harder to pass.

Second week will conduct the argument in its final form. Ministers will confront the finance in the shape the officials leave them, the texts will be completed or bracketed, and the presidency will have to judge what can be closed and what must be carried forward. Belém has now settled the negotiations into their course, and the direction of that course is the story the summit currently tells: the technical work is largely done, the finance conversation is still running, and the closing days will decide whether the product matches the drama of the venue and the year of preparation behind it.
