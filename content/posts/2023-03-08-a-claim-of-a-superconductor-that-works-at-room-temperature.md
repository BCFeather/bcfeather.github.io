Nature published the paper on 8 March, describing a compound of lutetium, hydrogen and nitrogen. The authors said it conducted electricity without resistance at 21 degrees Celsius and at a pressure of about 10,000 atmospheres. That combination, if it held, would be the first material to superconduct at room temperature without extreme cold. The compound was named reddmatter by the authors, and other groups referred to it by its chemical formula.

Superconductors carry current with no loss, which is why they are used in hospital scanners and in some power cables. All known materials need either very low temperatures or very high pressures, and most need both. A room-temperature version would change transmission, motors and magnets, and the search for one has produced a claim roughly every two years since 1986. The first superconductor was found in 1911, and the theory that explains the effect was written in 1957.

Pressure needed here is still high, about 10,000 times atmospheric, which is a tenth of what the copper-based superconductors of the 1990s required. The sample was a few micrograms, and it was made by heating the elements in a diamond anvil cell. The authors reported a sharp drop in resistance and a change in heat capacity at the same temperature, which are the two signatures the field looks for. The diamond anvil cell is a device about the size of a coin that squeezes samples between two gems.

Replication began within days. Two groups said they had made the material and were testing it, and one reported partial success in April. Others said the synthesis was difficult and the sample too small to measure reliably. The lead author published the raw data in response to questions, which is unusual in the field. Replication usually takes weeks, and the groups that tried said the lutetium was the expensive part. The claim followed several retractions by other groups in the previous two years, including one in Nature in 2020 and one in Physical Review Letters. That history made the community cautious, and the journal said the paper had been through the standard review. Critics noted that the resistance drop could be explained by a structural change rather than by superconductivity. The 2020 retraction involved a carbon-sulfur compound that was later found not to superconduct.

If it holds, the applications are still years away, because a material that needs 10,000 atmospheres cannot be used in a cable. The value of the result would be in what it says about the mechanism, and theorists spent the spring arguing about whether the hydrogen lattice could support the effect at lower pressure. Several said the next step was to find a compound that worked at one atmosphere. A material working at one atmosphere would still need to be made in lengths rather than micrograms.

By the end of the year two replication attempts had produced partial results, and the question of whether the effect was real remained open. The authors said they would publish a second paper on the mechanism, and the group's funding came from a national laboratory and a university. Work on similar compounds continued in a dozen laboratories. The field has produced about 20 claims of high-temperature superconductivity since 1986, and most were withdrawn.
