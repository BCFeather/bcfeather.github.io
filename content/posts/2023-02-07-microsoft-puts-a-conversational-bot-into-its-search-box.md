Microsoft held a short event at its campus on 7 February and showed a search engine with a chat panel beside the results. The bot answers questions, cites the pages it used and offers follow-up prompts, which is a different arrangement from the list of links the product was built around. The company described it as a copilot for the web rather than as a replacement for the search box. The demonstration was recorded rather than live, which the company said was because the models are non-deterministic. The panel sits on the right of the page, and the answers can be exported to a document with citations attached.

Underneath the product sit models from OpenAI, which had signed a new investment agreement two weeks earlier. Microsoft said the models had been trained on its own search index, and that the index is what makes the answers current. The event was held on a Tuesday and lasted forty minutes, and it was scheduled at short notice, which was read as a response to a competitor's announcement. The index has about 1.5 trillion documents, and the models were given access to a slice of it during training.

A waiting list opened within a week, and a million people had been let in by the end of the month. Reviewers found the chat useful for research questions and unreliable for simple navigation, where the old list of links is faster. Some early users reported the bot inventing sources, which the company said it was working to reduce. The waiting list reached 3 million by the middle of March, and access was widened to all users in May. The company limited answers to 15 turns per conversation, which it said prevented the bot drifting.

Advertising did not disappear, and that was the commercial question. The company said sponsored links would remain beside the answers, and that it was testing formats that put a single ad inside the chat. Search advertising was worth 12 billion dollars a year to the company, against 68 billion for the market leader. The formats being tested put a link and a short answer inside the chat, and the pricing was not disclosed.

Rivals felt the pressure at once, and the company that had dominated search for two decades moved within weeks. That company announced its own assistant upgrades the same week and rushed a model into testing, which went badly and was corrected later. Regulators in Europe asked both firms how the answers would be ranked and labelled. The rival's demonstration showed an incorrect figure in a promotional video, and the company corrected it within a day. The competition authority in Britain asked for details of the arrangement in March.

What the episode showed was how quickly a research result can become a product. The models were public in 2022, and by February they were inside a browser. The company's search share rose by less than a point in the quarter, which suggests habits are the real barrier rather than technology. Habits are hard to change, and the company's own research showed most people tried the bot twice and returned to the box.
