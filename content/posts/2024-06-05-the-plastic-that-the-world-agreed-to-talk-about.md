Environment day this June goes to plastic, and the treaty meant to control it is stuck. A committee has met four times since 2022 to draft an agreement. Delegations agree about the problem and disagree about the scope. One word carries the disagreement. The problem is measurable. Several hundred million tonnes of plastic are made each year and less than a tenth of it is recycled. What remains is burned, buried or lost. A small number of rivers, mostly in Asia, carry the lost part to the ocean, which is why a cleanup on one coastline changes very little.

Two camps sit across the table. One group wants an agreement that limits the production of virgin polymer under a global rule. Another wants an agreement that improves waste systems and leaves production to each country. Importers of waste sit in the first group. Makers of polymer sit in the second. Recycling is the claim both camps make. A bottle that is collected is sorted, washed and turned into a pellet. The pellet is worth less than virgin material for most uses. That gap explains why the collection rate has climbed while the recycling rate has stayed low. The mechanical process also shortens the polymer chain each time the material is melted, so a bottle can go round a few times before it has to be downcycled into a bench or a bin liner.

Substitutes are a market of their own. A fibre from a plant, a film grown by a bacterium, a cup made of paper with a lining. Each has a cost that a buyer compares with the plastic it would replace. Plastic usually wins the comparison on price.

Chinese policy has moved on a separate track. A ban on thin bags in supermarkets. A deposit scheme for bottles in some cities. A rule requiring a share of recycled content in packaging. These are national measures carried out by municipalities, which is why the results differ from one city to the next. The science has moved faster than the drafting. Researchers now find polymer fragments in soil, in rain and in the blood of large animals, at concentrations that the toxicology cannot yet interpret. A negotiator asked to limit production is therefore asked to act on evidence that is incomplete. The delegates have agreed to meet again before the year ends, in a city that has a port and a plastics industry of its own.

An institutional history sits behind the date. It was set by a conference in 1972, and the agencies have used it ever since to publish a report. This year the report is about the treaty. The treaty is unfinished.

A shared problem turning into a negotiation about cost is what plastic shows. Every delegation accepts that the ocean is filling. No delegation wants to accept the bill for the polymer. The talks will continue. Waste will keep arriving. That distance measures the difficulty, and it has been the same distance for three years. Behind the negotiation sits a smaller question, of who pays for the collection that no treaty can order. Nobody has answered it, and the answer will decide whether the agreement works.
