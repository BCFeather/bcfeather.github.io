description: "In a randomized trial of 846 children with uncomplicated appendicitis, antibiotics failed in 34 per cent against 7 per cent after appendicectomy, a gap well beyond the margin the trial set for non-inferiority."
---

Appendicectomy has been the treatment for acute appendicitis for more than a century, and the operation is one of the most common in paediatric surgery. Trials in adults have shown that antibiotics alone can work in a substantial share of cases, and the finding has changed practice in some countries, where a patient with uncomplicated appendicitis may be offered the choice.

Children were the group for which the question stayed open. The appendix behaves differently in a child, the diagnosis is harder, and a missed perforation carries a cost that a trial in adults does not measure. A trial published in *The Lancet* on 18 January enrolled 846 children at centres in several countries and randomized them to antibiotics or to appendicectomy, with an open-label design because the treatments cannot be blinded.

The primary outcome was treatment failure within a defined follow-up period, and failure in the antibiotic group included appendicitis that recurred, that progressed to perforation or that required an operation. The rates were 34 per cent with antibiotics, or 153 of 452 children, and 7 per cent with surgery, or 28 of 394. The difference was 26.7 percentage points, with a 90 per cent confidence interval from 22.4 to 30.9.

That interval sits far outside the 20 per cent margin the investigators had set for non-inferiority, which means the trial did not merely fail to show equivalence, it showed a clear advantage for the operation on the endpoint it was designed to test. There were no deaths and no serious adverse events in either group, so the comparison is about failure and recurrence and not about mortality.

What antibiotics offer is the avoidance of an operation. A child who is treated successfully spends no night in a theatre, has no wound and returns to school sooner, and the trial's secondary analyses looked at days away from normal activity, complications of surgery and the cost of each pathway. Those benefits are real, and the question is whether a one-in-three chance of failure is a price a family will accept.

The clinical reading is that the choice belongs to a conversation. A surgeon who offers antibiotics to a child with uncomplicated appendicitis has to explain that a third of children will need an operation within the follow-up period, that the need may arrive as an emergency rather than as a scheduled procedure, and that the follow-up has to be reliable enough to catch it.

What the trial cannot settle is the long view. Appendicitis that is treated with antibiotics leaves an appendix in place, and the risk of a later episode extends beyond the follow-up window that a trial can afford. The recurrence rates reported at two and five years in adult cohorts have been the argument against antibiotics in adults, and the paediatric data will accumulate the same way, on a schedule that no trial can hurry.
