Multicellularity is common among eukaryotes and rare among archaea, where a single transition had been described and nothing like a tissue had been seen outside a biofilm. Biofilms are collectives held together by secreted glue, and their cells keep their own shapes and their own fates. A tissue is a different object: cells that change shape, divide their labour and build junctions with one another. Whether archaea could cross that line, and what would push them across it, had no experimental answer until this spring.

Work from groups at Brandeis University, the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology and the Max Planck Institute for Biology in Tübingen, published in *Science* on 4 April 2025, put a haloarchaeon under a press. Rados and colleagues grew *Haloferax volcanii* beneath agar pads and squeezed the cells along a single axis. They followed the colony with light microscopy and then widened the survey, imaging compressed cells from 52 species drawn from 14 genera.

Compression did most of the work. Cells stopped separating after division and stacked into a sheet, first one cell deep and then several, and 61.6 per cent of the tested species produced these tissues, with at least one instance in every genus examined. The sheet passed through a multinucleate stage, several nuclei sharing one cytoplasm, and then split into separate cells again. That second step ran without tubulin and began once a cell reached a critical size, with membrane tension deciding where the new walls would land.

What lifts the result above a curiosity is the geometry of the finished tissue. Its junctions stiffened until their elasticity matched that of animal epithelia, and the sheet organised itself into two cell types, a peripheral ring and a central population of scutoid-shaped cells, the same geometry that packs curved surfaces in animal organs. Actin and the pattern of protein glycosylation acquired a polarity, pointing outwards at the rim and inwards at the centre, which is the kind of asymmetry a tissue needs before it can do anything directional.

The caveats are the ones a compression experiment invites. The trigger is a laboratory pad, not a condition anyone has shown these microbes meet in a salt lake, a crust or a sediment, so the natural history of the behaviour is unknown. The genomic basis is untouched as well: nobody has found the switch that reads pressure and starts the programme, and without it the comparison with animal epithelia rests on appearance and on physics rather than on shared ancestry.

The wider frame is convergent evolution. Eukaryotes managed the transition to multicellularity dozens of times, by one count 45 independent events, and bacteria have done it too, though rarely. Archaea had one known case, and it looked like the shallow end. A second route, opened by a physical cue and closed by a programme that rebuilds junctions, suggests the barrier was never genetic machinery but the right trigger, which any cell with a membrane and a division cycle might answer.

What would settle the question is a natural test. Squeezing cells inside a salt crust, tracking tissue formation in a brine pool across a season, or finding the pressure-sensing protein and showing that deleting it removes the behaviour would each move the work from a demonstration to a mechanism. Until then it stands as a proof of principle with an unusually clean phenotype: pressure in, tissue out, and the same physics that animal cells use to hold themselves together.
