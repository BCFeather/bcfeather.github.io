A market value of four trillion dollars was reached on 9 July, in intraday trading, and the company that reached it sells the shovels of the artificial intelligence boom rather than any of its products. NVIDIA closed the session just below the mark and crossed it again the following day, and the figure is roughly the annual output of Japan.

The climb is the story. The company was worth about 300 billion dollars at the start of 2023, became the most valuable semiconductor firm in 2024, and passed Apple and Microsoft this year on the strength of data centre revenue that grew more than seventy per cent in the last four quarters, which is a rate that no large company sustains for long. A valuation that doubles twice in three years is a valuation that has priced a decade of growth into three years of earnings, and the company's own guidance has been the only thing that kept the multiple from rising faster than the price.

Concentration is the uncomfortable part. Four customers account for a large share of revenue, hyperscalers are the buyers, and the company's own guidance depends on their capital budgets, so a four trillion dollar valuation is a valuation of four or five balance sheets and the assumption that they will keep spending.

Competition is arriving slowly and from several directions. Custom chips designed in house by the hyperscalers, accelerators from AMD and from a crop of start-ups, and export controls that limit what can be sold in China, which the company has said will cost it about 8 billion dollars of revenue in the current quarter. An in-house chip is cheaper per unit and more expensive per engineer, and the hyperscalers have hired enough engineers this year that the second number has started to fall.

Margins are the part that the market debates. Gross margin has run above 70 per cent on an adjusted basis, which is what a monopoly on a scarce input looks like, and every customer with a fab and a design team has an incentive to break it, so the valuation embeds a bet on how long the scarcity lasts.

The broader market has followed. Semiconductor indices, power equipment makers, cooling suppliers and grid companies have all re-rated on the same story, and the equal-weighted index has lagged, which is the signature of a market where the average stock is not invited to the party. An equal-weighted index that lags by twenty points is an index in which the median company is having a normal year while the market reports an extraordinary one, and the gap is the measure of how much of the gain belongs to five names.

What would settle the year is the capital spending guidance that the hyperscalers give in the autumn. A raise would make four trillion a way station, a hold would make it a ceiling, and the difference between those two outcomes is the difference between a boom and a bubble that has not yet been called one.
