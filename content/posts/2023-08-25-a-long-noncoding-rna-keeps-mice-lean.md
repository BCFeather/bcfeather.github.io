Obesity is a disorder of energy balance, and the genes setting that balance are a small set. A noncoding RNA regulating them is an unexpected kind of regulator. In *Science* in August 2023, one is identified. The authors find a long noncoding RNA expressed in adipose tissue, and its loss makes mice gain weight while its overexpression keeps them lean.

Mechanism turns out transcriptional. The RNA binds a protein that would otherwise activate a set of lipogenic genes, and that binding holds the genes in check; the partner's identity is the mechanism's core, since an RNA sequestering a transcription factor acts as a decoy.

Tissue specificity is the appeal. An RNA acting in fat and not in liver is a target with a narrow effect, and a drug mimicking it would have fewer systemic consequences. Metabolism is measurable here. Calorimetry and body composition show the lean mice eat the same and expend more, which places the effect in energy expenditure; a metabolic cage measures oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production, and the ratio names the substrate being burnt.

Depot matters as well. Visceral and subcutaneous fat behave differently, and measuring each is what localizes the effect, while the cellular context is the adipose stem cell, because a depot grows both by expanding adipocytes and by recruiting new ones.

Human relevance stays open. A homologous RNA would be a target, and conservation between mouse and human is the evidence that its function carries over. Delivery is the obstacle. An RNA drug must reach the tissue, and adipose-targeted delivery is a problem the field has not solved; a chemically modified RNA reaches the liver easily, and reaching fat needs a carrier still under development.

Model sets the limitation, because mouse adipose biology differs from a person's and a result in one is a hypothesis in the other. The RNA's folded domains are unresolved, since a long RNA folds into domains and the domain binding the protein is what a drug design would need.

Metabolism research gains a regulator outside the protein-coding genome, since targets have been enzymes and receptors and an RNA adds a class. Leptin offers the frame, because a hormone suppressing appetite is one route to leanness and an RNA raising expenditure is another. A tissue-specific knockout would show whether the RNA acts only in fat or also in the liver, and the answer decides whether the target is narrow enough to be worth a drug programme. An antisense oligonucleotide delivered to adipose tissue would be the first therapeutic test, and its chemistry rather than its sequence is where such a programme would most likely fail. A measure of the RNA's own turnover would show whether it sets a steady level of repression or responds to the animal's feeding state, and a regulator that answers to diet is a more interesting target than one that does not. A measurement of the RNA in human fat biopsies would show whether the homologue is expressed at a level a drug could modulate.
