Underwater adhesion is a hard problem with an old solution. Mussels hold to wet rock with filaments that resist both salt and shear, and the proteins behind that grip have been studied for decades. What has been missing is a way to search the space of possible protein-inspired adhesives deliberately, instead of making one candidate at a time and testing it in a tank. The search space is enormous, because a hydrogel's behaviour depends on the ratio of water to polymer, on the charge of the chains, and on how the network is cross-linked, and small changes in any of those shift the result from a stiff film to something closer to jelly.

A paper on the cover of *Nature* on 7 August describes such a search. The team, led by Jian Ping Gong and Hailong Fan at Hokkaido University with Hu Yang of the Central University of Finance and Economics, began from the protein database and built a three-part strategy: mine the existing sequences for the motifs that matter, design biomimetic candidates around them, and let a machine-learning model rank the results before any material was made.

Every candidate was a hydrogel: a soft polymer network with a high water content. That water is why such gels interest medical and marine engineers, and why they are difficult to stick to anything. The model pointed to compositions that had not been tried, and the laboratory made them. The best of them reached adhesion strengths of the megapascal order under water, a figure that the authors describe as a record for this class of material. Megapascal adhesion is roughly the strength of a structural adhesive, and reaching it in a wet environment is the part that previous formulations could not manage for long.

Two things follow from that number. The first is practical: a glue that works while submerged is useful for wound closure, for coatings that must survive washing, and for sensors that sit in wet environments for years. The second is methodological. Materials discovery has usually been described as an expensive search guided by intuition; here the intuition was replaced by a data set and a surrogate model, with the laboratory used to confirm rather than to explore.

The claim the paper makes is not that machines now design materials on their own. The training data came from proteins that living things already use, the design rules came from physical chemistry, and the ranking was tested against experiments that took months. What the model did was narrow a very large space to a small number of plausible recipes, which is exactly where the cost of discovery usually sits.

For the field, the interesting question is what happens next. If the approach generalizes, the same loop could be pointed at other soft materials: membranes for filtration, gels for drug delivery, coatings that must hold under salt water. The record adhesion figure will be beaten, probably within a year. The design loop is the part worth watching. If it holds up outside the laboratory, the cost of finding a new material stops being dominated by the number of experiments and starts being dominated by the quality of the data set.
