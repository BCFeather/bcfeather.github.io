A device inside the body needs a way to report its position and its state, and wires are impractical. A radio link works through tissue, and a passive resonator responding to an external field avoids a battery altogether. In *Science* on 2 June 2023, such devices are described. The authors build magneto-mechanical resonators small enough to implant, and read their resonance with an external coil.

Coupling a mechanical resonance to a magnetic one is the physics to a magnetic one. A magnetostrictive element changes shape in a field, and that shape change shifts the resonance in a way the external coil detects. Size is the achievement, since a resonator's frequency rises as it shrinks, and a small high-frequency device can be read with a small antenna.

Tracking is the first application. A resonator's position is inferred from the field strength at the coil, and several resonators with different frequencies can be distinguished at once. Multiplexing is the practical advantage, because devices with separated frequencies read through one coil, and a set of sensors in one organ becomes a map rather than a single reading.

For sensing, the second application, a resonator whose frequency depends on a local quantity such as pressure or a chemical's presence is a sensor without electronics. Stability of that frequency is the design's core, since a resonator drifting with temperature reports a change that is not the quantity of interest, and material choice limits the drift.

Fidelity of the readout is the test. A signal through several centimetres of tissue is attenuated and distorted, and the measured signal-to-noise ratio defines the range. Spatial resolution follows the coil's geometry, because a large coil reads a device anywhere inside it while a small one localizes, and the trade-off is the same as in any magnetic measurement. Biocompatibility is the caveat, as a magnetostrictive material in tissue must be encapsulated, and an encapsulant that stiffens the resonator changes its frequency. Orientation sets another limitation, because a resonator's response depends on alignment with the field, and a device that tumbles reports a varying signal.

A chronic implant is what the paper does not show, since a device working for hours in a phantom has not faced a year in a living animal, and encapsulation durability is the test. Medical engineering gains a class of device needing no power, because a passive sensor can be left in place and the absence of a battery removes the failure mode dominating implanted electronics.

Radio-frequency identification offers the comparison, since a tag needs a chip and a rectifier while a resonator needs neither, and the second is simpler where the first is more capable. The clinical route is long regardless, because a device that must be sterile, biocompatible and MRI-safe faces requirements a laboratory demonstration never addresses. A phantom with a moving device would show whether tracking follows a path or merely locates a point, and a trajectory is what a clinician following a capsule actually needs.
