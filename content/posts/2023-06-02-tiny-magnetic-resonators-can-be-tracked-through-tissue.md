A device inside the body needs a way to report its position and its state, and wires are impractical. A radio link works through tissue, and a passive resonator that responds to an external field avoids a battery.

The devices in *Science*, 2 June 2023 are that kind. The authors build magneto-mechanical resonators small enough to implant, and read their resonance with an external coil.

The physics is a mechanical resonance coupled to a magnetic one. A magnetostrictive element changes shape in a field, and the shape change shifts the resonance in a way that the external coil detects.

The size is the achievement. A resonator's frequency rises as it shrinks, and a small device with a high frequency is one that can be read with a small antenna.

The tracking is the first application. A resonator's position is inferred from the field's strength at the coil, and several resonators with different frequencies can be distinguished at once.

The sensing is the second application. A resonator whose frequency depends on a local quantity, such as pressure or a chemical's presence, is a sensor without electronics.

The readout's fidelity is the test. A signal through several centimetres of tissue is attenuated and distorted, and the authors' measurement of the signal-to-noise ratio defines the range.

The biocompatibility is the caveat. A magnetostrictive material in tissue must be encapsulated, and an encapsulant that stiffens the resonator changes its frequency.

The limitation is the orientation. A resonator's response depends on its alignment with the field, and a device that tumbles reports a varying signal.

What the paper does not show is a chronic implant. A device that works for hours in a phantom is a device that has not yet faced a year in a living animal, and the encapsulation's durability is the test.

For medical engineering, the value is a class of device that needs no power. A passive sensor is one that can be left in place, and the absence of a battery removes the failure mode that dominates implanted electronics.

The frequency's stability is the design's core. A resonator whose frequency drifts with temperature reports a change that is not the quantity of interest, and the authors' choice of material is what limits the drift.

The multiplexing is the practical advantage. Several devices with separated frequencies can be read by one coil, and a set of sensors in one organ becomes a map rather than a single reading.

The comparison with radio-frequency identification is instructive. An RFID tag needs a chip and a rectifier, and a resonator needs neither. The second is simpler and the first is more capable.

The spatial resolution is set by the coil's geometry. A large coil reads a device anywhere inside it, and a small one localizes. The trade-off is the same as in any magnetic measurement.

The clinical route is long. A device that must be sterile, biocompatible and MRI-safe faces requirements that a laboratory demonstration does not address.
