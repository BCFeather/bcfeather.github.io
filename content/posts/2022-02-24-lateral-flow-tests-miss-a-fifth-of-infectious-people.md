description: "Linking Innova test results to viral culture and contact tracing put the share of infectious people missed at 20 per cent in a symptomatic testing service and at 81 per cent in university screening."
---

Rapid antigen tests became the backbone of school and workplace testing because they are cheap, fast and usable without a laboratory. Their sensitivity against any infection is lower than that of PCR, and the argument for using them rested on a claim that people with low viral loads are not infectious anyway.

That claim needed an empirical test and not on a modelling assumption. A linked data analysis combined the accuracy of the Innova test in 70 people with confirmed infection, viral culture results from 246 samples, secondary cases among 2,474,066 contacts, and the distribution of cycle thresholds from 231,497 index patients. The analysis appeared in *The BMJ*, 23 February 2022. Culture is the closest available proxy for the ability to infect someone, and the analysis treats a positive culture as the marker of a person who could transmit.

Among people who were culture positive, the test missed 20 per cent of those attending an NHS Test-and-Trace centre with symptoms, 29 per cent of people without symptoms attending municipal mass testing in Liverpool and 81 per cent of students without symptoms at a university screening programme. As sources of secondary cases the corresponding figures were 38, 47 and 90 per cent.

Two widely cited mathematical models were compared against those numbers, and both overestimated sensitivity. One predicted that 8, 10 and 32 per cent would be missed across the three settings, and the assumptions of the second made it impossible to miss an infectious person at all. The second model assumed a sensitivity that was high enough to make missed infectious cases arithmetically impossible, which is a modelling choice rather than a measurement.

The difference between settings comes from the distribution of viral load. People who seek a test because they have symptoms are tested near the peak of their infection, while people who are screened weekly without symptoms are caught at every stage, including the early and late tails where antigen is scarce. Weekly screening also tests people who have already been infected for a day or two, when the antigen load is still below the limit of the strip.

Model inputs were scarce and heterogeneous, which the authors concede. Culture positivity is a proxy for infectiousness, not a measurement of it, contact tracing cannot attribute every secondary case to a known index, and the three settings are British, so the proportions will differ where testing behaviour differs. The Liverpool and Birmingham cohorts were small, at 70 and 62 people, and the Birmingham figure was partly imputed, so the setting-specific numbers carry wide uncertainty.

The policy conclusion cuts against the idea that a negative lateral flow result is a licence. A negative result lowers the probability of being infectious and does not remove it, and the residual risk is largest exactly where screening is used most, among people without symptoms. A symptomatic person who tests negative and stays home is behaving reasonably, while a student who tests negative and attends a lecture hall is relying on a test that misses most of the silent cases.
