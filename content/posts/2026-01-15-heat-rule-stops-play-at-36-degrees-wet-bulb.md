Heat policies in sport were written in air temperature and now use wet-bulb globe temperature, a composite that accounts for humidity, radiation and wind. A tournament applying a 32.2 degree wet-bulb threshold suspended play for three hours this month, and the decision was correct on a day when the air temperature read 34 and the humidity was 70 per cent.

The physics explains why the measures diverge. Human cooling depends on evaporation, and evaporation depends on how much water vapour the air can still accept. At 34 degrees and 70 per cent humidity, sweat evaporates slowly and core temperature rises even at rest, which is why a wet-bulb reading above 32 is dangerous in a way the air temperature does not convey.

Rule's structure is tiered, and the tiering is the practical design. Play proceeds with scheduled breaks above 30.1 wet-bulb, medical timeouts are extended above 32.2, and play is suspended above 34.6. The tiers allow a tournament to continue in conditions that would otherwise cancel a day's schedule.

Player acceptance came from the measurement rather than the rule. The tour introduced on-court sensors that report wet-bulb in the shade and in the sun separately, and players can see the number their own court is reading. A rule that a player can verify is one that a player will accept.

Implementation problems remain. A retractable roof changes the reading inside the arena, and the rule specifies the measurement point as 1.2 metres above the court in the shade, which is not where the roof's shadow falls. Two suspensions last season were triggered at a court whose sensor sat in sun.

Scheduling is the structural fix the rule cannot provide. A tournament played in a subtropical summer will hit the threshold several times regardless, and the long-term response is a shift in the calendar rather than a change in the threshold. Two events have moved their dates, and both lost revenue in the short term.

Physiology sets the ethical frame. Exertional heat stroke is the leading cause of death in sport, and the risk rises sharply above a wet-bulb of 32 because sweat no longer cools the body effectively. A rule that stops play at the point where cooling fails is not cautious; it is the boundary of the possible.

Acclimatization is the variable the rule treats as a fixed quantity. A player who has trained in heat for two weeks sweats more and loses less salt, and the physiological strain at a given wet-bulb falls by about 15 per cent. The rule does not distinguish between an acclimatized and an unacclimatized player, and the tour's medical staff have proposed a two-tier threshold.

Cooling breaks are the second instrument. Ice towels, cold drinks and shade at changeovers reduce core temperature by 0.4 degrees over ten minutes, which is enough to keep a player below the danger line for another set. The rule specifies the breaks and not the method.

For a reader, the transferable point is that the metric matters more than the number. Sport's older rules used air temperature and were triggered on dry days that were not dangerous, and missed humid days that were. The change to wet-bulb is the substantive reform, and it has been adopted faster in tennis than in most outdoor sports.
