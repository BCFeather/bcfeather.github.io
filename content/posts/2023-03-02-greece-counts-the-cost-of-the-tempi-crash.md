description: "Two days after the train collision near Tempi, the Greek government faced strikes and protests over a railway that had been left with manual signalling, and the transport minister resigned."
---

Strikes stopped the trains on 2 March, which was the day after the transport minister resigned. The minister, Kostas Karamanlis, said he was leaving because the state had failed the people who died, and the prime minister accepted the resignation within an hour. Railway workers had already announced a walkout, and the hospitals joined it, and the two of them together closed the country's public transport for a day. He had held the job for four years, and the line through Tempi was one of the stretches he had promised to modernize.

The accusations were specific rather than general. The union that represents the drivers said the automatic signalling on the line had been out of service for years and that the section where the crash happened was controlled by a switch that one person had to set. The stationmaster at Larissa, who was on duty alone, was charged with negligent homicide and with disrupting transport, and his lawyer said he had been made responsible for a system that had been failing before he arrived. The charges were read out on the day of his arrest, and his lawyer asked for an adjournment that was granted.

Alongside the staffing, the contracts were the second accusation. The line had been rebuilt with money from the European Union, and the signalling had been part of the project, and the completion date had slipped from 2020 to 2023 while the cost rose. The state auditor had flagged the delay in a report in 2019, and the report had been filed without a response, which the opposition in parliament read aloud during the debate that followed the crash.

On the streets the public response was measured in the size of the crowds. Protests in Athens and in Thessaloniki drew tens of thousands of people, and the banners carried the first names of the dead rather than a political slogan. The police used tear gas in Syntagma Square on the second night, and the images of that were broadcast across Europe, which turned a domestic accident into a question about the state's capacity.

In politics the consequence arrived faster than the inquiry did. The prime minister had been expected to call an election in the spring, and he brought it forward to 21 May, and he framed the campaign as a referendum on whether the state could be trusted to run anything. The opposition, which had been behind in the polls for a year, found a subject that it could run on without having to invent one.

The inquiry's findings took months, and the first ones described a cascade of small failures rather than a single mistake. A switch set wrongly, a supervisor who was alone, a signalling system that had been promised and postponed, and a train that was carrying more people than the platform could hold. None of those would have caused a crash on its own, and the combination of them did, which is how most infrastructure failures are built. The first findings also described a roster that the union had been complaining about since the previous summer.
