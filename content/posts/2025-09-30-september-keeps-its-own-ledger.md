Rain on the coast and a warm spell inland close the month, with the typhoon season still running and the heating season not yet begun. September is the month that measures itself in completions. The harvest is in across most of the north, the school term has stopped being new, and the shops have moved from mooncakes to the next festival's stock without a pause.

A ledger for the month would have three columns. Weather takes the first: six tropical cyclones in the western Pacific, three of which came ashore, and a super typhoon that crossed the Guangdong coast and then twice more on its way west. The second column belongs to the term, which began in heat and ends in the cool, with the first set of assessments behind it, the reading lists now shorter than they were in the first week, and the timetable settled into the shape it will keep until January. The third column is the ordinary one, in which nothing happened that will be remembered and everything that mattered took place.

Garden and street keep a separate account. Plane trees start dropping leaves in the last week, and the councils sweep them into piles that smell of dust and rain. Persimmons arrive in the market, then chestnuts, then the first mandarins from the south. The light in the afternoon has turned the particular yellow that photographers like, and the days are shortening at two minutes a day, which is fast enough to notice in a single week and slow enough to be forgotten by the next one.

Nowhere is the accounting more visible than in the inbox. September is when the year's postponed decisions come due, and the inbox is where the month does its most honest bookkeeping: the report that was going to be finished in July, the application with a deadline at the end of the month, the meeting that was moved twice and can no longer be moved. A month of thirty days has to hold the work of the summer as well as its own, and the last week of it is usually the week in which that arithmetic is done in public, in meetings that could have been held in July.

A week from full, the moon will empty the campuses and fill the roads when the holiday arrives. That is the shape of the year in this hemisphere: the month that ends the third quarter is also the month that begins the long weekend, and the two are close enough together that the last day of September feels like a door rather than a wall.

Tomorrow the calendar turns to a month with a holiday in its first week, and the ledger closes with the entries it has. The storms will be counted in damage and in rainfall; the term in credits and in pages; the ordinary days in nothing at all, which is how most days are counted and why they go unrecorded.
