---

Eurozone consumer prices rose 2.9 per cent in the year to October, the flash estimate said on 31 October, down from 4.3 per cent in September. The core measure fell to 4.2 per cent from 4.5. The figure was in line with the forecast, and the measure is now below 3 per cent for the first time since the summer of 2021. The month was the first since the summer of 2021 in which the measure was below 3 per cent, and the figure came a day before the bank's own forecast.

Energy did the trick. The prices fell by about 11 per cent over the year as the gas and the electricity settled below the peaks, and the base effects from last year's spike are now working in the other direction. A base that passes is a headline that falls without a recession. The gas prices are about 40 per cent below their level a year earlier, and the electricity is about a quarter lower, which the households will see in the winter bills.

Services are still the weight. The prices rose by about 4.6 per cent, and the restaurants and the holidays were the strongest as the wages fed through. A service price is a wage that arrives in the bill. The wages have been rising by about 5 per cent under the agreements, and the bank has said the services will not fall to the target until the pace slows.

Food is cooling. The food, alcohol and tobacco rose by about 7.4 per cent, down from 8.8, and the fresh vegetables fell as the harvest arrived. A grocery line that falls is the measure the shopper sees first. The food prices are still about twice the target, and the analysts expect the line to keep falling as the input costs of the farmers ease.

Output contracted. The economy shrank by 0.1 per cent in the third quarter, the first decline since the end of last year, and the industry and the construction fell while the services grew. A contraction that is mild is a recession that the statistics will confirm only with the next quarter. The largest economy contracted by about 0.1 per cent, and the surveys show the industry in a recession while the services hold near the stagnation line.

What the month shows is that the inflation has reached the level the bank wanted and the economy has stopped growing, and the two together are the argument for the first cut. A headline at 2.9 per cent and a shrinking economy is the combination the doves have been waiting for, and the waiting is nearly over. The next meeting is in December, and the traders have priced the first cut for the spring. A cut that arrives in the spring is a cut that the bank can present as the response to the data rather than to the politics.
