The Arctic Ocean appears to have reached its yearly minimum in the second week of September, and the provisional extent is close to the lowest in the forty-seven-year satellite record. The number that will be quoted is the area of ocean with at least fifteen per cent ice cover, and on that measure the year sits within the small cluster of extreme minima, a few hundred thousand square kilometres below the long-term average and within measurement noise of the 2012 record.

Thickness is the more telling number. Satellites that measure the height of the ice above the water line, and the freeboard that implies, show that much of the central basin is covered by first-year ice a metre or so thick rather than the multi-year floes that survived several summers. Thin ice melts faster in the next season, and it also moves faster, because it offers less resistance to wind. The result is a pack that looks, from above, roughly as extensive as it did a decade ago, and behaves quite differently when the weather turns.

Weather this year was not especially kind. A persistent high-pressure pattern over the Beaufort Sea in June and July pushed ice towards the Siberian coast and opened water early, and the clear skies that come with high pressure let more sunlight into the ocean. Those are the same conditions that produced the 2012 minimum, and the fact that the extent did not quite reach that record is a reminder that the minimum is a noisy quantity, sensitive to which way the wind blows in July as much as to the long-term trend.

The trend itself is not in doubt. The September minimum has been declining at roughly thirteen per cent per decade since the late 1970s, and the last eighteen years include all ten of the lowest minima. What has changed in the past few years is the character of the decline: the extent has flattened a little while the thickness has kept falling, which is what a pack looks like when it is being replaced rather than merely shrinking.

Regional and immediate are the consequences. Coastal communities in Alaska and Siberia face autumn storms that now run across open water instead of being damped by ice, and the waves that reach the shore are larger than the seawalls were built for. Wildlife that depends on the ice edge, seals and the bears that hunt them, is being pushed into a narrower band. Shipping has gained a longer season along the Northern Sea Route, which is an opportunity and a governance problem at the same time, because the rules for that water were written for a colder ocean.

For climate scientists, the interesting question is no longer whether the ice is disappearing but whether the feedbacks that come with its disappearance are strengthening. A darker ocean absorbs more heat in summer, which delays the autumn freeze-up, which shortens the season in which ice can grow thick. The provisional minimum announced this month is a data point in that argument. It is also, for anyone who lives on that coast, a description of the water they now see in September.
