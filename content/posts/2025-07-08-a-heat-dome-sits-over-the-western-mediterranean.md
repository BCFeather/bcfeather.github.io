A heat dome is a ridge of high pressure that stops the atmosphere from mixing, and the one that settled over the western Mediterranean in the first week of July pushed temperatures above 40 degrees Celsius in Andalusia, Extremadura and parts of Portugal for six consecutive days.

The mechanism is subsidence. Air descending inside a high pressure system compresses and warms, the sky clears, the soil dries, and the sun then heats a surface that has no water left to evaporate, which is how a dome converts a warm air mass into a record at ground level. A six-day event is long enough that the outdoor worker, the elderly resident and the person with a heart condition are all exposed at the same time, and short enough that a city can still act on the second day. A ridge that lasts six days is a ridge that survives several attempts by the jet stream to move it, and the persistence rather than the peak is what turns a hot week into a public health event.

Nights stayed hot, and that is the part that kills. A day above 40 degrees is uncomfortable, a night above 26 degrees is dangerous, because the body recovers at night and a city that never cools accumulates heat in people and in buildings over several days, which is why the third night of a dome is worse than the first and why the deaths cluster at the end of the event.

Sea surface temperatures made it worse. The Mediterranean was warmer than its seasonal average across a wide area, and a warm sea raises the humidity of the air that the dome holds down, so the same temperature becomes a wet-bulb reading that much closer to the threshold of survivability. A warm sea is also a reservoir of moisture that a later weather system can use, which is why a heatwave over water is sometimes the first chapter of a storm a week later.

Health systems counted as they always do. Emergency departments recorded more admissions for heat illness among the elderly and among outdoor workers, several regions opened cooling centres, and the mortality effect of a six-day event is usually estimated weeks later from excess deaths and not from certificates.

Attribution is a separate exercise. Studies of previous European heatwaves have found that a large share of the probability of an event at this temperature is attributable to the warming of the background climate, and the same method applied to this one will produce a ratio and not a verdict. A ratio of that kind is a statement about probability and not about cause, and the distinction matters to a council that has to decide whether to spend on shade or on a generator.

What would settle the policy question is whether the building stock changes faster than the frequency of the events. Shade, insulation, night ventilation and a public room with air conditioning are cheap relative to a hospital admission, and a city that installs them is a city that has decided the summer is a season to plan for.
