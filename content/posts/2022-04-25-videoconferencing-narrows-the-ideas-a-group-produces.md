description: "A laboratory study and a field experiment across five countries found that videoconferencing reduced creative idea generation but not idea selection, an effect traced to the narrower cognitive focus that a screen induces."
---

Remote work became a natural experiment in 2020, and the question of whether it is as creative as a room full of people has been argued from anecdote. The answer depends on which part of the creative process is being measured, because generating ideas and choosing between them are different tasks. A manager who has read the paper can act on the distinction without needing to know the neuroscience, which is the property that makes it useful outside a laboratory.

A laboratory study and a field experiment across five countries compared pairs working in person with pairs working by video, on tasks that required generating ideas and on tasks that required selecting one. The results appeared in *Nature*, 27 April 2022. The tasks were drawn from a standard battery of divergent thinking, and the scoring was automated for fluency and for semantic distance between ideas.

Videoconferencing reduced the number of creative ideas generated, and the effect was traced to the physical nature of the interaction rather than to the amount of information exchanged. Eye-gaze measures and recall tests showed that a screen directs attention to a narrow field, and latent semantic analysis showed that the ideas produced were less diverse. The laboratory study compared the same pairs in both conditions, which controls for the people, and the field experiment compared teams assigned to one condition, which controls for the setting.

Selection was different. Groups that met by video were not worse at choosing which idea to pursue, and there was preliminary evidence that they were slightly better, which suggests that the narrow focus helps with evaluation and hurts with generation.

A cognitive mechanism explains it: a person looking at a screen sees a face in a frame and not a room, and the peripheral information that a shared physical space provides is missing. That information is what allows a mind to wander towards an association that the conversation has not yet named. The interpretation is that a shared physical space provides a continuous stream of cues that a screen compresses into a face, and the loss of that stream is what narrows attention.

Its field experiment ran inside a real organization with real teams, which strengthens the claim, and the tasks were still artificial in the sense that the ideas were not implemented. A company that adopts the finding would hold brainstorming sessions in person and review sessions by video, which is a specific and testable policy.

This result does not say that remote work is worse, only that one part of it is. A team that meets in person once a month to generate and works remotely the rest of the time may capture most of the benefit, and the cost of the travel is the price of the ideas. A distributed team can also generate ideas asynchronously, where the absence of a screen removes the narrowing effect entirely, and that mode deserves the same experimental attention.
