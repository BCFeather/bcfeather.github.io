A soap film hanging in a wire frame drains as gravity pulls the liquid down, and the thinning is not smooth. The film goes through a series of abrupt jumps, each a few nanometres thick, and between the jumps it holds its thickness as if it had found a floor. The steps are visible as coloured bands that move down the film, and the colour is the interference of light in a layer whose thickness is a few hundred nanometres and whose index is close to that of water.

The structure behind the steps is a stack of micelles. The surfactant in the film assembles into spherical or cylindrical micelles above a certain concentration, and near a surface the micelles arrange themselves into layers. A layer is held in place by the pressure of the neighbouring layers, and the film thins only when a whole layer is expelled at once, which is why the thickness changes in discrete steps instead of continuously.

Disjoining pressure is what resists thinning, and it is the sum of the van der Waals attraction, the electrostatic repulsion of the charged surfaces and the structural force from the confined micelles. The structural term oscillates with thickness, with a period equal to the size of the micelle, and the oscillation produces the steps. A film that is thinner than a few micelle diameters crosses into the black film regime, where the two surfactant layers are in contact and the film is stable at a thickness of a few nanometres.

The measurement is done with a thin-film pressure balance, which holds a film between two menisci and measures the force as the thickness is changed. The same experiment gives the disjoining pressure isotherm, and the oscillations in it are the fingerprint of the micellar structure. Interferometry gives the thickness independently, and the two measurements together show that the steps in thickness line up with the peaks in the pressure.

Foam stability is the application. A foam that drains slowly is a foam that lasts, and the stratification is one of the mechanisms that slows the drainage. Food foams, fire-fighting foams and the foam in a beer glass all depend on the same balance, and the surfactant concentration and the salt content are the variables that a formulator adjusts. A foam that stratifies strongly can be stable for hours and one that does not collapse in minutes, from the same surfactant at a different concentration.

What the film shows is that a confined liquid is not a liquid with the same properties as a bulk one. The micelles are the same molecules that would be disordered in a beaker, and between two surfaces they take up positions that change the way the film drains. A theory of foam that treats the film as a continuum misses the steps, and the steps are the part of the behaviour that a formulator can see.
