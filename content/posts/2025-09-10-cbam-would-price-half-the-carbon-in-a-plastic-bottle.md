The carbon border adjustment mechanism began reporting in 2023 and will levy a charge on imports of steel, cement, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen. Chemicals are next on the list, with an extension proposed before 2030, and the design of that extension decides how much of a plastic bottle's carbon actually pays. The mechanism works by making importers buy certificates that mirror the carbon price their European competitors pay under the emissions trading system.

A paper in *Nature Sustainability* answers with plant-level data. The authors assemble life-cycle assessments from 4,470 chemical plants worldwide and follow ethylene and polyethylene from fossil feedstock through cracking to polymer, which is the chain that the levy would cover if it covered chemicals at all. Ethylene is the largest chemical feedstock by volume after ammonia, and polyethylene is its highest-volume derivative, which makes the pair a fair proxy for the sector.

Coverage is where the gap shows. Under the framework as drafted, the charge reaches 50 to 60 per cent of the emissions attributable to production, and the emissions upstream of the chemical plant, in extraction and refining, go unpriced. A bottle therefore arrives at the border carrying a carbon price that reflects part of its supply chain. The upstream share is larger than the plant share for a simple reason: cracking is energy-intensive, but extracting and refining the feedstock emits more.

Two consequences follow from that reading. Competitiveness claims rest on the levy equalizing costs between EU producers and importers, and an importer whose feedstock emissions are free pays less than a domestic producer whose electricity and process emissions are priced. Leakage then continues through the segment the mechanism does not see. A levy that covers half the chain also gives importers an incentive to bring in the half that is cheap. That incentive is leakage operating one step upstream of the plant the levy sees.

Two changes follow from that reading. Fossil feedstocks and refined products should enter the scope, so that the carbon in the barrel is priced where the barrel enters the chain, and default values for embedded emissions should be set high enough that reporting is cheaper than accepting the default. A low default invites every importer to skip verification. Both changes raise the administrative burden, which is why they are proposed with a transition period instead of as a single step.

The limits are those of any life-cycle accounting exercise. Plant data vary in quality, allocation between co-products is a choice, and the study covers one polymer chain in a sector with many. The direction is nonetheless clear, and it matters before the extension is drafted instead of after. A second polymer, ammonia or methanol would test whether the gap holds outside the chain studied here. The authors say the method transfers, and the data for it are the next project. They scope it here and do not complete it.
