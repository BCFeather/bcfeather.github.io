description: "Comparing 154 animal genomes shows the move from water to land came in three waves and kept reusing the same biological functions, though each lineage reached them through different genes."
---

Animals left the water many times, and every departure was an independent attempt at the same problems: drying out, breathing air, reproducing without a pond, sensing a world lit by the sun. Because the experiments repeat, the transition to land is the clearest case in evolution for asking whether genomes converge on a few solutions or invent new ones each time. Earlier answers rested on vivid cases such as the eye and the wing; a comparison published in *Nature* on 12 November puts 154 genomes behind the question.

The dataset spans 21 animal phyla and their closest relatives outside the group. From those genomes the team reconstructed the protein-coding content of the ancestral animals at 11 points where a lineage moved onto land, then dated each node to build a timescale for colonisation. Every node is an independent experiment, which is what allows gains and losses to be compared between lineages that share no recent ancestry and no common route out of the water.

Gene content did not repeat. Each transition carried its own pattern of gains and losses, and no family of genes turns up as the common currency of terrestrial life. Function did repeat. Water regulation, metabolism, reproduction and sensory perception appear at node after node, assembled from different starting material. Genes gained along the way cluster around stress and desiccation, while genes lost cluster around regeneration, diet and the timing of daily activity. The overlap in function is what the authors read as convergence, since the same problem keeps drawing the same kind of answer.

Where a lineage stands on the way out of water shapes how much it converged. Species that still depend on aquatic surroundings, mostly small invertebrates, resemble one another closely, which fits a partly aquatic life that leaves the original problems in place. Semi-terrestrial here means a life still tied to damp ground for reproduction or respiration, not a half-finished attempt at land. Fully terrestrial lineages, arthropods and vertebrates among them, diverge far more, having solved the same problems by separate genetic means and with far greater loss of genes than gain.

The dates organise the story into three windows of colonisation across 487 million years, each with its own ecological setting. That framework is what turns a list of transitions into a sequence that can be read against the record of soils, plants and atmospheric oxygen. It also makes clear that the move onto land was not one event that later radiated, but a recurring opportunity taken at intervals separated by tens of millions of years.

Convergence here sits at the level of function and not of sequence, so the claim that adaptation to land was predictable is narrower than it sounds. The problems are finite and the solutions plainly are not: the same job is done by different genes in different lineages, and the evidence for functional overlap rests on a subset of species examined under controlled stress. What the comparison does establish is that the constraints of dry land were strong enough to be felt repeatedly, and that no lineage was free to invent its own physics.
