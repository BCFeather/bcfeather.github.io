---

Consumer prices rose 0.5 per cent in January from a year earlier, the fastest pace in more than a year, and factory gate prices fell 2.3 per cent. The gap between the two is the interesting part: households paid more for services and food during the holiday, and producers continued to sell into a market with too much capacity. Energy prices fell, which held the headline below the level that holiday food alone would have produced.

The lunar new year distorts January. Travel, hospitality and food service all raise prices in the weeks around the holiday, and the statistics bureau adjusts its basket for the seasonal pattern but cannot remove the effect of a holiday that moves between January and February. A 0.5 per cent print that includes a holiday is not evidence of a change in trend. A holiday that falls in January one year and February the next shifts the comparison, and the bureau's seasonal adjustment is built for that but not perfectly.

Food prices were the largest single contributor. Vegetables and pork rose on holiday demand and on cold weather that disrupted transport, and the year-on-year comparison was against a low base in January 2024. Core inflation, which excludes food and energy, was softer, and services inflation remained below one per cent. Pork has its own cycle, and the herd rebuilding that began in 2024 will show up in prices later this year. A basket that is one-fifth food is a basket in which a vegetable shortage can move the headline by a tenth of a point.

Factory prices tell the other story. Producer prices have now fallen for more than two years, and the decline in January was concentrated in steel, cement and chemicals, which are the sectors tied to construction. A property market that is still contracting transmits through those prices faster than through consumer prices. Chemicals are the sector where excess capacity is most visible, because new plants commissioned in 2021 and 2022 are still ramping.

Nothing in the release suggests that the deflationary pressure has gone. A central bank looking at the two series sees a headline lifted by a holiday and an underlying rate that is still below target, and the appropriate response is to look through the first and to act on the second. Rate cuts in the first quarter were small for that reason. Credit data show the same split, with household lending flat and corporate borrowing concentrated in refinancing.

One risk is that a holiday effect is mistaken for a recovery. A single month of higher consumer prices, reported in a year when the government has promised to boost consumption, will be read by some as evidence that demand has returned. The composition of the basket says otherwise, and the next two prints will show whether the January number was a season or a shift. Base effects will fade by March, and the March print will be the first clean read of the year.
