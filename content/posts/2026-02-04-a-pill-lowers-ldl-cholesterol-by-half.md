PCSK9 inhibitors arrived as injections because the target is a protein in the bloodstream and antibodies were the obvious way to reach it. Inclisiran turned the same idea into a twice-yearly injection by silencing the messenger RNA instead. A tablet was the missing format, since a daily pill fits into the routine of the patient who most needs lipid lowering, and small molecules that block the PCSK9 protein have been pursued for a decade without reaching late-stage trials.

Enlicitide is the oral molecule that reached them. The CORALreef Lipids trial enrolled 2,909 participants with a history of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or at risk for a first event, randomising them to enlicitide or placebo, and the results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 4 February. Mean age was 63, 39.3 per cent were women, and baseline LDL cholesterol averaged \(96.1\pm38.9\) milligrams per decilitre.

The lipid effect was large. LDL cholesterol fell by 57.1 per cent at 24 weeks on enlicitide against a rise of 3.0 per cent on placebo, an adjusted difference of minus 55.8 percentage points with a confidence interval from minus 60.9 to minus 50.7. The change persisted to week 52, and non-HDL cholesterol, apolipoprotein B and lipoprotein(a) all fell significantly, which is the profile a clinician expects from a working PCSK9 inhibitor.

Safety was the question that a decade of small-molecule development kept raising. The incidence of adverse events did not appear to differ between the groups, which in a trial of nearly 3,000 participants over a year is a modest reassurance rather than a guarantee. Oral drugs are easier to take and harder to stop taking, and the trial's safety database will grow with post-marketing surveillance if the drug is approved.

The population was not unselected. Everyone had established disease or a risk profile that justified treatment, and the mean baseline LDL of 96 milligrams suggests many were already on a statin, so the trial tests add-on therapy and not monotherapy. Whether a tablet alone would suffice for a patient who cannot tolerate a statin is a question the trial does not answer.

Comparator design is the constraint here. Placebo is right for a lipid endpoint and wrong for a clinical one. A drug that lowers LDL by 56 per cent will reduce events by roughly the fraction that every LDL-lowering trial has found, and that inference is borrowed from other trials. Payers will ask for the outcome data in the population that matters, and those data do not exist yet.

Cost decides the rest. Injected PCSK9 inhibitors cost thousands per year and are used in a minority of eligible patients, largely because of price and inconvenience. A tablet priced near a branded statin would change the size of the market by an order of magnitude or more, and a tablet priced like a biologic would change nothing at all.
