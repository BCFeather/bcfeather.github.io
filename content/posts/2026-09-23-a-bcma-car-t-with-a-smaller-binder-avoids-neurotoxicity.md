CAR T therapy against BCMA transformed the treatment of relapsed myeloma and then ran into the two complications that limit every CAR T product. Cytokine release syndrome is manageable but severe in a minority, and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome is the event that frightens clinicians, because it can leave a patient confused for days and can occasionally be fatal. Both scale with the strength of the interaction between the binder and its target, and stronger binding has been the direction of travel.

Anito-cel takes the opposite route deliberately. Its antigen-binding domain is a single domain derived from a heavy chain, with a faster off-rate than the dual binder used in earlier constructs, and the design argument is that a brief engagement is enough for killing without the sustained activation that drives cytokine release. A phase 1 study enrolled 40 patients with relapsed or refractory myeloma and treated 38, and the results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 23 September.

Efficacy did not suffer from the gentler binder. All 38 treated patients had a response, 79 per cent had a complete response, and at a median follow-up of 38.1 months the median progression-free survival was 30.2 months with a 24-month rate of 57 per cent. Overall survival at 36 months was 65 per cent in a population that had received a median of several prior lines.

Safety was better than the class average. Among patients treated at the recommended phase 2 dose, 94 per cent had cytokine release syndrome of grade 1 or 2 and none had grade 3 or worse; neurotoxicity of grade 1 or 2 occurred in 16 per cent and a single patient had a grade 3 event. There were no delayed neurotoxic effects, which is the complication that has been hardest to predict across the class.

Every patient had at least one adverse event and 97 per cent had an event of grade 3 or higher, which reflects how heavily pretreated the population was and how much of the toxicity belongs to lymphodepletion and to myeloma itself. The comparison with other products is indirect, since phase 1 studies enrol different patients at different stages, and the authors are careful to describe their binder as producing less cytokine release in laboratory assays.

The laboratory comparison supports the clinical observation. The single-domain binder dissociated from its target faster than the dual binder, and cytotoxicity was similar, while cytokine release was lower and there was no activation in the absence of antigen and no off-target killing. Those assays are the mechanistic argument that the safety profile is a property of the design and not of the patient mix.

The next question is durability, since myeloma is the disease that teaches every new therapy to be humble. A median progression-free survival of 30 months is a strong result in this population and not a cure, and the field is now testing CAR T earlier in the treatment pathway, where the same product may behave differently.
