title: "First half ends with a 5 per cent gain and a weaker dollar"

description: "American equities closed the first half of 2025 about 5 per cent higher, with the S and P 500 near 6,200, while the dollar fell roughly 10 per cent and Treasury yields stayed near 4.2 per cent."
---

A half year that began with a record and spent April in a tariff panic ended with the index up about 5 per cent, which is what happens when the panic is followed by a truce. The S and P 500 closed June near 6,200, having touched a record in February, fallen close to 10 per cent in April and recovered through May and June, and the Nasdaq did slightly better on the strength of a handful of semiconductor and software names.

Concentration is the uncomfortable part. The ten largest companies account for a share of the index that has no precedent outside the late 1990s, and two thirds of the first half's gain came from a group that can be counted on one hand. A market that rises on five stocks is a market that has priced a story about artificial intelligence and not a story about the economy.

Dollar did the opposite. The trade-weighted index fell about 10 per cent in the first six months, the worst first half since the float in the early 1970s, as the administration's tariff policy and its pressure on the Federal Reserve persuaded foreign investors that the currency's safe haven status was conditional. A reserve currency that falls while its economy grows is a currency that is being repriced for policy risk.

Rates were the anchor that did not move. The ten-year Treasury yield ended the half near 4.2 per cent, roughly where it started, which is a remarkable outcome for a period that included a downgrade, a war in the Middle East and a truce with China. A long yield that stays put while everything else moves is a yield that reflects expectations of slower growth and of a Federal Reserve that will cut eventually.

Earnings carried the index. First quarter profits grew at a double-digit pace, the second quarter was guided to a slower but positive rate, and the companies that sell chips and power equipment to data centres raised their forecasts twice. A market that is paid for by earnings can absorb a tariff, and a market that is paid for by a story cannot.

Rotation was narrow and instructive. Defensive sectors lagged, utilities and consumer staples underperformed, and the equal-weighted index trailed the capitalised one by a wide margin, which is the signature of a market in which the average stock is not participating. An average that is not participating is an average that hurts when the leaders stumble.

What would settle the second half is whether the tariff truce holds past August and whether the artificial intelligence capital expenditure keeps growing at the pace that the guidance implies. A half year that ends with both intact is a half year that ends higher, and one that loses either is a half year that gives back the gain. A half year in which the index rises, the currency falls and the long bond does nothing is a half year in which the market has priced a company story and a country discount at the same time.
