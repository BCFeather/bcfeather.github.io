Coastal cities sink, and the rate matters as much as the sea level. Subsidence of a few millimetres a year compounds over decades, and a city built on delta sediments can subside by centimetres, which is the difference between a defence that holds and one that is overtopped. Delta cities combine sediment compaction, groundwater extraction and sea level rise, and the three are hard to separate from orbit.

Interferometric radar from satellites measures that motion by comparing the phase of signals returned from the ground across repeat passes, and the technique has produced maps of subsidence for cities worldwide. A report in *Science* on 11 June finds that independent analyses of the same cities often disagree, sometimes about the rate and sometimes about which neighbourhoods are sinking fastest. The disagreement is not confined to one city or one research group, which suggests a systematic problem in how the phase is converted rather than a series of local mistakes.

The technical reasons are several. Radar measures motion along the line of sight, so a horizontal displacement contaminates the vertical estimate; the signal also changes with soil moisture, vegetation and the geometry of buildings, and the algorithms that turn phase into millimetres make assumptions that differ between groups. That ambiguity is the crux of the problem.

Policy consequences follow immediately. A city that reads one map will prioritize one district, and a city that reads another will prioritize a different one, and both may be spending on the basis of an artefact. Flood defence is expensive enough that a factor of two in the estimated rate changes the design of a wall. A city that overestimates subsidence may build a wall it does not need; one that underestimates may fail to raise an embankment in time.

Ground truth is the missing ingredient. Radar studies are rarely validated against levelling benchmarks or continuous GPS, partly because those networks are sparse in exactly the cities that subsidence threatens, and partly because validation is unglamorous work that no satellite mission is funded to do. InSAR is also sensitive to the reference point chosen for the analysis, and two groups that pick different stable pixels can produce maps that differ by more than the subsidence itself.

The constructive reading is that the disagreement is a measurement problem rather than a refutation of the technique. Radar can resolve millimetres over a city; what it cannot yet do is certify the number without an independent reference. Funding dense ground networks in deltas would convert a contested map into an engineering input, and that is cheaper than rebuilding a sea wall on a bad estimate. A dense network of benchmarks and continuous GPS in a delta costs a fraction of a sea wall and would make the satellite maps usable as engineering evidence. Reference networks are also politically useful, because a city can point to a benchmark and ask a satellite team to reproduce it.
