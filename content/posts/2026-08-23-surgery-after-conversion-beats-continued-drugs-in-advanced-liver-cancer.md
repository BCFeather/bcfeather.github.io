description: "A Chinese multicentre trial randomized patients whose liver cancer became resectable on atezolizumab and bevacizumab to hepatectomy or continued systemic therapy, and time to treatment failure nearly doubled."
---

Advanced liver cancer with vascular invasion used to be a surgical question answered in the negative, because the tumour was unresectable and the drugs were weak. Atezolizumab with bevacizumab changed the arithmetic, and a share of patients now shrink enough to become operable. What to do at that moment is a genuine dilemma: operate and interrupt the drug that produced the response, or keep giving it and forgo a chance at a cure. Half a million people are diagnosed with liver cancer each year, most of them in East Asia, and the proportion who reach a surgeon after systemic therapy is small.

The TALENTOP trial, run by Chinese investigators across 24 hepatobiliary centres, took patients with locally advanced disease and vascular tumour thrombus through four cycles of the combination. Those without progression whose tumours remained resectable were randomized: 489 patients were treated and 201 entered randomization, with one arm receiving hepatectomy and a year of continued atezolizumab and bevacizumab, and the other continuing systemic therapy alone. Recruitment took five years, which is what a trial of a surgical decision costs when the alternative is a drug regimen that is already standard.

Time to treatment failure, the primary endpoint, was 20.4 months with surgery against 11.8 months with drugs alone, a near doubling, and the interim analysis met its endpoint. Subgroup analyses pointed in the same direction. The result appeared in *The Lancet* in August, with a commentary from Alejandro Forner describing it as the first randomized evidence that resection benefits patients who respond to systemic therapy.

Debulking is the mechanism the trial tests. Residual tumour after a response contains cells that survived the drug, and removing them, together with the visible vascular thrombus, may prevent the regrowth that eventually defeats continued treatment. That is a hypothesis about why the endpoint moved, and the trial measures the endpoint rather than the mechanism. Whether time to failure is a fair proxy for benefit is disputed in surgical oncology, since time to failure counts a second-line drug as a failure even when the patient is well.

Cost appears in the adverse events. Grade 3 or 4 treatment-related events occurred in 39 per cent of the surgery arm and 21 per cent of the drug arm, which is what a major hepatic resection adds to a regimen that already has toxicity. The survival data are still maturing, and an interim analysis that shows a time-to-failure benefit does not establish that patients live longer.

The broader question is how to select. Not every responder becomes resectable, and the trial randomized only those who did, so the benefit applies to a subgroup that a multidisciplinary team has already judged operable. Converting that judgement into a reproducible criterion is the next step, and it is the kind of question that a trial of this size can pose but not settle. A registry that follows patients who were eligible but declined randomization would widen the evidence without another trial.
