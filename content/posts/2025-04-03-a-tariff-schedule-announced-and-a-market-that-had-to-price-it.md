A tariff announcement on 2 April set a ten per cent baseline duty on most goods entering the United States and a higher schedule for a list of trading partners. The legal basis was an emergency economic powers statute that has been used for sanctions more often than for tariffs, and the rates were presented as a response to foreign barriers rather than as a negotiation. Importers were given days to comply, which is shorter than the transit time of a container. A statute written for a financial emergency has been read to cover a trade deficit, and the reading will be tested in court.

The immediate effect was a repricing of goods that were already on the water. A shipment that left Asia in March and arrives in April is dutiable at the rate in force when it enters, and the buyer and the seller had to decide who absorbs the difference. Contracts that were signed at a fixed price became arguments about force majeure, and the arguments were resolved by splitting the cost in most cases. A container that is rerouted to a different port does not avoid the duty, because the duty attaches to the country of origin.

Supply chains responded before the announcement rather than after. Importers had front-loaded orders through the first quarter, and the ports recorded unusually high volumes in February and March. A tariff that is announced with a date is a tariff that pulls demand forward, and the demand it pulls forward is demand that will be missing later in the year. A front-loaded quarter is a quarter that borrows from the next one, and the warehouses that filled in March empty in June.

The sectors most exposed are the ones with thin margins and long lead times. Apparel, furniture, toys and consumer electronics are assembled in a handful of countries and sold at prices that consumers compare with alternatives. A ten per cent duty on a product with a twenty per cent gross margin is a decision about whether to raise the price or to absorb the margin, and both options lose something. A retailer that raises a price on a ten-dollar item by a dollar loses sales to a competitor that absorbs the duty and waits.

Retaliation is the part that the schedule did not settle. A trading partner that is named in the list can respond with its own duties, and the escalation that follows is a political process with an economic cost. Agricultural exporters are the group that has historically absorbed the first blow, because their products are easy to target and their customers are easy to substitute.

What the announcement changes for a firm is the planning horizon. A tariff that can be changed by an executive action is a tariff that cannot be modelled, and the capital that would have gone into a new distribution centre goes into inventory instead. The economy that results is one with more stock and less investment, and that is a slower economy even when the trade volume is unchanged. A firm that cannot model the duty builds inventory, and inventory is the form of insurance that a small company can afford.
