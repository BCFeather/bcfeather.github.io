Colombia's presidential election went to a runoff on 29 May, after no candidate passed the fifty per cent threshold required to win in the first round. With 99.93 per cent of polling stations counted, Gustavo Petro of the Pacto Histórico coalition had 40.32 per cent, equivalent to about eight and a half million votes, and Rodolfo Hernández, a former mayor of Bucaramanga standing for a movement he founded, had 28.15 per cent, or nearly six million. Federico Gutiérrez, the candidate endorsed by the outgoing president, finished third with 23.9 per cent and was eliminated.

The result ended a campaign that had been treated as a two-way contest for months. Petro had led every poll since the winter, and the question was whether a left-wing coalition that had never held the presidency could convert that lead into a majority in a country where the urban vote and the rural vote are counted in different worlds. Hernández's rise came late, built on videos in which he spoke directly to camera in plain language, promised to end corruption and to cut the size of the state, and refused to appear in debates with his rivals.

Turnout was around fifty-five per cent of the electorate, higher than in 2018, and the count produced a picture of a country dividing along lines that the war in Ukraine and the pandemic had sharpened. The regions that had suffered most from armed conflict voted for the candidate who promised to dismantle the security apparatus, and the cities that had grown rich voted for the one who promised to build it. Abstention was highest in the poorest departments, which is where the state's absence is most visible.

Petro's programme is a set of promises that would make Colombia the most ambitious left-wing experiment in Latin America since the pink tide of the 2000s: to halt new oil exploration, to move the economy towards agriculture and industry, to tax unproductive land, to reform the police, and to negotiate a settlement with the remaining armed groups. He has also promised to end the policy of extradition to the United States and to renegotiate the free trade agreement, positions that the armed forces and the business chambers have opposed.

Hernández campaigns against all of that. He says he will cut the number of ministries, sell state assets, build a railway and govern like a company, and he has refused to be photographed with the traditional parties that have ruled the country for two centuries. His record as mayor of Bucaramanga includes a period of popularity and a period of investigations, and his campaign had spent almost no money until the final weeks, when the videos began to work.

The runoff is set for 19 June, and the arithmetic of the second round shapes it. Gutiérrez's voters are the ones both candidates need, and the third-placed candidate declined to endorse either of them on the night. Colombia has never elected a left-wing president in the modern era, and the country's establishment spent the weeks after 29 May discovering that the choice it had prepared for belonged to a different election.
