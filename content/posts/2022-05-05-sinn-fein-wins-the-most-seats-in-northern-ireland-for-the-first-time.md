Voters in Northern Ireland went to the polls on 5 May and the count that ran into the weekend of 7 May produced a result no previous election had: Sinn Féin finished first, with twenty-seven of the ninety seats in the assembly and twenty-nine per cent of the first-preference vote. The Democratic Unionist Party took twenty-five seats on twenty-one per cent, the Alliance Party seventeen, the Ulster Unionists nine, the Social Democratic and Labour Party eight, and two smaller parties one each.

Under the rules of power-sharing, the largest party nominates the first minister and the largest unionist party nominates the deputy, and the two posts carry equal authority. Sinn Féin's leader in Belfast, Michelle O'Neill, therefore became the first Irish nationalist in the history of the devolved institutions to be entitled to the top job, and she said she would take it. The DUP said it would not nominate a deputy until the trading arrangements that followed Brexit were replaced, which meant the assembly could not even begin, because the speaker must be elected by a cross-community vote.

That the result arrived at all was something of a surprise to the parties that ran the campaign on the cost of living, the health service and the protocol. Sinn Féin had spent the previous year arguing about its own handling of expenses and allegations about its press operation, and its vote share fell slightly compared with 2017. The DUP lost three seats from its previous total, the Alliance gained nine and became the third force in the chamber, and the unionist vote divided between a party that promised to fight the protocol and one that promised to make it work.

The protocol is the mechanism that keeps Northern Ireland inside the European single market for goods while placing checks on shipments arriving from Great Britain. Unionist parties call it a border in the Irish Sea, traders call it paperwork, and the government in London calls it a problem it intends to legislate away. Legislation to override parts of the agreement was being drafted in May, which made the assembly's deadlock a dispute with a wider frame than Belfast.

There is another arithmetic in the room. Power-sharing requires majorities in both communities for contentious decisions, and a petition signed by thirty members can force a vote that must be carried across the divide. With twenty-seven seats Sinn Féin holds exactly the number that creates a blocking minority, the DUP holds fewer than it once did, and the Alliance's seventeen seats sit between them as the only sizeable group that both sides court.

So the election changed who stands first in the queue and left the queue itself in place. O'Neill will wait for a deputy who has not been nominated, the assembly will wait for a speaker it cannot elect, and the government in London will present a bill that unionists want and nationalists oppose. The result is the most significant shift in the region's politics since 1998, delivered by a mechanism that cannot act on it.
