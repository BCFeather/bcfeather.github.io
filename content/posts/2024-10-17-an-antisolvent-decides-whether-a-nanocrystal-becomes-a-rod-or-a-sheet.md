Lead halide perovskite nanocrystals are bright emitters and are made in a flask within seconds, which is convenient for synthesis and inconvenient for understanding. A paper in *Nature Communications* on 17 October 2024 slows the reaction down in the observation rather than in the chemistry, and resolves how a nanorod and a nanoplatelet diverge from the same starting point. A reaction that finishes in seconds is one that a fast probe can still resolve, and the probes here are fast enough to catch the cluster stage.

In situ X-ray scattering and photoluminescence spectroscopy run during the synthesis, so that the size, the shape and the emission of the growing crystals are recorded continuously. Scattering reports the shape of an ensemble while emission reports the states that radiate, so the two techniques constrain different parts of the picture.

Both products begin as emissive prolate nanoclusters, formed as soon as the two precursor solutions are mixed. The starting point is therefore the same, and the anisotropy that follows is decided later. Prolate clusters are the intermediate that both products share, which is why the divergence has to be kinetic rather than compositional.

The antisolvent decides it. Adding the antisolvent changes the solubility of the precursors and the rate at which the clusters assemble, and the two products follow from different kinetics rather than from different reagents. Kinetics rather than reagents is the cleaner conclusion, because it names a variable that a chemist can control without changing the recipe.

Rods grow along one axis, platelets along two, and the scattering data show which facet the additional material is added to under each condition. That is the level of detail that a synthesis described only by its final product cannot supply. Facet-specific addition is a mechanism that microscopy could confirm, and the scattering evidence is indirect.

Practical control is the aim. A lighting application wants a specific emission wavelength, and the wavelength depends on the thickness of a platelet or the diameter of a rod, so a rule that connects an antisolvent to a dimension is a rule for a manufacturer. A rule that maps a variable to a dimension is what manufacturing needs, and the mechanism is the first half of such a rule.

The paper does not supply that rule in a form a chemist can dial in, since the antisolvent's effect depends on concentration, temperature and the rate of addition, and each of those is a variable the study fixes rather than varies. What it does supply is a mechanism, and a mechanism is what makes a parameter sweep intelligible rather than a search. Holding variables fixed is the standard price of a mechanistic study, and the follow-up work can pay it by varying them one at a time. A mechanism also tells a chemist which variables not to bother with, which is a saving that a parameter sweep cannot offer.
