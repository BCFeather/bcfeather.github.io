Fluorinated waxes reduce friction by forming a water-repellent layer between the ski base and the snow, and they persist in the environment: perfluorinated compounds do not degrade and have been found in snowpack and in human blood. A competition ban that began with elite racing has extended to amateur events this season.

The rule is simple and the enforcement is not. Elite races test skis with a fluorescence detector before the start, and a positive result means disqualification. A local race has no detector, and the national federation's guidance asks competitors to declare their wax and imposes a protest procedure.

Chemistry of the substitutes has improved. Hydrocarbon waxes with a nanostructured additive match fluorinated performance within 1.5 per cent in glide tests at warmer snow temperatures, and lag by 4 to 6 per cent in very cold, dry snow, which is where the fluorinated compounds were most effective.

The equipment change is a cost. A wax bench for a team now needs a structure to apply the new additives and a thermometer that reads snow temperature to a tenth of a degree, and the top teams spend 15,000 dollars a season on wax and testing, against 9,000 before the ban.

Health exposure is the argument for extending the ban. Wax technicians who applied fluorinated powders with an iron reported blood levels of perfluorooctanoic acid several times the general population's, and the sport's medical commission published those measurements in 2023.

Compliance is uneven by design. A national federation with a budget tests at its championships; one without does not. The result is that the same rule is enforced at different standards in different countries, which is the usual pattern for an equipment rule without a technical officer.

What the ban has changed visibly is the smell. Fluorinated waxes release a distinctive vapour when ironed, and technicians who worked with them for twenty years describe the absence as the clearest sign the rule is working.

Ski base preparation is where the money moved. A base that is structured correctly holds wax better, and a stone grind costs 40 dollars per pair and lasts a season. The teams that lost the most glide after the ban are the ones that had relied on a wax rather than on a base.

Junior development is the equity concern. A family that cannot afford a wax bench and a thermometer competes on equipment, and two national federations now fund a shared service at regional races. That is a small intervention aimed at a real gap.

For a reader, the interesting point is that equipment regulation in sport is usually about fairness and occasionally about health. This ban is about an environmental and occupational hazard, and the enforcement model, a detector at the elite level and an honour system below it, is what every such rule looks like when the cost of testing exceeds the cost of compliance.
