description: "In a trial of 2,246 unvaccinated high-risk adults, five days of nirmatrelvir plus ritonavir reduced hospitalization or death by 89 per cent compared with placebo."

Antiviral treatment for COVID-19 arrived late and mostly intravenously. Remdesivir required an infusion, monoclonal antibodies required a clinic and were defeated by each new variant in turn, and the oral options were limited to drugs with modest effects. A pill that could be prescribed at diagnosis was the missing piece of the outpatient pathway. The gap mattered most for the patients whose risk of progression was highest, and those were the patients the trial enrolled.

Nirmatrelvir inhibits the main protease of SARS-CoV-2, an enzyme the virus needs to cut its polyprotein into functional proteins, and ritonavir is added to slow the metabolism of nirmatrelvir rather than to treat the virus. The phase 2-3 trial randomized 2,246 symptomatic, unvaccinated, non-hospitalized adults at high risk to 300 milligrams of nirmatrelvir with 100 milligrams of ritonavir every 12 hours for five days, or placebo. It appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 16 February 2022.

In the interim analysis of patients treated within three days of symptom onset, the incidence of COVID-19-related hospitalization or death by day 28 was lower by 6.32 percentage points, with a confidence interval from -9.04 to -3.59. The overall reduction in the risk of progression was 89 per cent. Viral load fell substantially in those treated early. The absolute reduction of 6.32 percentage points amounts to roughly one hospitalization prevented for every sixteen patients treated.

Safety was unremarkable in the trial. Any adverse event occurred in 22.6 per cent of the treatment group and 23.9 per cent of the placebo group, serious adverse events in 1.6 and 6.6 per cent, and discontinuation in 2.1 and 4.2 per cent. Dysgeusia and diarrhoea were more common with the drug, which is consistent with the ritonavir component. The lower rate of serious adverse events in the treatment group follows from the lower rate of severe COVID, since hospitalization is counted as a serious event.

The population is the limitation. Participants were unvaccinated and uninfected at entry, which was the group at highest risk in 2021 and is no longer the typical patient in most countries. The trial also excluded people already hospitalized and people on drugs that interact with ritonavir, a long list that includes common cardiovascular medicines.

Ritonavir's interactions are the practical constraint on prescribing. A five-day course is short enough to manage a temporary stop of some concomitant drugs, but the pharmacist's review becomes part of the treatment, and the drug's benefit depends on being started within days of symptom onset. The label restricts use to patients with at least one risk factor, and the list of interacting drugs is long enough that a pharmacist reviews every prescription.

What the trial cannot show is effect against Omicron, which emerged after enrolment closed, or effect in vaccinated people, whose baseline risk is lower and whose absolute benefit is therefore smaller. Both questions were taken up by later observational work. Regulators approved the drug for high-risk outpatients, and supply rather than evidence became the constraint in the first months.
