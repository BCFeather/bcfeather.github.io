title: "A report that measured the window"
date: "2023-03-20T23:30:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change published its synthesis report on 20 March 2023, stating that global surface temperature had reached 1.1 degrees Celsius above the pre-industrial level and that between 3.3 and 3.6 billion people live in highly vulnerable contexts."
---

Governments approved the summary line by line in Interlaken over a week of meetings that ran into extra time, and the report was published on 20 March. It is the synthesis of three working group reports and three special reports produced since 2018, and it is written to be read by finance ministries rather than by scientists. Its central statements are short and each carries a confidence level in brackets. The summary runs to about thirty pages and the report behind it to several thousand, and the two are meant to be read together.

The first number is 1.1 degrees Celsius, which is the rise in global surface temperature between 1850 and 1900 and the period from 2011 to 2020. Human activities have caused essentially all of that warming, and the report states the attribution as unequivocal. Emissions have continued to rise, and the growth has been uneven across regions and within countries, which the report treats as a finding rather than as an aside. Its attribution chapter states that the last decade was the warmest on record and that each of the last four decades has been warmer than any decade before it.

Between 3.3 and 3.6 billion people live in contexts that are highly vulnerable to climate change, and that is the second number in the summary. Those people have contributed least to the problem and are affected most, and the report states that the vulnerability is produced by inequity as much as by the hazard. Extreme events have already pushed millions into acute food insecurity and have reduced water security in regions that were already dry.

For the third number the report puts the remaining carbon budget at about 2,500 gigatonnes of carbon dioxide for a fifty per cent chance of staying within 1.5 degrees. The world had emitted most of that budget by the time of publication, and the report's language about the window is careful: it says the window is closing rather than that it has closed, and it says the choices made in this decade determine the outcome for the next century.

Broader than its predecessor's is the report's policy content. It assesses adaptation as well as mitigation, it gives a chapter to finance and to the gap between the money available and the money needed, and it describes the technologies that could remove carbon from the atmosphere without pretending that they are a substitute for cutting emissions. It also notes that some adaptation is now unavoidable, which is a sentence that governments had resisted in earlier drafts.

The reception was shaped by what the report could not do. It is an assessment of published science, and the science had been available in the working group reports for two years, and the synthesis was a repetition with a louder voice. The voice was heard in the negotiations that followed, and the negotiations produced a text about fossil fuels that was weaker than the report's findings, which is what usually happens to assessments. The text that governments negotiated in the following months referred to fossil fuels in language that the authors had avoided, and the authors said so in private.
