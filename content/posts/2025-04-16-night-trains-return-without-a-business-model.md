date: "2025-04-16T09:20:00+08:00"

description: "Sleeper services have multiplied across Europe on subsidies and political enthusiasm, and the operators still lose money on every passenger they carry."
---

A night train is a hotel that moves, and the economics of a hotel that moves are worse than the economics of either a hotel or a train. A sleeper carriage carries forty passengers and weighs fifty tonnes, and it occupies a path on a railway that could carry a seated train with four times the capacity. The operator collects one fare and pays for a berth, a attendant and a slot. A bed on wheels is a bed that has to be made, cleaned and turned over in the eight hours between arrival and departure.

The subsidies are the reason the network has grown. Several European governments have decided that a night train is a climate policy, and they pay the operating deficit that the service generates. A route from Zurich to Vienna that carries a few hundred passengers a night is a route that an airline serves at a lower cost, and the state has decided that the comparison is not the point. A route that is justified by a carbon price is a route that a carbon price has to keep justifying.

Rolling stock is the constraint. A sleeper carriage is a bespoke vehicle with a small production run, and the manufacturers that build them have full order books and long lead times. New routes have been announced and then delayed because the carriages did not exist, and a delay of three years is normal. A order for twenty carriages is a small order for a factory that builds five hundred coaches a year.

The service model is being rebuilt. Couchette carriages, which offer a bunk rather than a compartment, carry more passengers per carriage and are cheaper to operate. Several operators have ordered them to increase capacity on routes that cannot justify a full sleeper, and the result is a product that sits between a train and a hostel. A couchette that carries six people in a compartment is a product that sells on price, and the price is what fills a November train.

Demand is real and seasonal. A summer route to the coast fills in July and runs empty in November, and an operator that cannot fill the off-season runs a deficit for half the year. The airlines respond by cutting winter capacity, and the train cannot cut a daily service without losing the passengers who depend on it. A daily service that is cut to three days a week loses the business traveller who needs to be back on Thursday.

What the revival has proved is that people will take a night train when it exists and is priced reasonably. What it has not proved is that the service can pay for itself, and the political commitment that funds it is a commitment that lasts as long as the climate target is in fashion. A subsidy that pays the deficit is a subsidy that grows with the network, and the network is growing faster than the budget.
