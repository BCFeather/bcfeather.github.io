By the first week of September the shops have already built their mooncake walls. Boxes sit three deep on pallets near the entrance, red and gold, tied with ribbon, priced at levels that invite a second look. The festival itself falls in early October this year, which means the selling has been under way since July, and the discount cycle has already passed through its first and second stages.

The pastry is the smaller half of the transaction. What a household buys is a box, and what a box does is travel: to a teacher, a client, a relative in another city, a colleague who covered three shifts in August. A gift that can be re-gifted has an economics of its own, and mooncakes are the clearest example in the calendar. The tin keeps, the label is respectable, and the receiver can pass it on without embarrassment if the timing is right. A box that has travelled twice is a small social document, and nobody in the chain asks where it started.

Shops understand this better than anyone. Corporate orders are placed in July, when the weather is at its worst and the festival is a rumour. Companies buy in the hundreds for staff and clients, and the invoice is booked as a welfare cost, which is the most honest description of what it is. By September the retail trade is mostly individuals buying two boxes: one to keep and one to give, or one to give and one kept back in case somebody gives first.

The pastry itself has changed less than the packaging. A lotus-paste mooncake with a salted yolk still costs roughly what it did a decade ago in the plain version, and the novelty is in the margins: snow-skin, ice-cream, tea-flavoured, or a box printed with a museum's collection. Some of these will be eaten. Many will be opened, admired, and left in a fridge until the middle of October, when they are thrown out with a small pang of guilt.

Regional styles have held their ground through all of it. Cantonese bakeries still sell the lotus and five-kernel types by weight, Suzhou shops still make the savoury pork version with a flaky crust, and Yunnan keeps its ham and rose fillings, and the north keeps its heavy nut-and-sugar bricks that are cut with a knife and eaten in slices. A buyer in one city can order the style of another, which is new, and the arrival of a courier box is now part of the ritual alongside the department-store queue.

None of this is a complaint. A festival needs a commerce or it does not survive, and the box trade has kept the pastry alive through decades in which nobody had time to bake. The moon will be full on the sixth of October whether or not the shops sell out, and the queues at the counter in September are part of how a city prepares to look at it.
