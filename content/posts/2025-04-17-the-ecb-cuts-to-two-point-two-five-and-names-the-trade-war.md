The European Central Bank had been cutting at every meeting since June 2024, and by April the question was whether the sequence had a natural end. Inflation had fallen from a peak above 10 per cent to something close to 2 per cent, wage growth was slowing, and the deposit rate at 2.5 per cent was still restrictive in real terms. A pause was the alternative the Governing Council weighed, and it chose another cut.

On 17 April the bank lowered all three key rates by 25 basis points. The deposit facility rate, which steers the stance, moved to 2.25 per cent; the main refinancing rate to 2.40 per cent; and the marginal lending rate to 2.65 per cent. The statement said disinflation was well on track, that headline and core inflation had both declined in March, and that services inflation had eased markedly over recent months.

Supporting detail is about wages and profits. Wage growth is moderating, and profits have partly absorbed the effect of still elevated wage growth on prices, so unit labour costs are no longer the threat they were in 2023. Most measures of underlying inflation were described as consistent with a return to the 2 per cent target on a sustained basis. That sentence is the justification for cutting while growth is weak: the bank is arguing that inflation risk has become two-sided rather than one-sided.

Growth is where the trade war enters. The euro area economy had shown some resilience against global shocks, and the statement said the growth outlook had deteriorated because trade tensions had increased, with uncertainty likely to weigh on investment and exports. A central bank cannot offset a tariff with an interest rate, but it can make the financial conditions facing firms slightly easier while the trade policy works itself out.

Market expectations before the meeting had put the probability of a cut above 80 per cent, and the reaction afterwards was muted, which is what happens when a decision is priced. The more informative move was in the language: the bank stopped describing the direction of policy and started describing the risks around it. Traders read that as a signal that the pace from here depends on the data and not on a calendar.

The constraint is the deposit rate itself. At 2.25 per cent the policy rate is close to the range that economists call neutral, where it neither restrains nor stimulates, and below that the bank would be easing into an economy that is not in recession. Several Governing Council members had said publicly that rates were nearing the level where the argument for caution gets stronger, and the April cut narrowed that distance.

What would settle the path is the inflation data for the next two quarters and the behaviour of the euro. A tariff that raises import prices would push inflation up and argue for holding; a tariff that hits demand would push it down and argue for cutting further. The bank has set itself up to choose between those two cases, and the choice will be made meeting by meeting.
