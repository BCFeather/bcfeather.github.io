A tournament that expands from twelve teams to sixteen is a tournament that has decided growth is worth a few lopsided group games, and the women's European Championship does that this month in Switzerland. The competition runs from 2 to 27 July across eight venues, with England arriving as the defending champion and Spain as the team that most neutrals expect to win.

Expansion is the story of the decade. The 2017 edition had sixteen teams and the 2022 edition cut to twelve after the pandemic, so this is a return and not a novelty, and the qualifying route now gives smaller federations a path that did not exist when the event was an eight-team invitational. A wider field is how a sport acquires a middle class.

England's defence is the subplot that the British press will follow. The team won the 2022 final at Wembley in front of a crowd of 87,000, lost its manager to a foreign appointment, and arrives with a squad that has aged in the positions where pace matters. A defending champion with a rebuilt spine is a champion that the group stage will test.

Spain is the favourite by the numbers. The senior team won the World Cup in 2023, the under-19 and under-17 sides have won their tournaments, and the league has produced a generation that passes in a way that the rest of the continent has struggled to match. A favourite that has already won at every other level is a favourite with a system and not with a single player.

Hosts are the variable that a tournament cannot control. Switzerland has four stadiums with capacities above 20,000, a public that has been lukewarm about the women's game, and a team that qualified without much difficulty. A host that fills a stadium in Basel and not in Thun is a host that tells you where the audience is.

Commercial picture has moved. Television rights for the event have been sold in more markets than in 2022, the prize fund has risen, and several clubs have agreed to release players earlier than the regulations require, which is the concession that a growing tournament extracts from a growing league. Money follows attendance and attendance follows the product.

What would settle the month is whether a team outside the four favourites reaches a semi-final. A tournament that produces the same last four as the seeding is a tournament that has grown in size and not in depth, and the group stage is where that question gets answered. A tournament that is decided by the fourth-best team rather than by the best one is a tournament that has become a league, and leagues are what a sport needs before it can sell a season. A competition whose group stage produces four close games and four routs is a competition that has bought its depth with a month of mismatches.
