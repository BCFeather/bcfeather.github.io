title: "The Supreme Court blocks the workplace vaccine rule and upholds one for health staff"
date: "2022-01-13T23:55:00+08:00"
categories: [news]
description: "Two rulings on the same afternoon drew a line between a general power to regulate safety and a narrower power over hospitals that receive federal money."
---

The Supreme Court blocked the federal vaccine-or-test requirement for large employers on 13 January, holding that the agency that issued it, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration, had exceeded the authority Congress gave it. In a separate ruling the same afternoon, the court allowed a requirement that health workers at facilities receiving federal Medicare and Medicaid money be vaccinated, with no test-out option.

A split turned on statutory language rather than on epidemiology. Congress had empowered the safety agency to set standards for hazards that workers face in their own workplaces, and the court's majority said a virus that spreads everywhere is a hazard the agency may address only through a rule narrowly aimed at occupational exposure. The health-worker rule rested on a different statute, which conditions federal funding on conditions the secretary may impose, and the majority found that the requirement was a reasonable exercise of that spending power. The majority added that the rule stopped short of a vaccine mandate, since testing was allowed as an alternative, and that the agency had described it as a temporary measure.

Employer rule had been written to reach about eighty million workers. It required either vaccination or weekly testing and masking, and it had been stayed by a lower court within days of its publication, which meant it never took effect nationally. Employers had spent the autumn preparing for it, and many had already imposed their own mandates on their own legal authority, which is why the ruling changed less in practice than it did in principle.

Two of the justices dissented from the employer ruling in terms that described the pandemic as the sort of emergency in which agencies act first and litigate later. The dissenters accepted that the agency's reading had been aggressive and argued that the alternative was a court telling a regulator that it could not respond to the deadliest workplace hazard in a century. They also noted that the agency had acted after two years of the pandemic, and that the court's reading left it with powers that no previous emergency had tested.

Rulings leave the country with a patchwork. Hospital systems that had already required vaccination kept their mandates under state and private contracts, large private employers kept the mandates they had adopted on their own, and states with Republican governors moved to forbid such requirements. The virus did not adjust its behaviour to the map, and neither did the politics.

An administrative state is built out of statutes written for ordinary hazards, and a pandemic sits outside that category. Courts can say which statute applies, and they cannot say what a government should have done; the ruling resolved a legal question and left the practical one exactly where the winter had put it. Employers were left to decide for themselves, and several of the largest kept requirements of their own, which moved the argument into state courthouses rather than the federal one. The practical result was that a worker's protection depended on the state where the employer operated, which is the outcome the agency had tried to avoid.
