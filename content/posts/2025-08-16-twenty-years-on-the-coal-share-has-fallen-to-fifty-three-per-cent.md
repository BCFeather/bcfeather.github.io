National Ecology Day fell on 15 August, twenty years after the phrase about green mountains and clear water being worth as much as gold entered the policy vocabulary. The occasion produced a set of figures that are more useful than the speeches, because they show what has actually changed in the energy system and what has not.

Coal supplied 56.8 per cent of primary energy in 2020 and 53.2 per cent in 2024. That is a fall of 3.6 percentage points in four years, in an economy that kept growing, which means the absolute tonnage did not fall much even as the share did. Coal is still the anchor of the grid, and the dispatch order in most provinces puts it after renewables but ahead of almost everything else. Energy intensity per unit of GDP fell 11.6 per cent against 2020, a reduction the government equates with about 1.1 billion tonnes of avoided carbon dioxide.

The supply side has moved faster than the demand side. Installed wind and solar capacity has grown every year, and the cost of Chinese-made modules and turbines has fallen far enough that the global price of wind power dropped more than 60 per cent and solar more than 80 per cent over the decade, according to the figures released at the event. New energy vehicles reached annual production and sales above 12 million units.

Industrial policy has been organized around lists. The country counts 66 national strategic emerging industry clusters, more than 6,400 national-level green factories and about 490 green industrial parks. Whether those categories change behaviour or merely label it is a fair question, and the answer probably differs by province. A green factory certificate requires metering, reporting and an audit, and the audit is where most of the cost sits for a small firm. Restoration work has its own numbers. Fifty-two integrated projects covering mountains, rivers, forests, farmland, lakes, sand and grassland have restored more than 8.3 million hectares; about 1,780 kilometres of coastline and 53,000 hectares of coastal wetland have been repaired; mangrove area has reached 30,300 hectares, ahead of the target set for the end of the five-year plan; and more than 350,000 hectares of abandoned mine land have been treated.

The awkward part is the shape of the curve. A coal share that falls three points every four years reaches 40 per cent around 2035 if nothing accelerates, which is slower than the peak-emissions date the country has set for itself. Much of the remaining coal sits in steel, cement and chemicals, where the fuel is used as a reagent as well as a heat source and cannot be swapped for a wind farm.

For the twentieth anniversary, then, the honest summary is that the direction is settled and the pace is contested. Solar farms can be built in a year; the industries that burn coal to make things take longer to change.
