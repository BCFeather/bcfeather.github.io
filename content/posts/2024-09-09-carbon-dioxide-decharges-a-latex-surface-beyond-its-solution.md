description: "Airborne carbon dioxide strips far more charge from carboxylate latex than its dissolved products can account for, and the surface acidity constant moves two units."

A colloidal particle stays suspended because its surface carries charge and the counter-ions around it screen that charge from its neighbours. Alter the surface chemistry and the stability changes with it, which is why the charge state of a latex particle is a quantity worth measuring rather than assuming. Carboxylate groups on a polymer surface are weak acids: some stand dissociated into the surrounding electrolyte and some do not, and the split depends on pH, on ionic strength and on what else is dissolved in the water.

By measuring conductance instead of titrating, Vogel, Beyer, Holm and Palberg read that split. A suspension of carboxylate-stabilized latex is diluted and its conductance compared with a model in which only the freely mobile counter-ions carry current, the rest staying bound near the surface. Poisson-Boltzmann cell calculations with charge regulation at the boundary supply the other half of the comparison, and the two agree on an apparent \(\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}\) of 4.26 for suspensions kept clear of carbon dioxide.

Two further regularities come out of the same fit, and both concern where the charge sits. Only a fraction of the counter-ion population conducts, and the remainder exchanges freely with added sodium, potassium or caesium ions, which is the check that the bound fraction belongs to the surface and not to one particular salt. A renormalized charge inferred from the cell calculation tracks the number of mobile counter-ions closely, so the two routes to the same quantity do not drift apart.

Set against ambient air instead of clean water, the same suspension conducts far less than dissolved carbon dioxide and its dissociation products could account for, and no counter-ion exchange appears afterwards. Reproducing the measurement requires an effective \(\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}\) of 6.48, two units above the value obtained in clean water, and the gap between the two fits carries the argument.

Since the effect follows the gas and not the acid it forms, only two readings survive. Molecular carbon dioxide can adsorb at the surface and enter the charge-regulation balance without ever dissociating, and the earlier report of a carbon-dioxide-induced fall in zeta potential points the same way. Either reading makes the gas act on the surface groups directly, with bulk acidity an incidental product; the alternative, that dissolved carbonic acid simply changes the pH, is excluded by the size of the effect.

One latex chemistry, one electrolyte composition, one ambient concentration, and a surface state inferred from conductance: the claim is bounded by its material. No spectroscopy in the paper looks at the carboxylate groups themselves, so the change in dissociation is a conclusion drawn from transport and not an observation of the bond. Surfaces other than carboxylate latex may respond differently, and the effect at lower partial pressures is unmeasured.

Running the same colloid experiment on a bench in air and again in a glovebox no longer measures the same particle, since the ambient gas sets the charge state before any salt is added. Desalination membranes and biological membranes, both of which present charged surfaces to aqueous electrolyte, sit in the same exposed condition. The manuscript was submitted to *Soft Matter* and posted as a preprint on 4 September 2024, and a direct titration under controlled carbon dioxide would settle which surface species is responsible.
