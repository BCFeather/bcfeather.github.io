Hydrides under pressure hold the record for superconducting transition temperature, and the record comes with a caveat: the material exists only inside a diamond anvil cell, at pressures of a hundred gigapascals or more. A superconductor that requires such a press is a subject of study, not a material. The question that follows is whether some hydride can be persuaded to keep its hydrogen arrangement after the pressure is released, and whether such a phase would still carry a current.

Hydrogen is nearly invisible to X-rays, which makes its position in a lattice hard to measure, and theory usually assumes an ideal structure in which every hydrogen sits in the same kind of site. The preprint posted on 29 November 2023 works instead with the migration of hydrogen between two site types in lanthanum dihydride, and with what happens on the way back down in pressure.

The mechanism is a site change. Under pressure, hydrogen moves from tetrahedral sites to octahedral ones, producing an occupancy pattern that mixes the two and stabilizes a structure that the ambient phase does not adopt. When the pressure is released, the octahedral occupancy is retained, and the resulting phase is dynamically stable at ambient pressure, which turns compression and decompression into a synthesis route.

Superconductivity survives the trip. Electron-phonon coupling in the metastable phase strengthens as the octahedral occupancy rises, and the calculated transition temperature reaches 10.8 kelvins at ambient pressure. That figure is far below the hydride records, and it is high enough to be interesting for a material that needs no press to exist.

The paper also maps the threshold pressure for a family of four lanthanide dihydrides. For yttrium, scandium, neodymium and lutetium, the pressure at which hydrogen migration begins falls linearly as the ionic radius of the metal grows, which gives a design rule rather than a single result. A larger ion opens the lattice, and an open lattice lets hydrogen move at lower pressure.

Optimization is the next step the authors propose. Since the transition temperature tracks the octahedral fraction, the highest values in a face-centred cubic dihydride should come from tuning that fraction and not from changing the metal alone. Whether such a fraction can be controlled during decompression, and whether it survives cycling, are experimental questions.

Caveats are those of a calculated result. The structures are predicted rather than measured, dynamical stability is not thermodynamic stability, and a metastable phase can relax over long times or on heating. Transition temperatures from electron-phonon calculations carry their own uncertainty, which is largest for exactly the hydrides where the coupling is strong. The work changes the search strategy: instead of asking which hydride reaches the highest temperature under pressure, it asks which hydride can be brought back to ambient conditions with its hydrogen still out of place, and it supplies a family and a scaling rule for that question.
