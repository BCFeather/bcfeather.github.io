description: "The Democratic Alliance won 29.5 per cent and 79 seats in Portugal's election on 10 March, ending nine years of Socialist government, while Chega quadrupled its representation and the result left no party with a workable majority."
---

Democratic Alliance, the coalition led by the Social Democratic Party, won the Portuguese general election of the tenth of March with 29.5 per cent of the vote and 79 seats, taking first place from the Socialists by a margin of less than one percentage point. Socialist Party took 28 per cent and 77 seats after nine years in government. Chega, the party of the far right, quadrupled its share to 18.1 per cent and won 50 seats, becoming the third largest force in the 230-seat parliament.

Cause of the early election was a corruption investigation that brought down the previous government. Prime Minister António Costa resigned in November after prosecutors searched his official residence and detained his chief of staff in connection with the awarding of energy contracts. He denied wrongdoing and was never charged, and the president dissolved parliament in January after the opposition and the government failed to agree on a budget.

Result leaves no obvious coalition. The Democratic Alliance has 79 seats, which is well short of the 116 needed for a majority, and its leader, Luís Montenegro, said during the campaign that he would not govern with Chega. Socialist Party said it would not support a government that it had just been defeated by, and the smaller Liberal Initiative and the Left Bloc have incompatible demands. Negotiations over the following weeks produced a minority government that depended on case-by-case support in the parliament.

Rise of Chega is the story that outlasted the result. Party was founded in 2019 by André Ventura, a former football commentator and lawyer, and it went from one seat to twelve in 2022 and to fifty in March. Its campaign was built on promises to cut immigration, to increase pensions and to end what it describes as corruption in the political class. Several of its candidates had previously been associated with neo-Nazi groups, and the party has been accused of antisemitism and of attacking the rights of Roma communities.

Issues that decided the vote were housing, health and wages. Rents in Lisbon and Porto have risen by more than fifty per cent in five years, and the government's attempts to control them were judged ineffective by voters on both sides. Public health service waiting lists reached record lengths after the pandemic, and the emigration of young professionals to other European countries became a subject that every party addressed and none could explain.

Consequence of the tenth of March is a country that has moved to the right without choosing a government that can act. Montenegro took office in April with a cabinet of his own and a promise to govern for four years, which depends on votes from a party he had ruled out. Portugal has been one of the more stable democracies in Europe for five decades, and the election showed that its party system had fragmented in the way that others have. Whether the fragmentation is temporary depends on whether the centrist parties can recover the voters who left them, and on whether the far right can be kept out of government without its support being needed.
