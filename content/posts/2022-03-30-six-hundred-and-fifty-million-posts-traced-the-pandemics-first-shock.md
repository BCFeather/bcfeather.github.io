title: "Six hundred and fifty million posts traced the pandemic's first shock"
date: "2022-03-30T22:50:00+08:00"

description: "Within a week of the pandemic being declared, expressed sentiment fell by almost a standard deviation in more than a hundred countries, and it climbed back more slowly than it dropped."
---

The dataset behind the paper is 654 million geotagged posts from more than a hundred countries, which between them hold about 74 per cent of the world's population. Each post was scored by a multilingual classifier trained to read sentiment, and the scores were averaged into daily indices for nations and for provinces or states. The authors checked the index against the Gallup-Sharecare well-being survey and against earlier work on weather and disasters, and the correlations held closely enough to treat the index as a thermometer. Building a classifier that scores the same phrase the same way in a hundred languages took the longest part of the four years.

Two designs did the analytical work. A regression discontinuity separated the shock from the ordinary drift of a timeline, and a synthetic control compared countries that locked down with ones that resembled them and did not. Around the declaration of the pandemic on 11 March 2020, expressed sentiment fell in every country in the sample, by just under a standard deviation on average, and the fall was statistically significant in 91 per cent of them.

The size of the shock varied enormously. Australia recorded the deepest drop, Spain and the United Kingdom followed, and Colombia came fourth, while Botswana, Tunisia, Oman, Bahrain and Greece moved by less than a sixth of a standard deviation. To give the numbers a scale, the authors compared them with ordinary variation: the difference between Sunday and Monday came to about a fifth of a standard deviation, and a heatwave above 40 degrees to about a quarter, which puts the pandemic at four or five times the swing of a normal week.

Recovery behaved differently from the fall. It was slower, it was asymmetric in most countries, and its speed correlated with things the authors could measure only crudely. Countries with more confirmed cases fell further, countries with looser cultural norms fell further, and countries that scored higher on a governance index came back faster. None of those correlations was offered as a cause, and the paper says so twice.

Lockdowns got the most attention and the least effect. Comparing a country with a synthetic twin built from its own past and from similar countries, the authors found moderate to no change in expressed sentiment after an order, with wide variation between countries. Reviewers pointed out that the orders followed the shock rather than preceded it, which makes the estimate hard to read as a clean test of policy, and the authors conceded the timing while keeping the finding.

What the paper establishes is an instrument, and the case for reading it is the case for any instrument. Expressed sentiment on a public platform tracks survey well-being closely enough to be useful and loosely enough to mislead, and the users of any one platform skew young and urban in most countries. Read it as a map of where the shock landed and how long it lasted, and read the lockdown result as a first pass at a question that surveys, slower and more expensive, will have to settle.
