About one in ten colorectal cancers carries a *BRAF* V600E mutation, and those tumours have been the worst behaved in the disease. Chemotherapy alone gives a median survival near a year, and the targeted drugs that work in melanoma have worked poorly here, because a BRAF inhibitor in the colon activates the epidermal growth factor receptor pathway and the tumour grows back within months, which is why a single-agent BRAF inhibitor is not used in the colon even though it works in the skin.

BREAKWATER trial tested the obvious next move: block both. The regimen combined encorafenib, a BRAF inhibitor, with cetuximab, an antibody against that receptor, and mFOLFOX6, the standard chemotherapy backbone, in patients who had received no prior treatment for metastatic disease. Results were presented on 30 May 2025 at the American Society of Clinical Oncology meeting and published in *The New England Journal of Medicine*.

Survival numbers are large for this disease. Median overall survival was 30.3 months against 15.1 months for chemotherapy with or without bevacizumab, a hazard ratio of 0.49 with a confidence interval of 0.38 to 0.63, and *P*<0.0001. That is a 51 per cent reduction in the risk of death in a population that usually lives about a year, and it is the first time a targeted triplet has moved overall survival in this subgroup.

Progression-free survival met the other half of the dual endpoint. The hazard ratio was 0.53, with a confidence interval of 0.41 to 0.68, and median progression-free survival was 12.8 months. The trial was designed with two primary endpoints, which is unusual and which regulators accept when a disease is this aggressive.

Three drugs together is a difficult regimen. Encorafenib causes fatigue, arthralgia, anaemia and skin changes, cetuximab causes a rash that is itself a prognostic sign, and mFOLFOX6 causes neuropathy that accumulates. Adding a targeted pair to chemotherapy raises toxicity in a population that is often older, and the trial's dose intensity data are the part a clinician reads second, after the survival curve and before the cost.

Comparison is standard of care and not a placebo, which is the right design and which limits what can be inferred. Patients on the control arm did not receive a BRAF inhibitor, so the trial answers whether the combination beats chemotherapy and not whether the combination beats chemotherapy plus a BRAF inhibitor given sequentially.

What would settle practice is duration and sequencing. A regimen that works this well in the first line raises the question of what to give in the second, and a targeted combination that costs more than chemotherapy raises the question that every health system asks, which is whether the months bought are worth the price. The survival curve is the argument for paying, and it is a strong one, in a disease where the last decade produced no survival gain at all. The next trial in this subgroup will have to decide whether the triplet is a platform or a first step.
