The world expo opened in Osaka on the thirteenth of April, on an island in the bay that was built for the purpose and will be redeveloped after the fair closes in October. The site is large, the pavilions were designed by architects who were asked for a statement, and the national participants number more than a hundred and fifty. The first week's coverage was mostly about the queues, which is the traditional subject of a world fair, and about the weather, which is the other one.

The form is a century and a half old and its function has changed. The great exhibitions of the nineteenth century were the place where a visitor first saw an electric light, a telephone, a refrigerator, and the fair was the medium through which a new technology entered public life. A visitor today has seen the technology in a video before arriving, and the pavilion has to offer an experience rather than an exhibit, which is why the halls are full of projections and immersive rooms.

The economics are the part that the organizers discuss least. A fair of this size costs a sum measured in billions, the site requires a decade of planning, and the visitors who come are the revenue that pays for part of it. The projection that the organizers published assumed a number of visitors that the first weeks did not reach, and the shortfall is the subject that the local newspapers have been covering since the opening.

The architecture is the durable product. A pavilion that is designed to be dismantled is a pavilion that leaves nothing behind, and the ones that leave something are the ones that are reused, as a museum, a school or a piece of infrastructure. The island itself is the largest object that the fair will leave, and its future use is being negotiated while the fair is still running.

What the fair does for the city is the part that the balance sheet cannot show. Osaka has used the event to rebuild a piece of its waterfront and to extend a metro line, and the infrastructure will outlast the exhibition by decades. The visitors who come for the pavilions will also spend money in the shops and the hotels, and the spending is the argument that every city makes when it bids, and the argument is usually optimistic.

The deeper question is what a world fair is for in an age when every country has a tourism campaign and every company has a launch event. The answer that the organizers give is that the fair is the only place where the participants agree to appear together, and the agreement is a diplomatic object rather than a commercial one, which is why the host government pays for the site and the participants pay for their own pavilions. A hundred and fifty countries in one place for six months is a thing that no platform can organize, and the cost of it is the price of that coincidence.
