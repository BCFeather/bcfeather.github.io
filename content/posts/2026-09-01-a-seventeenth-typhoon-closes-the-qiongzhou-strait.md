Typhoon Nari came ashore on the Leizhou Peninsula late on 31 August, and by the next morning the Qiongzhou Strait had been closed to ferries for the second day, stranding several thousand vehicles at the ports on both sides. The storm was not a monster. Its peak winds stayed near 150 kilometres per hour, which in the South China Sea is a strong but familiar typhoon, and its track took it west across the peninsula rather than north into the dense cities of the Pearl River Delta. What made it notable was the timing. Nari was the seventeenth typhoon of the year, and it arrived on the first day of the autumn surge, the period when the subtropical ridge shifts and storms begin to curve towards the coast rather than out to sea.

The ferry crossing is the obvious casualty. The strait is only about twenty kilometres wide, but it carries a disproportionate share of the island's freight and almost all of its tourist traffic, and there is no bridge. When the wind crosses a threshold, the ferries stop, and everything behind them queues: lorries loaded with vegetables, coaches full of holidaymakers, and the ordinary traffic of an island of ten million people that depends on the mainland for much of what it eats. Port authorities said the service would resume once the wind dropped and the swell decayed, which in practice means a day or two of backlog after the weather clears.

Along the coast the damage was local rather than spectacular. Fishing villages on the eastern side of Hainan reported roofs stripped and cages torn from their moorings, and the provincial government said several thousand people had been moved to schools and village halls before the storm arrived. The pattern is familiar from every typhoon season: the death toll and the property damage are decided less by the peak wind than by whether the evacuation happened early enough and whether the fishing fleet came in. This time the warnings went out two days ahead, and the fleet did come in.

The economics are where the storm will be felt. The autumn surge overlaps with the tail of the summer tourist season and the beginning of the fruit harvest, and a two-day closure of the strait delays both. Hainan's tropical fruit, mango and dragon fruit in particular, moves off the island by lorry and ferry, and a hold of forty-eight hours at the port is enough to shift prices in the wholesale markets of Guangzhou. Nothing about that is dramatic. It is the ordinary arithmetic of an island economy with one throat to swallow through.

Forecasters expect the rest of September to stay busy. The same ridge that steered Nari west is forecast to weaken over the coming week, which typically opens a corridor for storms to recurve towards Guangdong and Fujian, and the sea surface temperatures in the northern South China Sea remain above the level that sustains rapid intensification. The province has already moved its emergency teams into position, and the fishing fleet in the mouth of the Pearl River is under an order to stay in port until further notice. For a coast that has done this every year for centuries, the ritual is well rehearsed; the difficulty is that each storm now lands on a slightly larger, slightly more expensive coastline than the last.
