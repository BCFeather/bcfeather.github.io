---

Triple-negative breast cancer lacks the three receptors that give other subtypes their targets, which left chemotherapy as the only option for decades and made the subtype the one with the worst prognosis. Pembrolizumab added to chemotherapy improved outcomes in the subset whose tumours express PD-L1, and the question became whether a better chemotherapy partner would improve them further. Sacituzumab govitecan is an antibody-drug conjugate against a trophoblast antigen that is expressed in most triple-negative tumours.

The ASCENT-04 trial randomised 443 patients with previously untreated, PD-L1-positive, advanced triple-negative breast cancer to sacituzumab govitecan plus pembrolizumab or to chemotherapy plus pembrolizumab. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival by blinded independent central review, and the trial was open-label, which is unavoidable when one arm is an infusion of a conjugate and the other is conventional chemotherapy. Results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 21 January, funded by Gilead.

Progression-free survival was 11.2 months with the conjugate against 7.8 months with chemotherapy, a hazard ratio of 0.65 with a confidence interval from 0.51 to 0.84. Objective responses occurred in 60 per cent against 53 per cent, and among responders the median duration was 16.5 months against 9.2 months, which is the difference between a response that carries a patient through a year and one that does not.

Overall survival data were immature, so the trial cannot yet say whether the progression-free gain translates. That is a familiar pattern in oncology, and it is a real limitation rather than a formality, since drugs that delay progression without extending life impose toxicity and cost for a benefit that patients may not value.

Toxicity was comparable in frequency and different in kind. Grade 3 or higher adverse events occurred in 71 per cent of patients on the conjugate against 70 per cent on chemotherapy, but discontinuation because of adverse events occurred in 12 per cent against 31 per cent, which suggests that the conjugate's side effects were more manageable even though they were as common. Adverse events leading to death occurred in 3 per cent of both groups.

Interpretation has to account for the open-label design and for the fact that the control arm is a regimen that many oncologists already consider suboptimal in this setting. A trial that beats a modest comparator establishes superiority and leaves open the question of how the combination compares with the best available care, which in some health systems now includes a different conjugate.

The practical question is sequencing. A conjugate that works in the first line will be used in the first line, and the patients who progress will then need something else, which is a problem that the field has not solved for any conjugate. For patients and clinicians, the result adds months and a better tolerated alternative, which is a meaningful gain in a subtype that has had few.
