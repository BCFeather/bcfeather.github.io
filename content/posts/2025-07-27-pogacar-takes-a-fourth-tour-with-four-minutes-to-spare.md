Four titles in six years is a record that invites comparison with the riders of another era, and Pogacar's fourth Tour, sealed in Paris on 27 July with a margin of 4 minutes 24 seconds over Vingegaard, is the one that settles the argument about who is the strongest stage racer of his generation.

Turning point came in the second week. Pogacar took the yellow jersey on a mountain finish, defended it in the time trial, and then extended the lead on the climb to the summit finish that had been designed as the decisive stage, and the gap that had been seconds became minutes in a single afternoon. A margin built in one afternoon and defended for eight days says more about the strength of the winner than about the tactics of the race, and the two attacks that failed in the third week confirmed the reading.

Vingegaard was the only rider within ten minutes. The Danish rider attacked twice in the third week, once on a descent and once on the final climb, and each attack was answered within a kilometre, which is what a race looks like when the strongest rider also has the strongest team and a directeur who can count the seconds that remain to the summit.

Team is half the result. UAE Team Emirates controlled the flat stages, set the pace on the climbs and chased every breakaway that threatened the jersey, and a squad that costs more than most of its rivals combined is a squad that can afford to spend riders on a single objective. A rival who is answered twice has run out of ideas, and the podium behind him was settled by a time trial rather than by a climb, which is the part of a modern Tour that rewards the rider who can hold a fixed position for an hour.

Route suited the winner. Twenty-five kilometres of time trial, four summit finishes and no cobbles is a course that rewards a climber who can ride alone, and the organisers' choice was criticised in the winter and vindicated in July by a race that was decided uphill.

Field was thinner than the headline suggests. Several contenders crashed out in the first week, two teams lost their leaders to illness, and the podium was settled before the final weekend, which is a race that produces a clear winner and a less interesting month. A route with no cobbles and no crosswinds removes the two stages where a weaker climber can lose the race by misfortune, which is an argument the organisers made in the winter and the result answered in July.

What would settle the comparison is next year's route and the arrival of the next generation. A course with more time trialling would favour a different rider, and a season in which the young climbers close the gap would tell us whether four titles is a plateau or a midpoint.
