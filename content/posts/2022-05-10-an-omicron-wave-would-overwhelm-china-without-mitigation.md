China's zero-COVID policy was justified by the argument that its health system could not absorb a wave, and that argument was made without a quantified projection. A model calibrated to the Shanghai outbreak supplied one. The projection was made at a moment when the country had kept infections near zero for two years and had accumulated little natural immunity outside the vaccinated population.

The model is an age-structured stochastic compartmental system, fitted to the initial growth phase of the Shanghai outbreak in early 2022, and it projects cases, hospitalizations, intensive care demand and deaths under several mitigation scenarios. The paper appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 10 May 2022. A wave that is concentrated in the unvaccinated elderly is a wave that fills intensive care beds, because the beds are occupied by the people whose immune response is weakest, and the age structure of the population is the input that dominates the output.

With the immunity conferred by the vaccination campaign of March 2022 and no change in behaviour, an Omicron wave would produce an intensive care peak 15.6 times the existing capacity, and about 1.55 million deaths. The number of deaths is the projection that made the paper widely cited in policy discussions. A country that has suppressed transmission for two years has almost no immunity from infection, and the vaccine coverage is concentrated in the young, which is the opposite of the distribution that protects a health system.

Inputs include age-specific vaccine coverage, efficacy against different endpoints, waning, antiviral therapies and non-pharmaceutical interventions. Each of those is an input with an uncertainty, and the output is a distribution rather than a point. The intensive care demand is the binding constraint, since a bed can be built and a ventilator can be bought but a nurse cannot be trained in a month, and the ratio of demand to capacity is the quantity that a health ministry can act on. Each of those parameters carries an interval, and the projection propagates them, so the 1.55 million figure is a central estimate of a wide distribution and not a certainty.

Protecting vulnerable people through vaccine and antiviral access, together with continued non-pharmaceutical measures, could prevent the system from being overwhelmed. That is the scenario the authors recommend, and it is a combination rather than a single lever.

Calibration is to Shanghai, a city with a particular age structure, a particular hospital capacity and a particular outbreak, and the national projection scales those parameters. A country as heterogeneous as China would not experience a uniform wave. A policy that relaxes without building capacity and without protecting the vulnerable is the scenario the model was designed to price, and the price is the number that made it influential. A city of 25 million people with a modern hospital network is not a rural county with one, and the national average understates the gap in the places that would be least able to absorb a wave.

Such a model is a planning instrument and not a forecast, and its value is in showing which parameter dominates the outcome. The 15.6-fold figure is the reason that the argument about reopening was about hospital capacity and not about case counts.
