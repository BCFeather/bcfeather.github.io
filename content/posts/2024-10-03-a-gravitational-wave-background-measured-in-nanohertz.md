Millisecond pulsars are the most stable clocks that nature provides, and that stability is what makes them useful for something other than timing. A pulsar is a neutron star spinning at hundreds of revolutions a second and sweeping a beam of radio emission past the Earth, and the arrival times of those pulses can be predicted to a precision of microseconds over years. Any departure from the prediction is a residual, and a residual that appears in every pulsar at once, with a particular pattern in its arrival, is the signature of a gravitational wave passing through the galaxy between the star and the telescope. The precision is the point: a clock that loses a microsecond in a decade can register a change in the path length smaller than the width of an atom. A neutron star that old has been through the mill of accretion and spin-up, and the ones chosen for timing are the survivors.

The signature that the pulsar timing arrays look for is not the wave itself but its effect on the clock. A gravitational wave stretches and squeezes the space along the path, so the pulses arrive slightly early or slightly late, and the shift depends on the direction of the pulsar relative to the wave. A single pulsar cannot separate that effect from its own rotational noise, which is why the method needs an array of them and why the analysis is a correlation rather than a detection. The angle is computed on the sky instead of in the data, so the correlation can be evaluated for every pair however far apart the two pulsars sit. The three arrays that now publish do not share telescopes or software, and their agreement is therefore a check on the method as much as on the sky.

What the arrays reported, after fifteen years of data from telescopes in North America, Europe, Australia, India and China, is a correlation of that kind. Pulsar pairs separated by a small angle on the sky show residuals that vary together, pairs separated by a large angle do not, and the curve that describes the fall-off is the one predicted for a gravitational-wave background. The shape of that curve, named for the two theorists who derived it, is the evidence; the amplitude is the measurement. Three arrays published within a few months, their curves overlapped within the error bars, and that agreement is what moved the result from a claim to a measurement. The Hellings-Downs curve is a prediction from general relativity alone, which is why its shape is worth more than any single detection.

The frequency of the background is set by the size of the orbits that produce it. A binary of supermassive black holes separated by a fraction of a light-year emits at nanohertz frequencies, with a period of years and not milliseconds, and no other source in the catalogue of plausible emitters sits in that band. Cosmic strings, a phase transition in the early universe and primordial tensor modes have all been proposed as alternatives, and the current data cannot separate them from the black hole population on amplitude alone. Anisotropy is where the candidates diverge, since a population of binaries follows the large-scale structure and a cosmological background does not. A binary that stalls at the final parsec, as some models require, would leave the band emptier than the data suggest.

Amplitude is where the interpretation lives. A background built from the mergers of galaxies should carry a characteristic strain that scales with the fourth power of the mass, and the value the arrays measure sits near the upper end of what population models predict, which is interesting and not decisive. The measurement is also isotropic as far as it goes: the correlation is averaged over the sky, so a single loud source would look like a background unless the analysis is done in a way that can pick out anisotropy. A background built from mergers should also steepen towards lower frequencies, and the measured slope fits that expectation without yet excluding a flat spectrum. The amplitude also depends on how many galaxies merge, and that number is a free parameter in the models instead of a measurement.

Almost every systematic in the analysis is a claim about noise. A pulsar's residuals include the wandering of its rotation, the dispersion of the signal by the interstellar medium, the errors in the solar system ephemeris, and the clock at the observatory, and each of those has to be modelled before a common signal can be seen. The arrays have converged on the same amplitude from independent data sets and independent pipelines, which is the strongest statement the field can currently make, and it is still a statement about a correlation and not about a source. Detecting a common signal against red noise is the hard part of the work, and the arrays published their noise models beside their results so that the models could be argued with. The interstellar medium is a particular nuisance at low frequencies, because the dispersion changes as the line of sight to a pulsar drifts.

More pulsars and better clocks are the next step. Radio telescopes now coming into operation will roughly triple the number of timing-grade pulsars, and the improvement in sensitivity is what would let the arrays resolve an individual source, or place a limit on the isotropy that would rule some models out. What the field has at the moment is a background that nobody predicted in detail and nobody has explained to everyone's satisfaction, measured with a clock that took fifteen years to build. The array that comes next will be large enough to ask whether the background is a crowd or one loud source, which is the question the current data cannot answer. Improving the clock means more than more pulsars: it means a timing model that accounts for the plasma and for the motion of the Earth better than the current one.
