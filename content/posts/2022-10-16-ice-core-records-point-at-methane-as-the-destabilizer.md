description: "A stochastic model fitted to eight hundred thousand years of Antarctic ice-core records finds methane and nitrous oxide pushing glacial cycles towards instability while carbon dioxide and temperature pull back."
---

The EPICA Dome C core carries eight hundred thousand years of Antarctic ice, and with it a record of atmospheric carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and a temperature proxy. Each series shows the hundred-thousand-year glacial cycle, and each also carries fluctuations across a wide band of shorter periods. The four records are sampled at different depths and resolutions, and aligning them onto a common age scale is a precondition for any coupling analysis, since the depth-age relation at Dome C is itself a model whose uncertainty grows with age. Whether those fluctuations reflect noise or coupling between the gases is a question that a spectral analysis alone will not answer.

Work posted to arXiv on 1 October 2022 fits each series to a stochastic model and then couples them with linear terms, a procedure that has to be justified variable by variable because a coupling can be an artefact of a shared trend. The authors first apply a multifractal, time-weighted detrended fluctuation analysis, which reports near red-noise behaviour below the hundred-thousand-year scale and near white-noise behaviour above it, in all four records. That difference in behaviour is what allows a one-dimensional periodic stochastic system to describe each series separately.

Coupling the four systems with linear terms turns the fit into a statement about influence. Methane and nitrous oxide emerge with significant destabilizing contributions, while carbon dioxide and temperature contribute smaller stabilizing ones. Read as a decomposition of the cycle's variability, that assigns the pushes and pulls to different gases rather than to the system as a whole. The coefficients carry signs and magnitudes, so a gas can be destabilizing at one period and neutral at another.

The method is worth separating from the conclusion. A linear coupling fitted to four noisy series can recover a direction of influence without proving a mechanism, and the authors treat their coefficients as diagnostics of the record rather than as physical rates. They also test whether simulated series reproduce the statistics of the observed ones, which is the check that a stochastic fit can offer; a fit that matched the variance of each gas but missed their cross-correlation would fail it.

What the analysis cannot do is resolve the transitions themselves. A glacial termination is a rapid, nonlinear event, and a model built for stationary statistics describes the approach to an event better than the event, which the authors acknowledge by limiting their claims to the variability around the cycle, and by presenting the couplings as one description of the record among several that a reader might fit. Whether the fitted couplings change across a termination is not reported.

For paleoclimate the result suggests where to look next: methane's role in the glacial cycle has been argued from wetland extent and from ice-core records of the gas itself, and a model that gives it a destabilizing coefficient points at the intervals around terminations as the places to measure it.
