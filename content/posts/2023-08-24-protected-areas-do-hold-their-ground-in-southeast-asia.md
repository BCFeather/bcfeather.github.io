description: "A survey of protected areas across mega-diverse southeast Asia finds that they slow the loss of vertebrate biodiversity, according to work published in *Nature* on 24 August 2023."
---

A protected area is a line on a map and a claim about land use, and its effect on biodiversity has been argued more than measured. A measurement needs a comparison between what is inside the boundary and what is outside it.

The survey in *Nature*, 24 August 2023 makes that comparison across southeast Asia. The authors assemble vertebrate records from protected areas and from matched unprotected sites, and compare the trends in each.

The result is that protection helps. Sites inside protected areas retain more species and lose them more slowly than comparable sites outside, and the effect is largest where the surrounding land is under pressure.

The design is the study's strength. A comparison of a protected site with its neighbour controls for climate and for history, and the control is what makes the difference attributable to protection.

The variation is the second result. Some protected areas perform well and others do not, and the difference correlates with staffing, with funding and with the area's size.

The species groups differ. A large mammal's population responds to hunting pressure and a bird's to habitat, and the authors' separation of the groups shows which threat the protection addresses.

The limitation is the counterfactual. A site was protected because someone chose it, and a chosen site may differ from an unchosen one in ways that the matching cannot remove.

What the paper does not show is a global answer. Southeast Asia's forests, its species and its pressures are specific, and a conclusion from the region is a conclusion about the region.

The policy reading is direct. An underfunded protected area is a boundary without an effect, and the correlation between funding and outcome is the paper's most actionable finding.

For conservation, the value is an evaluation rather than an assertion. The claim that parks work has been a premise, and a matched comparison is what turns it into a result.

The matching is the design's core. A protected site is paired with a similar unprotected one on climate, elevation and soil, and the pairing is what allows the difference to be read as an effect.

The pressures are the context. A park surrounded by cleared land faces edge effects and hunting, and a park inside a forested landscape faces less, and the authors' stratification accounts for both.

The time series matters. A comparison at one moment measures a state, and a comparison over years measures a trend. The authors use records from repeated surveys.

The species' traits explain the pattern. A wide-ranging animal needs area beyond a boundary, and a small one survives inside it. The trait analysis is what tells a manager which species a park can hold.

The funding is the practical lever. A ranger's salary is a small number, and the paper's correlation between budget and outcome is an argument that money spent on enforcement returns biodiversity.

The comparison with the global literature is sobering. Other regions' evaluations have been more pessimistic, and the difference may be in the pressure's intensity rather than in the protection's value.
