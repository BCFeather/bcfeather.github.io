date: "2023-08-24T09:20:00+08:00"

description: "A matched comparison of vertebrate records inside and outside protected areas across mega-diverse southeast Asia finds that boundaries slow the loss of species."
---

A protected area is a line on a map and a claim about land use, and its effect on biodiversity has been argued more than measured. Measurement needs a comparison between what is inside the boundary and what is outside it.

In *Nature* on 24 August 2023, that comparison is made across southeast Asia. The authors assemble vertebrate records from protected areas and from matched unprotected sites, and compare trends in each. Protection turns out to help. Sites inside protected areas retain more species and lose them more slowly than comparable sites outside, and the effect is largest where surrounding land is under pressure.

Design is the study's strength. Comparing a protected site with its neighbour controls for climate and history, and the control is what makes the difference attributable to protection. Matching is the core, since a protected site is paired with a similar unprotected one on climate, elevation and soil.

Variation is the second result. Some protected areas perform well and others do not, and the difference correlates with staffing, funding and size. Funding is the practical lever, because a ranger's salary is a small number and the correlation between budget and outcome argues that money spent on enforcement returns biodiversity.

Species groups differ. A large mammal's population responds to hunting and a bird's to habitat, so separating the groups shows which threat protection addresses. Traits explain the pattern, since a wide-ranging animal needs area beyond a boundary and a small one survives inside it.

Counterfactual sets the limitation. A site was protected because someone chose it, and a chosen site may differ from an unchosen one in ways matching cannot remove. Time series matters too, because one moment measures a state and repeated surveys measure a trend, and the authors use records from repeated surveys.

No global answer follows, because the region's forests, species and pressures are specific. Policy reads it directly, since an underfunded protected area is a boundary without an effect, and that correlation is the most actionable finding. Conservation gains an evaluation rather than an assertion, since the claim that parks work has been a premise and a matched comparison turns it into a result. Other regions' evaluations are more pessimistic, and the difference may lie in pressure intensity rather than in the value of protection. A before-and-after comparison around the moment a site was gazetted would remove the siting bias that matching cannot, and a set of parks with such records would be the strongest test the question admits, since the counterfactual would be the same land under the previous regime. Whether the effect survives when the surrounding land is already cleared is the sharper question, since a park in a sea of pasture has a boundary that animals cross, and the paper's stratification only partly answers it. A ranger's patrol record would be the direct measure of enforcement, and pairing it with the biodiversity trend would test the mechanism the correlation only suggests.
