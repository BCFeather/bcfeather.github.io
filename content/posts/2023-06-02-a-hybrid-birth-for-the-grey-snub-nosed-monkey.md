A species is usually drawn as a branching line, and hybridization as a complication the line survives. Genomic data have made the complication common, and the grey snub-nosed monkey is one of its clearest cases.

In *Science* on 2 June 2023, the case is made. The authors compare the monkey's genome with those of its relatives, and find that its ancestry is a mixture of two lineages rather than a single branch.

Mixture structure supplies the evidence. A genome assembled from two sources carries segments from each, and the lengths of those segments record how many generations of recombination have passed since the mixture formed. Dating follows from that, because a long segment indicates a recent contribution and a short one an old one, and the distribution of lengths gives the hybridization a date.

Parents of the mixture can be identified. The analysis places the contributing lineages among the monkey's relatives, and that placement turns a mixture into a history. Selection adds a second finding, since some inherited segments carry genes the recipient lineage lacks, and those are the segments natural selection would keep if they conferred an advantage.

Taxonomy takes a correction. A species arising by hybridization has a history a tree cannot represent, and a conservation plan treating it as a single lineage may misjudge its diversity. Demography frames the event, because hybridization needs two lineages in the same place at the same time, and climate-driven range shifts are the usual mechanism, which the reconstruction of past ranges supplies.

Plausibility follows from the habitat. Snub-nosed monkeys live in high forests that shifted with climate, and a shifting range brings lineages into contact. Method is where the advance lies, since a single reference genome cannot show a mixture while a comparison across many relatives can, and the panel of primate genomes is what makes the inference possible. Sample size sets the limitation, because one genome describes one individual and a hybrid population's structure appears only when several are compared. Fertility stays unestablished, as a hybrid's viability is a separate question from its existence, and the genomic evidence speaks to ancestry rather than to the offspring's fitness.

Evolutionary biology gains a case where the exception is the rule, since hybridization has been treated as a rarity and a primate owing its existence to it shows the tree to be a simplification. Conservation reads it practically, because a species with two source lineages has diversity a population count misses, and a breeding programme pairing unrelated animals may still pair close relatives within a lineage; stabilization of a fertile hybrid into a species is rare, and this appears to be one such case. A further test would look for the same signature in the parents' ranges today, since a hybridization that happened once may be one of several contacts the same two lineages made. Whether the two lineages still meet anywhere is the question a survey of the monkey's present range would answer, and a contact zone would show whether the event was a one-off.
