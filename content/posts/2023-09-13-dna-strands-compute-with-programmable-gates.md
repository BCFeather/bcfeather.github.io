A DNA computer uses strands as its wires and its gates, and the field's circuits have been small. A programmable array that can implement different functions is a step from a demonstration to an architecture.

The array in *Nature*, 13 September 2023 is that. The authors build a gate array from DNA origami tiles, program it with input strands, and run circuits that implement logic and arithmetic.

The programmability is the advance. A fixed circuit does one function, and an array whose gates are configured by strands does many, and the configuration is the paper's contribution.

The self-assembly is the fabrication. A tile carries a gate and a connector, and the tiles assemble into a lattice whose connections the strands specify.

The computation's speed is the weakness. A DNA circuit's reactions take minutes, and a computation is a sequence of them, so the architecture is slow compared with silicon.

The application is not arithmetic. A slow circuit is useful where a computation is coupled to a molecular recognition, and a diagnostic that decides on a set of markers is the niche.

The error rate is the constraint. A strand that mispairs produces a wrong output, and the authors' redundancy is what keeps the circuit reliable.

The scale is the limitation. A lattice of a few hundred tiles is a circuit of modest size, and a larger array needs a fabrication that the field has not demonstrated.

What the paper does not show is a general-purpose computer. A programmable array that runs a few circuits is a platform, and a compiler for arbitrary functions is the next step.

For molecular computing, the value is an architecture rather than a circuit. The field's results have been individual computations, and an array is a substrate for many.

The tile's design is the gate. A origami tile presents strands that react with an input, and the reaction's product is the output that a neighbour reads.

The connector is the wiring. A tile's edge carries a sequence that binds a specific neighbour, and the set of edges is the circuit's topology.

The input's encoding is the program. A set of strands represents a value, and the array's gates transform it according to the strands' sequences.

The leakage is the error. A gate that reacts with a wrong input produces a spurious output, and the authors' design of orthogonal sequences is what limits it.

The readout is the interface. A circuit's output must be detected, and a fluorescence or a gel readout is the laboratory method.

The comparison with silicon is unfair but useful. A DNA circuit is slower by orders of magnitude and operates in water, and the comparison identifies the niche rather than a competitor.
The gate's logic is a strand displacement. An input strand that invades a duplex displaces an output, and the displacement is the reaction that implements a gate.
The circuit's depth is the speed's limit. Each layer of gates adds a reaction time, and a deep circuit is slow even by the standards of molecular computing.
