---

Meeting on 16 March, the governing council raised the three policy rates by half a point. The deposit rate reached 3 per cent, the highest since 2008, and the main refinancing rate 3.5 per cent. The decision had been flagged in February, when the bank said it would take a large step unless the inflation outlook changed. It did not change, and the step was delivered. The three rates are the deposit rate, the refinancing rate and the marginal lending rate.

Acknowledging the tension in banking markets, the statement said the bank was monitoring liquidity. It also said the inflation outlook was still too high and that further increases were likely. That combination, a rate rise with a warning, is unusual and it was read as a signal that the bank would not be diverted by a crisis in another jurisdiction. The bank's balance sheet was 8 trillion euros, and it had begun to shrink it in March.

Staff projections published the same day cut growth for the year to 1 per cent from 1.1 and raised the inflation forecast to 5.3 per cent from 5.1. The projection for 2024 was 2.9 per cent, still above the target, and the bank said wage growth would keep services inflation high. The forecast for core inflation was raised to 4.6 per cent. The projections were cut for growth and raised for inflation, which is the combination that worries the bank.

Market rates fell after the decision, because investors read the warning as a reason to expect a pause. The two-year German yield dropped 0.2 points, and the spread with Italy narrowed. The bank's president said in the press conference that the bank had no tools for a credit crunch and that governments should use fiscal policy if needed. Credit default swaps for European banks widened by 30 basis points in the week.

Two American banks had failed in the previous five days, and a third was being sold over the weekend. The timing was the difficulty. European lenders had limited direct exposure, but the funding markets had tightened and several banks had drawn on the central bank's facilities. The bank said it had seen no sign of a systemic problem. The third bank was sold to a larger rival over the weekend for a nominal sum.

Inflation in the currency area had peaked at 10.6 per cent in October and was 8.5 per cent in February. The bank had raised rates by 3.5 points since July 2022, the fastest cycle in its history. The bank's target is 2 per cent, and the president said the last mile would be the hardest. The bank's balance sheet had grown by 3 trillion euros since 2015, mostly through bond purchases. The next meeting was in May, and the bank raised by a quarter point then. Two members of the governing council had argued for a pause in March, according to accounts published later, and the vote was not unanimous. The split became public in the autumn, when the minutes were released.
