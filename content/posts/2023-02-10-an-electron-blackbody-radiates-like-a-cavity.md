A blackbody is an ideal absorber, and Planck's law for its radiation started quantum mechanics. The concept is defined for photons in a cavity, and the question of whether an electron gas can be made to behave as an ideal absorber has been theoretical.

The device in *PNAS*, 7 February 2023 makes one. Ke Zhang, Guo Chen, Kaili Jiang and colleagues use carbon nanotubes, whose electronic structure gives a broad absorption across a range of energies, and measure the radiation spectrum that the electrons emit as they are heated.

The spectrum follows a thermal form. An electron gas at a temperature emits with a distribution that has the same mathematical shape as a photon blackbody's, and the paper's measurements show that shape over the range the device covers.

The nanotube's structure is what allows the analogy. A carbon nanotube is a one-dimensional conductor with many subbands, and a population of nanotubes with varied diameters absorbs across a continuum rather than at discrete energies. That continuum is the condition for a blackbody.

The temperature is the control parameter. Heating the electrons, rather than the lattice, is possible because the electron-phonon coupling in a nanotube is weak enough that the two can be out of equilibrium. A hot electron gas radiating while the lattice stays cool is a system in which the radiation comes from the electrons alone.

The application is detection. A calibrated electron blackbody is a source whose spectrum is known, and a known source is what a radiometer needs for calibration in the infrared, where blackbody standards are difficult to build and to transport.

The measurement's difficulty is the coupling to the outside. An electron gas radiates into the cavity modes around it, and the spectrum that emerges depends on the environment. The paper's design places the nanotubes in a geometry that approximates free space over the band of interest.

What remains open is the efficiency. A device that heats electrons and radiates is a heater with a light output, and the fraction of the electrical power that becomes radiation is the number that a practical source would need.

The theoretical interest is the analogy's limits. An electron gas has a Fermi surface and a degeneracy temperature, and a blackbody of fermions behaves differently from a blackbody of bosons at low temperature. The paper's regime is above the degeneracy temperature, and the quantum regime is a separate problem.

For metrology, the value is a source that can be built small. A blackbody the size of a chip would calibrate detectors in places where a cavity never fits.

The spectrum's shape is the test of the analogy. A photon blackbody's curve is fixed by Planck's law, and the electron gas's curve matches it over the measured band, which is the evidence that the emission is thermal rather than a set of discrete transitions.
The device's geometry matters for the comparison. A nanotube film is an open structure with a large surface area, and radiation escapes from the whole volume rather than from a surface, which is what an ideal absorber would do.
