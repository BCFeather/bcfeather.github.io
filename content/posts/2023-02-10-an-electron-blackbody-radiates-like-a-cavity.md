A blackbody is an ideal absorber, and Planck's law for its radiation started quantum mechanics. The concept is defined for photons in a cavity, and whether an electron gas can be made to behave as an ideal absorber has stayed theoretical.

In *PNAS* on 7 February 2023, one is made. Ke Zhang, Guo Chen, Kaili Jiang and colleagues use carbon nanotubes, whose electronic structure gives a broad absorption across a range of energies, and measure the radiation spectrum that the electrons emit as they are heated.

Thermal is the shape of the spectrum. An electron gas at a temperature emits with a distribution that has the same mathematical form as a photon blackbody's, and the measurements show that form over the band the device covers, which is the evidence that the emission is thermal rather than a set of discrete transitions.

Because a carbon nanotube is a one-dimensional conductor with many subbands, a population of them with varied diameters absorbs across a continuum rather than at discrete energies, and that continuum is the condition for a blackbody. Heating the electrons rather than the lattice is possible because electron-phonon coupling in a nanotube is weak enough for the two to sit out of equilibrium, so the radiation comes from the electrons alone while the lattice stays cool.

Coupling to the outside is the measurement's difficulty, since an electron gas radiates into the cavity modes around it and the spectrum that emerges depends on the environment, which is why the nanotubes are placed in a geometry that approximates free space over the band of interest. Geometry matters for the comparison as well, because a nanotube film is an open structure with a large surface area, and radiation escapes from the whole volume rather than from a surface, as an ideal absorber would manage.

Efficiency remains open, because a device that heats electrons and radiates is a heater with a light output, and the fraction of electrical power that becomes radiation is the number a practical source would need. The analogy has limits, since an electron gas carries a Fermi surface and a degeneracy temperature, and a blackbody of fermions behaves differently from one of bosons at low temperature; this work sits above the degeneracy temperature, and the quantum regime is a separate problem. Metrology gains a source that can be built small, because a blackbody the size of a chip would calibrate detectors in places where a cavity never fits, and a calibrated emitter whose spectrum is known is exactly what a radiometer needs in the infrared.

Absolute radiance is what the spectrum cannot fix, because an emitter calibrated by its shape still needs a reference, and the reference an infrared radiometer wants is exactly what metrology lacks. A cavity that emits without a material has one more advantage, because the same structure can be retuned by changing its size and the radiation follows.
