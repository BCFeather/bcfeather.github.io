Duchenne muscular dystrophy has been transformed at the genetic level by exon-skipping drugs and by steroids, and the transformation has been concentrated in the years before the wheelchair. Once the disease reaches its later stage, the arms follow the legs, and the loss of upper-limb function is what turns independence into dependence. Therapies aimed at that stage are scarce, because the muscle is already largely replaced by fat and fibrosis. Earlier attempts at this stage of the disease failed on endpoints that were too coarse to detect a slowing.

Deramiocel is an allogeneic cell product derived from cardiosphere-derived cells, given intravenously every three months. The HOPE-3 trial, reported in *The Lancet* on 30 July 2026, enrolled 106 boys and young men aged 10 to 22 with advanced disease at 20 sites in the United States, randomizing 54 to deramiocel and 52 to placebo. The primary endpoint was the change in the Performance of Upper Limb scale at 12 months.

At one year, upper-limb decline was 54 per cent slower on deramiocel than on placebo, and elbow function declined 65 per cent more slowly. The primary endpoint's least-squares mean difference in percentage change was 4.55 points, with a confidence interval from 0.47 to 8.63 and a *P* value of 0.029. Cardiac function did not differ across the whole cohort, though it was better preserved in the 64 participants who already had cardiomyopathy and usable scans. The scale is a functional one, and a 4.55-point difference on a scale of this kind is a modest gain in absolute terms even when the percentage looks large.

The mechanism is immunological rather than genetic. The infused cells do not restore dystrophin, and they do not engraft into muscle in any meaningful number; what they appear to do is shift the inflammatory environment that drives fibrosis. That explains both the effect size, which is modest, and the cardiac signal, since the same inflammation is at work in the heart.

Three limits matter. The trial is small for a phase 3 study, and an effect measured on a functional scale in 106 patients is fragile to the choice of analysis. The cardiac findings come from subgroups that were not powered for them, and two of the comparisons rest on 22 participants with paired scans. Open-label extension data will show whether the slowing persists or merely delays a decline that resumes. The trial also enrolled patients who were still able to lift objects, which makes the endpoint's interpretation depend on baseline severity.

Approval would make deramiocel the first therapy for this stage of the disease, and its practical value depends on logistics. Quarterly outpatient infusions are easier than weekly injections and harder than a daily pill, and the product is allogeneic, so it can be stocked rather than manufactured per patient. The cost of that convenience is the question every health system will ask.
