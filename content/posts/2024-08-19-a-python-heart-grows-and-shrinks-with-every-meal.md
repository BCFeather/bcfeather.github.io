Constricting pythons eat rarely and enormously, and after a meal their heart can grow by half again within days before returning to its resting size. The response is the fastest reversible hypertrophy known in a vertebrate, and it has been studied as a model for what limits growth in a mammalian heart. Metabolic rate rises several-fold after a meal, and the heart has to keep pace with the digestion that follows.

A paper in *PNAS* on 19 August 2024 takes the response apart at three levels. The authors measure force in isolated myofibrils, calcium handling in cardiac tissue, and the epigenetic state of the muscle cells after feeding. Measuring three levels in one animal lets the authors ask which change causes which rather than only reporting that all of them occur.

Force rises without a change in sarcomere ultrastructure. The filaments are the same length and the same arrangement, so the increase in output is not the result of new contractile material being laid down at a different scale, and the energy cost of contraction does not rise with it. A thicker wall would raise force at a cost in filling, and this heart avoids that trade by leaving the wall's structure alone.

Calcium transients lengthen after feeding, while the sensitivity of the myofibrils to calcium stays put. A longer transient keeps the channels open for a larger fraction of each beat, which increases force without demanding a stronger signal or more ATP per contraction. Prolonging a transient is cheaper than enlarging it, since the same calcium is used for longer instead of more of it being pumped in.

Titin is the third lever. Feeding reduces the tension that titin-based springs contribute, and the tissue becomes less stiff, which lets the ventricle fill at a lower pressure and accommodate a larger stroke volume. Stiffer tissue resists filling, so the change in titin is what allows the ventricle to accept the volume a meal demands.

The epigenetic layer is where reversibility lives. Sirtuin activity falls after feeding, chromatin accessibility rises, and the open state is enriched for binding sites of transcription factors Yin Yang 1 and NRF1, which is a plausible route to a transient transcriptional programme. Reversibility then follows from the chromatin state, which can close again once the meal is digested, rather than from muscle that cannot be removed.

Whether the same levers work in mammals is open, since a python's heart is anatomically different and its metabolic range is far wider. The paper's value is that it identifies a small set of mechanisms that can be tested one at a time. For cardiac medicine the attraction is the reversibility. A hypertrophy that can be switched off would be worth understanding, and this animal switches one off every time it digests. A drug that copied any one of them would have to be reversible, or it would trade a temporary need for a permanent change.
