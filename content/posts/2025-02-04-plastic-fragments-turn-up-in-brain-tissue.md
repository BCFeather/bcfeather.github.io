A paper published in Nature Medicine in early February reported that every human brain sample its authors examined contained microscopic plastic fragments, with concentrations higher than those found in liver or kidney tissue. The median concentration was on the order of a few thousand micrograms per gram of tissue, and polyethylene was the most common polymer identified. The samples came from autopsies and from a tissue bank, and the study compared them with samples collected decades earlier. The comparison across decades found concentrations rising over time, which is the part of the result that reads as a trend rather than as a snapshot.

The method explains both the strength and the limits of the finding. Pyrolysis gas chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry heats a sample until the polymers break into identifiable fragments, and the fragments are counted. The technique measures mass rather than particle number, so it can say how much polymer is present and not how many pieces or how large they are.

The correlation with dementia is the part that drew attention. Concentrations were higher in the brains of people who had been diagnosed with dementia, and the difference was large enough to be visible in a small sample. Correlation of that kind is not causation: a brain that is already inflamed may clear particles more slowly, and a damaged blood-brain barrier may admit more of them. The dementia finding came from a subset of a few dozen cases, which is enough to raise a question and not enough to answer it.

Exposure routes are the practical question. Polymers reach the body through food packaging, through drinking water, through airborne fibres and through medical devices, and the smallest particles cross the gut and the lung into the bloodstream. A brain is on the other side of a barrier that is supposed to exclude them, and the presence of particles there suggests the barrier is more permeable than the model assumed.

Contamination is a serious methodological worry for the whole field. A laboratory that handles plastic equipment while measuring plastic particles can measure itself, and the studies that have been most careful about blanks have generally reported lower concentrations than the studies that were less careful. The authors of the February paper reported procedural controls, and independent replication is what will settle the number. Blood samples from the same donors carried particles too, and the ratio between blood and brain is what would tell a toxicologist where the particles accumulate.

What the finding does not support is advice. Avoiding plastic entirely is not possible in an industrialized economy, and the exposure that matters most may be the one in the air of a kitchen rather than the one in a bottle. The useful conclusion is that the measurement now exists, and that a measurement which did not exist five years ago is the precondition for deciding whether the exposure is worth regulating.
