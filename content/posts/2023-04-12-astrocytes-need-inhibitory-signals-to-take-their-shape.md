Astrocytes are the most numerous cells in the brain, and their shape is their function. A branched astrocyte contacts many synapses, a flat one contacts few, and how a progenitor becomes branched has long been treated as a question about intrinsic programmes, answerable from the cell's own genes.

In *Nature* in April 2023, the answer arrives from outside the cell. Inhibitory input, acting through a GABA-B receptor on the glial membrane, directs astrocyte morphogenesis, and without that signal the cells fail to branch at all.

Because GABA is the transmitter of inhibitory neurons, a GABA-B receptor on an astrocyte is a route by which a neuron's activity reaches a glial cell's developmental programme. Timing makes the route consequential: astrocytes branch while circuits are forming, so a signal that arrives with circuit activity couples the shape of the glia to the wiring of the neurons around it.

Receptor activation drives transcription. The programme it switches on changes the cell's cytoskeleton and its adhesion, and the paper identifies components of it, which is what turns a correlation between activity and shape into a mechanism. Glutamate from excitatory neurons does not produce the same effect, so inhibition's developmental role is not merely to suppress.

Shaping the glia in turn shapes the circuit, since a developing circuit that produces inhibition builds the glia that will later support it, and the loop is a form of self-organization. Two qualifications follow. Mouse cortex is not human cortex, and the timing of astrocyte maturation differs across species; human astrocytes are larger and more complexly branched, and whether the same signal operates there is a question for organoid work.

The emphasis reverses for the field. Astrocytes have been treated as responders to neuronal activity, and the paper makes them participants in the construction of the circuit, which is a claim about development rather than about signalling alone. The assay behind it is morphometry: a branched astrocyte has a large surface, a flat one little, and reconstructions quantify the difference, so a shape becomes a measurable property. Two details carry consequences. GABA-B is a heterodimer and the glial version differs from the neuronal one, which is why a systemic drug can affect neurons and glia differently; and because astrocytes branch in a window that closes before adulthood, a signal absent then may not be recoverable, which is the clinical stake. What remains unmeasured is behaviour, since a brain with underbranched astrocytes may still work and no test yet connects the defect to a deficit.

Whether the branching defect is permanent is the question the design leaves open. A signal absent during a window may be supplied later without recovering the lost arbor, and an experiment that restores the receptor after the window would separate a delay from a loss. The answer would decide whether the target belongs to development or to repair, and either answer would change the target and the timing alike.
