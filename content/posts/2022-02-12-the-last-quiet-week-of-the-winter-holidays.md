The winter holiday ends the way it always does, without a ceremony. One morning the fog sits on the fields and the next morning the light is different, and the shops begin to sell exercise books and the covers that the schools ask for. Nothing announces the change, and nothing needs to. The holiday has been long enough that it has stopped feeling like a holiday and started feeling like a life, which is the point at which it is about to end.

Most of these days have been spent indoors. Reading took longer than planned because the book that seemed interesting in December turned out to be slow, and the alternative was a list of things that the term will require and that nobody has done. There is a particular texture to a holiday afternoon when the work is neither done nor started: the light on the desk, the radiator clicking, the noise of the street arriving through a closed window.

Outside, the weather has been the kind that makes people stay in. It rained for three days and then cleared, and the cold that followed was dry and sharp, the sort that makes the air in the stairwell feel like metal. In the town the new year decorations came down slowly, a few lanterns at a time, and the square that had held a crowd in the first week of February was empty by the middle of the month.

Television carried other people's weather. Reports from the far side of the continent showed soldiers on a border, and men in suits explaining what would happen next, and the same footage run three times a day. It was hard to connect the pictures to anything in the room. The news was loud and the room was quiet, and the distance between them was the measure of an ordinary life.

The list in the notebook has twelve lines and four of them are crossed out. The crossing out is the part that feels like progress, although what remains is the part that counts. There is a habit in this kind of preparation that mistakes the arrangement of work for the work itself: the new folder, the sorted papers, the pen that writes well. The arrangement takes a morning and the work takes a term.

On the last evening the bag is packed and the uniform is laid out, and the house is quieter than it has been for a month. In the morning there will be the bus and the corridor and the names of the people who will be met again, and the small surprise of finding that the classroom has not changed. What the holiday leaves behind has less to do with rest, which it promises, than with a sense of what an uninterrupted hour is worth, and that is worth carrying into the term. The term will begin with the usual inventory of what was assigned and what was done, and nobody will say anything about the week that went by without an event. This is how a holiday ends, in the ordinary arithmetic of a Saturday evening, with the bag by the door and the lights on in the rooms that will be empty tomorrow.
