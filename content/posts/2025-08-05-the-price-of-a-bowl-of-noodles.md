A bowl of beef noodles in a shop near a metro station costs what it costs, and the number on the wall is one of the cheapest ways to read an economy. It moves slowly, and it moves for reasons the customer never sees. The wheat, the beef, the rent, the wage of the person who cooks it and the wage of the person who washes the bowls, and the electricity that keeps the broth at a simmer all night.

Rent is usually the largest of those, which is why the same bowl costs twelve yuan in a county town and thirty in a shopping centre in a provincial capital. The second is labour, and it has been rising for a decade in a way that has changed the shape of the industry. A noodle shop that once employed three people now employs two and a machine, and the machine does the kneading while the two do the serving.

The beef is the volatile item, and every kitchen has a story about a week when it doubled. Prices for pork and beef have moved by double digits within a year several times in the last decade, and the shop cannot reprice the wall every time the wholesale market moves, so it absorbs the difference and adjusts the bowl size. A customer who notices that the portion has shrunk is observing a price increase that was not announced.

What has happened this summer is that the cost of the ingredients has been flat and the cost of everything else has not. Wholesale food prices have been weak for a year. Rents in commercial streets have fallen in some districts as shops closed. Wages have continued to rise, slowly, because the labour market for service work remains tight even when the wider economy is soft.

What follows is a period in which noodle prices have been unusually stable, and stability in this business is a sign of something. A shop that does not raise its price is a shop that expects to keep its customers, and a shop that expects to keep its customers is a shop that is worried about how many it has.

There is also a competitive layer that did not exist a decade ago, and it has rearranged the arithmetic for every shop within delivery range. Delivery platforms take a commission on every order, and a shop that sells through them has to price for the commission. The same bowl costs more on the app than on the wall, and the difference is not a delivery fee, it is the platform's share appearing in the base price.

None of this is visible from the outside, which is the point. The bowl is a small, dense object, and its price contains the rent of the street, the wage of the city, the harvest of the province and the commission of a company in another province. The customer pays once, at the counter, and reads none of it, which is roughly how every price in a city works.
