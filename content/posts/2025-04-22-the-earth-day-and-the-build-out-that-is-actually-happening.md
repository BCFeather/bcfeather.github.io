The twenty-second of April is the earth day, and the theme this year is the electricity system, which is a change of emphasis from the earlier years that were about consumption and recycling. The change reflects the state of the argument. The question that the energy ministries are arguing about is no longer whether a grid can be built on renewables, because several grids already are, and it is how fast the rest of them can follow.

The numbers behind that are large and they have become ordinary. The world installed more solar capacity in the last two years than in the previous decade, the cost of a module fell by more than half, and the battery that makes the evening peak manageable has become cheap enough for a utility to buy without a subsidy. The build out is now a construction business with a supply chain, a workforce and a financing market, and the pace is set by those rather than by the ambition of a conference.

The constraint that has moved to the front is the grid itself. A solar farm can be built in a year and the transmission line that carries its power takes five, and the queue of projects waiting for a connection is long in every market that has grown quickly. The permitting, the land, the line and the substation are the bottleneck, and they are local decisions in a system that is national in its targets.

The other constraint is the price of the alternative. A grid with a large share of renewables needs something to run when the wind does not blow, and the something is either a gas plant that runs rarely and must be paid to exist, or a storage system that is still expensive for a week of calm, or a demand that can be shifted. The market design that pays for capacity rather than for energy is the part of the transition that the engineers have solved and the regulators have not.

What the day is good for is the reminder that a climate policy is an industrial policy. The country that builds the modules, the cells and the inverters employs the people who make them, and the country that buys them imports the equipment and the jobs. The trade policy and the climate policy are the same policy seen from two rooms, which is why the tariffs on the equipment have become a climate argument.

The consumption side has its own arithmetic and it is smaller than the supply side. A household that insulates, replaces a boiler and buys a smaller car reduces its emissions by a fraction of what the same money spent on a utility scale project would reduce, and the household does it for comfort and for the bill. The two are not in competition, and the policy that treats them as alternatives is a policy that has misunderstood the size of the problem, which is a supply problem first, and the demand side follows it at its own pace.
