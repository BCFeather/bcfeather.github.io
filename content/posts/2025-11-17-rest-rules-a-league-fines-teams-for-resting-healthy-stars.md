A professional league's product is its stars on television, and a team's product is a healthy roster in April. A league has fined three teams this month for resting healthy players in televised games, and the policy is an attempt to price a conflict that the league does not control.

Narrow terms define the policy. A team may rest a healthy player in a nationally televised game only if the player has played in at least one of the previous two games, and the fine is 100,000 dollars for a first offence. A team that violates it twice in a season loses a draft pick.

Medical staff dispute the premise. A player's workload is measured by minutes, distance and accelerations, and a sports scientist's model says a back-to-back sequence raises a soft-tissue injury risk by 40 per cent. Resting one of those games is a medical decision, and the policy treats it as a marketing one.

The players' association is the third party. Its collective agreement protects a player's right to decline to play if injured, and it does not address a coach's decision to withhold a healthy player. The union has filed a grievance.

Empirical evidence is thin on both sides. A study of 400 players found that load management reduced a player's in-season injury rate by 12 per cent and had no measurable effect on playoff performance. The league's own data show a 20 per cent rise in televised-game absences.

The revenue argument is the league's case. A televised game without its stars draws 30 per cent fewer viewers, and the broadcast contract's value depends on the audience. That is a real cost borne by the league and not by the team.

Comparison with another league's approach is instructive. A league that publishes injury reports with a designation for rest and requires a medical certificate has fewer disputes, because the disclosure is procedural rather than punitive.

A rest rule is a restriction on a team's own asset, which is why its enforcement is unusual. A league that fines a team for not playing a healthy player is regulating a product rather than a competition, and its legal basis is the broadcast contract rather than the sport's rules.

The players' side has an interest in the rule and not the same one. A rest day protects a player's body, and a rule that forbids it shifts the risk to the player. A union that accepts the rule in exchange for a revenue share has made a trade that its members may later dispute.

A rest rule's enforcement is a scheduling problem disguised as a disciplinary one. A league that fines a team must also publish the medical criteria it accepts, and a criterion that a team disputes becomes an arbitration. The rule's first season produced two arbitrations and no fines.

Two costs attach to one decision, and the question is who owns it. A rest protects a player and damages a broadcast, and a fine is a way of charging the second cost to the party that decides the first.
