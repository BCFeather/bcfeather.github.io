A museum that stages a blockbuster exhibition is borrowing almost everything in it. The paintings belong to other institutions, the objects travel under conditions negotiated one at a time, and the cost of a show is dominated by loan fees, insurance, transport and security rather than by curatorial work. A major touring exhibition can cost several million dollars before a single ticket is sold. A show of two hundred objects from forty lenders is a logistics project with an art gallery attached.

Loan fees have become a revenue line for lenders. A large museum that holds a deep collection can charge for a loan, and the fee is set by the rarity of the object and by the competition for it. Smaller museums that once borrowed freely now find themselves bidding for the same twenty works that every blockbuster wants. A lender that charges a fee is a lender that has to justify it to its own board, and the justification is the cost of the empty wall.

Insurance follows the value. A painting that has been reassessed upward is a painting with a higher premium, and the premium is paid by the borrower. Condition reports, courier travel and climate-controlled cases add fixed costs per object, which is why a show with a hundred small works can be cheaper than a show with twelve masterpieces. A condition report that records a scratch is a document that a courier signs, and the courier travels with the work at the lender's insistence.

Security is the part that nobody discusses. A high-value loan requires a guarded gallery, a vetting of staff and a plan for evacuation, and those costs fall on the borrowing institution's operating budget rather than on the exhibition's project budget. A museum that runs three blockbusters a year is a museum whose permanent collection is guarded for the benefit of the temporary one. A gallery that is guarded for a loan is a gallery whose own collection is guarded by the same staff on the same rota.

The attendance model is what justifies the expense. A blockbuster can triple annual attendance, and the tickets, the shop and the cafe are what pay for the next show. A museum that fails to fill the galleries is a museum that has spent the money and lost the subsidy, and several have discovered that a famous name is not a guarantee. A shop that sells a catalogue and a print is a shop whose margin is higher than the ticket's, and the shop is planned with the show.

The curatorial argument against the model is that it turns a collection into a schedule. A museum that plans its programme around what will travel is a museum that stops researching its own holdings, and the exhibitions that come out of a collection are the ones that take a decade to prepare. That tension is not resolvable, and it is the reason museum directors change every five years. A director who leaves after five years leaves behind a programme that the next director inherits, and the loans are the part that cannot be rebooked.
