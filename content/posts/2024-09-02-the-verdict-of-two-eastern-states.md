Two eastern states voted on the first of September. Results redrew the map that the federal parties use to plan. Thuringia and Saxony are the two states where the far-right party has been strongest for a decade. In Thuringia the Alternative for Germany finished first, with about a third of the vote. It is the first time that the party has won a state election. The first time that a party classified as right-wing extremist by the domestic intelligence service has done so.

In Saxony the same party came second, close behind the Christian Democrats. Difference between the two states is the difference between growth and a ceiling. The new party on the left took a share that nobody predicted. An alliance built around a former eastern German politician took more than a tenth in both states. It did so by campaigning on peace and on wages rather than on identity.

Coalition arithmetic is the hard part. A majority in Thuringia requires three parties that agree on little, and one of the possibilities is a government that governs without a majority and asks the opposition for votes on each bill. The firewall is the phrase that the federal parties use. Every party except the far-right one has ruled out working with it. Firewall holds in the state capitals even where the arithmetic would suggest otherwise.

Analysts will study the voters who switched. A share of the electorate that voted for the Social Democrats in 2019 voted for the new left party. A share of the conservative vote stayed at home. The economy of the east is the background. A state that lost its industry after 1990 has wages that are lower and plants that are newer. A voter who was forty in 1990 is seventy-four this year.

The federal government is the party that pays. A coalition in Berlin that is unpopular in the east loses votes in the east. Three parties in the federal government all lost share in both states. State constitution is the other constraint. A government in Thuringia needs a majority for its budget. A parliament in which no bloc holds one produces a budget that is negotiated line by line with the opposition.

Federal parties are the ones that lose. A national coalition that campaigns on stability has lost voters in the two states where the far-right party is strongest. Loss is larger than the national polls imply. The campaign for the federal election has already started. A year is a long time in a country with a coalition that argues in public, and the two eastern states will be cited in every debate about immigration that follows. The national election is a year away and the campaign has already begun in the two states that voted. A state result is a signal rather than a forecast, and the parties that read it will change their programmes before the campaign rather than after it. By the end of the month neither state had a government, and the negotiations that would produce one were being watched in Berlin more closely than in the two capitals.
