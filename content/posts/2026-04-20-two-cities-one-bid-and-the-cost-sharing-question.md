A joint bid for the 2034 Winter Games, submitted this month by two mountain regions an hour apart by rail, has cleared the technical review with a host contract that splits nearly everything: venues, accommodation, transport corridors, sponsorship revenue and, in a clause that will be quoted for years, liability for cost overruns.

Olympic cost sharing is not new. What is new here is the explicit ratio. The contract allocates 54 per cent of the operating budget and 46 per cent of the capital works to the larger partner, matching the share of events held on its territory, and it splits any overrun in the same proportion rather than by venue responsibility.

The mechanism at work is a guarantee, not a budget. Hosts do not pay the International Olympic Committee a fee; they commit to deliver the Games and absorb the difference between the operating budget and actual cost. When two governments sign one guarantee, each is exposed to the other's failure, which is the risk that the ratio is meant to manage.

Revenue sharing creates the second problem. Broadcasting and sponsorship income flow to the committee and are distributed as services and cash, not as profit, so the two hosts are not sharing a surplus but a set of obligations with different visibility. The smaller partner gets 46 per cent of the cost and a smaller share of the hotel revenue, because most of the rooms are in the larger city.

Sporting logic favours the split. Two clusters reduce the need for a single enormous athletes' village, put the alpine events near existing lifts, and cut the new-build requirement to two arenas. The International Olympic Committee's own agenda has pushed in this direction since 2014, and the technical review praised the plan for using facilities that already host World Cup races.

Accountability is where the arrangement is thin. A joint organising committee answers to two governments whose budget cycles, procurement rules and audit offices differ. If the alpine venues run over, the larger partner pays 46 per cent of a bill it does not control, and its parliament will ask why. The contract anticipates this with a dispute clause that sends unresolved cost questions to an independent panel, which can allocate but cannot compel.

Transport is the clause worth watching in operation. The two clusters are 58 minutes apart by a rail line that runs hourly, and the contract assumes a service every twelve minutes during the Games. That frequency requires rolling stock the operator does not own and a timetable that displaces regional services for two weeks, and neither cost appears in the capital budget because both are carried by the national rail operator under a separate agreement.

Accommodation tells a similar story. The larger partner has 4,100 hotel rooms within the Games footprint; the smaller has 900 and a stock of second homes that the organising committee plans to lease. Leasing private housing for a month is cheap and politically visible, and the contract's public-consultation clause was written because of it.

For cities watching, the arithmetic is instructive. Joint bidding lowers the per-city capital bill and raises the coordination bill, and coordination failures in Olympic delivery are historically more expensive than construction ones. A single host that builds too much wastes money; two hosts that disagree waste money and time, and time is the one resource the Games calendar does not extend.
