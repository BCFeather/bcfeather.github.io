The standing committee of the National People's Congress closed its August session on 28 August with a long list. It passed a medical insurance law, a law on farmland protection and quality improvement, a revision of the agriculture law, a national defence mobilization law and amendments to the lawyers law. The revised agriculture law takes effect on 1 January 2027, and the others take effect on dates the official texts will specify. The session had opened earlier in the month, and the closing vote is the point at which a draft becomes a statute and the commentary begins. Most of the new laws take effect on dates set in their final articles, and the implementing regulations, which determine how they work in practice, will follow over the winter.

The medical insurance law is the one that will be felt most widely. It puts on a statutory footing a system that has been run by regulation for two decades, covering the basic scheme, supplementary schemes and the rules on how providers are paid. Statutory status matters because it constrains the discretion of local governments, which have varied considerably in what they reimburse. The scheme covers urban employees, urban residents and rural residents through separate arrangements, and the new law keeps that structure while standardizing the rules that govern each. Statutory status also gives patients a basis for appeal when a local office refuses reimbursement, a remedy that regulations alone provided only weakly.

The farmland law addresses a quieter crisis. China has protected a fixed area of arable land for years through targets rather than through a single statute, and the new law combines protection with quality improvement, which means drainage, soil health and the productivity of the land rather than its mere area. A country that imports soybeans and feeds a large population has reason to care about yield per hectare. The new law also writes quality into the mandate, which means that improving yield on existing land becomes a legal duty and not only a programme. Enforcement remains the difficult part, because farmland is protected by local governments that also benefit from selling it for construction.

The defence mobilization law belongs to a different register. It sets out how the state requisitions people, transport and industry in an emergency, and it consolidates rules that were previously scattered across regulations and military documents. Legislatures rarely pass such laws in quiet times, and the timing will be read by outside governments as a signal regardless of intent. The mobilization law sets out categories of requisition, the compensation that follows and the authority that orders it, and it consolidates rules that were previously scattered. Such laws are routine in many countries and are read differently in this region, where the timing of any defence statute is treated as a signal.

Amendments to the lawyers law were the least noticed and the most practical. They touch on practice qualifications, on the obligations of law firms and on the handling of cases that involve state secrets, subjects that lawyers argue about constantly and that the public notices only when a trial goes wrong. The amendments also address the confidentiality obligations that attach to cases involving state secrets, a subject that has produced disputes between lawyers and prosecutors. Practitioners will spend the next year reading the implementing rules, because the difference between a right and a procedure is usually written there.
