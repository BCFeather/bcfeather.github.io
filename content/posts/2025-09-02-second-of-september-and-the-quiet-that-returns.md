For a week now the campus has been running at half speed, and the half that is missing is the loud one. Corridors that were empty in August carry the shuffle of people who have not yet learned which staircase saves a minute. The canteen opens at eleven and closes at one, and between those hours it is a place of trays and low conversation rather than the roar of June.

Small logistics dominate these days. A student card must be activated at a machine on the ground floor, and the machine is particular about the angle of the card. A printing queue grows at ten in the morning because everyone has the same idea about the same reading list, and the queue moves at the speed of one tired assistant changing a toner cartridge. A room whose number changed over the summer leaves a first-year standing in a corridor with a phone in one hand, turning slowly, like a compass looking for north. None of it is difficult; all of it takes time, and time is what the first week has instead of work, which is why the days feel long and the month feels short.

September has become a second January, with resolutions that are smaller and more concrete: read the set text before the lecture rather than after it, sleep at a fixed hour for the first three weeks at least, speak once in every seminar and not wait for the fourth. Nobody writes these down. They are made in the queue for the card machine and abandoned with less ceremony than they were adopted, which is roughly how every year's intentions go.

Weather helps the mood and then spoils it. The mornings are cool enough for a jacket, the afternoons still insist on summer, and by the end of the week the air carries the smell of rain that has not yet arrived. Daylight shortens by minutes nobody notices individually and everybody notices collectively, round the twentieth of the month, when the walk back from the library stops being bright.

Evenings are the best evidence of how new the term still is. At nine the reading rooms are half full, and the bicycles outside are parked in rows that have not yet turned into the usual heap. Somebody is practising a violin badly in a room with the window open, and nobody complains, because the building has not yet acquired the acoustics of habit. By ten the bicycles have thinned out and the last lit window belongs to the department that always keeps one on.

A term beginning is not news, and that is the point of it. The machine engages, the first week passes with the shape it has had for a century, whatever the century happens to be doing outside the walls. Noise will return soon enough, and with it the ordinary press of deadlines. For now the corridors keep their half-volume, and the year is still being assembled.
