A molecule's smell is a perceptual property, and chemists have described it with metaphors rather than with numbers. A map that places a molecule in a perceptual space would let a prediction replace a panel.

The map in *Science*, 1 September 2023 is that. The authors collect a large set of human ratings for a library of odorants, reduce the ratings to a small number of dimensions, and show that the coordinates predict the ratings of molecules outside the library.

The reduction is the method. A panel's vocabulary is a few hundred words, and a principal component analysis compresses them into a space of a few dozen dimensions in which nearby molecules smell alike.

The prediction is the result. A molecule's structure is fed to a model that places it in the map, and the placement predicts the ratings better than a panel's average agreement would suggest.

The chemistry is the input. A molecule's functional groups and its shape are the features, and the authors' comparison of models shows which features carry the perceptual information.

The biology is the caveat. A receptor's response and a person's perception are different measurements, and the map is a model of the second.

The application is a screen. A flavour house that wants a molecule with a particular profile can search the map rather than synthesize and test a series.

The limitation is the panel. A rating is an average over people whose sensitivities differ, and the map's dimensions are the panel's shared structure.

What the paper does not show is a mechanism. A perceptual dimension is not a receptor, and the mapping from a molecule to a receptor's response is a separate problem.

For sensory science, the value is a quantitative description of a subjective quality. The field's vocabulary has been metaphorical, and a coordinate system is a language that a model can use.

The principal components are the space's axes. A set of ratings that co-vary defines a direction, and the leading directions are the ones that a molecule's position is described by.

The validation is the held-out set. A model that predicts molecules it never saw is one that generalizes, and the authors' test is the map's credibility.

The structure's features are the model's input. A fingerprint of a molecule's substructures is a standard representation, and the authors' comparison shows how much of the perception it captures.

The panel's size is the experiment's cost. A rating per molecule per person is a slow measurement, and the library's scale is what makes the reduction possible.

The comparison with the perfume industry is the useful frame. A perfumer's vocabulary is a craft, and the map is an attempt to make it a measurement.

The receptor's identity is the biology's question. A set of olfactory receptors responds to a molecule, and the map's dimensions may correspond to combinations of their sensitivities.
The perceptual space is not a receptor's space. A mixture of odorants activates a combination of receptors, and the map's dimensions compress that combination into a description that a person can use.
