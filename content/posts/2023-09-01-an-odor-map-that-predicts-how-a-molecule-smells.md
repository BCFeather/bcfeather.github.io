Smell is a perceptual property that chemists describe with metaphors, so a map placing a molecule in a perceptual space would let a prediction replace a panel, and a panel is the slow and costly step in any such measurement. Reported in *Science* on 1 September 2023, the map is built from human ratings for a large library of odorants, and the ratings are reduced to a space of a few dozen dimensions in which nearby molecules smell alike.

Reduction supplies the method. A panel's vocabulary runs to a few hundred descriptive words, principal component analysis compresses them into directions that co-vary across molecules, and the leading directions are the axes on which each odorant's position is read. Prediction is the result, since a molecule's structure is fed to a model that places it in the map, and the placement reproduces the ratings of unfamiliar compounds better than a panel's internal agreement would suggest.

Chemistry is the input. Functional groups and molecular shape are the features, and comparing models built on different feature sets shows which of them carry the perceptual information; a fingerprint of substructures is a standard representation, and the comparison measures how much of the perception it captures.

Biology stays the caveat. A receptor's response and a person's report are different measurements, and the map models the second; the perceptual space is not a receptor's space, because a mixture activates a combination of receptors and the dimensions compress that combination into something a person can use. A mechanism is the gap, since a perceptual dimension is not a receptor and the mapping from molecule to receptor remains a separate problem. The panel is the limitation, because a rating averages people whose sensitivities differ and the dimensions are the panel's shared structure.

Sensory science gains a quantitative description of a subjective quality, since the field's vocabulary has been metaphorical and a coordinate system is a language a model can use. Held-out molecules are the credibility test, and a model predicting what it never saw is the standard the map has to meet.

A flavour house can search the map instead of synthesizing a series, and that search is the commercial use the paper points to. A perfumer's vocabulary survives as a description of the axes, since a craft term names a direction that the ratings then give a number. A receptor-level study of a few mapped molecules would test whether the axes correspond to combinations of receptor sensitivities, and that comparison is the experiment the map invites next.

The panel's size sets the experiment's cost, since one rating per molecule per person is a slow measurement and the library's scale is what makes the reduction possible. A library of several thousand odorants, each rated by dozens of people, is a dataset that only a coordinated laboratory could assemble. Whether the axes would survive a different panel is the replication question, because a shared structure may reflect the vocabulary the raters were given. A second laboratory using a different word list would test that, and a stable set of axes across vocabularies would be the map's strongest claim.
