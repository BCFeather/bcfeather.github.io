A retailer buys its holiday stock in June and sells it in December, and the difference between what it sells and what it bought is January's problem. This season's data show a 5-point shortfall in sell-through turning into a 12-point markdown, and the relationship is not linear.

The arithmetic is the reason. A retailer that sells 80 per cent of its stock at full price recovers its cost and a margin, and the remaining 20 per cent is sold at a discount that costs 40 per cent of the unit's price. A retailer at 75 per cent sell-through has twice the leftover and a thinner margin.

Markdown dollars rose 22 per cent this season across the sector, and the shortfall in sell-through was 4 points on average. Two categories, apparel and electronics, accounted for 70 per cent of the increase.

The cause was a demand shift rather than a supply one. Consumers bought later, and the last ten days before the holiday accounted for 38 per cent of sales against 30 per cent in the previous year, which gives a retailer less time to react. A shortfall in one size is not a shortfall in a style, and a retailer that marks down a style when one size lags loses the margin on units that would have sold.

Inventory carry is the second cost. A leftover unit occupies warehouse space for a year and depreciates in fashion terms, and the carrying cost is 20 per cent of its value. That is why a 40 per cent markdown is cheaper than holding.

Made-to-order is the strategic alternative on offer. A retailer that produces after orders arrive has less leftover and a longer lead time, and two online retailers have moved to that model for their private-label lines.

The accounting treatment matters to the reported numbers. A markdown is a reduction in revenue and an inventory write-down is a charge, and a retailer that takes the charge in December reports a lower margin and a cleaner January.

A markdown is a decision about a curve, not about a price. A retailer's sell-through target assumes a weekly rate, and a shortfall at week six means the remaining weeks must sell more per week than the product's demand supports. The markdown is the price cut that changes the rate.

Timing is where the arithmetic bites. A 12-point markdown taken in week eight recovers two-thirds of the margin that the same markdown taken in week twelve would lose, because the units sell at the higher price for four more weeks.

A markdown's size is set by a target and not by a cost. A retailer that wants its inventory cleared by a date works backwards from the date to the discount, and a discount that is too small leaves units that must be written off at a larger loss.

A cost that arrives after its season is the subject here. A sell-through number in December sets a markdown in January, and the markdown is what a retailer's year actually earned.
