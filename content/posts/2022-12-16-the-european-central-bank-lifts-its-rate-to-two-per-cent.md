date: "2022-12-16T23:30:00+08:00"

description: "A fourth increase this year takes the deposit rate to its highest since 2008, and the bank says the inflation fight has further to run."
---

The European Central Bank raised its deposit rate by half a point on Thursday, to two per cent, and its main refinancing rate to two and a half. The increase is smaller than the three-quarter-point moves of September and October, and the bank said the step did not mean the work was finished. It also announced that it would start reducing the bond portfolio it built during the pandemic, at a pace of about fifteen billion euros a month from March. The decision was taken by the governing council in Frankfurt, and the statement was published at a quarter past two.

Inflation in the euro area was 10.1 per cent in November, down from a peak of 10.6 in October and still more than five times the bank's target. The fall came mostly from energy prices, which had been the reason for the spike, and the measure that excludes food and energy continued to rise. The bank's staff raised their forecast for inflation next year and cut their forecast for growth, and they now expect the economy to contract in the fourth quarter and the first. Wages have begun to rise in response to prices, which the bank says is the risk it is watching.

Shrinking the balance sheet is the new part. The bank bought about five trillion euros of bonds through two programmes, and the portfolio has been reinvested in full since the first rate rise. From March the bank will let some of it run off, which raises the cost of borrowing at the long end without the bank having to sell. The announcement was described as predictable and slow by analysts, and the bank's chief economist said the aim was to avoid a repeat of the disorder that followed the British budget in September. The reduction will be reviewed every quarter.

Trading after the statement pushed the euro up against the dollar and pulled Italian bond yields down, and the spread between Italian and German debt narrowed to its lowest level since the summer. Banks' shares rose because the rate increase improves the margin on their lending. The reaction lasted less than a day, and by Friday the market had returned to the argument about how high rates will go. Money markets now price a peak above three per cent in the middle of next year.

Politically the decision is awkward for the governments that are borrowing. Italy's new administration has promised tax cuts and higher pensions, and its debt is the largest in the euro area after Greece's. France has capped the price of electricity for households and is borrowing to pay for it. The bank's mandate does not include the budgets of member states, and its president has said that fiscal policy and monetary policy have to point in the same direction.

For borrowers the effect is already visible in the mortgage market. Rates on new loans have doubled in a year in several countries, and the number of approvals has fallen by a third in Germany. The bank's own surveys show that firms expect to cut investment next year, and the construction industry is the first to feel it. The governing council meets again in February, and its president has said the next step will be decided by the data rather than by the calendar.
