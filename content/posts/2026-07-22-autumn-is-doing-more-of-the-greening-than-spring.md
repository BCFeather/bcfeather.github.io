Earth has been getting greener for four decades, and the satellite record says so with unusual consistency. The usual summary treats that as one phenomenon, a planet responding to more carbon dioxide and a warmer climate by growing more leaf. The summary hides two different processes: vegetation can become denser or more productive at its peak, or it can keep its canopy for longer at the start and the end of the season. A canopy that stays green for an extra fortnight is measured as greening even when those two weeks add little carbon, because the index counts leaf area rather than photosynthetic rate.

A study reported in *Nature* on 22 July 2026 separates the two. Jiaqi Tian and colleagues decomposed long-term greenness observations into a maximum-greenness term and a growing-season-length term, and then asked which one accounts for the trend in different biomes. The decomposition is the contribution, because a single annual greenness number cannot distinguish a taller peak from a wider one.

The answer splits by land cover. In non-forest areas, the greening is dominated by higher maximum greenness, driven by elevated carbon dioxide and by land-use change. In extra-tropical forests, the dominant term is a longer growing season, and within that term the autumn extension matters more than the spring advance. The finding reframes the map: the far north is greening because autumn lasts longer, not because summer is more productive. Forests at the temperate margin showed the pattern most strongly, and the tropical signal was dominated by the peak term, which is consistent with a light-limited rather than a temperature-limited system.

Autumn phenology is the harder half to model, which is why it has been underweighted. Leaf senescence depends on day length, on the first frost, on soil moisture and on whether the tree has stored enough carbon, and models that handle spring well often treat autumn as a fixed date. A trend that runs through autumn therefore points at a process the models are least equipped to project.

The limits are those of any decomposition. Satellite greenness is a proxy, not a measure of carbon uptake, and a longer canopy season does not automatically mean more photosynthesis if autumn light and temperatures are falling. The record also mixes direct effects of carbon dioxide with the indirect effects of warming, and separating them cleanly is beyond what a statistical decomposition can do. Attribution also runs into the problem that carbon dioxide and warming are correlated in the record, and a decomposition that treats them as separable imposes a structure the data do not test.

What follows is a modelling target. If autumn is carrying a large share of the trend, then projections that keep autumn fixed will understate future greening in boreal and temperate forests, and carbon-cycle estimates will inherit the error. Field campaigns that measure senescence directly, rather than inferring it from greenness, are the check that the decomposition still needs.
