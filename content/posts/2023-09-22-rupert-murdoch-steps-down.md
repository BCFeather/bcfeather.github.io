Murdoch, who is ninety-two, told employees on 21 September that he would leave the chairmanship of both Fox Corporation and News Corp in November, and that his son Lachlan would become sole chair of both. He will keep the title of chairman emeritus and has said he will continue to visit the offices and to read the papers, which is the kind of arrangement that a founder's retirement usually involves. The companies' shares moved little, because the succession had been signalled for a decade.

The empire he built changed the politics of three countries. He bought newspapers in Australia and Britain in the 1960s and 1970s, moved into American television in the 1980s, and created Fox News in 1996, which became the most watched cable news channel in the United States and a force in the Republican Party. The News of the World closed in 2011 after a phone-hacking scandal that led to a public inquiry and to criminal convictions, and Fox paid a settlement of seven hundred and eighty-seven million dollars in 2023 to settle a defamation case brought by a voting machine company that its hosts had accused of rigging the 2020 election.

Lachlan Murdoch has run Fox since 2019 and has been executive chairman of News Corp since 2015. He is fifty-two, he has spent his career inside the companies, and his interests have been in broadcasting and in the American market rather than in the British newspapers his father loved. Colleagues describe him as more operationally minded and less political than his father, and analysts expect him to sell assets that the family has kept for sentimental reasons, with the British tabloids the most likely candidate.

Companies he leaves are different from the ones he built. Print advertising has collapsed, and News Corp's newspapers are kept alive by digital subscriptions, by the Dow Jones data business and by the Australian pay-television assets that the family has owned since the 1990s. Fox depends on cable carriage fees that are falling as households abandon pay television, and on advertising that is exposed to the election cycle. The Fox streaming service has grown, and the company has bought a free ad-supported service to reach younger viewers.

Political legacy is harder to measure than the balance sheet. Fox News gave American conservatism a shared vocabulary and a shared set of grievances, and its hosts have moved between the channel and political office for a decade. Murdoch's British papers campaigned for Brexit and against successive governments, and his Australian papers have shaped elections. The outlets will continue, and the man who decided what they said will no longer decide, though his son has said he intends to keep the newspapers that his father regarded as the family's inheritance.

A founder has handed his companies to his son, and the institutions he created will keep operating as he designed them. What changes is that the family will now be run by someone who grew up inside the business rather than someone who invented it.
