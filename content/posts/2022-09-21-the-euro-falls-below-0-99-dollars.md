Falling through 0.99 dollars in the London session on 21 September, the euro touched 0.988, a level last seen in 2002. It had started the year at 1.13 and had lost 13 per cent. The move came a day before the Federal Reserve raised rates again and two weeks after the ECB's large increase.

Energy was the first cause. The currency area imports most of its gas, and the price of the Dutch benchmark had risen twentyfold in a year. That transfer of income to exporters of fuel widens the trade deficit and weakens the currency. The euro area's current account moved from a surplus of 3 per cent of output to a deficit in the second quarter. Gas storage was full, and the futures curve still priced a winter at three times the normal level.

Interest rates were the second. The Federal Reserve had raised its rate to 3 per cent by September, and the ECB's deposit rate was 0.75. The gap drew capital into dollar assets and out of European ones. Traders said the market was pricing the end point of the cycle rather than the current level, and the American end point was higher. The war changed the risk premium. Investors were pricing the chance of energy rationing, of a recession, and of political stress in member states. The Italian spread widened with each gas price rise, and the bank's new bond-buying tool had not been tested. Those risks were not present in the dollar's own story.

Parity mattered for prices. A weaker euro raises the cost of imports priced in dollars, including oil and gas, and it feeds into inflation with a lag of about a quarter. Economists estimated that a 10 per cent fall adds 1 point to inflation in the following year. The bank's own projections assumed an exchange rate of 1.03, which by September looked optimistic. The bank's deposit rate was negative until July, and the first increase had been only half a point.

German manufacturers said the cheaper currency made their cars and machines more competitive outside the currency area, and orders from outside Europe held up. The fall also helped exporters. The benefit was smaller than in previous cycles because energy costs had risen more than the currency had fallen. Officials did not welcome the move but did not fight it. The bank's president said the exchange rate was not a policy target, and the finance ministers said they were watching the market. No government asked for intervention, and the bank's holdings of foreign currency were small. The bank's tool was designed for countries that met conditions, and no country had asked for it by September.

Parity held for six weeks, and the euro recovered in November as gas prices fell. It ended the year at 1.07 and rose through 2023 as the energy shock faded. The episode was the first time since 2002 that the currency traded below the dollar, and it was read as a verdict on the currency area's energy dependence.
