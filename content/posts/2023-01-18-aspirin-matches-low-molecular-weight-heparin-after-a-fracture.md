---

Orthopaedic trauma produces a patient who cannot walk, has a broken bone with torn tissue around it, and carries the risk of a clot that travels to the lung. Prophylaxis is standard, and the standard has been low-molecular-weight heparin, an injectable drug that costs more and is harder to give at home than a tablet. The trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 19 January 2023 asks whether aspirin is good enough. Patients with a fracture of an extremity were randomized to aspirin or to low-molecular-weight heparin, and the primary outcome was death from any cause within 90 days, a composite that captures the events prophylaxis is meant to prevent.

The numbers are close. Death occurred in 47 of 6,101 patients assigned aspirin, a 90-day probability of 0.78 per cent, and in 45 of 6,110 assigned heparin, 0.73 per cent. The difference of 0.05 percentage points fell inside the prespecified margin for noninferiority, and the trial reported low rates of deep-vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism and death in both groups.

Design is what makes the result usable. A trial powered on mortality in a population with a low event rate has to be large, and 12,000 patients is large enough that the confidence interval is narrow. The pragmatic protocol, with treatment given as each centre would give it, means the answer applies to hospitals rather than to idealized conditions.

The secondary outcomes matter for the choice. Aspirin is oral, cheap and familiar, and adherence after discharge is easier. Heparin requires injection and monitoring in some protocols, and the burden falls on patients who are already recovering from major injury.

Bleeding is the risk that has to be weighed. Aspirin irreversibly inhibits platelet cyclooxygenase, and a trauma patient may need surgery. The trial's bleeding rates were similar, which is the finding that makes the substitution defensible rather than the mortality result alone.

What the trial cannot answer is the patient at highest risk. A subgroup with pelvic fractures, or with a history of venous thromboembolism, may need more than either agent, and a noninferiority result in an average population does not license a uniform protocol. The guideline consequence is likely to be a menu. Aspirin for most patients with a limb fracture, heparin for those whose risk is higher, and a decision that a clinician can make with the patient rather than a rule imposed by protocol.

For the economics of trauma care, the difference is not trivial. An injectable drug that a patient must learn to give, or a nurse must visit to give, carries costs that a tablet does not, and a trial that shows equivalence is a trial that changes what hospitals buy. The trial also enrolled patients whose fracture was treated operatively and those treated in a cast, and the protocol did not require a particular agent for either. That heterogeneity is the point of a pragmatic design: the answer covers what orthopaedic surgeons actually do, not what a protocol committee would prefer.
