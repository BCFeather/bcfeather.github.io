The last market before the new year happens on the twenty-eighth of the twelfth lunar month, and by then the bargaining has mostly stopped. Traders who have been shouting prices for three weeks have no patience left for the theatre of it, and customers who have left everything to the last day are not in a position to walk away. A market that sounds quieter than the ones before it is the opposite of what a festival should sound like, and somehow more festive for it.

Cars are kept out for the length of the street, and the stalls are pushed to the edges so that a lane opens down the middle. Dried fish hang in rows from a frame of bamboo, and the smell of them mixes with the smell of the fried dough stall and the smell of the coal brazier where a man roasts chestnuts in a drum. Nothing in the arrangement is new. Same families have been coming to the same stretch of pavement for thirty years, and their children now run the stalls while the older ones sit behind them on plastic stools and count the money.

Inventory has changed. Red lanterns are sold in flat packs and shipped from a factory two provinces away, the paper cuttings for the windows are printed rather than scissored, and there are fewer of both than there used to be, because nobody has the time to paste them on the glass any more. What has not been replaced is the food. Pickled vegetables, cured pork, cakes steamed in bamboo, and the sweets made of malt that stick to the teeth are still made at home by people who have been making them at home for a very long time.

Bargaining that remains is not about money. A woman at the vegetable stall asks for a bunch of celery, then asks whether the seller will be back after the holiday, and he says he will not, his son is coming from the city and they will close for a week. She takes the celery and asks where the son works now. He tells her. She tells him about her own daughter, who is not coming back this year because the train tickets were impossible. Neither of them reaches for a bag while they talk, and the celery lies on the counter between them.

By four in the afternoon the light is going and the stock is going with it. Fish are down to the last few, sweets are in paper bags, and the man with the chestnuts has sold the drum down to the bottom. Traders begin to fold the tables and stack them against the wall, and the street fills with people carrying bags in both hands. In a week the same street will be full of red paper and ash and the sound of firecrackers, and then it will be empty again.

What the afternoon leaves behind is the ordinary kind. Nothing about it will be photographed, and nothing about it will be remembered as the festival. This is the part that the holiday is actually built on: the last round of errands, the money counted out in small notes, the one chance to see the neighbours before everybody withdraws into their own houses for two weeks. By the end of the day the street is swept and the shutters are down. The year is about to be closed, and it is being closed the way it was lived, without ceremony.
