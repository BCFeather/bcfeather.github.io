American consumer prices rose 3.7 per cent in the year to August, the Bureau of Labor Statistics said on 13 September, up from 3.2 per cent. Core measure fell to 4.3 per cent from 4.7. Economists had expected 3.6 per cent, and the month was the second in a row in which the headline rose as the fuel got more expensive.

Petrol is the driver of the headline. The price at the pump rose by about 10 per cent on the month as the crude rallied, and the energy contributed more than half of the monthly increase. A headline that follows the barrel is a headline that the weather and the cartel control. The national average price of petrol rose to about 3.85 dollars a gallon, and the seasonal switch to the cheaper winter blend will lower it in the autumn. The diesel price rose faster than the petrol, and the trucking companies have said they will pass the cost into the freight rates by the winter.

Shelter is the driving force in the core. The rents rose by about 0.3 per cent on the month and seven per cent over the year, and the measure lags the market by about a year, which is why the economists expect it to fall through 2024. A rent that is signed is a statistic that arrives late. The rent measure is about a third of the basket, and the new leases being signed show a rise of about three per cent, which is half the level of a year ago.

Used cars provided relief. The prices of the second-hand vehicles fell for a third month, and the new cars were little changed as the dealers' inventories recovered. A car that is cheaper is a core that falls. The prices of the used vehicles fell by about two per cent, and the auction houses report that the supply is returning to normal.

Services other than shelter held up firmly. The prices of the transport and the medical care rose, and the measure that the Fed watches for the domestic pressure was firm. A service that does not fall is a wage that is still passing through. The transport services rose as the airline fares climbed, and the insurance went up by about 20 per cent over the year as the repair costs rose.

What the report shows is that the headline has been pushed up by the fuel and the core is coming down slowly, and the two together are the case for holding the rate rather than raising it. The Fed meets next week, and the report leaves the decision open in a way that the chairman has described as data-dependent. A headline at 3.7 per cent is a headline that the White House will find uncomfortable, and the core is the number that decides the rate.
