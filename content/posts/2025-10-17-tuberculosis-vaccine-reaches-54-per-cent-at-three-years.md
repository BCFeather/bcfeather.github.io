Tuberculosis vaccine development has a long record of candidates that worked in animals and failed in trials, and the field has learned to report efficacy with care. A trial in 12,000 adolescents reported this month that a candidate reduced sustained infection by 54 per cent over three years.

The endpoint is a design choice, and it decides what the trial is measuring. The trial measured sustained infection, defined as a positive test at two consecutive visits six months apart, rather than disease. That endpoint catches the transition that a vaccine should prevent and it requires a smaller sample than a disease endpoint.

Candidate here is a cytomegalovirus-vectored vaccine expressing three antigens. It was chosen over a protein subunit because the vector persists and presents antigens continuously, and the trial's immunogenicity data show a T-cell response that lasted beyond 24 months.

Efficacy varies by prior exposure. Participants with a negative baseline test showed 62 per cent protection, and those with evidence of prior infection showed 31. That difference is consistent with the hypothesis that the vaccine boosts a response rather than creating one. Site selection, enrolment and the laboratory's confirmation each add a delay between a case's occurrence and its appearance in the trial's count.

Safety was unremarkable. Injection-site reactions in 40 per cent, transient fever in 6, and no serious events attributed to the vaccine across 12,000 participants.

How the candidate compares with the existing vaccine is the policy question. The BCG vaccine protects infants against severe forms and has inconsistent protection against pulmonary disease in adolescents, and a 54 per cent addition on top of it is a real gain at a population level.

Cost and delivery are the constraints that will decide use. The candidate needs a cold chain and two doses, and a trial in a high-burden setting will have to show that the 54 per cent translates into cases averted per dollar.

A vaccine's endpoint is a choice about what counts as disease. A trial that counts confirmed pulmonary disease reaches a different number from one that counts infection, and a 54 per cent efficacy against disease at three years is a result whose meaning depends on that definition.

Trial sites determine the comparison. A site with high transmission tests a vaccine's ability to protect against repeated exposure, and a site with low transmission tests its ability to protect a naive immune system. The same candidate can look different in each.

A vaccine's three-year readout is a milestone and not a licence. A trial that reports at three years must continue to five, and a regulator's file needs the longer interval because tuberculosis's latency makes a short follow-up uninformative about protection against reactivation.

Two numbers carry the weight of this result, 62 and 31. A vaccine that works best in people who have never been infected is a vaccine for adolescents, and that is a program a health ministry can plan around.
