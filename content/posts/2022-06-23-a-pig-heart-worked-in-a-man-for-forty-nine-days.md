A porcine heart that is not rejected by a human immune system requires the removal of the antigens that the human antibody recognizes, and the pig that supplied this organ carried ten gene edits. The operation was a first, and the outcome is a case report rather than a trial. A pig organ is rejected within minutes by preformed antibody, and the edits that make it survive are the edits that remove the epitopes those antibodies recognize.

A 57-year-old man with heart failure who was not a candidate for a conventional transplant, and the graft was a porcine heart with ten edits, including the removal of three galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose epitopes and the addition of human regulatory genes. Immunosuppression was based on CD40 blockade. The paper appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 22 June 2022. Ten edits is a number that the field has been building towards for two decades, and each one answers a specific antibody or a specific complement pathway.

Weaning from extracorporeal membrane oxygenation succeeded membrane oxygenation, and the xenograft functioned normally without apparent rejection for weeks. Sudden diastolic thickening and failure occurred on day 49, and life support was withdrawn on day 60. A graft that functions for seven weeks is a graft that has passed the hyperacute and the acute stages, which are the stages that killed the earlier attempts.

At autopsy the graft was oedematous and had nearly doubled in weight, with scattered myocyte necrosis, interstitial oedema and red-cell extravasation, and no microvascular thrombosis. Those findings are not the signature of typical rejection, which is why the authors describe the mechanism as unresolved. An absence of thrombosis removes one of the usual explanations, and the authors report the finding without a mechanism because the mechanism is not yet established.

A heart that doubles in weight is a heart that has accumulated fluid, and the fluid can come from an inflammatory response, from a capillary leak or from a failure of the lymphatic drainage that a graft does not have. The absence of thrombosis removes one of the usual explanations. A graft does not have the lymphatic drainage that a native organ has, and the absence is one of the reasons that oedema accumulates in a transplanted tissue.

What the case establishes is a pig organ can sustain a human circulation for weeks, which is the proof of principle that the field needed, and it also establishes that the failure mode is not the one that the immunosuppressive protocol was designed to prevent. A proof of principle is the contribution, and a failure mode that is not rejection is the surprise that the paper reports.

A single patient with a compassionate-use indication cannot separate the contribution of the gene edits from the contribution of the immunosuppression or from the patient's own condition, and the trial that would do so is the one that the field has to run next. The trial that would separate the contributions has to be run in patients who are candidates for a conventional transplant, and those patients are the ones a trial can ethically enrol.
