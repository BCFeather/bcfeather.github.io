Sexual deception in *Ophrys* orchids works because the flower looks and smells like a female bee or wasp. Males attempt to mate with it, carry the pollinaria away, and visit another flower, and because each orchid species mimics a different insect, the mistake keeps the species apart. The flower's advantage is that a male learns nothing, so the same mistake repeats and pollen keeps moving. Each species has its own pollinator, so the isolation is premating and it is chemical as much as visual.

A paper in *Nature Communications* on 26 July 2024 reports the genome that underlies the trick. The assembly covers 5.2 gigabases at chromosome scale for *Ophrys sphegodes*, which is large even for an orchid and which is large partly because of repetitive DNA. Chromosome-scale assembly matters here because the candidate locus has to be placed on a chromosome rather than on a scaffold.

Transposable elements expanded before the radiation of the *O. sphegodes* group. The expansion predates the split into species, which places it as a background condition for the diversification rather than a consequence of it, and it is the kind of event that creates raw material for new regulation. Repetitive DNA is usually treated as noise in plant genomics, and this genome gives it a role in diversification.

Gene duplication supplied the chemistry. Copies of genes in the scent pathway allow one copy to keep its original function while another diverges, and the authors find duplications associated with the compounds that make up the mimic's bouquet. Duplication is the standard route by which a pathway gains a copy free to specialize, and scent chemistry is a good place for it.

A candidate region on chromosome 2 is highly differentiated, which is what a genome scan shows when a locus has been under divergent selection between populations. Pollinator preference is the plausible pressure, and the region is the first mapped candidate for it in this genus. Differentiation of that kind is what a scan detects when gene flow between populations is restricted at a locus.

The genome also makes the repeated evolution of deception tractable. If independent *Ophrys* lineages recruit the same duplicated families, then the innovation is a matter of tweaking a shared toolkit rather than inventing a pathway each time. Comparative genomes from other *Ophrys* species would test whether the same duplications recur, and that is the obvious next step.

Caveats are the usual ones for a large plant genome. Repetitive sequence compresses the assembly in places, and one species cannot represent a genus of more than a hundred. What the paper establishes is architecture. Mimicry in this group is built from mobile DNA, duplicated scent genes and a locus that responds to pollinator choice, which is a mechanism rather than a list of ingredients. Even so, one well-assembled genome can anchor a genus as large as this one, and the anchored genus is a model for the rest.
