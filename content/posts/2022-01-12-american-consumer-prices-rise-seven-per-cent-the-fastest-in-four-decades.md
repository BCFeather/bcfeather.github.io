Figures published by the Labour Department on 12 January put the consumer price index 7.0 per cent above its level a year earlier, the fastest annual pace since 1982 and the seventh consecutive month above five. The monthly increase was 0.5 per cent, and the index for all items less food and energy rose 5.5 per cent, the highest in thirty years.

Composition had changed through the year. In the spring the increases had come from a narrow set of goods whose supply had been disrupted, especially used cars, which had risen by more than a third over the year. By the winter the pressure had broadened: shelter costs, which carry a third of the index, were rising at the fastest rate since the 1980s, and prices for services that require workers were climbing as wages rose. The index for shelter rose 0.4 per cent in the month and 4.1 per cent over the year, a pace that lags the rents written into new leases and therefore had further to run.

Macro arithmetic explains the broadening. Congress had sent households three rounds of payments, the Federal Reserve was still buying bonds, and demand for goods had outrun the capacity of ports, trucking and factories. As spending shifted back towards restaurants and travel, the firms that had absorbed higher input costs began to pass them on, and the wage data showed that they could.

Politics of the number is unforgiving. Real average hourly earnings had fallen for nine months, and the White House spent the winter describing the increase as a supply problem inherited from the pandemic and a consequence of the oil price, while Republicans campaigned on $4 petrol and grocery bills. Economists inside the administration had begun to argue in public that the labour market was tight enough to sustain the prices.

Markets drew a conclusion about policy. Futures priced the first rate increase for March with near certainty, and the ten-year yield rose above 1.7 per cent for the first time since the pandemic began. Mortgage rates followed, and the yield curve flattened, which is the shape it takes when investors expect tightening to slow growth later rather than sooner. The ten-year yield had started the year near 1.5 per cent, and the move of a quarter point in three weeks was the sharpest of the cycle.

A separate argument got settled by the report as well. Those who had argued through 2021 that inflation would fade once supply chains healed had to explain why shelter and services, which no country imports, were accelerating, and those who had blamed demand had to explain why the increase began in the categories where supply was tightest. Both explanations were true at different moments in the year, which is why the argument was so hard to end. By the time the February report appeared the annual rate had reached 7.5 per cent, and the question of whether the peak had passed was settled by a war rather than by the data.
