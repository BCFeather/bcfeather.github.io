---

A laboratory notebook is three documents at once. It is a record that a patent office or a misconduct inquiry can rely on, a memory aid for the person who wrote it, and a habit that forces a researcher to state what happened before deciding what it means. Software has improved the second and the first and left the third behind.

Legibility and completeness are what the legal function rewards. A page with a date, a witness signature and a description of a procedure is evidence; a page with a sketch and an abbreviation is not. Notebooks kept for a patent are kept carefully and read by no one until a dispute, which is the definition of a document written for an audience that does not exist yet.

Memory is the function software does best. A searchable electronic notebook with a timestamp per entry can answer what a protocol was in March and what a result was in June, and a paper notebook can answer that only if its index was written well. The habit is disappearing, and quietly. A researcher who types into a system designed for compliance writes what the system's fields ask for, and the fields were designed by someone who wanted dates, authors and linked samples. The observation that does not fit a field is the observation a notebook is for.

I keep a paper notebook and a file, and the paper one holds the things that did not work. A failed reaction with a smell, a temperature that ran away, a suspicion that the pump was the problem: these are the entries that a search cannot retrieve because the search was never given the words.

Twenty minutes costs little and its absence costs a lot. Twenty minutes at a bench writing up what happened is a day saved when a result has to be explained six months later, and the explanation's quality depends on whether the person who did it recorded the anomaly. A laboratory notebook is a legal instrument as well as a record. A patent's priority rests on a dated, witnessed entry, and an electronic system that logs a timestamp achieves the same thing with an audit trail that a court accepts. The paper's advantage is that it needs no system and no administrator.

The failure mode differs. A paper notebook is lost once and cannot be lost again; a database is lost once and takes the whole record with it. Institutions that keep both are insuring against the second failure, and the cost is the duplication. A hybrid arrangement is what most laboratories have settled on. A paper notebook records the day and a database records the result, and the notebook's residual function is the one an auditor asks for: a contemporaneous entry that a person wrote and signed.

A reader may reasonably ask whether this is nostalgia. A laboratory's records need to be findable, shareable and auditable, and paper is none of those. The answer is that a hybrid arrangement serves all three functions and that the paper half is the one that gets dropped first.
