The Pantanal is a basin that fills and empties, and its year is organised around that. The Paraguay river runs through the middle of it and its tributaries come down from the surrounding plateau, and in the wet season the water cannot leave fast enough, because the outlet is narrow and the slope is almost nothing. The result is a sheet of water that spreads over tens of thousands of square kilometres, connects lakes that in the dry season are separate, and then recedes over months, leaving the ground covered in grass and in the silt the flood carried. The outlet is the Paraguay itself, and it narrows where the basin ends, which is why the water backs up rather than drains. The difference between a big flood and a small one is measured in metres and not in weeks.

The flood pulse is the reason the place is productive. Water that arrives slowly and leaves slowly delivers nutrients without scouring, and the grasses that grow on the floodplain are the base of a food chain that runs through fish, through birds and through the cattle that the ranchers keep. The fish move into the flooded grassland to spawn, the birds follow the fish, and the jaguars that the region is known for hunt along the edges where the water meets the forest. The nutrients come from the Andes and from the plateau, and the flood spreads them across a plain that would otherwise be poor. Grass that stands under water for part of the year is the reason the cattle gain weight without supplement.

Dry season is the other half of the cycle, and it is the harder half. As the water falls the lakes shrink, the fish concentrate in the remaining pools, and the animals that depend on the water come to the same few places. Fires set to clear land can run through the dry grass and into the forest, and the fires of 2020 burned a large fraction of the basin because the water had not come as it should. Fire is the threat the flood mitigates, since a wet year leaves too much damp in the grass to burn, and 2020 was a dry one. Fires also burn the peat, which smoulders for weeks and takes the soil with it.

Cattle have grazed the natural grassland for two centuries, and the people who run them have learned the flood's habits. The pantaneiro traditions include moving the herd to the higher ground before the flood and burning at the right moment, and the system works because the flood is predictable in its timing even when it is not in its size. What has changed is the upstream: the plateau above the basin is now farmland, and the sediment and the pesticide that come down with the water are different from what arrived a hundred years ago. The ranch is a working landscape and not a wilderness, and its conservation record owes something to how little of it has been converted. Fertiliser and lime from the plateau have changed the chemistry of the water, and the change is measurable downstream.

Connection to the wider world runs through the river, and the river's health is the basin's health. Dams proposed upstream would change the pulse, and a pulse that is flattened is not a smaller version of the same thing: it changes when the fish spawn, what the birds find, and how long the water stays on the grass. The argument about those dams has been running for two decades and is not settled. The sediment the flood carries is part of the system, and a river that no longer carries it rebuilds its channel differently. A dam built for power also blocks the fish that migrate, and the migrations are part of what the pulse supports.

At the end of the dry season the basin looks like a plain with a few ponds in it, the grass bleached, the cattle standing in the shade, and the sky enormous. Then the first rain of October arrives, the ground softens, the ponds join, and within weeks the plain is a lake with trees standing in it. What the flood does every year is reset the system, and what the people who study it watch is whether the reset keeps its timing. The uncertainty lies in the timing and not in the amount, and the people who live there plan around the timing. When the flood comes late, the fires come early, and the two failures compound.
