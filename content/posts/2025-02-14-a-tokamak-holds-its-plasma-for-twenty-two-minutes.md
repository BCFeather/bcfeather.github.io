A tokamak in southern France held a fusion plasma for one thousand three hundred and thirty-seven seconds on 12 February, more than twenty-two minutes and a large step beyond the previous duration records. The machine is called WEST, it is operated by the French atomic energy commission, and its purpose is not to produce power but to test the materials that a power plant would use. The pulse was longer than the previous record by a factor of several, and the machine ran it with the same heating power it uses for shorter shots.

Plasma facing components are the point of the experiment. A tokamak's inner wall absorbs the heat and the particle flux that escape the confinement, and a reactor will run at fluxes far above what any current machine sees. Tungsten is the leading candidate for that wall because it has the highest melting point of any metal, and WEST is one of the few machines with a full tungsten divertor. Tungsten has a drawback that the experiment also measures: it erodes under bombardment and the atoms it releases cool the plasma.

Duration changes the physics of the wall. A plasma that lasts seconds heats a thin surface layer; a plasma that lasts twenty minutes drives heat deep into the material, and the impurities it sputters into the plasma accumulate. The experiment is a test of whether a tungsten wall can stay stable through an exposure that resembles a reactor's pulse. Impurity accumulation is the mechanism that ends most long pulses, and the control system that removes helium ash has to work continuously.

The temperature that matters is not the plasma's but the wall's. Fusion plasmas run at millions of degrees while the wall must stay below the point where tungsten becomes brittle, and the two are separated by a boundary layer of a few centimetres. Controlling that layer is the hardest engineering problem in the field. A boundary layer that is too thin melts the wall, and one that is too thick radiates the power away before it reaches the divertor.

Running for twenty-two minutes also tests the auxiliary systems. Heating, fuelling, diagnostics and the control loops that keep the plasma centred all have to operate without interruption, and a component that drifts over twenty minutes is a component that would fail in a reactor that runs for hours. Real-time control of a plasma for twenty minutes requires models that run faster than the plasma changes, and those models are the quiet achievement.

Nothing in the result shortens the path to a power plant, and it removes one class of doubt. ITER, the international machine being assembled in the same region, is designed around pulses of a few hundred seconds, and a commercial plant will need to run far longer. Demonstrating that the wall survives a long pulse is a necessary step, and necessary steps are what the programme has been short of.
