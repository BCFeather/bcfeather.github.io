Incretin therapy is injected, and the injection is the part of the treatment that patients most often cite when they stop. An oral small molecule that activates the same receptor would remove that barrier, and several companies have been chasing one for a decade. The injections are weekly, self-administered, and require refrigeration, and each of those requirements removes some patients from treatment. Patients report that the injection is the part of the regimen they most resent, more than the nausea.

Aleniglipron is a non-peptide GLP-1 receptor agonist, which is the pharmacological achievement: the receptor's binding pocket is designed for a peptide, and finding a small molecule that fits it and survives digestion took years of medicinal chemistry. The receptor is a class B G protein-coupled receptor with a large binding pocket, and small molecules that activate it have to mimic a peptide's surface with a fraction of its size.

A phase 2b trial reported in *Nature Medicine* on 5 June randomized people with overweight or obesity to aleniglipron or placebo and measured weight change over the treatment period. The magnitude reported is in the range the class now expects, with the caveat that a phase 2b is sized to find a dose rather than to establish a claim. Dose-ranging is the purpose of a 2b, and the trial tested several doses against placebo with a treatment period long enough for the plateau to appear.

Oral small molecules have a practical advantage beyond convenience. Peptide drugs have to be injected because they are digested, and a molecule that survives the stomach can be manufactured at a different cost and distributed through ordinary pharmacies rather than cold chains. An oral drug can also be combined with other oral agents, which is the direction that diabetes treatment has taken for two decades. Tablets also remove the sharps disposal and the travel that injection therapy requires.

Tolerability is the same problem in a new form. Nausea and vomiting are mechanism-related rather than molecule-related, and an oral drug taken daily produces a different exposure profile from a weekly injection, which can change how the side effects are experienced even if the average concentration is the same. Daily dosing also produces a lower peak and a steadier trough than a weekly injection, and that profile changes how nausea is distributed across the week.

Phase 3 will decide whether the pill is competitive rather than merely convenient. Weight loss of a few per cent less than an injected competitor, in exchange for no injection, may be enough for many patients and is a decision that regulators and payers will make with different weights. Adherence to a daily pill is usually worse than adherence to a weekly injection, which is a counter-intuitive finding that the phase 3 programme will have to measure. The pill's success will also depend on price, since convenience is worth more when the alternative is expensive and scarce.
