Ice cores are read as archives of temperature, and the reading depends on an assumption about when the snow fell. In West Antarctica, most accumulation happens in summer, so a core's isotope record is weighted towards one season, and a temperature reconstructed from it is a summer temperature whether the reconstruction says so or not. The study in *Nature*, 11 January 2023 makes that assumption explicit and then tests it. Tyler Jones, Kurt Cuffey, James White and colleagues analyse a continuous record of water-isotope ratios from the West Antarctic Ice Sheet Divide core and compare it with orbital calculations of insolation at different seasons.

Annual maximum insolation dominates the Holocene signal. Summer sunshine at high southern latitudes varies with the Earth's axial tilt and precession, and the core's isotopes track that variation rather than the annual mean. The West Antarctic climate, on this evidence, has been paced by its summers.

The implication for the present is about which season matters. A warming trend measured as an annual average can hide a larger change in summer, and a summer that melts more snow is a summer that adds mass loss even if the annual mean looks stable. Ice-sheet mass balance is sensitive to the season in which the energy arrives.

Continuity in the core is what makes the comparison possible. A record with gaps can be aligned to orbital cycles only loosely; a continuous one can be matched wiggle for wiggle, and the match is what identifies the seasonal weighting rather than the annual one. What the study does not resolve is the spatial pattern. West Antarctica is not uniform, and the divide site is one location on a continent whose coast and interior behave differently. A second core with the same continuity, at a different elevation, would show whether the summer pacing holds across the region.

The climate-model comparison is the other test. Models that reproduce the core's isotope record must be reproducing the seasonal weighting, and a model that matches the annual mean while missing the summer would produce an isotope series that looks similar in trend and different in phase. The phase is where the evidence is.

For ice-sheet projections, the result changes the diagnostic. A reconstruction that reports an annual temperature understates the summer, and the summer is the season that decides whether a surface melts.

Seasonality also matters for the interpretation of past ice-core carbon dioxide. Air is trapped in the firn at the end of summer, and a record dated by accumulation assumes that the trapping happens at a known time of year. A summer-weighted temperature and a summer-weighted trapping age are consistent with each other, which the older reconstructions were not. A summer-paced record also explains why some earlier West Antarctic reconstructions disagreed with each other. Cores with different accumulation seasons weight the same insolation forcing differently, and the disagreement was in the weighting rather than in the ice.
