Brain atlases have moved from maps of anatomy to maps of gene expression, and the move changes what a map can answer. A region of cortex that looks uniform under a microscope contains dozens of cell types in proportions that shift with age, and bulk sequencing averages over all of them until the shift disappears into a mean. Single-cell sequencing recovers the proportions, and it costs enough that most studies sequence a handful of donors.

The PsychAD programme sequenced many. The largest paper in the collection, led by Hui Yang, presents 1.3 million gene expression profiles from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of 284 donors without neurological disease, spanning infancy to late adulthood. A companion paper led by Donghoon Lee analyses the full dataset of 6.3 million profiles from 1,494 donors, including people with Alzheimer's disease, other dementias, Parkinson's disease, schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. The papers appeared in *Nature* on 23 September.

The developmental trajectory turned out to have three phases. Gene expression changed in ways that track the remodelling of early brain development, stayed stable through midlife up to roughly 24 years of age, and then showed declines in cell composition after that point, with a distinct reprogramming of cells after 65. That last phase is the one that matters for disease, because it coincides with the age at which neurodegenerative diagnoses begin to appear.

Comparing diseases produced the result that will be most quoted. All the disorders studied shared alterations in genes involved in basic cellular function, which is unsurprising given that every disease perturbs metabolism and stress responses. The stronger finding was similarity between the different forms of dementia and Parkinson's disease, particularly in genes tied to neuronal development and synaptic function, which suggests that the downstream signature of neurodegeneration is more common than the upstream causes.

Population scale brings its own problems. Donors differ in ancestry, medication, cause of death and the interval between death and processing, and each of those variables leaves a mark on gene expression that has to be modelled out. The consortium reports its covariates and its sensitivity analyses, and any reader who has worked with post-mortem tissue will want to know how much of the signal survives adjustment.

An atlas is also a resource rather than a finding. The value of 6.3 million profiles is that future studies can ask questions the consortium did not, by projecting their own data onto a common coordinate system, and the papers describe the coordinate system as carefully as the biology. That is the part of the work that will outlast the specific claims.

What the collection cannot supply is causation. A gene expression difference between donors with and without dementia is a correlation in tissue that has already been damaged, and distinguishing cause from consequence requires experiments that perturb the candidate genes in model systems. The atlases define the candidates, which is the necessary first step.
