The feature was found in 2023 and looked like a mistake. A narrow straight line of bright knots stretched between a galaxy and a point of nothing, and the obvious explanations were an edge-on jet, a string of background objects or an artefact of the reduction. Van Dokkum and colleagues argued that it was a wake, produced by a black hole moving fast enough to trigger star formation along its path.

Follow-up imaging has now confirmed the interpretation. The object is a supermassive black hole that was ejected from its host galaxy, presumably by the gravitational slingshot of a merger in which three bodies interact and one is thrown out, and it is now moving through the intergalactic medium. The trail behind it consists of young stars, formed from gas that the black hole's passage compressed. The research highlight appeared in *Nature* on 20 February, with the underlying work in *The Astrophysical Journal*.

A black hole cannot form stars directly, and the mechanism runs through the gas. A massive object moving through a medium at supersonic speed produces a wake, and the wake of a black hole massive enough to carry a galaxy's worth of gravitational influence compresses the gas behind it. Compression raises density, density raises cooling, and cooling triggers collapse. The stars form in the wake and then stay there while the black hole continues.

Speed is the number that makes the story. The black hole must be moving faster than the escape velocity of its galaxy, otherwise it would fall back, and the length of the trail implies that it has been travelling for a long time. Estimates put the velocity in the range of kilometres per second, which is fast for a galaxy and slow relative to light, which is why the feature is visible at all. Gas in the wake is not merely pushed, and the shock compresses it into a sheet whose collapse time is short compared with the black hole's crossing time.

Confirmation rests on spectroscopy rather than on morphology. The knots show young stellar populations with the emission lines that star formation produces, the velocities are consistent with gas being swept along, and the black hole's own position coincides with a source of X-ray or radio emission that matches an accreting massive object. Morphology suggested the story and spectra test it.

Rarity is part of the interest. Ejected black holes were predicted for decades and observed rarely, and a clean example with a visible wake is a measurement of a process that had been theoretical. The implication is that some of the black hole mass in the universe is not in galaxies but in the space between them, moving.

What the observation cannot settle is how much of the intergalactic medium this heats. A single object stirs a region the size of a small galaxy, and the total contribution of all such objects to the thermal state of the cosmic web is a number nobody can yet measure.
