description: "Memphis released sixty-seven minutes of police video on 27 January 2023 showing the beating of Tyre Nichols, a twenty-nine-year-old Black man who died on 10 January after a traffic stop, and five officers were charged with second-degree murder."
---

Tyre Nichols was stopped by police in Memphis on the evening of 7 January, was beaten by five officers, and died in hospital on 10 January. He was twenty-nine and worked in a shipping warehouse. The stop was for what the police described as reckless driving, and the video shows that the officers had no reason to suspect him of anything more serious. The officers said afterwards that he had fled on foot, and the video shows that he was caught within a hundred metres of his car.

On the evening of 27 January the city released sixty-seven minutes of footage drawn from body cameras worn by the officers and from a camera fixed to a pole. The officers are shown pulling him from the car, holding him down, and striking him with fists, boots and a baton while he screams. At one point he is propped against a car and left there for several minutes while the officers talk to one another. He can be heard asking what he had done, and no one in the recording answers him. The footage was released in full, without redactions of the faces of the officers.

The five officers were dismissed within a week of the encounter and charged with second-degree murder and a list of other offences that includes aggravated assault and kidnapping. All five are Black, which was treated by the city's own officials as a fact that complicated the simplest version of the story and did not change the legal one. The district attorney said the charges reflected conduct the video had documented, and he declined to say whether more officers would be charged.

Scorpion was the name of the unit they belonged to, a street crimes squad of about thirty officers created to patrol high-crime areas, and it had collected complaints for years. It was disbanded on 28 January, two days after the charges were announced, and the police director said the unit's methods had been the subject of concern before the video was released.

Memphis has a long history with the distance between the police and the neighbourhoods they patrol. Martin Luther King was killed there in 1968 while supporting a sanitation workers' strike, and the city's police department has been under federal oversight and out of it again in the decades since. The video was released in a city that knew what it was looking at. The department's budget had grown while the complaints against the street units had grown with it, and the disbanding was the first cut it had made in either.

The cameras were there and the supervision had been absent, which is the finding that the recordings make without needing an investigation. Sixty-seven minutes is longer than most documentaries and shorter than the time it takes a family to bury a son, and the city spent the week between those two numbers. The footage came out on a Friday evening, which is when American cities release the things they hope will be forgotten by Monday.
