Carbon dioxide cycling between an atmosphere and a rocky interior is what keeps a terrestrial planet's climate within a range that allows liquid water. The gas weathers rock, the weathered rock is buried, volcanism returns the gas, and the loop runs negative feedback for billions of years. Whether that loop always holds is a question about the endpoints of the cycle rather than about its middle. A thermostat needs a range over which it can act, and a gas that leaves the atmosphere altogether takes that range with it.

Simulations reported in a preprint posted to arXiv on 1 October 2022 find a second stable state for ocean-bearing planets around G- and F-type stars, and the same runs do not produce it around M dwarfs. In that state carbon dioxide condenses at the surface rather than remaining in the atmosphere, forming a blanket of clathrate hydrate or of liquid carbon dioxide, and the weathering feedback that stabilizes the familiar climate stops working as the partial pressure falls far enough that the greenhouse contribution of the gas is switched off.

The second state is not a transient. The authors report that it can appear early in a planet's history and persist for billions of years, and that at higher instellation, with weathering ineffective, it oscillates between a cool condensing phase and a warm non-condensing one. The oscillation is a limit cycle of the coupled atmosphere and surface, not a drift towards a fixed point, and the authors note that the trend in carbon dioxide with instellation reverses between the two states, so a planet moving outward along the habitable zone can pass through a condensing phase on the way.

Why the star's spectral type matters comes down to where the incoming radiation is absorbed. Around an M dwarf the near-infrared flux is larger relative to the visible, the surface temperature responds differently, and the bistability does not appear in the simulations. The authors are careful to say that their runs use clear-sky radiative transfer, so clouds, which are the usual complication for M-dwarf habitability, are not in the model.

What the work does not claim is an occurrence rate. Two states exist in a model; how often a real planet sits in the second one depends on the initial carbon inventory, on the weathering rate, and on processes the paper sets aside, including the deep interior's ability to store carbon and the loss of atmosphere to space. None of those is small at the ages where the second state is said to persist.

For observers the result sharpens a target and narrows a guess. A planet whose carbon dioxide has condensed onto the surface carries a spectrum with features that a thick atmosphere would not produce, and the bistable band of instellations is narrow enough to be searched deliberately, provided the planet's orbit is known well enough to place it inside the band.
