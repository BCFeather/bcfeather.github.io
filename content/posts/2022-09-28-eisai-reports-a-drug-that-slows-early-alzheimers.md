title: "Eisai reports a drug that slows early Alzheimer's"

description: "The trial of lecanemab found a 27 per cent slower decline on a clinical scale, and the side effects included brain swelling."
---

Patients on the drug declined by 0.45 points less than those on the placebo, which is a 27 per cent slowing. The results were presented on 27 September at a conference in Stockholm and published in the New England Journal of Medicine. The trial enrolled 1,795 patients with early Alzheimer's disease, and half received lecanemab every two weeks for eighteen months. The drug is an antibody that removes amyloid plaques from the brain. The primary measure was the clinical dementia rating, a scale that scores memory, judgment and daily function. The difference is small enough that patients and families may not notice it, and large enough that regulators accepted it as evidence of benefit.

About 17 per cent of patients on the drug had brain swelling visible on a scan, and 13 per cent had bleeding, compared with 2 per cent on the placebo. The side effects were the argument against. Most cases were without symptoms, and some caused headaches or confusion. Two deaths in the extension phase were linked to the drug by investigators.

Measuring a blood biomarker called p-tau217 gave the trial a second readout, and it fell 90 per cent on the drug. That gave the researchers a way to show the drug was doing what it was designed to do, and it supported the amyloid hypothesis, which had been doubted after several failed trials. The plaque was removed, and the decline was slower, and the two facts were connected.

Regulators moved quickly. The Food and Drug Administration granted priority review in the summer, and it approved the drug in January 2023 under the accelerated pathway. The European regulator received the file in the autumn and took a different view, rejecting it in July 2023 on the ground that the benefit was too small against the risk. The cost was announced at 26,500 dollars a year, and the infusion every two weeks requires a clinic visit. Insurers hesitated, and the American scheme for the elderly said it would cover the drug only inside a registry. The company said it would price according to the value, and the price did not change.

Some clinicians said a quarter of a point on a scale is a rounding error, and others said eighteen months of slower decline is what families ask for. The debate in the field was about what counts as a benefit. The trial's own authors wrote that the effect was modest and that the drug was a beginning rather than an answer. The company said it would submit the full data to the European regulator in October.

A second antibody, donanemab, reported stronger results in 2023, and it was approved in 2024. Both drugs carry the same risk of swelling, and both require confirmation of amyloid before use. The plaque theory survived the year, and the argument about how much it is worth moved from the journals to the regulators. Neurologists said the drug would require scanners and specialists that many clinics do not have.
