A Janus monolayer is a transition metal dichalcogenide with a different chalcogen on each face, which breaks the mirror symmetry of the layer and leaves a permanent out-of-plane polarization across its thickness. The polarization is what makes the material interesting for optoelectronics, and the methods that produce it, grown from a melt or by sulfurizing one face of an existing layer, are energetic enough to leave vacancies and interstitials behind. Molybdenum selenide sulfide, MoSSe, is the best studied of the family, and it is the system on which the defect inventory was built.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 10 March 2024 maps those defects. The authors combine high-resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy, density functional theory and cryogenic optical spectroscopy on the same material, and the three techniques are used so that each one checks the others. The microscopy was done on monolayers suspended or supported so that both chalcogen faces could be imaged, since a defect on the sulfur face looks different from the same defect on the selenium face.

Microscopy supplies the inventory. The images show single chalcogen vacancies on each face, double vacancies that remove one sulfur and one selenium from the same column, metal interstitials, and substitutional metal impurities, and the paper records the structural signature of each. Dose matters here, because the electron beam that images a monolayer can also create the vacancies it is looking for, and the authors report the imaging conditions alongside the counts.

Theory supplies the energetics. Formation energies computed for each defect say which ones are stable enough to survive cooling, and the same calculations place localized bands inside the forbidden gap, which is the link between a structural defect and an electronic state. A chalcogen vacancy leaves three metal atoms with dangling bonds, and the resulting state sits near the middle of the gap, which is why the emission from a vacancy is so distinct from band-edge luminescence.

Spectroscopy supplies the optical signature. Cryogenic emission from h-BN-encapsulated monolayers shows narrow lines, and the authors correlate those lines with the defect species that theory predicts to emit at those energies, so the assignment rests on a match in both position and character. Localized emission of that kind is the working definition of a single-photon source in this material, and the catalogue therefore doubles as a list of the emitters a device might contain.

What the catalogue does not yet give is a recipe. Knowing which defects are present and what they emit does not say how to remove them, and the growth conditions that produce one species rather than another are the practical question the paper leaves to the materials side. An electrical measurement would help, since a charged defect shifts the gate voltage at which the channel turns on and the shift is characteristic of the species.

The value is as a quality standard. A device made from a Janus monolayer inherits whatever defects the growth left, and a list that ties each defect to a line in the spectrum turns an optical measurement into a structural assay, which is what a process engineer needs in order to compare two batches without cutting both open.
