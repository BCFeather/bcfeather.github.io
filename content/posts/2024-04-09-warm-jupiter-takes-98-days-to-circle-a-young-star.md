Transit surveys find planets whose transits are frequent, and Doppler surveys find planets whose reflex motion is fast, so the two methods together leave a gap at periods of tens of days. Long-period transiting planets sit in that gap, and they matter because a planet further out keeps more of the atmosphere it formed with, which transmission spectroscopy can then read.

The candidate came from a single transit in TESS data. One event fixes a period badly, so the team followed the star with photometry from TESS, NGTS and EulerCam and with spectroscopy from CORALIE, FEROS, HARPS and PFS, enough to confirm the planet, measure its mass and sharpen the ephemeris. A planet found this way is confirmed by the company it keeps and not by any single instrument.

A single transit gives the depth, which fixes the radius against the radius of the star, and it gives a chord across the disc, which constrains the impact parameter. Everything else has to come from follow-up work: a second transit fixes the period, radial velocity fixes the mass, and neither number can be guessed from the first event, however clean it looks in the light curve.

NGTS-30 b, also catalogued as TOI-4862 b, circles a G-type star of about 1.1 Gyr on a period of 98.29838 days. Its radius is 0.93 Jupiter radii and its mass 0.96 Jupiter masses, which makes it a warm Jupiter and not a hot one, and the orbit is moderately eccentric at 0.294.

Eccentricity does visible work. Because the orbit is not circular, the star-planet distance swings, and the equilibrium temperature runs from about 274 K at apoapsis to 500 K at periastron. That range is what makes the planet a test case: an atmosphere that survives the hot end of the orbit has to redistribute heat or lose it, and the light curve of a transit near apoapsis would show which.

Interior modelling gives a heavy-element mass fraction of 0.23 and an enrichment twenty times the stellar value, which is high for a planet of this mass and invites comparison with the population more than a conclusion about this one. No transmission spectrum is reported, so the atmospheric interest is a reason to observe and not yet a result.

The ephemeris rests on one transit plus follow-up, and further photometry would tighten it. Transmission spectroscopy at 98 days is expensive but feasible for a planet this size, and the eccentricity adds a handle: a spectrum taken near periastron samples a hotter atmosphere than one taken near apoapsis, so the same planet can be read at two temperatures.
