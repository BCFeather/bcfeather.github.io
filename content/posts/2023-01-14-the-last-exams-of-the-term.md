The examinations began on a Monday and ran for four days, which meant that the first week of January belonged to the timetable rather than to the calendar. The classroom was cold in the way that concrete rooms are cold, a cold that comes up through the floor and settles in the ankles. We wrote with our coats on and with the sleeves pulled over our wrists. The heating worked for two hours in the morning and then gave up, and the teachers wore their coats as well. The corridor smelt of chalk and of the oranges that someone had brought for the break.

The papers came in the order that they always came. Chinese first, then mathematics, then English, then physics and chemistry on the same day, and history and politics at the end. The mathematics paper was the one that decided the mood of the week, because it was the one that everyone compared answers about at the gate. Physics and chemistry shared a morning, which meant the same hand wrote for a hundred and fifty minutes without a break, and the second paper was always the worse one.

I remember the invigilator walking between the desks with her hands behind her back, and the sound of thirty pens stopping at the same moment when the bell rang. The clock on the wall was four minutes fast and everyone knew it, and the four minutes were the subject of a complaint that was made every term and never fixed. She stopped beside the radiator for a few minutes at a time and looked at the ceiling, and we took that as permission to look at the clock.

Marks came back on the Friday, in the order of the register, and the sheet was passed down the rows so that each of us could see the whole list. There is a particular kind of quiet that follows a list like that, and it has nothing to do with the quiet of a classroom that has done badly. It is the quiet of a classroom that has done well enough and knows that the next list will be harder. The list had a column for the class average at the bottom, and the teacher read the average out before the sheet was handed round.

I walked home in the dusk with the paper folded in my bag, past the shops that had put up their red decorations, and I did the arithmetic that everyone does: the marks divided by the total, the total compared with the last total, the distance between where I was and where I wanted to be. The arithmetic came out better than I had feared and worse than I had hoped, which is the usual result.

One more term and the building would belong to other people. That was the fact that the examinations could not measure, and it was the one that stayed with me on the walk home. The building would keep the desks and the marks, and the marks would be filed in an office that nobody visited.
