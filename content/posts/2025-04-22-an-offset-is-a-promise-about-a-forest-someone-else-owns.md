description: "Carbon credits from avoided deforestation are the cheapest kind of offset and the hardest to verify, and the market has spent three years discovering that."
---

A carbon offset is a payment for a reduction that happens somewhere else, and the cheapest reductions are usually the ones that are hardest to prove. A project that protects a forest sells credits on the difference between the carbon it stores and the carbon that would have been released if the forest had been cleared. The second number is a counterfactual, and a counterfactual cannot be observed.

Verification methods build a baseline from historical deforestation rates and from a model of pressure on the land. A project that is credited with avoiding ten thousand hectares of loss may be a project in a region where the loss would have happened anyway, or one where the loss moved to the next valley. The studies that examined a large sample of projects found that the credited reductions exceeded the plausible baseline in most of them. A model that projects pressure from roads and from commodity prices is a model with assumptions, and the assumptions are where the credits come from.

Market response has been to standardize. A set of core principles, issued by an industry body, requires projects to demonstrate additionality, to use conservative baselines and to submit to independent verification. A registry that follows the principles can sell credits at a higher price, and the higher price is meant to cover the cost of better measurement. A project that follows the principles pays for audits and for monitoring, and the cost is several dollars per tonne.

Buyers have changed too. A company that bought credits for publicity has become a company that is being asked by its auditors to justify the claim, and the justification is difficult for a credit that is a promise about a forest. Several large buyers have shifted towards removal credits, which have a physical product, and towards contracts that pay only on verified delivery. A removal credit from a direct-air-capture plant is measurable because the carbon ends up in a tank.

A deeper problem is that offsets are a substitute for a price on carbon. A firm that can buy a cheap credit has a cheaper route to a target than a firm that has to change its process, and the cheap route is the one that gets used. A policy that expects offsets to do the work of a carbon tax is a policy that has chosen the least reliable instrument. A buyer that publishes a claim is a buyer that its auditors will ask to justify, and the justification is a document.

What survives is the narrow case. A project that protects a forest with a documented threat, a conservative baseline and a long contract is a project that can be verified, and those projects cost more than the market's average price. The market that pays for them is small, and the market that pays for the rest is a market in promises. A credit that funds a real reduction is worth buying, and a credit that funds a story is worth avoiding.
