Systemic lupus erythematosus is an autoimmune disease in which the body reacts to its own nucleic acids, and the rare monogenic cases show which molecules hold that reaction in check. Phospholipase D family member 4 had been suspected because mice without the gene develop autoimmunity, but no human disease had been tied to it. A study in *Nature* now reports five patients with lupus and recessive loss-of-function variants in *PLD4*, which turns the mouse phenotype into a human one and gives the enzyme a job.

The five patients carry homozygous or compound heterozygous variants that leave little working protein, and the assays run on their cells show what that costs. Recombinant enzyme hydrolyses single-stranded RNA and single-stranded DNA from their ends, and the mutant forms lose most of that exonuclease activity. The specificity is informative: PLD4 does not cut double-stranded substrates or structured RNA, so its task is to clear short single-stranded fragments before they accumulate. The assays separate end trimming from internal cleavage, which is why the loss is a loss of debris clearance instead of a loss of repair.

Those fragments are ligands. Toll-like receptor 7 senses single-stranded RNA and toll-like receptor 9 senses unmethylated DNA, and both sit in the endosome where debris from dying cells arrives. In the patients both receptors were over-activated, and the pathways downstream, type I interferon above all, ran hot in dendritic cells. The mechanism explains the interferon signature that rheumatologists measure in lupus without needing a second explanation for it. The receptors sit in the endolysosomal membrane, so the substrate PLD4 removes never reaches them in a cell that has the enzyme.

*Pld4*-deficient mice reproduce the human picture. They develop autoimmunity with cell-intrinsic expansion of plasmacytoid dendritic cells and plasma cells, and the expansion is intrinsic because it survives in mixed bone marrow chimeras. The mouse work supplies the causal chain that a series of five patients cannot, and it ties the receptor activation to the two cell types that make interferon and antibody. The chimeras also show that the dendritic cell expansion drives the interferon, since mice lacking those cells do not produce it.

The therapeutic test is the part clinicians will read. Baricitinib, a JAK inhibitor that blocks type I interferon signalling downstream of the receptor, reversed the phenotype in the mice, and the authors present that as a rationale for treating patients with PLD4 deficiency. The argument is plausible because the pathway being blocked is the one the mutation activates, which is the condition under which a drug repurposing claim has a chance. The dose used in the mice is not the dose used in patients, so the result is a mechanism argument as much as a protocol.

Five patients are few and their variants differ, so the genotype-phenotype map stays thin. Whether partial loss raises lupus risk in the general population, and whether interferon blockade helps these patients as it does in other monogenic interferonopathies, are questions for a cohort instead of a case series. The result nonetheless adds a nuclease to the short list of genes where losing one function produces lupus. Biallelic loss, which is what these patients carry, is a different situation from the heterozygous variation a population study would detect.
