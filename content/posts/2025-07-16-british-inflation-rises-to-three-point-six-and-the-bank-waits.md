A inflation print that rises while the central bank is deciding whether to cut is a print that decides the meeting, and June's numbers arrived one month before the Bank of England's August decision with headline at 3.6 per cent, up from 3.4 per cent in May, and services at about 4.7 per cent, which is the component the committee has said it needs to see fall.

Food did the damage. Prices in the shops rose at the fastest pace in more than a year as the cost of cocoa, coffee and packaged goods fed through, and the groceries line that had been falling for eleven months turned positive, which is the sort of reversal that reaches the weekly shop and the inflation expectations that go with it.

Core inflation was steady at about 3.5 per cent and goods inflation was mild, so the report is a story about domestic services and imported food and not a broad reacceleration. A central bank can look through a commodity shock if wages are calm, and the committee's problem is that the wage data have been calm and slow to fall. Shop price inflation at that pace is what turns a headline number into a household argument, and the argument is the one that the committee hears from every business it surveys.

The labour market is loosening from a tight level. Vacancies have fallen for two years, private sector pay growth has slowed to below 5 per cent, and unemployment has drifted up to about 4.7 per cent, which is a combination that argues for cutting and a pace that argues against cutting quickly.

Growth is the other argument. Output rose 0.4 per cent in the first quarter and barely in the second, the construction sector is shrinking, and the fiscal consolidation announced in the autumn is still feeding through, which is a set of conditions in which a 4.25 per cent policy rate is clearly restrictive. A labour market that loosens slowly is the reason the bank has been able to cut twice this year without declaring victory, and it is the reason the next cut will be argued about rather than announced.

Market pricing moved after the release. Investors cut the odds of an August cut to roughly two thirds from four fifths, the pound firmed and gilt yields rose a few basis points, and the move was small because the market's view of the autumn mattered more than the month.

What would settle it is the July print and the services number that comes with it. A fall back towards 4 per cent in services would make an August cut comfortable, a second rise would push the decision into September, and a bank that has to choose between a food shock and a cooling labour market will probably choose to wait one meeting and not two. A few basis points in either direction is a rounding error against the two hundred that separate the current rate from the neutral estimate, and that gap is the only number that matters for the autumn.
