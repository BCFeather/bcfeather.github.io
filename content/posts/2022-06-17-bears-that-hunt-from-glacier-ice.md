description: "A population of polar bears in southeast Greenland lives through more than a hundred ice-free days a year by hunting from chunks of glacier ice, and a paper in *Science* argues the arrangement has kept it genetically apart for centuries."
---

A paper in *Science* on 17 June describes a group of polar bears living on the southeast coast of Greenland, between sixty and sixty-four degrees north, that has been isolated long enough to become the most genetically distinct population of the species. The authors, led by Kristin Laidre of the University of Washington, call it the twentieth recognized subpopulation. Roughly a few hundred animals live there, and they occupy a stretch of fjord coastline where sea ice is absent for more than a hundred days a year.

One problem the species faces everywhere is the absence of sea ice. Polar bears hunt seals at breathing holes and along the edge of floes, and when the ice retreats they fast for months, living on stored fat. In most of the Arctic the ice-free season is shorter than the period a bear can survive without food, and projections for the end of the century assume that in many places it will become longer.

Bears in southeast Greenland have found a substitute. Icebergs and floes calved from the Greenland ice sheet pile up in the fjords and stay there through the summer, and the bears climb onto this freshwater ice to wait for seals. The paper reports that the ice-free period in their range is more than a hundred days longer than the fasting threshold for the species, and that the animals nonetheless maintain body condition, which is what distinguishes them from bears elsewhere.

Genetics supplied the evidence of isolation. Samples from the region differ from neighbouring populations more than any two of the nineteen previously known groups differ from each other, and the difference is consistent with a population that has been separate for a long time rather than with recent mixing. Females in the group are smaller than in most regions and produce fewer cubs, which the authors attribute partly to the difficulty of finding mates in a landscape of fjords and mountains.

Fieldwork behind the paper depended on local knowledge. Inuit hunters told the researchers where bears were seen on glacier ice, and flights along the coast confirmed the reports over several years, with collars fitted to a small number of animals to record movement. That combination of indigenous observation and satellite tracking is now standard in Arctic wildlife work, and the paper is careful to describe how the two sources were reconciled.

Caution accompanies the finding. Glacier ice exists in southeast Greenland because a large ice sheet sits next to a coast, and most polar bear habitat has no such neighbour, so the population offers a local answer rather than a global one. Sea ice in the region is also projected to keep shrinking, and the authors note that the freshwater platforms are sensitive to the same warming that melts the rest. What the paper adds is a case where a species survives a condition that models predicted would be fatal, and the reason is geography rather than adaptation. Conservation arguments that treat sea ice as the only possible platform now have an exception to explain, and the exception is a few hundred bears in one corner of the Arctic.
