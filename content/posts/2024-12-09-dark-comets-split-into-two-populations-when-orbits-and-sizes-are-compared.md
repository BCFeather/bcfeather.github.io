Small bodies deliver volatiles and organics to the inner planets, and the ones that do so without a visible coma have been hard to classify. A paper in *PNAS* on 9 December 2024 separates them into two populations. Many apparitions are needed to separate a small acceleration from noise, and the requirement is why the sample grows slowly. A small sample is a limit the authors state rather than extrapolate from, and stating it is what makes the division usable.

Dark comets are objects with asteroidal appearance and non-gravitational acceleration, which is the signature of outgassing. The acceleration is small, and detecting it requires precise astrometry over several apparitions. Non-gravitational acceleration is a small effect, and a small effect is what requires many observations to separate from noise. A route is what a delivery model has to specify, and a source region is the only route specification the observations support. Consistent astrometry across surveys is the resource the field lacks, and a survey with uniform reduction would enlarge the sample.

Orbits and sizes were compared across the known sample, and the comparison divides the objects into an inner group and an outer group. The two differ in orbital elements and in the size distribution of their nuclei. A source region implies a delivery route, and a route is what a model of volatile delivery has to specify. Size distributions for point sources are uncertain, and the uncertainty is why the two-population claim is provisional.

The division is not a taxonomic convenience, because each group implies a different source region and a different delivery history. A delivery model that treats the population as one reservoir will overestimate the contribution of one and underestimate the other. Size distributions are hard to measure for objects that are only points of light, and the uncertainty is the reason the division is provisional. A target list for a mission depends on the division, and the division is what makes the taxonomy useful rather than decorative.

Size matters for delivery, since a small nucleus loses its volatiles quickly and a large one survives the journey. A population weighted towards small objects therefore contributes less than a count of objects suggests. Two populations are a hypothesis about origin, and a hypothesis about origin is what a mission target list would depend on.

The sample remains small, and the classification will be revised as more objects are found. The value of the paper is that it supplies a testable division rather than a descriptive list. A small sample is a real limit, and the paper states it instead of extrapolating from it.

Whether either group can be sampled by a spacecraft is the question a mission designer would ask, and the answer depends on the orbits the division identifies. Standardized astrometry is the resource the field needs, and a survey with consistent reduction is what would enlarge the sample.
