title: "Bennu carried water, salts and the letters of life"

description: "Samples returned from the asteroid Bennu contain amino acids, all five nucleobases used by DNA and RNA, and salts formed in ancient brines, according to two papers published on 30 January in *Nature* and *Nature Astronomy*."
---

The OSIRIS-REx spacecraft collected material from the surface of Bennu in 2020 and delivered 121.6 grams of it to Earth in September 2023, which is the largest sample from an asteroid ever returned and the first that was kept sealed from the atmosphere on the way home. The sample is a piece of a body that formed in the outer solar system 4.5 billion years ago and has been altered little since.

First paper, in *Nature*, reports the organic inventory. The team identified amino acids, including several that are used by terrestrial biology, and all five nucleobases that carry genetic information in DNA and RNA: adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine and uracil. The abundances are small and the compounds are mixed with thousands of others, and the point is the presence, because a mixture is what a prebiotic experiment produces and a pure sample is what a laboratory buys.

Second paper, in *Nature Astronomy*, reports the salts. The sample contains evaporites, minerals that form when a brine dries out, which means that the parent body of Bennu held liquid water for long enough to concentrate its dissolved components and then lost the water. The salt assemblage is the kind that laboratory experiments produce from a brine of a specific composition at a specific temperature.

Together the papers describe a body that had the ingredients and the setting. Liquid water, organic molecules, the salts that a wet and drying environment leaves behind and a mineral matrix that preserved them for the age of the solar system are the conditions that prebiotic chemistry experiments use when they try to make the components of a cell from simple starting materials.

Contamination is what makes the result credible. The sample was returned in a sealed capsule, handled in a nitrogen cabinet, and analysed with blanks and with terrestrial controls, and the authors report that the amino acids include forms that are rare on Earth. A meteorite that falls in a field collects biology on the way down; a capsule that lands in Utah and is opened in a clean room does not.

What the papers do not show is that life on Earth came from an asteroid. The delivery of organic material by impacts is one source among several, and the endogenous synthesis of the same compounds in hydrothermal systems is another. The value of the Bennu result is that it establishes what an asteroid can carry and how it was made, which turns a hypothesis about delivery into a calculation about supply.

Sample is also a resource for the next decade. Most of it has been catalogued and set aside for proposals, and the instruments that will analyse it have not all been built. The two papers are the first look at a material that will be studied for as long as the Apollo samples have been, and the first look is always the one that sets the questions for every laboratory that follows.
