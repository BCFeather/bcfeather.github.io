April looked like resilience and May looked like arithmetic, because the same goods cannot be front-loaded twice. Exports rose 4.8 per cent in the year to May, down from 8.1 per cent in April, and the fall is what happens when importers have already filled their warehouses and the tariff on the other side has not yet been negotiated away.

American line is doing most of the work. Shipments to the United States had already fallen 21 per cent in April, and May extended that decline, while exports to Southeast Asia and to Europe kept growing at a pace that reflects both real demand and transshipment. A customs official looking at a container bound for Hanoi cannot tell whether the buyer is Vietnamese.

Base effect is the other half of the explanation. May 2024 had been a strong month, which makes a comparison of any month against it harder, and the year-on-year rate in a series that grows unevenly will always bounce. A single print is a poor guide and three prints are a better one, which is why the customs ministry publishes a year-to-date figure alongside the monthly one and why analysts who forecast the monthly number usually get it wrong.

Prices are the quiet variable. A tariff war fought in the middle of a deflationary impulse produces exporters who cut margins to keep a customer, so volume and value can move in different directions for a quarter or more. A 4.8 per cent rise in dollar terms is compatible with a flat volume and a falling price, and the detailed schedule is where that shows up.

Policy response has been to look inward. Beijing has been subsidising trade-in purchases of appliances and cars since last year, which keeps factories running and shifts some of the demand that the American market is no longer providing, and the May retail numbers suggested that the programme was still doing its work.

London talks of 9 and 10 June were meant to settle the framework and produced a commitment to implement what had been agreed in Geneva rather than a new bargain. Markets read the absence of escalation as good news, and exporters read it as a reason to wait before rebooking a container.

What would settle the summer is whether exports to the United States stabilise around the new lower level or keep sliding. A trade that has moved to third countries can be measured in Vietnam's imports and in Chinese exports to Vietnam; a trade that has disappeared cannot be measured anywhere, and that is the case a tariff negotiator has to argue. A 90-day truce that is renewed every 90 days is a planning horizon of 90 days, and a factory that has to plan further than that will look for another customer. Whether the year ends with a surplus near a trillion dollars depends on imports, and imports are the line that a stimulus for domestic demand would raise.
