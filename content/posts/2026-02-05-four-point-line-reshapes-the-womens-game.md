Basketball's three-point line rewards a shot that is worth 1.5 times a two-pointer at equal accuracy. A league added a four-point line 2.4 metres beyond the arc this season, and the first half-season of data shows what happens when the reward is doubled.

Shot distribution moved immediately. The share of attempts from beyond the four-point line is 14 per cent league-wide, and for the three teams with the best shooters it is above 20 per cent. The share of mid-range attempts, already declining, fell by a third.

Accuracy tells the story of the rule's calibration. A four-point attempt converts at 27 per cent league-wide, which yields 1.08 points per attempt, against 1.02 for a three-pointer at 34 per cent. The line is close to neutral, and the league's stated intention was to make it a choice rather than an obvious gain.

Defensive geometry is what changed most. Defending a four-point line requires an extended close-out, and the data show a 12 per cent rise in blow-by drives from the perimeter. Teams have responded by switching to a zone in 18 per cent of possessions, up from 6.

Rebounding changed with the spacing. Longer shots produce longer rebounds, and the offensive rebounding rate on four-point attempts is 34 per cent against 26 on threes, because the ball comes off the rim at a flatter angle and travels farther from the box-out positions.

Injury data are being watched. The league's medical staff report a 9 per cent rise in ankle injuries in the first half-season, and they attribute it partly to the wider defensive stance the extended line requires. The sample is too small for a conclusion and large enough for concern.

Commercial effects are the reason the league tried it. A four-point shot is a highlight, and broadcasters report a 7 per cent rise in viewers during the fourth quarter of close games. Whether that persists when the novelty fades is the question the experiment is designed to answer.

Coaching adaptation has been faster than expected. Two teams now run a five-out offence built around four-point spacing, and their assist rate has risen 22 per cent because the extended floor creates passing lanes that a packed defence did not leave open.

Officiating is a quieter change. The extended line adds a second arc for referees to judge, and the league reports a 3 per cent rise in disputed calls, concentrated on shots taken with a foot near the line. A review protocol is being written.

Attendance and broadcast are part of the case for the line. The league's average margin fell from 12 points to 9, and its broadcaster reports a 6 per cent rise in viewers for matches decided by fewer than 10, which is most of them now.

For a reader, the interesting variable is the calibration. A line placed where the expected value equals the three-pointer's creates a strategic choice; placed too close it becomes a mandatory shot, and too far it becomes a novelty. The league's numbers suggest it is close to the neutral point, which is the hardest place to place a rule and the only place worth placing it.
