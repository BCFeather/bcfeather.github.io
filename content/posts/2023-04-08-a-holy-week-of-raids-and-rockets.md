Israeli police entered the Al-Aqsa compound in Jerusalem before dawn on 5 April, during the last week of Ramadan, and cleared worshippers from the mosque with stun grenades and batons. Palestinian officials said several dozen people were injured and more than 350 were detained, and the police said they had acted against people who had barricaded themselves inside with fireworks and stones. The compound is administered by a Jordanian trust under an arrangement that has held since 1967 and that both sides describe as fragile, and which no government has been able to redefine without a crisis.

Two days later rockets were fired from southern Lebanon, about thirty of them, the largest volley from that territory since 2006, and the air force struck targets near Tyre in response. Rockets were also fired from Gaza, and Israeli aircraft hit sites in the strip over two nights. No deaths were reported on the Israeli side, and Palestinian health officials reported two deaths in Gaza and damage to a school in the north.

Timing is what made it dangerous. Ramadan, Passover and Easter fell in the same week, which happens three times in a decade, and the overlap puts the two holiest sites of the two peoples on the same calendar. The government had said it would allow Jewish visitors onto the compound during Passover and that it would restrict numbers, and the police entry on the 5th was justified as a response to plans by worshippers to stay overnight.

Jordan and Egypt, the two states with peace treaties, condemned the raid and warned that the arrangement around the holy sites was the one thing they could not defend. The foreign ministry in Amman summoned the Israeli ambassador, and the Egyptian intelligence service, which had brokered the ceasefires of the past year, suspended its mediation for several days. Both governments have internal reasons to be seen to act, and both have reasons not to let the situation become a war.

The internal Israeli argument was about who had lost control. The opposition said the raid had been authorized by a minister who wanted a confrontation, the coalition said the police had acted to prevent a worse one, and the security services briefed that the operation had produced the rocket fire it was supposed to deter. The prime minister's coalition depends on two ministers whose constituents want the compound opened further, which makes restraint a coalition question rather than a security one. Two ministers had campaigned for a change in the rules at the compound, and their parties could bring the government down if they left.

What followed was a week that everyone had predicted and nobody had prevented. The rockets stopped within days, the mediation resumed, and the quiet returned to a level that is worse than it was in March. The pattern of the past three years is that a police operation at one site and a volley from one border produce a round of strikes and a ceasefire, and each round leaves the arrangement that produced it a little weaker.
