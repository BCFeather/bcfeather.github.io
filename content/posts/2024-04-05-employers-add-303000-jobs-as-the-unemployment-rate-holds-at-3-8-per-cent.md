American employers added 303,000 jobs in March, well above the 200,000 forecasters had expected, and the unemployment rate held at 3.8 per cent for a second month. The report was published on 5 April, and it arrived after a week in which the market had begun to doubt that the economy could keep growing at this pace. The report reversed the expectation that hiring was cooling, and it did so in a month when the previous two reports had been revised up as well.

Composition matters more than the total. Health care and government added a large share of the jobs, and construction continued to hire, while manufacturing shed a small number. A total that rests on two sectors is a total that the next month can reverse without any change in the wider economy. The breadth of the gain, measured by the share of industries adding workers, was the strongest in a year, which is the detail forecasters cite when arguing the labour market is not narrowly supported.

Participation rose to 62.7 per cent, which means the positions were filled by people entering the labour force rather than by the same workers moving between employers. An economy that draws people in can grow without bidding wages up as quickly as one that is simply reallocating a fixed pool of workers. Immigration has added to the supply of workers at the same time, and the economists who watch it say that the participation gain is partly a composition effect rather than a behavioural one.

Wages rose 0.3 per cent on the month and 4.1 per cent on the year, slower than the previous reading and consistent with inflation continuing to fall towards the target. A wage number that cools is the number the central bank has said it needs to see before it can cut. The annual figure remains above the rate that the committee considers consistent with its target, and it has been falling by about a tenth of a percentage point every few months.

Yields rose after the release, and traders pushed back the expected date of the first cut, because a labour market as tight as this gives the committee no reason to hurry. A strong report is a report that delays an easing, and the delay is paid for by households with mortgages to renew. Mortgage rates moved with the yields, and the household that refinanced last year at a lower rate is the household that feels the delay most.

What the month shows is that the economy has absorbed the fastest tightening cycle in four decades without a visible break in employment, and the open question is how long that lasts as the lagged cost of higher rates reaches the firms that borrow. The labour market is the last place the slowdown shows, and the month's report is the reason the committee can wait without much political cost.
