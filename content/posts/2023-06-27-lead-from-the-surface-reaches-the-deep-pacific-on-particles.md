description: "Measurements and modelling show that lead deposited at the ocean surface is carried to the deep Pacific by sinking particles."
---

An element entering the ocean at the surface has to reach the interior somehow, and the route is usually a particle. Lead is a trace metal with an industrial history, and its isotopes record where it came from. In *PNAS* on 27 June 2023, those isotopes are used. The authors measure lead concentrations and isotopic compositions in seawater profiles from the Pacific, and combine the measurements with a model of particle transport.

A pathway is the result. Lead deposited from the atmosphere at the surface adsorbs onto sinking particles, and those particles carry it into deep water where it is released as they dissolve. Scavenging is the chemical step, since a dissolved metal binds to a particle's surface and the binding's strength depends on particle composition and metal chemistry, and lead's affinity is high enough to make adsorption efficient.

Isotopes supply the fingerprint. Industrial lead has a different isotopic composition from natural lead, and the gradient between surface and depth shows how much of the deep lead is anthropogenic. Depth profile is the evidence, because a concentration decreasing with depth in the modern ocean, rather than increasing, is the signature of a surface source not yet in equilibrium with the interior.

As the carrier, particles matter most. A metal adsorbing onto organic matter and mineral grains travels with them, and the sinking flux sets the speed. Release is the reverse step, since a dissolving particle releases its cargo and the release depth sets where the metal accumulates, with a shallow release returning it to the surface and a deep one storing it.

Modelling supplies the inference. A tracer model including scavenging and remineralization reproduces the profiles, and the fit is what lets the authors attribute the deep signal to the surface. Timescale is the useful number, because a transit of decades means the deep inventory still responds to emissions that peaked in the twentieth century.

Biogeochemistry gains a general implication, since a contaminant released at the surface is now part of the deep ocean, and its memory there outlasts the emission itself. Atmospheric input is the source term, because industrial emissions peaked and then declined after leaded petrol was phased out, and the ocean's profile records that decline.

Sampling sets the limitation, as a profile is a point in space and time, and the Pacific's circulation varies enough that one section is not the basin. Effect stays unresolved, because lead at these concentrations is not toxic to plankton, and the concern is about the record rather than the harm. Oceanography gains a demonstration of the particle pump's reach, since the biological pump moves carbon and the same machinery moves metals, and the isotopes make that machinery visible. A second section across the same basin would test whether the profile is a basin-wide feature or a local one, and the circulation model is the tool that would decide. A time series at one station would show whether the deep concentration is still rising, and a rising trend would confirm that the ocean has not caught up with the atmosphere.
