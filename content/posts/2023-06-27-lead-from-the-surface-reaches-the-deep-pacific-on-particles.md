description: "Measurements and modelling show that lead deposited at the ocean surface is carried to the deep Pacific by sinking particles, according to work published in *PNAS* on 27 June 2023."
---

An element that enters the ocean at the surface has to reach the interior somehow, and the route is usually a particle. Lead is a trace metal with an industrial history, and its isotopes record where it came from.

The analysis in *PNAS*, 27 June 2023 uses those isotopes. The authors measure lead concentrations and isotopic compositions in seawater profiles from the Pacific, and combine the measurements with a model of particle transport.

The result is a pathway. Lead deposited from the atmosphere at the surface is adsorbed onto sinking particles, and the particles carry it into the deep water, where it is released as the particles dissolve.

The isotopes are the fingerprint. Industrial lead has a different isotopic composition from natural lead, and the gradient between surface and depth shows how much of the deep lead is anthropogenic.

The depth profile is the evidence. A concentration that decreases with depth in the modern ocean, rather than increasing, is a signature of a surface source that has not yet reached equilibrium with the interior.

The particles are the carrier. A metal that adsorbs onto organic matter and onto mineral grains travels with them, and the sinking flux is what sets the speed of the transport.

The modelling is the inference. A tracer model that includes scavenging and remineralization reproduces the profiles, and the fit is what allows the authors to attribute the deep signal to the surface.

The timescale is the useful number. A transit time of decades means that the deep ocean's lead inventory is still responding to emissions that peaked in the twentieth century.

The biogeochemical implication is general. A contaminant that people released at the surface is now a component of the deep ocean, and the deep ocean's memory of the emission is longer than the emission itself.

The limitation is the sampling. A profile is a point in space and time, and the Pacific's circulation varies enough that one section is not the basin.

What the paper does not resolve is the effect. Lead at these concentrations is not toxic to plankton, and the concern is about the record rather than the harm.

For oceanography, the value is a demonstration of the particle pump's reach. The biological pump moves carbon, and the same machinery moves metals, and the paper's isotopes make the machinery visible.

The scavenging is the chemical step. A dissolved metal binds to a particle's surface, and the binding's strength depends on the particle's composition and on the metal's chemistry. Lead's affinity is high enough that the adsorption is efficient.

The release is the reverse step. A particle that dissolves releases its cargo, and the release's depth sets where the metal accumulates. A shallow release returns the metal to the surface, and a deep one stores it.

The atmospheric input is the source term. Industrial emissions peaked in the twentieth century and declined after leaded petrol was phased out, and the ocean's profile is a record of that decline.
