Every transatlantic telephone cable carries optical amplifiers at intervals of forty-five to ninety kilometres, installed to keep the signal alive across an ocean. A paper in the 20 May issue of *Science* reports that the light travelling between those repeaters can be read as a measurement of strain in the ground under them, which turns a commercial cable into a chain of seismometers that nobody had to install. The demonstration used a cable running 5,860 kilometres across the Atlantic.

Earlier work had shown that a submarine cable can sense the ocean, but only as a single number per cable. Interferometry over the whole length, or polarization measurements, integrate every disturbance between one end and the other, so an event could be dated and characterized but never located: the answer to where something happened was the entire ocean. The new result isolates each span between repeaters, which is the scale at which a seismic array becomes useful, and it does so on a cable that was carrying commercial traffic at the time.

The technique compares the phase of light returned from successive amplifiers, so a change in the length of one span shows up as a change in that span alone. With spans of tens of kilometres, the resolution is coarse by the standards of a land network and far finer than a single continental average, and the group reported earthquakes and ocean signals recorded on individual stretches of the cable rather than on its total length. The signals they describe include body waves from distant events and the slow deformation that tides and currents impose on the sea floor.

What makes the result matter is the inventory. Telecom companies have already laid cables across most of the ocean floor, and each one contains thousands of spans with power, housing and a data path attached, so the marginal cost of a sensor is close to nothing. Distributed acoustic sensing can instrument only the first and last tens of kilometres of a cable, because the interrogator has to reach the fibre directly, and this method reaches the middle.

The obvious uses are the ones seismology has wanted for decades: faster location of earthquakes offshore, better constraints on the waves that become tsunamis, and continuous records from a region where instruments are scarce because moorings are expensive and ships are slow. There are conditions attached, since the measurement rides on traffic that has to keep flowing and the cable operators have to agree to share the data, and no span gives a continuous profile of the sea floor, only a running average across its own length.

Whether the ocean gets wired this way depends on arrangements rather than on physics: who owns the record, how it is archived, and whether a telecom company's cable can be asked to carry an instrument as well as a conversation. The hardware is in place, the method works on a cable that was built for something else, and the next step is an agreement between the companies that own the cables and the agencies that want the data.
