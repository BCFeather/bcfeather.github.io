The crowd began to move in the early afternoon of a Sunday, from the encampment outside the army headquarters along the Monumental Axis towards the square where the three powers face each other. By half past three the barricades at the Congress had been broken, and men in yellow football shirts were standing on the roof of the building that had been designed by Oscar Niemeyer. The encampment had stood there since November, with tents, food stalls and a demand that the army intervene, and the army had done nothing.

Inside, the damage was the work of an afternoon. Windows were smashed, sprinklers were set off and offices flooded, furniture was thrown from the upper floors, and the chamber of the Supreme Court was stripped of equipment. The presidential palace was entered through the ramp that Lula had walked up eight days earlier, and the offices on the second floor were emptied onto the floor. A television crew filmed a man carrying a chair out of the palace, and the picture became the one the newspapers used on the front page.

Lula was in Sao Paulo state when the first reports arrived and returned to Brasilia within hours. He decreed a federal intervention in the security of the federal district, which put a police command under a federal officer until the end of January, and he accused his predecessor of having encouraged the crowd. By that evening the military police had cleared the buildings and about three hundred people had been arrested; the total reached roughly fifteen hundred over the following weeks. The justice minister said the arrests would be made one by one, and the identification of the people in the videos took weeks.

The failure that mattered had happened before the crowd arrived. The military police of the federal district had been warned and had deployed thinly, and its commander was suspended within days. The Supreme Court removed the district's governor from office for ninety days, and the investigation that followed asked why the encampments had been allowed to grow for two months beside the army's own headquarters. The district's security secretariat had drawn up a plan for a crowd of a few hundred, which is the number the intelligence reports had mentioned.

Comparison with Washington was unavoidable, because the images were the same and the date was two years and two days later. The differences were also instructive: in Brazil the army stayed in its barracks, the governors of the states condemned the attack within hours, and the new president had been in office for a week rather than watching a count from a distance.

Democracies are defended by the ordinary paperwork of policing, on the days when nothing is happening. The buildings were rebuilt and reopened within a month, and the question that stayed was why the paperwork had been left undone. The buildings reopened within a month, with the glass replaced and the damaged paintings sent for restoration, and the square was empty again. The square is wide enough to hold a crowd and formal enough to make one look like an occupation.
