Fernando Villavicencio finished speaking at a rally in northern Quito on the evening of 9 August, stepped into a car with his team, and was shot as it pulled away. Three people with him were wounded, a suspect was arrested at the scene and then died of injuries, and the candidate's security detail fired at the attackers as they fled. Villavicencio was fifty-nine, a former journalist who had made his reputation by exposing corruption in the oil industry and in the government of Rafael Correa, and he was polling in the middle of a field of eight candidates with no obvious route to the runoff.

The election is in three weeks, on 20 August, and it had already become a referendum on security. Ecuador has moved in five years from one of the calmest countries in the region to one of the most violent, as cocaine routes from the Pacific and the Amazon turned its ports into battlegrounds for gangs with links to Mexican cartels. The murder rate has quadrupled, prisons have been taken over by inmates, and a presidential candidate was killed in the street in daylight.

The government's response was immediate and familiar. President Guillermo Lasso declared a state of exception in two provinces and called out the army, and the electoral authority said the vote would proceed. Six suspects, all Colombians according to the prosecutor, were arrested and later killed in prison, a sequence that has become so common in the region that it barely counts as news. The other candidates suspended campaigning for a day and then resumed, some in bulletproof vests and escorted by police.

Villavicencio's programme was a list of things that could not be done quickly: an anti-corruption commission, prison reform, and a break with the parties that had governed for two decades. His killing has made his platform the one that everyone now endorses, and it has made the argument about security the argument that decides the election. Luisa Gonzalez, the candidate of Correa's movement, has led the polls and promised a harder line on crime; the others are competing to sound like her.

What happened next said as much as the killing. Candidates appeared on television inside armoured vehicles, the election council moved some voting centres, and the prosecutor's office announced that it had opened cases against people it had not named. The country's institutions had spent the year fighting each other, with a president who had dissolved the assembly and a legislature that had tried to impeach him, and neither side had an answer to the question the killing posed. Turnout on 20 August will be read as a verdict on all of it.

A journalist who spent his career naming names was killed for it, and the country is being asked to vote while the reason he was killed remains in the streets. Ecuador's election will be counted on 20 August, and the count will decide who governs a country that has become a place where candidates wear vests.
