description: "Density operators in Weyl semimetals and in symmetry free topological insulators obey a higher bracket algebra that parallels the Girvin-MacDonald-Platzman algebra of the quantum Hall effect, with a topological term that is a cyclic cocycle."
---

A commutator measures how two operators fail to commute, and a higher bracket extends that idea to more than two operators by summing over all orderings. In a many body system the bracket of density operators encodes how the density responds, so its algebraic structure is a statement about the dynamics rather than about notation.

Work posted to arXiv on 18 January 2024 computes that structure for Weyl fermion systems in odd spatial dimensions and for topological insulators without a protecting symmetry in even spatial dimensions. The two families sit on opposite sides of the bulk boundary correspondence, so a calculation on one side predicts the other. Bulk and boundary are two descriptions of one system, and the pairing is what lets a bulk calculation speak about a surface.

The parallel that the authors establish is with the quantum Hall effect. In that system the density operators projected onto the lowest Landau level close under a commutator into the Girvin-MacDonald-Platzman algebra, and the higher bracket structure found here follows the same pattern. Projection is the tool in both cases, and what changes is the operator that does the projecting.

Normal ordering is where the interesting term appears. Once the density operators of a Weyl system are normal ordered with respect to the ground state, the higher bracket acquires a c-number part, which is the analogue of the Schwinger term that appears in the commutator of fermion currents. Normal ordering is a choice, and the paper makes it relative to the ground state of the free system, so the c-number part is defined once and for all.

Identification of that part is the second result. The authors match the c-number term with a cyclic cocycle, which is an object of Connes' noncommutative geometry and which is a topological invariant rather than a dynamical quantity. A cocycle is closed under the coboundary operator, and closure is what makes the object invariant under deformation. A closed object does not depend on the representative chosen, and that independence is the point.

The identification is what gives the calculation meaning. A term that is merely a number could come from any regulator, and a term that is a cocycle is fixed by topology, so its value is the same for any deformation that preserves the phase. A regulator would shift a bare number, and a cocycle cannot be shifted in that way. That rigidity is what turns an algebraic curiosity into a statement about the phase, and it is the part a reader should remember.

What the paper does not do is regularise the interacting case. The calculation is for free fermions with a specified ground state, and a reader interested in interactions would have to extend the normal ordering, which the authors do not attempt. Interactions change the ground state and therefore the ordering, and that extension is left for later work, with no regularisation scheme proposed here.
