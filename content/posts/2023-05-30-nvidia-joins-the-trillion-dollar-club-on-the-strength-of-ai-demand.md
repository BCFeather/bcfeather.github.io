description: "NVIDIA crossed a market value of one trillion dollars on 30 May after a forecast that beat every expectation on Wall Street. The company that made the chips for video games has become the supplier of the hardware on which the artificial intelligence boom runs."
---

NVIDIA's share price rose by about three per cent on 30 May, taking its market value above one trillion dollars for the first time, and making it the fifth American company and the first chip maker to reach that level. The move completed a run that began a week earlier, when the company reported quarterly results and told investors that revenue in the current quarter would be about eleven billion dollars, roughly fifty per cent above what analysts had expected. The stock has tripled since October.

Behind the number is a single business, the data centre. NVIDIA's graphics processors were designed for rendering images, and it turned out that the same architecture, which performs thousands of small calculations at once, is what training a large neural network requires. The company spent a decade building the software layer around that hardware, a programming platform called CUDA that most machine-learning frameworks depend on, and that layer is what keeps its customers from switching to the cheaper alternatives that have been designed in the meantime.

The demand is real. Microsoft, Google, Amazon and Meta have all said that they are spending billions on computing capacity for artificial intelligence, and the largest share of that money is going to servers built around NVIDIA's chips. Cloud providers are buying in volumes that would have sounded implausible a year ago, and the company has said that supply will remain short of demand for several quarters, which is why its customers are paying in advance and why its prices have held.

The supply chain is the constraint and the risk. The chips are made by TSMC in Taiwan, on a process that only a handful of factories in the world can run, and they are packaged with high-bandwidth memory that comes from a small number of suppliers in South Korea. The American export controls of October 2022 restrict the sale of the most advanced processors to China, and the company has designed a slower variant for that market, which accounts for a substantial share of its revenue and which the controls have made awkward to serve.

Valuation is the argument that the market is having with itself. At a trillion dollars, NVIDIA trades at roughly thirty times the sales that its own guidance implies for the year, a multiple that assumes the boom continues at its current pace and that the company keeps its share of it. The comparison that bulls make is with the build-out of the internet, which took a decade and produced several cycles of overinvestment. The comparison that bears make is with the telecom bubble of 2000, when the equipment makers reached valuations like this and then discovered that their customers had bought too much.

What is certain is that the world's computing architecture is being rearranged around a company that did not intend to be at the centre of it. NVIDIA's founders built a chip for games, and the accident of its usefulness for matrix arithmetic has made it the supplier of the industrial input of the decade. Whether the trillion is a milestone or a peak will be decided by whether the models being trained turn out to be worth the money being spent on them, and that is a question no quarterly report can answer.
