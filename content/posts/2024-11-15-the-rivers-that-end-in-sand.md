A river that crosses a desert has to be older than the desert, and the Tarim is. The basin it drains lies between the Tian Shan and the Kunlun, both of which rise high enough to catch snow, and the meltwater that comes off them in summer is the only water the basin has. The river runs east along the northern edge of the Taklamakan and ends in a lake that has moved and dried and refilled several times in the past century, and the rivers that feed it have been diverted so often that the map is a record of decisions as much as of geography. The basin is bounded on three sides by mountains and on the fourth by a desert that has no outlet to the sea. The water that arrives is a small fraction of what the mountains receive, since the plains are hot and the reservoirs lose a measurable share to evaporation.

The sand is the reason. The Taklamakan is a sea of dunes with almost no vegetation between them, and the dunes march downwind at a rate measured in metres per year. Sand that is blown onto a river channel blocks it, and a river that is blocked finds another course, so the channels on the northern edge shift with the wind and with the flood. The crescent of oases along the southern edge sits where the rivers come off the mountains and disappear into the sand within a day's walk, and each oasis is the length of the water that reaches it. A channel that has been abandoned keeps a line of tamarisk and reeds for a few years and then becomes a dune field.

Poplar is the tree that lives on that edge. The Euphrates poplar sends roots down to a water table that is several metres below the surface, and it reproduces by suckers from those roots rather than by seed, so a stand is a clone that spreads outward from a parent. A stand that loses its water table dies from the top down, and the dead trunks stand in the sand for decades, which makes the forest's retreat legible in the landscape. The corridors along the rivers are the last habitat for the animals that need cover in the basin. A poplar stand that is flooded in spring sends up new suckers, and a stand that is not flooded does not.

Water is allocated by committee. The basin's water is shared among agriculture, industry, towns and the ecological flow that keeps the lower river and its lakes alive, and the share given to ecology has been the residual for most of the past fifty years. A diversion upstream takes water that would otherwise reach a downstream stand of poplar, and the accounting is done in cubic metres against a requirement that is itself a judgement. The cotton that the basin grows is a major crop and a major consumer, and the price of the crop and the price of the water are set by different authorities. A town in the basin grows by taking water from agriculture, and the transfer is the cheapest way to supply a city. The ecological share is now written into the allocation, and the enforcement of it is the part that decides whether the lower river survives.

The green belt is the engineering answer. A line of planted vegetation has been pushed around the edge of the desert to hold the dunes, and the planting is irrigated from groundwater and from diverted river water. It works where the water reaches it and it fails where the water does not, and a belt that holds a dune for a decade is a belt that buys a decade. The dust that blows out of the basin crosses into the neighbouring provinces and, on the right wind, into the ocean, and the belt is justified by that dust as much as by the dunes. The belt is also a corridor for the roads and the pipelines that cross the desert, and the planting follows the infrastructure.

What the basin shows is a system where the water is the only variable that matters. The dunes, the poplar, the oases and the towns are all arranged around a supply that is finite and allocated, and every change in the allocation shows up somewhere downstream within a few years. The desert is not advancing by itself; it is being fed by the failures of the water accounting, and a river that ends in the sand is a river whose water was used before it arrived. A river that is measured at its head and not at its end is a river whose budget is unknown, and that is the accounting problem of the basin.
