Neutrino mass is measured indirectly, because the particle is too light for a direct weighing. An experiment that studies the beta decay of tritium measures the energy spectrum of the emitted electron, and a nonzero neutrino mass shifts the endpoint of the spectrum by that mass. A collaboration reported this month a limit of 0.3 electronvolts at 90 per cent confidence, improving on the previous 0.8.

Apparatus is the achievement. The experiment uses a magnetic spectrometer with a cryogenic, windowless gaseous tritium source, and the resolution near the endpoint is 1.5 electronvolts. Reducing the limit requires both a sharper resolution and a larger statistics sample, and the collaboration ran for 300 days to collect the spectrum it reports.

Analysis handles the systematics that dominate. The endpoint region is sensitive to the source's magnetic field inhomogeneity, to the energy loss of electrons scattering in the gas, and to the background from the vessel walls. The team fits a model with 14 nuisance parameters and reports a limit that is stable when those parameters are varied within their uncertainties.

Cosmology provides the independent constraint. Surveys of the cosmic microwave background and of large-scale structure bound the sum of neutrino masses to below about 0.1 electronvolts, which is tighter than the laboratory limit and model-dependent, since it assumes the standard cosmological model and a specific neutrino hierarchy.

The tension between the two is informative. If the laboratory limit falls below the cosmological bound, the cosmological model is wrong somewhere; if it stays above, the two are consistent and the laboratory measurement constrains the absolute mass scale rather than the sum.

Next generation is a different technique. The experiment's successor will use a larger source and a more precise spectrometer, targeting 0.04 electronvolts, and a separate approach based on measuring the time of flight of the electron is in development with a target of 0.1.

What a limit means is worth stating plainly. An upper limit of 0.3 electronvolts says the neutrino is at least a million times lighter than the electron, and it excludes the range in which the neutrino would account for the dark matter. It does not tell you the mass, and it cannot, until the effect is detected rather than bounded.

Systematics budget is where the limit is won. The dominant term is the source's magnetic field inhomogeneity, which shifts the endpoint by a few millielectronvolts if unmodelled, and the collaboration measured the field with a separate proton experiment to bound it. The second term is the energy loss in the gas, modelled with a Monte Carlo that the team validated against a dedicated measurement.

Reading the result, the interesting shift is the method's maturity. Beta-decay endpoint measurements were a niche technique twenty years ago and are now the most direct constraint on an absolute neutrino mass, which is a case of an instrument catching up with a theoretical question.
