---

An economy that exports cars and imports energy is an economy that feels a tariff before it sees one. Japan's first estimate for the March quarter, published on 16 May, showed real output down 0.2 per cent on the quarter and 0.7 per cent at an annual rate, the first contraction in four quarters. Nominal output rose 0.8 per cent, so the gap between the two measures is a price effect.

Consumption stalled. Private demand contributed nothing to the quarter, with household spending up 0.0 per cent as food purchases fell and restaurant spending rose, which is the pattern of a household trading down and not spending more. Capital spending rose 1.4 per cent on software investment, its fourth quarterly increase, and residential investment rose as builders raced a change in the building code, a pattern that adds to one quarter and subtracts from the next because the work is pulled forward and then stops.

Trade subtracted. Exports fell while imports rose, and net exports contributed negatively, which is the same mechanism that pulled American output down in the same quarter: firms brought forward purchases ahead of tariffs. A country that imports its fuel and exports its cars gets the worst of both lines when a tariff is announced.

Annual picture is better than the quarterly one. For the fiscal year that ended in March, nominal output rose 3.7 per cent to 617 trillion yen, the first time it has passed 600 trillion, and real output rose 0.8 per cent for a fourth consecutive year. A country whose nominal output is growing faster than its real output is a country with inflation, and Japan spent two decades trying to get one, so the fiscal-year number is the one the Bank of Japan watches when it decides whether the exit from zero rates has worked.

Central bank had raised its policy rate to 0.5 per cent in January and had been signalling another step if wages held up. A contraction complicates that, and the spring wage round had produced the largest nominal increases in three decades. A central bank with a weak quarter and a strong wage settlement has to decide which one is the signal, and the answer determines whether the next move is in June or in the autumn.

Government's reading was about prices. The economy minister said sustained price increases had weighed on consumption and household confidence, and named American trade policy as a risk even though its effect on the quarter had been limited. That framing is a forecast: the tariff effect arrives in the second and third quarters, when export orders are filled or not.

What would settle the year is the June quarter and the export data that leads it. A rebound would make the first quarter a stockpiling artefact, and a second contraction would make it the start of something, in a country that has less fiscal room than it had in 2020, and a government that has already spent the stimulus it had.
