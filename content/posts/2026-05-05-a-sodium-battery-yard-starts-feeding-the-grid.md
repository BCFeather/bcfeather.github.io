A storage yard at the edge of Ordos, in Inner Mongolia, began dispatching into the regional grid on 30 April, after a month of commissioning that the operator described as uneventful, which in this business is the highest compliment available. The plant holds 200 megawatts of power and 400 megawatt-hours of energy in sodium-ion cells, making it the largest installation of that chemistry anywhere in the world. The company that built it, a subsidiary of a state-owned generation group, is being paid a fixed capacity fee for standing ready, rather than only for the electricity it shifts from one hour to the next.

The significance of the chemistry is less about cost than about supply. Sodium is the sixth most abundant element in the earth's crust, and the salt it comes from is cheaper and more widely distributed than the lithium compounds that the previous generation of batteries depended on. Sodium-ion cells use no lithium, no cobalt and no nickel, and their cathodes can be made from iron, manganese and a form of carbon that is baked from biomass. For a country that imports most of its lithium and processes most of its cobalt elsewhere, a chemistry that removes three supply chain dependencies at once is worth paying a premium to learn.

What the yard demonstrated in its first month is that sodium cells behave well in the conditions that break lithium ones. Night temperatures in Ordos fell below minus ten degrees in April, and the operator reported that the plant retained more than ninety per cent of its rated capacity at minus twenty, which matters in a place where the winter peak is the hour that decides whether the lights stay on. Round-trip efficiency was measured at about eighty-eight per cent, a little below the best lithium iron phosphate systems, and the cells are warranted for six thousand cycles, which at one full cycle a day is a little over sixteen years.

The limits are also real and should be stated plainly. Sodium-ion cells store less energy per kilogram than lithium iron phosphate, roughly a hundred and sixty watt-hours against a hundred and eighty, which is why the yard occupies more land than an equivalent lithium plant would. The hard-carbon anode is the expensive component, and its production is concentrated in a handful of plants, so the cost advantage that the chemistry promises depends on a supply chain that has not yet been built at scale. The capacity payment that sustains the plant is also a subsidy, and subsidies are easier to start than to end.

What makes the Ordos yard more than a demonstration is the grid it sits on. Inner Mongolia curtailed about six per cent of its wind and solar output last year, and most of the waste happened in the middle of the day, when the wind blows hardest and the load is lowest. Storage that can absorb four hundred megawatt-hours is a small fraction of what the province would need to stop curtailing, and it is enough to prove that the economics work at the scale of a single substation. The next round of tenders, which the regional government has said will be four times larger, will show whether sodium becomes the default chemistry for hours-long storage or remains the option for cold and remote sites. Either way, the first commercial yard is now a line in a dispatch log rather than a slide in a presentation, and that is where technologies actually get tested.
