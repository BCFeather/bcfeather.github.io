Finland and Sweden submitted their applications to join NATO on the morning of 18 May, handing identical letters to the secretary general at the alliance's headquarters in Brussels. The Finnish ambassador, Klaus Korhonen, and his Swedish counterpart, Axel Wernhoff, delivered them together, and Jens Stoltenberg called the moment historic and said the alliance would move quickly. Finland's parliament had approved the decision on 17 May by 188 votes to eight, and the Swedish government had decided the day before, after a debate in which six of the eight parties in the chamber supported the move.

Sweden had been outside military alliances since 1814, and its policy of non-alignment had survived two world wars and the entire Cold War. Finland had fought the Soviet Union twice, lost territory in 1944, and then built a foreign policy around the careful management of its eastern neighbour, a phrase that produced the word Finlandization in other capitals. Public opinion in both countries moved within weeks of the invasion: support for membership in Finland rose from about a quarter of voters to more than sixty per cent, and in Sweden from a third to a clear majority.

Accession requires unanimity among the thirty members, and one of them objected at once. Recep Tayyip Erdoğan said on 18 May that Turkey would not look favourably on the applications, citing what he called the harbouring of Kurdish militants and of followers of the preacher Fethullah Gülen, whom Ankara blames for the failed coup of 2016. Turkey also wanted the two countries to lift the restrictions on arms sales that they had imposed after its incursion into northern Syria in 2019, and it wanted extraditions that Swedish courts had refused.

The objection turned a technical process into a negotiation with a price. Stoltenberg said he was confident the concerns could be addressed, and American officials said the applications should be ratified quickly, but the alliance's rules give every capital a veto and the Turkish parliament had no obligation to hurry. Talks between Ankara, Stockholm and Helsinki continued through the spring without a memorandum, and the matter was carried to the summit in Madrid at the end of June, where a joint agreement was signed on the eve of the meeting.

Moscow's response was calibrated. Vladimir Putin said the expansion posed no direct threat to Russia, then added that if military infrastructure were deployed on the new members' territory, Russia would respond, and he mentioned the Baltic Sea in terms that suggested a rearrangement of the region's balance. On the day before the applications, a Finnish grid operator said Russia had stopped supplying electricity, citing payment problems, and nobody in Helsinki missed the symbolism.

The applications did not make either country a member. Protocols were signed in July, ratification by thirty parliaments took months, and Finland entered the alliance in April 2023 while Sweden waited until 2024 for Hungary and Turkey to finish. What May produced was a decision that reversed a century of policy in three weeks, taken by two governments that had concluded that the old arrangement had been underwritten by a neighbour who no longer honoured it.
