title: "Earliest face in western Europe"
date: "2025-03-12T14:05:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A partial hominin face from a Spanish cave, dated to between 1.4 and 1.1 million years ago, is the oldest human face found in western Europe, and it does not look like the species that came later, as reported in *Nature*."
---

Sima del Elefante is a cave in the Atapuerca hills, and its sediment sequence has produced stone tools and fragments of bone since the first excavations in 2007. The layer known as TE7 contained a jaw fragment that was identified as hominin but too incomplete to assign to a species. What the new excavation recovered is most of the midface of the same individual, which includes the nasal region, the maxilla and part of the cheek.

Dating comes from the stratigraphy and from the palaeomagnetic record, which places the layer between 1.4 and 1.1 million years ago. That range puts the fossil among the oldest hominin remains in Europe, alongside the material from Dmanisi in Georgia and from Orce in southern Spain, and it places a face in the west at a time when the continent was being colonized from the east.

Morphology is the finding. The midface is primitive for the genus *Homo*: the nasal bones project less than a modern human's, the maxilla is broad, and the cheek is flatter. The authors compare it with *Homo antecessor*, the species from the nearby site of Gran Dolina that is dated to between 900,000 and 800,000 years ago and that has a much more modern face, and the comparison leaves the two clearly apart.

That separation is a problem for the simple story. If the later species at Gran Dolina descended from the earlier population at Sima del Elefante, the face should look like a halfway house. A fossil with primitive features that does not resemble the younger species suggests either a separate wave of dispersal or a population that was replaced, and the authors lean towards the second reading without being able to demonstrate it.

The tools in the layer are simple. They are flakes and choppers of quartzite and limestone that were made from locally available stone, and they belong to an industry that pre-dates the handaxe tradition of the later sites. A face and a tool kit from the same layer give a picture of a hominin that was living in a cool and wooded landscape and making tools that were adequate rather than refined.

Europe at that date was not empty of other predators. The site has produced cut marks on bone and evidence of fire, and the cave was shared with hyenas and with large cats. A hominin that occupied such a place had to compete for carcasses, and the debris that the excavation recovered is the record of that competition.

What the fossil does to the map is to move the western limit of the human story back by several hundred thousand years. The dispersal out of Africa is usually described as a series of waves, and the earliest of them left traces in the Caucasus and in Spain. A face from Spain that is 1.4 million years old is the westernmost trace of that wave, and it is the one that a future genetic study will try to place on the tree.
