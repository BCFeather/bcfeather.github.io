Sea ice thickness is measured from above by satellite altimetry, and the measurement fails where the ice is thinnest. A campaign reported this month sent three autonomous underwater gliders beneath the ice of a bay in the Canadian Arctic for five months, and the map they returned differs from the satellite product in exactly the places that matter.

Instruments are the reason the survey worked. Each glider carried an upward-looking sonar that measures the ice draft, the submerged thickness, at 1 hertz, and a conductivity sensor that corrects the speed of sound in water. Navigation under ice has no satellite fix, so the gliders dead-reckoned between acoustic beacons and surfaced through leads to fix position.

The survey found ice 30 to 60 centimetres thick in a zone where the satellite product reported 1.2 metres. The cause is a footprint mismatch: an altimeter averages over a 30-kilometre swath and interpolates across leads, and a region of thin, broken ice reads as thicker than it is.

Sampling density is the second gain. The gliders covered 1,900 kilometres of track at 20-metre resolution, against a satellite revisit of once per day and a footprint of tens of kilometres. For a bay that opens and closes within a season, the difference decides whether a model can resolve the ice's breakup.

Data are being used in two forecast systems. Both assimilate the draft measurements as a constraint, and both report a reduction in the error of the spring breakup date, by four days in one and seven in the other. That is a large improvement for a seasonal forecast.

Engineering limits are real. A glider under ice cannot surface to transmit, so the data arrive when the instrument returns, and a navigation error of 200 metres in a lead is enough to lose a day of survey. Two of the three gliders returned with 80 per cent of their planned track completed.

Cost is the argument for scaling. A glider mission costs 400,000 dollars for a season, against 40 million for a icebreaker cruise, and it collects a narrower set of variables. The scientists describe the gliders as a complement to ships rather than a substitute.

Buoyancy control is the part that limits endurance. A glider changes its depth by pumping oil into a bladder, and the pump's energy budget sets the number of profiles it can fly. The three gliders averaged 900 profiles each, and a fourth mission planned for next season uses a larger bladder to extend that to 1,400.

Data policy is the other constraint. The survey's track lines cross a shipping lane and a territorial boundary, and the release of the position data was negotiated with two coast guards. The thickness measurements are public and the navigation logs are not.

For a reader, the pattern is familiar from oceanography. A satellite gives coverage, an in-situ instrument gives truth, and the two disagree most where the field is most variable. The thin ice that the satellite misses is the ice that breaks up first, so the disagreement is not academic.
