Stroke trials have long excluded patients whose imaging shows a large infarct, on the reasoning that the tissue is already dead and reopening the vessel can only add bleeding. The exclusion became a self-fulfilling rule: because the patients were not enrolled, no evidence existed, and because no evidence existed, the guidelines kept them out. The rule was a reasonable default in the era before thrombectomy, when reopening a vessel had no proven benefit.

A Japanese multicentre trial enrolled exactly that group. Patients with large vessel occlusion and an Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomographic Score of 3 to 5, where 10 is normal and lower values mean a larger infarct, were randomized to endovascular therapy plus medical care or to medical care alone, within six hours of being last known well or within 24 hours if imaging showed no early change. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 9 February 2022. Patients with a score of 0 to 2 were excluded because the risk of haemorrhage was thought to outweigh any gain.

Of 203 patients, 101 received endovascular therapy and 102 medical care, and about 27 per cent of each group also received alteplase. At 90 days, 31.0 per cent of the endovascular group reached a modified Rankin score of 0 to 3, meaning independence or near independence, against 12.7 per cent of the medical group, a relative risk of 2.43 with an interval from 1.35 to 4.37. An improvement of at least eight points on the stroke severity scale at 48 hours occurred in 31.0 per cent against 8.8 per cent. The ordinal analysis, which compares the whole distribution of scores rather than a single cutoff, also favoured thrombectomy.

The benefit is real and the cost is visible. Any intracranial haemorrhage occurred in 58.0 per cent of the endovascular group and 31.4 per cent of the medical group, and the trial was open-label, so the treating team knew the assignment. A symptomatic haemorrhage that worsens the deficit is the outcome that matters, and the abstract reports the broader category. Haemorrhage that is radiological only is a different event from haemorrhage that changes the clinical course, and the paper separates the two.

Selection explains why this trial could show a benefit where a broader one might not. The patients were chosen on imaging within a narrow window, the operators were experienced, and the comparator was medical care in a Japanese system with rapid transfer times. Generalizing to a hospital that takes longer to assemble a team is not automatic. The trial took several years to enrol 203 patients, which is a measure of how unusual the eligible patient is.

The practical question is now one of triage rather than of principle. A patient with a large infarct and a salvageable penumbra should be considered for transfer, and the imaging criteria that identify that penumbra are the next thing to standardize. Whether a patient with a very large infarct and a poor baseline should be treated at all remains a judgement for the family and the clinician.
