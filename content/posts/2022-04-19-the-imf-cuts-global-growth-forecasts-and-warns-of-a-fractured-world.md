The International Monetary Fund cut its forecast for global growth this year to 3.6 per cent from 4.4 per cent in January, and put 2023 at the same figure, a reduction of 0.2 points for next year. The downgrade covered nearly every large economy, and the fund's chief economist described the war in Ukraine as a shock that had hit growth and raised prices at the same time. The figures appeared in the World Economic Outlook, the semi-annual forecast that finance ministries and central banks use as a baseline.

Regional numbers show where the damage lands. The euro area was cut to 2.8 per cent for this year, the United States to 3.7, China to 4.4 and the emerging and developing group to 3.8, a reduction of a full point from January. The fund also raised its inflation projections for advanced economies, and it noted that commodity prices, shipping costs and food security were transmitting the shock well beyond the two countries at war. The revisions were also unusually uncertain, because they depended on the course of a war nobody could forecast.

The mechanism is straightforward and unpleasant. Russia and Ukraine together supply a large share of the world's wheat, maize, sunflower oil and fertiliser, and the disruption to Black Sea shipping raised food prices everywhere, with the sharpest effects in import-dependent countries in North Africa and the Middle East. Energy prices did the same for industry, and the fund estimated that the war would slow growth through trade, financial conditions and confidence rather than through any single channel.

Its report also made an argument about the architecture of the world economy. It warned that sanctions and export controls were segmenting trade into blocs, that the longer those measures remain in place the more they will be built into corporate supply chains, and that a world of competing blocs trades less, produces less and pays more for what it makes. The fund's own projections assumed that the fragmentation stays limited, and it said so explicitly. The alternative, in its view, was a world in which countries trade inside blocs and pay a permanent efficiency cost for the privilege.

For poorer countries the arithmetic is worse than the average suggests. Many entered the pandemic with high debt, borrowed in dollars while the currency strengthened, and now face food and fuel bills that their reserves cannot cover. The fund's debt service relief mechanisms remained underfunded, and the countries that had received vaccine doses late were also the ones exposed to another wave of the disease.

Forecasts in wartime are usually revised again, and the fund said its baseline assumed the conflict stays contained and that sanctions remain as they were in April. Neither assumption is a prediction. What the report does establish is that the war's cost is being paid far from the front, in import bills, budget deficits and interest rates, and that the bill arrives before the fighting stops. The fund's lending capacity was under strain as well, since several members had borrowed heavily during the pandemic and had not yet repaid.
