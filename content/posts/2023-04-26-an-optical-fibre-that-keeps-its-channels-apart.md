A single-mode fibre carries one channel, and a multimode fibre carries many that mix. The mixing is the problem: a mode that couples to its neighbours arrives at the far end with a different delay, and the receiver has to untangle them.

In *Science* on 21 April 2023, the mixing is avoided. The authors design a fibre whose cladding confines light through a topological structure, so that the guided modes are separated by an invariant rather than by a refractive-index profile.

Topology supplies the mechanism. A photonic structure with a nontrivial topology supports edge states that cannot scatter into the bulk, and the invariance protects the guided mode from perturbations that would otherwise couple it. Capacity is the prize, since a fibre that carries many channels without mixing scales its capacity with the number of channels, and a data-centre link that needs more bandwidth is the customer.

Because a graded-index fibre reduces modal dispersion without eliminating coupling, a topological cladding addresses the coupling itself, which is the design's advantage over the conventional multimode product. Manufacture decides whether the advantage survives, since a fibre is drawn from a preform and a preform carrying a topological cladding has to survive the draw, and the fabrication reported is the evidence that the design can be made at all.

Loss sets the reach. A topological fibre that attenuates more than a conventional one trades capacity for distance, and the loss measurements are what place it in the market. Nonlinearity sets another limit, because many channels share one core of glass and high power in one channel affects the others, so a fibre with separated modes mitigates coupling but not the shared material.

Measurement turns the design into proof, since a mode-resolved characterization at the fibre's full length shows whether channels stay distinct after propagation, which is what separates a design from a simulation. A deployed link is still missing: a laboratory fibre with measured capacity is a component, and a system test with transceivers is the next step. The amplifier question follows, because a link carrying dozens of channels must amplify them without adding noise to each, and separated modes make that simpler than mixed ones. Optical communications gains a new degree of freedom, since a fibre's cladding has been a refractive-index profile for decades and a topological one is a different principle; the move is borrowed from condensed matter, where topological insulators confine electrons at an edge, and the photonic analogue confines light at a boundary. The headline number is the mode count, because a fibre that guides a few modes and one that guides dozens are different products, and this design targets the many-mode regime where mixing is worst.

What a topological cladding costs in manufacture is the number that decides adoption, since a preform that tolerates the draw on a laboratory scale may not tolerate it in a tower that produces kilometres at a time.
