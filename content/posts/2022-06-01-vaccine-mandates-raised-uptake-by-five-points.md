---

Whether a mandate raises vaccination or hardens refusal is a question that has been argued from principle and answered from anecdote. A natural experiment across four countries supplies the numbers, and the numbers are smaller than either camp claims. A policy that is argued from principle is rarely measured afterwards, and the absence of measurement is what allowed both camps to keep their prior.

The study exploits variation in the timing of mandate announcements across Canadian provinces, estimates the change in weekly first doses with a difference-in-differences design, and repeats the time-series analysis for France, Italy and Germany. The paper appeared in *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2 June 2022. A difference-in-differences design requires that the provinces were on parallel trends before the announcements, and the event-study plots in the paper are the test of that assumption.

Counterfactual simulations put the gain in the eligible population at up to 5 percentage points by 31 October 2021 in the Canadian provinces, with an interval from 3.9 to 5.8, which amounts to about 979,000 additional first doses nationally. France gained 8 points, Italy 12 and Germany 4.7, over windows of eleven to sixteen weeks. The intervals differ across countries because the populations differ, and the point estimates differ because the mandate designs differ in what they withhold from the unvaccinated.

A gain of five points is a substantial public health effect and a modest political one, because it moves a country from one plateau of coverage to the next without approaching the level that a mandate is supposed to deliver. The estimates are of first doses, and the second dose follows unless the person declines it. A first dose that is delivered to a willing adult is a dose that would have arrived eventually in most cases, so the estimate describes acceleration as much as addition.

A mandate works through the cost of refusal rather than through persuasion, and the mechanism predicts that the effect will be concentrated among people who were already willing and had not acted. That is the group the estimates describe, and it is the group that a mandate is designed to move. A person who was willing and had not acted is a person whose obstacle was friction, and a mandate removes friction by making the alternative inconvenient rather than by changing the belief.

Threats to the design are real, since provinces that announced earlier may differ in their epidemic situation and in their political composition, and the time-series analyses in other countries are the check on that. The four countries do not share a mandate design, which is why the range is wide. The time-series analysis in France, Italy and Germany is a check on whether the Canadian estimates are a provincial artefact, and the four countries agree on the direction if not on the magnitude.

The policy lesson is that a mandate is a nudge with a legal edge, and its effect is bounded by the size of the willing-but-unvaccinated population. A country that has already exhausted that group will not gain five points, and a country that has not will gain more. A country that has not exhausted its willing population will gain more than five points, and a country that has exhausted it will gain nothing, which is the fact that a mandate debate usually ignores.
