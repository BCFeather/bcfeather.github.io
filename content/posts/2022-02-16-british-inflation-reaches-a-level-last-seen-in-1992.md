Consumer prices in Britain rose 5.5 per cent in the year to January, the Office for National Statistics reported on 16 February, the highest rate since 1992. The figure excluding energy, food and tobacco was 4.4 per cent, and the prices of goods rather than services drove the increase. Fuel, gas and electricity accounted for a large part of it, and the Bank of England had already raised its policy rate twice, to 0.5 per cent, in anticipation.

The energy numbers were about to get worse. The regulator sets a cap on what suppliers may charge a typical household, and the cap was due to be reset on 1 April at a level about 54 per cent higher, which would take the annual bill from 1,277 pounds to 1,971. The forecast that the Treasury published alongside the budget in the spring put inflation above 7 per cent for the second quarter, and it expected the rate to fall only in the following year.

Wages had not kept up. Average pay rose about 4 per cent in the same period, so real pay fell, and the fall was steepest in the public sector, where settlements had been capped. The government was also raising national insurance contributions in April, by 1.25 percentage points, to fund health and social care, which took more from the same payslips that the energy cap was about to squeeze. Households that spent a large share of their income on gas and electricity faced a cut in living standards of several per cent.

The Bank faced the contradiction that every central bank faced that winter. Raising rates would slow the demand that was pushing prices up, and it would also raise the cost of the mortgages that a third of households pay, at a moment when their energy bills were doubling. Andrew Bailey, the governor, said the Bank would act, and it raised the rate to 0.75 per cent in March and to 1 per cent in May, and the market expected more.

For the government, the argument ran along a familiar line. The chancellor said the government had spent 9 billion pounds on support for the poorest households and could not borrow more without pushing prices higher, and the opposition said a windfall tax on the oil and gas producers would pay for a bigger package. In May the government announced a payment of 400 pounds to every household and a levy on the energy companies, which combined the two proposals that had been argued against each other.

Two years later the rate had come back to the target, and the bills had not. The cap that the regulator sets each quarter assumed that the wholesale price would fall, and the wholesale price did fall in 2023, and the standing charge that every household pays for the connection rose as the suppliers recovered the losses of the crisis. The winter of 2022 was the point at which the arithmetic of energy entered the politics of everything else.
