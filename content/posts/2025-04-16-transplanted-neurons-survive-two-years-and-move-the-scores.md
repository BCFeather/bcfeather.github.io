description: "Two early-phase trials of stem-cell-derived dopamine neurons in Parkinson's disease reported safety and modest motor gains, with the Japanese trial showing a 44.7 per cent rise in putaminal dopamine uptake."
---

Replacing the neurons that die in Parkinson's disease has been a surgical idea for four decades. The first attempts used tissue from aborted fetuses, produced mixed results, and in some patients caused dyskinesia that could not be switched off. Stem cells changed the supply problem: a pluripotent line can be grown to order and differentiated into midbrain dopamine neurons, and the cells can be characterized before they are implanted. What remained untested was whether such cells survive, connect and do anything in a human brain.

Two trials reported in *Nature* on 16 April 2025 answered the first part of that question. A phase 1 study in the United States and Canada, led by Tabar, implanted human embryonic stem cell derivatives into 12 patients in two cohorts: five received 0.9 million cells and seven received 2.7 million. A phase 1/2 study in Japan, led by Sawamoto, used induced pluripotent cells in seven patients. Both groups gave immunosuppression for a year and followed patients for two years.

Safety held. Neither trial reported a serious adverse event related to the cell product, no graft formed a tumour, and imaging showed no overgrowth. The Japanese trial recorded 73 mild to moderate events, most of them related to surgery or to the immunosuppression, and dyskinesia increased as patients' medication was adjusted. The American trial reported no graft-induced dyskinesia, which was the complication that ended the fetal-tissue era.

Efficacy signals were small, uneven and real. In Japan six patients were evaluable: four improved on the OFF-state motor score and five on the ON-state score, with mean changes of 9.5 points, about 20 per cent, and 4.3 points, about 36 per cent, against baseline. Hoehn and Yahr staging improved in four. Positron emission tomography with fluorodopa showed the putaminal uptake constant rising by 44.7 per cent, and the rise was larger in the high-dose group, which is the objective evidence that the grafts survived and were making dopamine.

The American trial's signal came later and in one cohort. At 18 months the high-dose group had improved by an average of 23 points on the OFF-state score, while the low-dose group changed little. A dose response is the kind of pattern that argues for a biological effect instead of a surgical placebo, and the two trials together show it in two different cell sources, embryonic and induced pluripotent. That convergence is the strongest part of the result.

What the trials do not show is that patients are better off in a way they can feel. The scores moved, quality of life did not change significantly in the Japanese cohort, and the open-label design means that both patients and assessors knew who had been operated on. A 20 per cent fall in a motor score after brain surgery, with immunosuppression for a year, is a trade that a larger trial has to price. Nobody has yet compared implantation with the best medical therapy.

The next trials will be blinded, will use a sham surgery arm, and will be powered on a functional endpoint rather than on safety. They will also have to solve the cell-dose question, because a dose that helps in the putamen may also produce the dyskinesia that the fetal grafts produced. Regenerative neurology has been promising this for twenty years; two small trials with a dose response are the first evidence that the promise has a mechanism behind it.
