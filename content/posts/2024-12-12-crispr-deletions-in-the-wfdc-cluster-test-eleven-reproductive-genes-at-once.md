Gene families evolve by duplication, and a cluster of similar genes is difficult to study because a knockout of one leaves its paralogues intact. A paper in *PNAS* on 12 December 2024 deletes the cluster in pieces. Eleven similar genes in one locus is a problem of redundancy before it is a problem of technique, and the deletion series is the design that redundancy requires. The series is finite and its interpretation depends on the order of the deletions, which is a design choice the authors had to make. A masked phenotype is the common case in gene families, and common cases are why the deletion series is worth the effort.

The cluster is the WFDC locus, which holds eleven reproductive tract genes that humans and rodents share after 96 million years of separate evolution. Conservation over that interval is evidence that the genes do something. Ninety-six million years of conservation is an argument that a function exists, and the argument is what justifies the mouse work. A couple trying to conceive is where a small effect becomes a clinical question, and the size of the effect decides what advice is possible.

Large CRISPR deletions remove several genes at once, and the phenotypes are read against deletions that remove fewer. A phenotype appearing only when the cluster is removed is one that the remaining genes mask. Removing several genes at once is the only way to expose a masked phenotype, and masked phenotypes are the reason the locus has been opaque. A diagnostic laboratory wants to know which genes can be lost, and a deletion map is the format in which that knowledge travels.

The human genes are the ones of interest, and the rodent is the system in which the experiment can be done. The comparison is what the paper uses to argue for conservation of function. Fertility is the endpoint a reproductive gene is expected to move, and a small movement is enough to matter for a couple trying to conceive. The order of the deletions is a design choice that constrains interpretation, and the constraint is the price of working in one locus.

Reproductive tract function is the phenotype, and fertility is the outcome a deletion series can measure. A gene whose loss reduces fertility slightly is a gene a single knockout would have missed. Clusters that compensate appear in immunity and in reproduction, so a method developed here can be applied there without modification.

On the modelling side, the approach is general for gene clusters, and it applies wherever paralogues compensate. Compensation is why single knockouts of clustered genes so often produce nothing. A human cell line would remove the species gap, and the gap is the objection a reviewer would raise first.

Whether the same series is feasible in a human cell line is a technical question. A deletion series also supplies a map of which genes can be lost without consequence, and a map of that kind is what a diagnostic laboratory wants.
