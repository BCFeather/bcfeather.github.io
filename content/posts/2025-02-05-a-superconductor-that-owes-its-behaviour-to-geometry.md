description: "Measurements of magic-angle twisted bilayer graphene in *Nature* show a superfluid stiffness far larger than ordinary theory predicts, which points to the quantum geometry of the flat band rather than to conventional electron interactions."
---

Twisted bilayer graphene is made by stacking two sheets of carbon at a small relative rotation, and at the angle of about 1.1 degrees the electronic bands become almost flat. Flat bands make interactions dominate, and the resulting phases have included superconductivity, correlated insulators and magnetism, all in a material that is little more than two layers of carbon.

Superconductivity in such a system is described by the stiffness of the superfluid, which is the energy cost of twisting the phase of the order parameter across the sample. A conventional superconductor made from a Fermi liquid has a stiffness set by the density of electrons and their mass, and the value can be calculated from the same parameters that give the normal-state conductivity, so a measurement of the stiffness is a test of the assumption that the electrons behave as a Fermi liquid.

A paper in *Nature* on 5 February measured the stiffness directly, using a microwave circuit that couples to the kinetic inductance of the superconductor. The authors worked at dilution refrigerator temperatures and at carrier densities where the twisted bilayer superconducts, and they extracted the stiffness from the frequency shift of the resonator as the sample entered the superconducting state.

The value is much larger than the Fermi liquid estimate. What the authors find instead matches predictions that treat the flat band as a quantum object with its own geometry, in which the wavefunctions of the electrons have a structure in momentum space that contributes to the stiffness independently of how many electrons there are. That contribution is the quantum metric, and in a flat band, where the bandwidth is comparable to the interaction energy, it can dominate everything else.

Temperature dependence is the second piece of the argument. The stiffness falls with a power law as the temperature rises, and the exponent does not fit the isotropic model in which the superconducting gap is the same everywhere on the Fermi surface. An anisotropic gap fits, and the anisotropy is what a flat band with a nontrivial quantum geometry produces.

This result matters for the interpretation of the whole family of moire superconductors. If the stiffness comes from geometry rather than from density, then the ratio of the superfluid density to the carrier density, which is the quantity that a conventional analysis uses to compare a superconductor with a Fermi gas, is not the right diagnostic. The phase diagrams of these materials will have to be read with the quantum metric in the model.

What remains is the gap itself. A measurement of the stiffness constrains the gap without measuring it directly, and the experiments that would resolve the gap structure, which are tunneling and thermal conductivity, have produced results that are consistent with several models. The stiffness narrows the field to a handful of candidates, and narrowing the field is what a good measurement does when the theory has more freedom than the data.
