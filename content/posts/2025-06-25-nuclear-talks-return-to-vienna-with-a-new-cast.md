Late June brought the resumption of the negotiations over the Iranian nuclear programme, with the cast that the year had produced: the American negotiators arrived with the new administration's transactional doctrine, the Iranian negotiators returned seeking the relief that the sanctions have made urgent, and the international observers watched from the sidelines for the signs of the deal that the previous rounds could not produce. The talks, conducted in the city that the nuclear diplomacy has used for generations, opened with the positions that the year had hardened and the expectations that the previous failures had lowered; the talks' prospects are the subject of the summer's diplomacy, and the subject's uncertainty is the honest measure of the distance that remains.

Negotiators and analysts have been parsing the American position since the opening. The new administration's approach to the nuclear file has carried the same transactional logic that the year's other policies have shown: the relief available for the compliance, the pressure applied through the sanctions, and the deals judged by the terms rather than the process; the negotiators have indicated, in the careful language, that the administration is prepared for the agreement that the previous attempts could not reach, and that the agreement's terms would be the ones that the American arithmetic requires. The position is the opening that the talks need and the condition that the negotiations will test.

Iran's position has been shaped by the economics of the sanctions and the politics of the succession. The relief that the agreement would bring, the oil revenues, the banking access, the investment, is the prize that the Iranian negotiators are pursuing, and the pursuit is conducted against the background of the domestic politics that the year's events have complicated; the negotiators have insisted, in the statements, that the programme's rights are not negotiable, and that the relief must be genuine and verifiable. The two positions, the American conditionality and the Iranian insistence, are the poles that the talks will have to bridge.

Negotiators describe the technical work as the foundation of whatever progress has been made. The verification arrangements, the inspection regimes, the enrichment limits, the timelines, all of it has been mapped through the previous rounds, and the mapping provides the technical language that the political terms will have to be written in; the technical sessions have proceeded, the experts' meetings have been conducted, and the texts have been circulated with the particular care that the file's history demands. The technical foundation is real, and the foundation's sufficiency will be tested by the political decisions that the talks will require.

As the sessions opened, the prospects were those of negotiations that have been through the previous failures: the parties know the shape of the deal, the parties know the terms that have blocked it, and the parties are testing whether the year's changes have moved the terms. The summer's sessions will conduct the test, and the test's outcome will be determined by the forces that the year has set: the American administration's willingness to take the deal that the previous administrations could not, the Iranian government's calculation of the relief's value against the programme's autonomy, and the international observers' role in the verification and the confidence. The nuclear talks returned to Vienna with a new cast in the summer of 2025, and the cast's ability to finish the work that the previous ones could not is the diplomacy's central question; the answer will be written in the sessions that the summer will hold, and the writing will be the year's most consequential security story if the answer is yes.
