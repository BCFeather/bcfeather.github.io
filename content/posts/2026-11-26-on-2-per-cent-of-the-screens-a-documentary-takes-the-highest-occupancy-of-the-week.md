Mainland cinemas took 162.3 million yuan in the week to 22 November, 6.3 per cent less than the week before, from 4.38 million admissions and 2.526 million screenings. The average ticket was 37.04 yuan, up 1.6 per cent. Divide the admissions by the screenings and the figure is 1.7 people per show, which is the number that explains the rest of the week: a market with many screens and thin audiences rewards the film that fills a room.

Leading the week was *The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping* at 39.3 million yuan, a quarter of it on its opening day, from 24.2 per cent of the week's screenings, and its first three days came to just under 40 million. Two other releases followed, at about 27.0 million and 20.4 million, each well short of the leader. Between them the top three took two-fifths of the week's revenue, which is ordinary for a frame with one strong opening.

The outlier was a documentary about the north face of Everest, made by National Geographic and directed by Elizabeth Chai Vasarhelyi. It earned 10.99 million yuan from 2.1 per cent of screenings, and its occupancy of 2.8 per cent was the highest in the top ten. Per screening, that is about 5.2 million yuan for each percentage point of the week's screen count, against 1.6 million for the leader, a difference of more than three to one.

Per-screen arithmetic explains why a distributor keeps a documentary alive. A screen given to the leader returned roughly a third of what the same screen returned for the Everest film, and the comparison holds even though the documentary played fewer shows per day and mostly in smaller auditoriums. It assumes both titles had access to comparable houses, which the documentary did not. The premium format helped: the film was sold in large-format and high-frame-rate houses, where a ticket costs more than the 37.04 yuan average.

The ratios need reading with care. Occupancy of 2.8 per cent is low in absolute terms, so the film was filling a small room and not turning away crowds, while a week's gross says nothing about what the film cost or what was spent promoting it, and neither figure is public. Nor does one week establish a trend: a documentary with a large-format release can post a strong first week and thin out quickly once the novelty passes. A second week would show whether the large-format houses hold their audiences.

What the week does show is that the value of a screen depends on what is on it, and that the arithmetic of per-screen revenue is the only measure a cinema operator can act on. The leader will keep its screens on the strength of its opening. The documentary has to earn them back one show at a time, and the week's table, which counts screens and seats rather than intentions, is the only evidence either of them has.
