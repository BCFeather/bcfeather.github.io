New York Court of Appeals overturned Harvey Weinstein's conviction on the twenty-fifth of April by a majority of four to three and ordered a new trial, four years after a jury found him guilty of rape and of a criminal sexual act. Weinstein remained in prison because of a separate sixteen-year sentence imposed in Los Angeles in 2023, and the ruling did not affect that case.

Reason for the decision was the testimony that the trial judge had allowed. Prosecutors called several women who described encounters with Weinstein that were outside the charges, and the judge permitted it on the theory that it showed a pattern of behaviour. The appeals court held that this was an error, that the evidence had been more prejudicial than probative, and that the defendant had been tried for conduct that the indictment did not describe.

Dissent was written by judges who said the majority had ignored the context in which the complainants had come forward. They said the testimony had been necessary to explain why women who had been assaulted by a producer with power over their careers had stayed silent for years, and they said the ruling would make it harder to prosecute cases of this kind.

Reaction from the women who testified was anger and determination. The actor at the centre of the case said the court had told survivors that they could be attacked again in public, and she said she would testify at a retrial. The movement that the case helped to start in 2017 issued statements that described the ruling as a setback and as a reminder that the legal system moves more slowly than the culture.

Legal consequence was a retrial, which the Manhattan district attorney said he would pursue. The office began preparing a new case, and it said it would seek to introduce some of the same evidence under the rules that the appeals court had set out. Weinstein, who was in poor health, was brought to court from prison for hearings in the summer, and the retrial was scheduled for the following year.

The Manhattan district attorney said the retrial would be scheduled for the autumn, and that the complainant in the case would testify again. Two of the complainants in the original trial said they had been told by prosecutors that they would be called again, and their lawyers said the ruling had given the defence a second chance to attack their motives. The Los Angeles case, which was brought by a different district attorney and which produced a separate conviction and sentence, remained unaffected, and the defendant's lawyers said they would appeal it as well.

Meaning of the twenty-fifth of April is a distinction that is easy to lose in the coverage. The court did not find that Weinstein was innocent and did not question the credibility of the women who testified, and it found that the trial had been conducted in a way that the law does not allow. That distinction is the whole point of an appeals court, and it is the reason that the ruling produced both a legal precedent and a political argument that the two sides read in opposite directions.
