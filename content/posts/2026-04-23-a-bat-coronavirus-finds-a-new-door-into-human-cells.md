Coronavirus entry is a receptor question. The spike protein on the surface binds a protein on the host cell, and which protein it binds determines which tissues can be infected and which species can be infected. Six receptors have been described, and almost all of the knowledge comes from betacoronaviruses, the group that includes SARS-CoV-2 and MERS-CoV.

Alphacoronaviruses are the other large group, they circulate mostly in bats, and they are poorly characterized. A team led by Giulia Gallo took a computational approach to select forty spike proteins that represent much of the group's genetic diversity, built them into pseudoviruses that can enter a cell but cannot replicate, and tested them against libraries of known coronavirus receptors from many species.

Most of the bat spikes could not use any known receptor, which is the expected result for a group that has been studied through the lens of its relatives. One could, and it could enter human cells without using any of the six. The virus came from *Cardioderma cor*, the heart-nosed bat, sampled in Kenya, and a screen of human cell-surface proteins identified CEACAM6 as the receptor it uses.

CEACAM6 is a carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule, expressed on the surfaces of epithelial cells in the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts. Structural work confirmed the binding interface, and functional work showed that removing CEACAM6 from cells abolished entry, which is the combination of evidence that turns a screen into a receptor and a hypothesis into a mechanism.

Related viruses from Africa, and to a lesser extent from Eurasia, use the same receptor, which suggests the capability is a property of a lineage and not a quirk of one isolate. Blood samples from people living near the sampling sites showed no evidence of widespread infection, so the viruses are not, at present, spilling over in a way that would be detected.

What the work amounts to is a risk-assessment result more than an outbreak result. Pandemic preparedness screens viruses for the ability to enter human cells, and a screen that tests only known receptors will miss a virus that uses a new one, which is exactly the class of virus that surveillance exists to catch.

Caveats are standard for pseudovirus work. A pseudovirus that enters a cell in a dish is not a virus that replicates in a person, and the barriers after entry, from innate immunity to viral polymerase compatibility, are the ones that determine whether an infection follows. Entry is necessary and it is far from sufficient. What the paper supports is a broader screening strategy. The receptor space is larger than the six proteins that have been described, and a systematic search against the whole surface proteome is the method that would map it, at a cost that a consortium could bear. The cost of that search is small compared with the cost of discovering the next receptor after an outbreak has begun.
