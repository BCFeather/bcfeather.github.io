title: "Remote schooling cost Brazilian pupils most of a year of learning"

description: "Comparing school quarters before and during the pandemic in Brazil, remote learning raised the dropout risk by 365 per cent and cut test scores by 0.32 standard deviations, as if pupils had learned 27.5 per cent of what a normal year would have taught."
---

Brazil closed its schools in March 2020 and kept most of them closed for the rest of that year, which makes it a natural laboratory for the effect of remote instruction on secondary education. The country also has a centralized assessment system, which makes the comparison measurable rather than anecdotal. A state that kept schools closed for a full calendar year has a rare advantage for research, because the alternative policy was not available to the same pupils in the same year.

A study used a difference-in-differences design across school quarters and a triple-differences design across municipalities that reopened at different times, with data from secondary pupils in the state of São Paulo. The paper appeared in *Nature Human Behaviour*, 26 May 2022.

Under remote learning, the dropout risk rose by 365 per cent, and test scores fell by 0.32 standard deviations. The authors translate the score decline into a learning loss: pupils learned about 27.5 per cent of what they would have learned in a comparable period of in-person teaching. A dropout risk that triples is a change in the flow of pupils through the system and not just in the stock of learning, and the two effects compound over the years that follow.

Partially resuming in-person classes raised test scores by 20 per cent relative to the control group, which is a smaller effect than the loss and reflects the fact that a partial reopening gives only part of the exposure. The gradient across reopening intensity is what the triple-differences design exploits.

Dropout is the outcome that compounds. A pupil who leaves school does not return in the next quarter, and the 365 per cent increase describes a decision made by families under financial stress as well as by pupils who disengaged from a screen. The comparison across municipalities that reopened at different times controls for the national trend of the pandemic, which a simple before-and-after comparison would not. The reopening effect is smaller than the loss because a partial return gives pupils part of the exposure, and the design cannot separate the effect of more time in class from the effect of a school that has adapted to the pandemic.

São Paulo is one state in one country, and its school system, its labour market and its pandemic policy are specific. The magnitude of the learning loss is nevertheless consistent with estimates from other countries, and the dropout figure is the one that has been least studied elsewhere.

The policy implication is that reopening decisions should weigh the learning loss against the infection risk with numbers on both sides, and that remedial teaching is the necessary complement to reopening. A lost year does not repair itself. A school system that responds only by reopening, without remediation, will carry the loss into the next cohort of university entrants and into the labour market that follows them. The cost of remediation is real, and it is the cheaper of the two options when compared with the lifetime earnings lost by a cohort that leaves school a year behind.
