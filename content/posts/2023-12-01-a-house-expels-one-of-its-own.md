The House voted 311 to 114 on 1 December to expel George Santos, the New York Republican whose biography, campaign accounts and expense reports had been invented in roughly equal measure. He became the sixth member removed in the chamber's history and the first since 2002, and the only one sent out before a single committee had finished with him. Two thirds of the chamber is the threshold, and the margin was comfortable: seventy-one per cent of the members present, with a hundred and fourteen voting to keep him.

Expulsion had been discussed for a year and attempted twice before, and each earlier attempt failed for the same set of reasons. Members worried about the precedent, about removing a person elected by voters who knew most of the story, and about acting ahead of a criminal conviction. The ethics committee changed the arithmetic on 16 November, when it published a report running to hundreds of pages: campaign money spent on cosmetic treatments, on subscriptions, on designer clothes, and a resume that placed him at Goldman Sachs and Baruch College, where nobody remembered him.

What the report could document mattered less than what it demonstrated. Thirty-seven charges were already pending in federal court, including wire fraud, identity theft and falsifying records, and the member had answered them by refusing to resign and by describing the whole affair as a campaign against him. Colleagues who had defended him in May on procedural grounds found themselves defending a man who had spent the interim selling access, mocking the committee that was examining his accounts, and charging donors for introductions to people he had never met.

The vote itself took less than an hour. Members walked to the well of the chamber in alphabetical order, and the parties divided almost evenly, with eighty-nine Republicans joining two hundred and twenty-one Democrats and one independent. The departing member left through a side entrance without speaking to the press, and his staff lost their salaries within a week, because the chamber pays the offices of removed members only up to the removal.

A special election would follow in a district that had been held by Democrats for a decade before 2022, and both parties treated the empty seat as a test of how far the affair had damaged the Republican label in the suburbs of Long Island. The majority in the chamber, already thin, would shrink by one for the weeks before that vote, which put a spotlight on an older arithmetic: the business of the House runs on a handful of votes, and a scandal can remove one of them without warning.

A precedent arrived with the case as well, since the ethics committee had not finished its work when the chamber acted, and the standard of proof applied was the standard of colleagues rather than the standard of a courtroom. Members from both sides said afterwards that they hoped the threshold would never be tested again, and that they knew it would be.
