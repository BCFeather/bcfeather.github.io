When vaccines were authorised for adults and not for children, the question for a household was indirect: could a parent's immunity protect a child who could not yet be vaccinated? The answer matters for school policy, for the timing of paediatric authorisation, and for the modelling that decides when a population reaches herd protection. Paediatric vaccines were not authorised in the United States until the autumn of 2021, which left a long window in which children depended on the immunity of the adults around them.

An Israeli group studied households with two parents and unvaccinated children and no history of infection, and compared the risk of a child testing positive according to parental vaccination. Two periods were analysed separately because the dominant variant changed: 17 January to 28 March 2021, when Alpha circulated and two doses were compared with none, and 11 July to 30 September 2021, when Delta circulated and a booster was compared with two doses. The paper appeared in *Science*, 27 January 2022.

One vaccinated parent was associated with a 26.0 per cent reduction in the child's risk during the Alpha period and 20.8 per cent during the Delta period. Two vaccinated parents were associated with 71.7 per cent and 58.1 per cent reductions respectively. The second dose added more protection than the first, which is consistent with the dose-dependent rise in antibody and with the reduction in household viral load. Dose dependence is the strongest internal check, since one vaccinated parent and two differ in exposure but not in household structure.

Household studies have a natural advantage over population comparisons because exposure is shared and measured. The design compares children within a defined living arrangement, so the difference in risk is tied to the parents' vaccination rather than to the neighbourhood's, and the exclusion of previously infected households removes the confounding of prior immunity.

What the study cannot show is the mechanism. Fewer infections in parents would reduce the chance that a child is exposed at all, and a lower viral load in a vaccinated parent would reduce the chance that exposure becomes infection. The analysis estimates the combined effect rather than separating the two. Serology would be needed to separate them, and the study relied on PCR tests rather than on antibody measurements.

The cohort is one country's households, and the periods are defined by variant dominance rather than by calendar. Variant dominance also shifted within each period, so the estimates are averages over a changing virus. Children's testing behaviour changed over the same interval, and a child tested because a parent was ill is more likely to be captured than one tested for a school requirement.

For policy the implication is that adult coverage protects children before paediatric vaccines arrive, and that a booster extends the effect. For a household the numbers are a reason to complete a parent's schedule even when the child's own turn has not come.
