A chemical reaction is a rearrangement of electrons, and the rearrangement is usually inferred from the products. A measurement that resolves the orbitals during the reaction would show the mechanism rather than its outcome.

The study in *Science*, 2 June 2023 makes that measurement. The authors follow a carbon-hydrogen bond's activation on a metal surface with a time-resolved technique that is sensitive to the orbital character of the states involved.

The technique is the advance. A pump pulse excites the system, and a probe measures the electronic structure at a series of delays, so that the sequence of orbital changes becomes a movie.

The system is a model catalyst. A hydrocarbon adsorbed on a metal is the simplest case of a C-H activation, and the surface provides the electrons that the bond's cleavage requires.

The result is a sequence. The bond's electrons first mix with the metal's states, and the mixing is what weakens the bond before it breaks, which is the step that a product-only analysis cannot see.

The orbital resolution is what makes the claim specific. A measurement of energy alone would show a state shifting, and a measurement of orbital character shows which states are mixing.

The comparison with theory is the check. A calculation of the same system predicts a sequence, and the agreement between the measurement and the calculation is what makes the assignment credible.

The implication for catalysis is a design rule. A surface whose states overlap the bond's orbitals at the right energy is a surface that activates the bond, and the rule is a way to screen catalysts.

The limitation is the model. A UHV experiment on a single crystal is far from an industrial reactor, and pressure and coverage change the mechanism.

What the paper does not show is a full catalytic cycle. Activation is one step, and the subsequent steps proceed on a surface that the first step has modified.

For catalysis, the value is a mechanism made visible. The field's pictures of transition states are drawn from calculations, and a measurement that resolves the orbitals is what turns a picture into evidence.

The surface's role is the electronic reservoir. A metal provides a continuum of states, and a molecule that adsorbs mixes its orbitals with that continuum. The mixing is the start of every surface reaction.

The time scale is the reason the technique is needed. A bond's vibration is tens of femtoseconds, and a reaction that follows it needs a probe with a comparable resolution.

The alternative methods are indirect. Infrared spectroscopy sees the bond's vibration and photoelectron spectroscopy sees the occupied states, and neither resolves the orbital's character during the transition.

The theoretical dispute is about the ordering. Whether the bond stretches first and then breaks or breaks as it stretches is a question that the time-resolved data can settle, and the two orders imply different intermediates.

The industrial relevance is a screening criterion. A catalyst that activates a C-H bond at a lower temperature saves energy, and an orbital-level rule is a way to find one without trial and error.
