A chemical reaction is a rearrangement of electrons, and that rearrangement is usually inferred from the products. A measurement resolving the orbitals during the reaction would show the mechanism rather than its outcome. In *Science* on 2 June 2023, such a measurement is made. The authors follow a carbon-hydrogen bond's activation on a metal surface with a time-resolved technique sensitive to the orbital character of the states involved.

Technique is the advance. A pump pulse excites the system and a probe measures the electronic structure at a series of delays, so the sequence of orbital changes becomes a movie. Time scale explains why the technique is needed, because a bond's vibration lasts tens of femtoseconds, and a reaction following it needs a probe of comparable resolution.

Model catalyst is the system. A hydrocarbon adsorbed on a metal is the simplest case of C-H activation, and the surface supplies the electrons the cleavage requires. The surface itself is an electronic reservoir, since a metal offers a continuum of states and a molecule that adsorbs mixes its orbitals with that continuum, which is the start of every surface reaction.

Sequence is the result. The bond's electrons first mix with the metal's states, and that mixing weakens the bond before it breaks, which is the step a product-only analysis cannot see. Orbital resolution makes the claim specific, because a measurement of energy alone shows a state shifting while a measurement of character shows which states are mixing.

Theory supplies the check. A calculation of the same system predicts a sequence, and agreement between measurement and calculation makes the assignment credible. Dispute about ordering is what the data can settle, since whether the bond stretches first and then breaks or breaks as it stretches implies different intermediates.

Catalysis gains a design rule. A surface whose states overlap the bond's orbitals at the right energy is a surface that activates the bond, and that rule screens catalysts. Industry reads it as a criterion, because a catalyst activating C-H at lower temperature saves energy, and an orbital-level rule finds one without trial and error.

Model sets the limitation, as an ultra-high-vacuum experiment on a single crystal is far from a reactor and pressure and coverage change the mechanism. Full catalytic cycle stays unshown, because activation is one step and later steps proceed on a surface the first step has modified. Catalysis also gains a mechanism made visible, since the field's pictures of transition states are drawn from calculations and a measurement resolving orbitals turns a picture into evidence. Infrared and photoelectron methods offer the comparison, as one sees the vibration and the other the occupied states, and neither resolves orbital character during the transition. A measurement at a stepped surface would show whether the sequence changes with coordination, and the comparison is the cheapest test of the rule the paper proposes. A second substrate would show whether the same ordering survives a different adsorption site, and the comparison would tell a reader how general the movie is.
