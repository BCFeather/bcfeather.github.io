Argentina voted on 22 October and produced a result that almost nobody predicted. The economy minister, Sergio Massa, took 36.7 per cent of the vote, a share larger than most polls had given him, and Javier Milei, the libertarian outsider who had won the primaries in August, took 29.99 per cent. Patricia Bullrich of the centre-right coalition finished third with about 23.8 per cent and was eliminated, which sent the two remaining candidates to a runoff on 19 November.

The campaign took place inside an economic emergency. Inflation was running at more than 140 per cent a year, the central bank's reserves were negative on a net basis, and the currency had been held at an official rate through capital controls that pushed transactions into a parallel market. Massa ran as the candidate of the governing Peronist coalition and also as the man who currently sets the price of the peso, which his opponents described as an unfair advantage and his supporters described as evidence that he understands the machinery.

Milei campaigned on abolishing the central bank, dollarizing the economy and cutting public spending with a chainsaw, a prop he brought to rallies, and his movement drew young men who had never seen a stable currency. His share in the first round fell short of the hopes set by the primary result, and the question for the runoff is where the votes of Bullrich's supporters go: her coalition is conservative on economics and hostile to Milei's cultural politics, and its leaders split within hours of the result.

Turnout was about 77 per cent, and the vote was compulsory for most adults. The parties spent the campaign arguing about the paste of the last twenty years rather than about the programme, and the candidates spoke to different segments of a fragmented electorate. Polling before the vote was wrong by more than ten points for the leading candidate, which has damaged the standing of the local polling industry. It was the first time since the return of democracy that the governing party's candidate led a first round while a libertarian finished second.

The runoff is now a choice between two models rather than two people. Massa offers continuity with a currency that has lost most of its value and with a welfare system that has absorbed part of the shock, and he has promised a government of national unity with a broader coalition. Milei offers rupture, with a plan that would remove the currency and much of the state, and a running mate who has had to reassure voters that the more radical parts of the programme will be gradual.

Whoever wins inherits the same balance sheet. Reserves are depleted, the fiscal deficit has been financed by printing, and the next government will have weeks rather than months before a payment schedule forces a decision. The vote expressed an appetite for change in a country that has tried many versions of it, and the runoff will decide which version gets the next attempt.
