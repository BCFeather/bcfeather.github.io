title: "Sotatercept cuts events in the sickest pulmonary hypertension"
date: "2025-03-31T09:20:00+08:00"

description: "In patients with high-risk pulmonary arterial hypertension, sotatercept cuts the rate of clinical worsening events from 54.7 per cent to 17.4 per cent, a trial in *The New England Journal of Medicine* finds."
---

Pulmonary arterial hypertension is a disease of the small arteries in the lung that narrow until the right ventricle cannot push blood through them. The approved drugs dilate those vessels through three pathways, and a patient who is breathless at rest despite all three has a prognosis measured in years. The population that the trial enrolled is that population, and the treatment options beyond it had been transplantation and a pump that delivers a continuous infusion.

Sotatercept is a fusion protein that traps activin and growth differentiation signals, which are molecules in the transforming growth factor pathway that drive the proliferation of the cells lining the pulmonary arteries. The pathology of the disease includes a growth of those cells into the lumen, which the vasodilators do not address, and the drug is the first approved therapy that targets the growth rather than the tone of the vessel.

The trial randomized patients in functional class III or IV to the drug or to placebo on top of standard therapy, and the primary endpoint was a composite of death, transplantation, hospitalization and an increase in the need for rescue therapy. It occurred in 17.4 per cent of the treated group against 54.7 per cent of the placebo group, a hazard ratio of 0.24 with a confidence interval of 0.13 to 0.43 and a *P* value below 0.001.

The effect size is the largest that the field has reported for this endpoint. A hazard ratio of 0.24 means that the trial was stopped early, which it was, and that a clinician treating a patient at this stage has a reason to expect a change in the course of the disease. The absolute difference of 37 percentage points over the follow-up is the number that a guideline will quote.

Secondary endpoints moved in the same direction. Exercise capacity improved, the pulmonary vascular resistance fell, and a composite of death and transplantation was lower in a subgroup that the trial was not designed to test alone. Quality of life improved, which matters in a disease whose main symptom is breathlessness on walking across a room.

Safety is the part that a reader should weigh. Telangiectasia, nosebleeds and an increase in haemoglobin were more common with the drug, because the pathway that it blocks also regulates vessel maturation and red cell production. Bleeding events were more frequent, and the label carries a warning, which is a cost that a patient with a life expectancy of years may accept.

What the result changes is the sequence of treatment. A drug that reduces events by three quarters in the sickest group will move into earlier lines as the data accumulate, and a disease that was managed with vasodilators and transplantation now has a therapy that modifies its pathology. The price is the remaining question, and the answer will decide how many health systems can afford it. The trial also followed patients for a median of little more than a year, which is long enough to show that events fall and not long enough to show that survival rises.
