Cooperative breeding is a puzzle for a theory built on individual advantage. A bird that helps raise another's offspring forgoes its own reproduction, and the explanation has to be a benefit that indirect fitness provides.

In *PNAS* on 23 May 2023, that benefit is measured over a lifetime. The authors follow acorn woodpeckers, a species in which several males and several females share a nest and helpers assist, and estimate each bird's inclusive fitness from its own offspring and from those of its relatives.

Unusual social structure is the setting. A group holds a granary tree, and the territory is inherited across generations, so a bird's reproductive success depends on a resource its relatives hold. The granary explains why the system is possible, since acorns stored in drilled holes are a larder a group defends and inherits, and a species without such a store would have less incentive to stay.

Helpers gain from the arrangement. A bird that stays and helps raises the fitness of its kin enough to offset its delayed reproduction, and the gain is larger in groups where relatedness is high. Transmission is the mechanism, because a territory passes from parents to offspring, and the offspring inheriting it also inherit the social position that makes reproduction possible, which is an ecological inheritance as well as a genetic one.

Comparison with simple cooperation clarifies it. Where helpers are unrelated the benefit has to be direct, while in the woodpecker it is largely indirect through kin. Demography is the backbone of the analysis, since a lifetime estimate needs decades of resightings, and this study population is among the longest-running in ornithology, which is why the question can be answered at all.

Pedigree sets the measurement's difficulty, because relatedness has to be estimated from observation and from genetics, and the long-term study is what makes a lifetime estimate possible. Counterfactual is the model's assumption, since a helper that left might have bred elsewhere and the fitness it forgoes is hypothetical, which the demographic data can only constrain.

Origin of the system stays unresolved, as a benefit maintaining cooperation now does not explain how it arose, and the historical route is a separate question. Behavioural ecology gains a case where the arithmetic works, because inclusive fitness is a bookkeeping device and a species whose books balance is evidence that the device describes something real. A group whose granary fails is the natural experiment, because the birds then face the choice between staying and dispersing under conditions the model can predict. Comparisons with species that store nothing would show whether the larder is the cause or the correlate, and that comparison is what the long-term data on other woodpeckers would supply. A helper that inherits a granary is a helper whose patience has a price attached, and the price is what the lifetime arithmetic actually computes. What the accounting still cannot price is the alternative of dispersing into unmated territory, which the model treats as a constant.
