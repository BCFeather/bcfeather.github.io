---

Cooperative breeding is a puzzle for a theory built on individual advantage. A bird that helps raise another's offspring forgoes its own reproduction, and the explanation has to be a benefit that indirect fitness provides.

The analysis in *PNAS*, 23 May 2023 measures that benefit over a lifetime. The authors follow acorn woodpeckers, a species in which several males and several females share a nest and helpers assist, and estimate each bird's inclusive fitness from its own offspring and from those of its relatives.

The social system is unusual. A group holds a granary tree, and the territory is inherited across generations, so that a bird's reproductive success depends on a resource that its relatives hold.

The inclusive fitness result is that helpers gain. A bird that stays and helps raises the fitness of its kin enough to offset its delayed reproduction, and the gain is larger in groups where the relatedness is high.

The transmission is the mechanism. A territory is passed from parents to offspring, and the offspring that inherit it also inherit the social position that makes reproduction possible. The inheritance is ecological as well as genetic.

The comparison with simple cooperation is instructive. In a species where helpers are unrelated, the benefit has to be direct, and in the woodpecker the benefit is largely indirect through kin.

The measurement's difficulty is the pedigree. Relatedness has to be estimated from observations and from genetics, and the authors' long-term study is what makes a lifetime estimate possible.

The counterfactual is the model's assumption. A helper that left might have bred elsewhere, and the fitness it forgoes is a hypothetical. The authors' demographic data are what constrain the estimate.

What the paper cannot resolve is the origin of the system. A benefit that maintains cooperation now is not an explanation of how it arose, and the historical route is a separate question.

For behavioural ecology, the value is a case where the arithmetic works. Inclusive fitness is a bookkeeping device, and a species whose books balance is the evidence that the device describes something real.

The policy use is a warning. A lake that is shrinking is a lake whose water rights were allocated in a wetter decade, and a basin plan that assumes the old level is a plan that will fail.

The uncertainty is in the extrapolation. A satellite measures area and a model converts area to volume, and the conversion's error is the largest term in the estimate. The authors' validation against gauges is what bounds it.
The granary is the resource that makes the system possible. Acorns are stored in holes drilled in a tree, and the store is a larder that a group defends and inherits. A species without such a store would not have the same incentive to stay.
The demography is the analysis's backbone. A lifetime estimate needs decades of resightings, and the acorn woodpecker's study population is one of the longest-running in ornithology, which is why the question can be answered at all.
