The European Commission published preliminary findings on 10 July accusing Meta of breaching the Digital Services Act through the design of Instagram and Facebook, and warned that the company could face a fine of up to six per cent of its global annual turnover. Investigators said the platforms relied on features that encourage compulsive use, including infinite scrolling, autoplay, persistent notifications and recommendation systems that keep pushing similar content. Meta said it would study the findings and that it had already introduced tools for teenage users. The commission said the file covered both platforms and that its staff had been examining the design since last year.

Substance of the case is about design rather than about individual posts. The act obliges large platforms to assess the systemic risks their services create, including risks to the mental and physical health of minors, and to mitigate them. Brussels argues that Meta's risk assessments understated those risks and that its mitigations, largely optional time limits and parental controls, left the default experience unchanged for the children who never turn them on. Its team has also been looking at the recommendation systems that push political content, a separate file that stays open.

Procedure gives the company room to answer. Preliminary findings are a stage rather than a verdict, and Meta has the right to see the evidence, respond in writing and propose remedies before the commission adopts a final decision. A final decision can be appealed to the General Court in Luxembourg, and the commission can impose interim measures while the case runs. Meta has submitted risk assessments for both services, and the commission's staff said the documents treated minors as one category among many.

Precedent shapes what the company can expect. The commission opened formal proceedings against several platforms after the act took effect, and its first decisions have mixed fines with orders to change features. The penalty ceiling of six per cent of turnover is far above the fines handed down so far, which is the point: the commission has been signalling that it will use the ceiling when a design, rather than a post, is the violation.

Business model is what makes compliance expensive. Meta earns most of its revenue from advertising priced against attention, and every feature the commission dislikes is a feature that raises the time a person spends in the app. The company has already moved some teen accounts into restrictive defaults in several markets, and it has argued that the changes reduce engagement without producing measurable improvements in well-being. Advertising sold in Europe is roughly a quarter of Meta's global revenue, which sets the size of any settlement.

Europe is now the place where the rules are written for everyone. A design change made in Brussels applies wherever the platform operates, because maintaining two versions of a feed costs more than complying. The company can litigate, and it can comply, and the features that survive will be the ones that keep advertisers paying without keeping children scrolling. The company has appealed earlier decisions and has won some of them on procedural grounds.
