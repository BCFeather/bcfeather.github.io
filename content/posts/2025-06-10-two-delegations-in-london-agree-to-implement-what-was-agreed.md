A negotiation that ends without a number is a negotiation that has agreed on a procedure, and the London meeting of 9 and 10 June ended that way. The two delegations said they had reached a framework for implementing the Geneva understanding of 12 May, under which tariffs had been cut by 115 points on each side for ninety days, and neither side announced a new rate.

The substance was in the annexes that were not published. American officials spoke of export controls and of student visas, Chinese officials spoke of rare earths and of the licensing of magnets, and the two lists are the real currency of a trade relationship that has stopped being mostly about goods. A tariff is a tax and an export control is a permission, and the second is the harder instrument to negotiate away.

Rare earths are the Chinese lever. China produces roughly 60 per cent of the mined material and the great majority of the separated oxides, and in April it had imposed export licensing on seven medium and heavy elements, which is a measure that a factory in Ohio cannot work around. Removing the licences is a concession that costs a ministry nothing and a purchaser everything.

Export controls are the American lever. Restrictions on advanced chips, on chip design software and on equipment had been tightened in the spring, and any easing would be partial and case by case, which is the form of relief that a company cannot plan around. A licence that may be granted is not a supply chain.

The timing was set by the calendar. The Geneva truce runs for ninety days from 14 May, which puts its expiry in mid-August, and both governments needed a reason to keep it before the domestic audiences that had been told the other side was cheating. A framework is a reason.

Businesses treated the meeting as a pause. Shipping rates on the Pacific routes eased, some importers resumed orders for the autumn, and equity markets in Hong Kong and Shanghai were firmer, which is what a market does when the risk it had priced is deferred rather than resolved.

What would settle the autumn is whether the framework is followed by a text. A truce that is renewed twice without a document is a truce that both sides can abandon at a moment of their choosing, and the next deadline is the one that matters. A framework agreed in a hotel is a document that both governments can describe as a win at home, which is precisely why it is worth having. The next test is not whether the two governments can meet again but whether a shipment of magnets leaves a Chinese port without a licence attached. Anything less than a published annex will be read by both sides as a signal that the other one has not yet decided how much it is willing to give up.
