---

House of Representatives passed a bill on the thirteenth of March that would force ByteDance, the Chinese company that owns TikTok, to divest the app within six months or face a ban in the United States. Vote was 352 to 65, with support from both parties and opposition concentrated among younger members and those in districts with large technology constituencies. Bill moved to the Senate, where the majority leader said he would consider it through the normal committee process rather than bringing it to a vote quickly.

Terms of the measure were unusually direct. It named ByteDance and TikTok rather than describing a class of applications, which is a departure from the way American law usually regulates foreign ownership, and it gave the president the power to extend the deadline by ninety days if a sale was under way. Companies that maintain app stores or provide hosting services would be liable if they continued to distribute the app after the deadline, which is the mechanism that would make a ban effective.

National security argument behind the bill is about data and influence. Officials have said that Chinese law obliges companies to hand over data to the intelligence services on request, and that TikTok's recommendation system gives a foreign owner the ability to shape what millions of Americans see. Company has said it stores American user data in the United States through a joint venture with Oracle and that it would not comply with a request that violated local law, and it has spent years negotiating a settlement that would have given the government oversight of its algorithms.

Politics of the vote cut across the usual alignments. The former president, who had tried to ban TikTok by executive order in 2020 and then reversed himself, opposed the bill, and his intervention reduced support among some Republicans. Several Democrats from competitive districts voted against it after the campaign of the incumbent president joined the app, which the White House said was a coincidence and which the company cited as evidence that the bill was aimed at the app rather than at the risk it poses.

Company's response was a lobbying campaign and a message to users. TikTok sent notifications to its American users urging them to call their representatives, and the app was flooded with videos about the vote. Share of American adults who use the platform is about a third, and among those under thirty it is more than half, which makes it the most politically consequential consumer app since Facebook and the hardest to regulate without visible costs.

What followed from the thirteenth of March was a bill that took another six weeks to become law. The Senate passed a modified version attached to a foreign aid package in April, and the president signed it on the twenty-fourth of April, with a deadline of January 2025 for a sale. ByteDance said it would challenge the law in court, and it did. What the House vote established was that both parties were willing to legislate against a single application, which is a precedent that will outlast the app itself.
