Astronomers working with the Event Horizon Telescope released the first image of Sagittarius A*, the supermassive black hole at the centre of the Milky Way, on 12 May. The picture shows a bright ring of gas around a dark central region, and the ring is the radiation emitted by matter falling towards a boundary from which nothing returns. The object sits about twenty-seven thousand light years from Earth, which puts it in the middle of the galaxy and behind everything else in it.

Patience explains the measurement behind the image. Eight radio observatories in Hawaii, Arizona, Chile, Spain, Mexico and at the South Pole recorded simultaneously at a wavelength of 1.3 millimetres, forming a virtual dish the size of the planet. The observations were taken in 2017 and the data took years to reduce, because the black hole at the centre of the galaxy is faint, variable and partly obscured by the dust and gas of the galactic plane, and because the telescope array produces more information than existing software was built to handle.

What the image confirms is a mass of about four million times that of the sun confined inside a region smaller than the orbit of Mercury. The ring's diameter corresponds to a shadow roughly fifty-two micro-arcseconds across in the sky, a size that the collaboration's scientists compared to a doughnut lying on the moon. Einstein's general relativity predicts that shadow precisely, and the agreement between the prediction and the picture is the strongest evidence yet that the object is a black hole of the kind the equations describe.

Comparison with the first such image, of the black hole in M87, is instructive. That one is six and a half billion solar masses and its ring is larger, brighter and steadier, which made the imaging straightforward by the standards of the technique. Sagittarius A* is a thousand times smaller in mass, its gas completes an orbit in minutes rather than weeks, and the brightness pattern changes between one night of observation and the next, so the team had to fit four separate images to the same underlying model, taken on different nights, and then show that all four were pictures of one ring rather than four artefacts.

The result is a set of papers in the Astrophysical Journal Letters rather than a single announcement, and it closes a case that had been argued for decades. The stars orbiting the galactic centre, tracked for thirty years, had already shown that something massive and invisible sits there, and the radio source at the same location had been studied since the 1970s. What the new work supplies is the shape of the thing.

Two images now exist, one at the centre of a nearby galaxy and one at the centre of our own, and both show the same dark disc surrounded by light. The telescopes that produced them are being upgraded to record at higher sensitivity and to add instruments, and the next target is the black hole's shadow at higher resolution, which would test whether the geometry holds at the very edge of the horizon.
