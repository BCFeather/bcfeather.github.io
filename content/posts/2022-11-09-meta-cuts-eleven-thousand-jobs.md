The message went out at six in the morning on 9 November, and it was signed by Mark Zuckerberg himself. It said that the company would reduce its staff by about thirteen per cent, and that more than eleven thousand people would leave. This was the first mass layoff in the eighteen years since the company was founded in a dormitory room, and it was the largest single cut any of the big technology firms had announced during that autumn. Employees learned the news from an internal post before the managers had been briefed, and by mid-morning the car park was full of people carrying cardboard boxes.

The letter did not pretend that the decision came from outside. Zuckerberg wrote that he had decided to shrink the team, that he took responsibility for the situation, and that he understood how much this would hurt people who had done nothing wrong. He added that the company had over-invested in the expectation that the growth of the pandemic years would continue. He also admitted that he had misread the signals when he expanded the payroll in 2020 and 2021, and he said the mistake belonged to him rather than to the people leaving.

Numbers behind the letter were plain. Advertising, which pays for almost everything the company does, had slowed as clients cut budgets in the face of inflation and as the economy cooled through the summer. At the same time the division building the metaverse had been spending billions each quarter on hardware, software and staff, and had yet to show a product that ordinary people wanted to use. The share price had fallen by more than seventy per cent during the year, and investors had begun to ask publicly whether the two businesses belonged inside one company.

People who left received sixteen weeks of pay plus two weeks for each year of service, and health cover for six months. The company also froze hiring until the end of the first quarter of the following year and began to look at its property, including offices it had leased in cities where the rent was high. Some teams were told to consolidate into fewer buildings, and the company confirmed that it would sublet space it no longer needed and review every large lease it held.

Cuts fell across every organization, and the engineering and product teams were hit hardest because they were the largest. Employees in Europe learned the timing of their consultations later, because the laws there require a process before the numbers can be fixed. Recruiters were cut early, since there was no hiring left for them to do, and the people who ran the internal tools were cut later in the same week. Several projects were suspended entirely, and their pages were taken down without any announcement.

There was an uncomfortable symmetry to the day. The company had renamed itself a year earlier to announce a future in which people would work and socialize in three dimensions, and the first big decision under the new name was to let go of many of the people who were meant to build that future. Zuckerberg ended his letter by saying that the company would emerge leaner and more focused, and that he remained confident about the road he had chosen.
