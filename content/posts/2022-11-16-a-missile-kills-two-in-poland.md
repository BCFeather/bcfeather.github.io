The explosion came at a quarter to four in the afternoon, in the village of Przewodow, on the Polish side of the border with Ukraine. It struck a farm dryer where two men were working, and both of them died. The village lies a few kilometres from the frontier, and the war on the other side of it had been visible from the fields for nine months. People there had grown used to the sound of air-defence missiles launching at night, and to the lights of the cities going out across the valley. The nearest town had taken in families from the other side, and the school had doubled its classes.

That afternoon Russia had fired more than eighty missiles across Ukraine, the largest single wave of the war, and the targets included power stations and substations in the west. Ukrainian air defences were firing at the same time, and the sky over the border was full of things travelling quickly. Several missiles were intercepted, and the debris fell in fields and on roads for a hundred kilometres. Engineers in Lviv were already switching the grid to emergency mode as the first reports came in.

For several hours the question stayed open, and the first reports attributed the missile to Russia. The Polish government called an emergency meeting, raised the alert level of some military units, and considered whether to ask for consultations under article four of the treaty that binds the alliance. The American president, at a summit in Asia, said it was unlikely that the missile had come from Russia, and his words were repeated on every channel before the Polish statement was drafted. Officials in Warsaw briefed journalists off the record, and the briefing changed twice in an hour.

By the next morning the assessments had converged. The Polish president said there was no evidence of a deliberate attack, that the missile was most likely a Ukrainian air-defence rocket which had missed its target and fallen short, and that the investigation would continue. The alliance's supreme commander for Europe briefed its council on what the military had seen, and the council issued a statement that held the same line. Two American officials, speaking to reporters, described the same conclusion, and their account matched what the wreckage suggested. Ukrainian investigators were invited to the site, and they accepted the invitation in public.

Politics of that night mattered as much as the findings. An attack on a member by Russia would have raised the question of article five, and the alliance had spent the year trying to keep the war inside Ukraine. Cautious wording from Washington and from Warsaw was the sound of that effort succeeding. The German foreign minister said that the alliance had to react calmly, and the phrase was repeated in capitals from Berlin to Ankara.

In Przewodow the two men were buried in the following week, and the village school held a service for them. In Kyiv the lights went out in a great many apartments, and repair crews began again on the substations. Both facts belong to the same war, and only one of them kept the world awake all night.
