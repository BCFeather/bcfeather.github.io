The brain's blood vessels have been mapped at the level of individual cells, and the map is the first that covers the organ rather than a slice of it. The work came from a consortium that collected tissue from many donors, dissociated each sample and sequenced the RNA of every cell it contained, then assigned the cells to types on the basis of their expression profiles. Endothelial cells, pericytes, smooth muscle cells and the fibroblasts of the vessel wall came out as separate populations, and each population carried a signature that said where in the vascular tree it had been sitting. The donors ranged in age from young adults to the very old, and each sample was paired with a neuropathological report so that a vessel could be read against the state of the tissue around it. The methods section reads like a census instead of an experiment, and that is what a reference atlas is.

Vascular trees are never uniform, and the map shows where the uniformity breaks. Arteries carry blood at pressure and their endothelium expresses the genes that keep the wall compliant; capillaries are where exchange happens and their endothelium expresses the transporters and the junctional proteins that make the blood-brain barrier; veins carry blood away and their endothelium is looser. The transition between these states is gradual along the vessel, and the map resolves it as a continuum of expression and not as three boxes. Between an artery and its capillary bed the endothelial cell narrows, its junctions tighten and the set of genes it expresses changes in steps that the clustering recovers as a gradient. A capillary in the cortex and a capillary in the white matter both express the barrier genes, and they differ in the genes that set the local demand for blood.

Barrier function is the part the field cares about, because it is what keeps drugs out. A capillary endothelial cell in the brain tightens its junctions, expresses the efflux pumps that send small molecules back into the blood, and is supported by a pericyte on the abluminal side that is itself in contact with the astrocyte endfeet. The map identifies the genes that distinguish a brain capillary from a capillary in the kidney, and the list is longer than the textbook version, which had three or four proteins in it. The efflux pumps are the reason a drug that works in a dish fails in a patient, and the map lists the ones expressed in the brain in the order of their abundance. The pumps are also the target of drugs, and knowing which ones a capillary expresses tells a pharmacologist which drug will be turned back at the door.

Pericytes came out of the analysis with more variety than expected. In the brain they are found on capillaries and on the smaller arterioles, and their numbers and their contacts vary with the vessel's size and with the region of the cortex. The map separates pericyte populations by the genes they express, and the separation corresponds to the vessel type they sit on and not to the region, which is a statement about the wall and not about the brain around it. Pericytes on the venules are sparser than on the capillaries, and the map suggests that the populations on the two sides of the bed are not interchangeable. The map also separates pericytes from the smooth muscle cells of the arteriole, which had been conflated in the older literature on the wall.

The atlas covers disease as well as health. Tissue from donors with Alzheimer's disease, with vascular dementia and with strokes showed endothelial signatures that differ from the healthy reference, and the differences are in the genes of inflammation and of the coagulation cascade. The samples are few per condition and the donors differ in age and treatment, so the disease comparisons are hypotheses and not conclusions, and the authors are careful to present them that way. In the stroke samples the endothelial signature of the penumbra looked different from that of the core, and the difference is in the genes of repair and not of injury. Disease comparisons of this kind are the reason the atlas was built, and they will only become useful when the sample sizes grow.

What the map cannot show is flow. A dissociated cell carries its expression profile and loses its position, and the position has to be recovered by other means, from imaging or from the geometry of a section. The map is also a description of abundance, so a rare cell type that matters for a small vessel can be missed if the sampling did not reach it, and the number of donors per region is not the same across the organ. Cell type abundance varies with age, and the atlas reports the variation instead of correcting it away, which is the more honest choice for a reference. What the atlas does not resolve is the difference between two cells of the same type a few millimetres apart, which imaging can see and sequencing cannot.

Comparison is what a map like this is for. A laboratory that studies a vessel in a mouse can ask whether the population it is working on corresponds to a human population with the same signature, and a laboratory that studies a disease can ask which cell type in the wall has changed. Answers of that kind used to require a literature search across a dozen papers with incompatible nomenclatures, and they now require a query against one table, which is the practical difference the atlas makes. None of this makes the atlas a substitute for an experiment, and the authors say so; it is a parts list that an experiment can start from, and the parts list was missing. The next version will add the spinal cord and the choroid plexus, and with those the reference will cover the compartments that a drug has to pass. A comparison against the atlas is the first thing a laboratory does with a new marker, and the atlas is the reference that makes the comparison possible.
