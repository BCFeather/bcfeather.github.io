title: "Europe fills its gas storage to 80 per cent"
date: "2022-09-01T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [economy]
description: "Storage sites reached the winter target three months early, and the benchmark price still sat six times above its ten-year average."
---

Storage levels across the European Union passed 80 per cent on 31 August, three months before the deadline the bloc had set for itself. The target was written into a regulation adopted in June, which required 80 per cent by 1 November and 90 per cent by the start of winter. Germany, the largest consumer, reported 84 per cent, and the Netherlands was the laggard at 72 per cent.

Gazprom cut flows through Nord Stream 1 to 20 per cent of capacity in July and stopped them altogether at the end of August, citing a turbine that had not been returned from Canada. The filling season had begun badly. Russia's share of European pipeline imports fell from 40 per cent before the war to 9 per cent by the autumn. Norway and Algeria took up most of the difference, and liquefied gas from the United States supplied the rest.

Price did not follow storage. The Dutch benchmark settled near 250 euros a megawatt hour at the end of August, about six times the average of the previous decade and ten times the level of 2021. Markets were pricing the winter rather than the current balance, and the risk they were pricing was a cold February with no Russian gas at all. Traders said the storage number was a floor, not a solution.

Demand had already fallen. Industrial gas use in Germany was 20 per cent below its five-year average, and fertilizer plants across the continent had shut because gas is their feedstock. Households cut consumption by 15 per cent in the first half of the year, according to network operators. Some of that was price, and some was the effect of a mild spring. The bloc had also agreed to cut consumption by 15 per cent between August and March, with exemptions for countries that export gas to neighbours. The agreement was voluntary at first and became mandatory if the alert level rose. Member states kept the right to declare a national emergency, which several said they would use if industry needed protecting.

Filling the sites had cost money. Governments bought gas at the top of the market and put it into storage on behalf of operators, and the bills ran into tens of billions of euros. Germany created a 100 billion euro fund in the summer, and Italy and France followed with smaller versions. The economics of that purchase will be settled when the gas is sold back.

Forecasters said the winter would be decided by the weather and by how much demand could still be cut. A normal winter with full storage would leave the bloc with 10 per cent at the end of March, according to one network model. A cold one would empty the sites in February and force rationing for industry. The commission proposed a price cap on gas in the autumn, and member states argued about the design for three months. The cap that emerged in December was a trigger rather than a fixed number, and it took effect in February 2023. By then the price had fallen to 50 euros, and the mechanism was never used.
