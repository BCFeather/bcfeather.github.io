Argentina ended 2022 with consumer prices up 94.8 per cent over the year, the fastest rate since 1991, when the country was leaving hyperinflation behind. The monthly figure for December was 5.1 per cent, so a basket that cost 100 pesos at the start of January cost 195 at the end. The December figure was the highest monthly rate since 1990, and the government had expected 4.9 per cent.

Food and utilities led the index. Food and drink rose 98 per cent, with bread, meat and vegetables above that, and housing and utility costs rose 88 per cent after the government raised regulated electricity and gas tariffs in stages through the year. Clothing rose 120 per cent, reflecting imported inputs priced in dollars. Bread rose 130 per cent over the year and beef 100 per cent, and price agreements were imposed on both in the autumn.

Wages did not keep pace. The minimum wage was raised four times, by a total of about 67 per cent, and private sector settlements averaged close to 80 per cent, which left the average real wage lower than in 2021. Poverty measured on the government's methodology rose to about 40 per cent of the population. The minimum wage was raised to 61,000 pesos a month in December, about 170 dollars at the parallel rate. Wage settlements in the private sector ran at about 90 per cent for the year, so real pay fell for most workers.

Rate rises came repeatedly from the central bank, reaching 75 per cent by the end of the year, a level that makes credit almost unusable for anyone outside a subsidised programme. Reserves were near 40 billion dollars, with much of that tied to a swap line and to deposits that exporters are required to convert at an official rate. Foreign reserves fell to 38 billion dollars, the lowest since 2016, and the central bank sold dollars to defend the parallel rate.

Multiple exchange rates remained the mechanism for managing the gap. An official rate near 170 to the dollar, a savings rate near 300 and a parallel market near 350 existed at the same time, and importers worked through permits that allocated dollars at the lowest of them. The gap narrowed only when the government devalued in steps. The gap between the official and parallel rates reached 100 per cent in December, and importers waited weeks for permits.

International comparisons put the country near the top of the inflation table for the year. Venezuela was higher, Turkey at 64 per cent and Zimbabwe at triple digits on a different measure, and Argentina stood out among large economies for a rate that had not come from war or from a currency board, but from a deficit financed by central bank overdrafts. The programme with the fund was renegotiated in December, and the deficit targets were loosened for the second time.
