BlockFi filed for Chapter 11 protection on 28 November in New Jersey. The filing listed 256 million dollars of cash and more than 1 billion of liabilities, and 100,000 creditors. The company had lent to Alameda Research, the trading firm owned by the founder of FTX, and that loan was the largest asset on its books. The filing was made in the same court that handled the FTX case, and the judge was the same.

BlockFi had signed an agreement in July that gave FTX the right to buy it, and FTX had lent it 400 million dollars. The collapse of FTX on 11 November made the filing inevitable. When FTX failed, the agreement became worthless, and the loan was unpaid. The company suspended withdrawals on 10 November, a day before the bankruptcy. The company said it had 10,000 retail customers and 1,000 institutional ones.

Growth had been quick in 2021. It offered 7 per cent on deposits and lent to funds that traded crypto, and it had 10 billion dollars of assets at its peak. The model worked while prices rose, and it failed when they fell, because the collateral lost value faster than the loans could be called. The deal with FTX valued the company at 250 million dollars, down from 5 billion in 2021.

Trouble had already arrived in the summer. It had lost 200 million dollars on a loan to Three Arrows Capital, a fund that collapsed in June. The FTX deal in July was described as a rescue, and the price was 250 million dollars, which was less than the company had been valued at in 2021. The examiner's report was published in 2023, and it ran to 300 pages.

Celsius filed in July, Voyager in July, and FTX in November. The bankruptcy was one of a series. The four failures wiped out 30 billion dollars of customer deposits, and the creditors were mostly individuals. The filings showed that the lenders had used customer deposits to fund their own trading. The regulators were slow to respond. The bankruptcy code treats crypto deposits as unsecured loans, which means that customers stand behind the other creditors. The judge in the BlockFi case said the customers would be treated as unsecured creditors, and the recovery was estimated at 30 to 50 cents on the dollar.

In a statement, the founder blamed the FTX failure rather than the company's own decisions. The bankruptcy examiner, appointed in 2023, found that the company had misrepresented the risk of its loans to customers. The estate settled with the creditors in 2024 for 875 million dollars. The bankruptcy was resolved in 2024, and the customers received about 100 per cent of their deposits at the value of the day they were frozen. That outcome was better than expected, because the estate recovered the loans it had made. The collapse of FTX was the largest of the cycle, with 8 billion dollars of customer money missing.
