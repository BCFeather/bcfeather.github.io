OpenAI needs compute, and NVIDIA sells it. The two companies signed a letter of intent in September under which OpenAI will build out at least 10 gigawatts of NVIDIA systems, with the first stage arriving in the second half of 2026 on the Vera Rubin platform and later stages following as sites are energized. The announcement names the Vera Rubin platform for the first phase, the generation that follows the part now shipping and that the company has described in public.

The financial structure is what makes the arrangement unusual. NVIDIA intends to invest up to $100 billion in OpenAI, and the investment is released in tranches as each gigawatt is deployed, so the money tracks the build-out instead of arriving as a single cheque at signing, and the total is a ceiling instead of a promise. A supplier that invests in its customer on a schedule tied to delivery is taking a position in the demand it is creating. The tranche structure also makes the money conditional on construction milestones that neither company has published.

Ten gigawatts is a power figure, and power is the constraint on data centre construction at this scale: a site needs land, grid connection, turbines or long-term contracts, and cooling, long before it needs accelerators. The commitment therefore depends on utilities and on local permitting as much as on either company. A gigawatt of accelerators is roughly the size of a large campus, so ten of them amount to a construction programme with its own schedule.

The timing sits beside a second transaction. Days earlier, NVIDIA agreed to invest $5 billion in Intel, which puts the company's balance sheet behind two partners in the same quarter. Neither commitment is a purchase order, and both are best read as attempts to shape the supply chain that the company depends on. The Intel stake is the more surprising of the two, since Intel competes with NVIDIA in some segments and supplies it in none that matter at this scale.

What the letter does not contain is a schedule that binds either party. There is no disclosed financing structure, no penalty for a delay, and no statement of how the investment is valued against OpenAI's current valuation. A letter of intent is a statement of intention, and the gap between that and a contract is where these announcements usually live. Investors read such announcements as demand signals, and the shares of both companies moved on the day.

Circularity is the question analysts will ask. A vendor that funds a customer's purchases with its own equity is effectively financing its own revenue, and the arrangement works while the customer's valuation rises and the build-out proceeds. Whether it survives a slowdown in demand is the test that no announcement can settle. The alternative reading is that the arrangement prepays for capacity instead of financing revenue, and the disclosure does not settle which it is.
