Ravi Vakil, president of the American Mathematical Society, was in a meeting of the society's council when the Riemann Hypothesis claim went public on 20 November. Five and a half hours later he posted a short entry on his blog under the heading 'hurried announcement', and with it a 940-page draft by Alessandro Maria Masullo titled The Music of the Primes.

The draft claims the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, the same family of L-functions that Alpöge's Lean library covers, and Vakil flagged the point that made it news: 'It is all human, with no AI.' Masullo took a Stanford doctorate in mathematics and now works independently in Rome; the manuscript is the product of about twelve years of work, and its acknowledgements close with a line to colleagues discouraged by the pace of the field, that researchers are still here and not leaving.

Vakil published ahead of schedule, and the draft shows it. Page 522 carries a standing note asking that the statement and proof of the main theorem on zeros be inserted, which means the central argument was still being written when the file went out. Brian Conrad of Stanford, who supervised Masullo's doctorate and has been reading the draft in private for months, described it as still under major revision. He said that publishing a proof its author has not mastered, as Alpöge had done, struck him as absurd.

The two options Conrad and Vakil confronted were both uncomfortable. Release an unfinished manuscript and invite the judgement that arrives with publication, or stay silent while a machine-certified claim to the same theorem travelled around the world and fixed the terms of the conversation. They chose the first, and Vakil attached a caveat to it: he does not vouch for the strategy, and every problem raised will have to be settled before the mathematical community accepts the work.

The problems arrived quickly. Eight hours after the post, Vakil added an update saying that several researchers, working with different AI models, had reported errors, and that the manuscript was being revised. It is a strange ledger. A proof written with AI was certified by software in a day, and a proof written without it was audited by software within a night, and neither route produced a document that a person had yet read end to end.

What Masullo's manuscript offers is the thing the Lean library cannot: a narrative. A reader can follow its twelve years of accumulated lemmas, see where the argument leans on a known theorem and where it introduces something new, and argue with the author about a choice of method. Page 522 shows the cost of that form, since prose cannot be compiled and a gap in it stays invisible until a reader reaches the page. The Lean export has the opposite property: no gap survives the kernel, and no reader can find the shape of the argument inside 17 million lines without a guide.

Neither document settles the Riemann Hypothesis on its own. The Clay Mathematics Institute wants a published paper, two years of scrutiny and general agreement, and its list still has the problem as open. What the week established is that the two forms of proof now arrive together, and that each one covers for the weakness of the other. Masullo's draft explains and cannot be trusted; Alpöge's library can be trusted and does not explain. The field has been handed both, with the same theorem in each, and the work of making them agree is only starting.
