President Biden spoke to President Xi Jinping by video link on 18 March for nearly two hours, and the message that the White House put out afterwards was unusually specific. The president said that China would face consequences if it provided material support to the Russian campaign, and he listed the kinds of support that the administration was watching: weapons, spare parts, and help with the evasion of sanctions. He also said the United States would respond if China mainland attacked Taiwan Island, a sentence that the Chinese readout did not mention.

Beijing's account of the call was different in emphasis. The foreign ministry said President Xi had told his counterpart that the two countries should respect each other's core interests, that sanctions solved nothing, and that the war in Ukraine showed the danger of a world organized around blocs. It repeated the position that China had taken at the United Nations, where it had abstained on the resolution that condemned the invasion, and it said the conversation had been candid and constructive.

The context of the call had changed since the two leaders met in November. In February, on the opening day of the Winter Olympics, they had issued a statement describing their partnership as one with no limits, and the Chinese side had denied any knowledge of the Russian plan. That denial, and the abstention in the Security Council, left Washington uncertain about how far the relationship with Moscow extended, and the call was an attempt to find out.

Trade was discussed, and it produced nothing. The tariffs that the previous administration had imposed were still in place, the two sides disagreed about the conditions under which they could be lifted, and the visit that had been expected from the speaker of the House to Taiwan was raised as a risk. Officials said afterwards that the tone had been firm and that no agreement had been reached on any of the subjects.

The warning about consequences had no deadline attached. American officials said the administration had evidence that Russia had asked China for equipment, and that the flows that mattered consisted of the components that go into weapons, the machine tools and the semiconductors that the sanctions had restricted, rather than of finished weapons themselves. A campaign of secondary sanctions that targeted Chinese banks or companies would be a step that Washington had avoided for three decades, and the call announced the possibility without describing the instrument.

What the conversation fixed was the shape of the relationship for the year. Two leaders who had called each other friends in 2021 spoke for two hours and produced a warning, a denial and a set of talking points, and the channel that remained was the one between their staffs. The material support that Washington feared did not become visible in the months that followed, and the trade between the two countries rose to a record, which is a fact that the readouts of both governments left out and that the customs statistics published in the autumn made impossible to ignore.
