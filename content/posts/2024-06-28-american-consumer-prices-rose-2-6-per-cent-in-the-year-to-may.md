Personal consumption expenditure inflation was 2.6 per cent in the year to May, down from 2.7 per cent, and the core measure that excludes food and energy held at 2.5 per cent, according to the report published on 28 June. The figure was in line with the forecast, and the detail that the analysts noted was the revision to the previous months, which lowered the path by a tenth. The report is the last before the committee's July meeting, and it gives the members a set of numbers that are consistent with a cut in the autumn.

Direction is what the committee asked for. The core measure has fallen in seven of the last eight months, and the three-month annualized rate is now below two per cent, which is the number that the staff uses when it judges whether the trend has changed. A trend that is below the target is a trend that justifies a cut. The shelter component is still the largest contributor, and the rents in the index are falling at about half the pace that the market rents fell a year ago.

Composition has shifted. Goods prices have fallen for six months, and the prices of services rose more slowly as the rents cooled, which is the pattern that the committee described as necessary for the last mile. A service price that decelerates is a wage that has stopped accelerating. The goods prices are falling because the retailers are discounting and because the freight costs have returned to their pre-pandemic level.

Households are spending at a moderate pace. The report showed that spending rose slightly on the month and that the saving rate was unchanged, which is the combination of a household that is neither confident nor retrenching. A saving rate that is flat is a consumer that is using the income rather than the savings. The income report in the same release showed wages and salaries rising, which is the reason the spending has held up while the confidence has fallen.

Market moved modestly. Yields fell by a few basis points and the probability of a September cut rose above two thirds, which is the reaction of a market that had expected the number. A report that confirms is a report that changes little. The committee meets in July, and the market expects a hold with a signal for September, and the report supports that reading. The saving rate was 3.9 per cent, which is higher than before the pandemic and lower than the average of the last decade.

What the month shows is that the disinflation is now broad enough to be seen in the measure the committee targets, and the question is whether the committee will act before the labour market gives it a second reason. A measure that is at the target on a three-month basis is a measure that the committee can defend cutting against, and the defence is the one it will have to make in public.
