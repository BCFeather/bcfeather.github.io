A quarter that begins with an import panic and ends with a 3 per cent growth rate is a quarter in which the arithmetic of trade did most of the work, and the advance estimate published on 30 July says exactly that. Output rose at a 3 per cent annual rate after a contraction of 0.5 per cent in the first three months, and the swing is larger than the underlying demand of either quarter.

Imports explain the reversal. Firms front-loaded purchases in the first quarter to beat the duties, which subtracted from measured output, and they drew down those inventories in the second, which added to it, so the same goods were counted twice in a way that flatters one quarter and punishes the other. A statistical artefact of that size is a reason to read the two quarters together, because the average of the two is about 1.2 per cent and that average is the number that describes what firms actually did with their inventories.

Consumer spending grew at about 1.4 per cent, slower than last year and slower than the first quarter, and services carried it while goods spending fell back from the March rush. Households are still spending more than they did a year ago and less than they did in the autumn, which is the shape of an economy that is decelerating gently and not one that is turning, and which shows up in the retail categories that sell experiences rather than goods.

Business investment was mixed. Spending on equipment rose, led by computing hardware and the electrical gear that data centres need, and spending on structures fell as some projects waited for tariff clarity, which is what a firm does when the cost of steel is a policy variable and not a market one, and which delays the projects that would have added to construction in the second half.

Housing was the weak line. Residential investment fell for a fourth quarter, with high mortgage rates and a slow turnover of existing homes keeping builders cautious, and the sector is now small enough that its weakness subtracts little from the total and visible enough that it shapes how the quarter feels to anyone who is trying to sell a house in a market where the buyer is waiting for a rate that has not arrived.

Prices in the report were firmer than the growth rate implies. The measure of domestic demand inflation rose at a pace above the target, which is the number the committee will read twice, because a quarter that grows on inventory arithmetic and inflates on services is a quarter that does not settle the argument.

What would settle it is whether the third quarter shows consumption holding near 2 per cent while imports stay low. A third quarter that repeats the second is a soft landing with a trade distortion in it, and a third quarter that reverses again is an economy that has been measuring the timing of containers and calling it a cycle.
