The scale sits under the bin by the tray return, and the screen hangs above it at eye level. When a tray is cleared, the weight of what is left on it is added to the day's total, and the total is displayed to everyone still eating. That is the whole intervention. There is no campaign, no pledge, no poster of a farmer in a rice field. A university canteen in the eastern part of the city installed the equipment in March, and by June the weight of plate waste per meal had fallen by roughly a third.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Most people do not set out to throw food away; they leave it because the portion was larger than they wanted, or because the dish was worse than it looked, or because they took a little of everything at a buffet and finished none of it. Feedback does not change any of those causes directly. What it changes is attention. A number that moves when you do something is the most reliable behavioural lever we have, and it works on institutions as well as individuals: the kitchen staff began watching the totals per dish, and the dishes that consistently came back half-eaten were the ones they changed.

The canteen's manager is careful about the claim. The reduction is measured against a baseline taken in the spring, and the spring menu is not the autumn menu, so some of the difference comes from factors the scale cannot see. The kitchen also changed portion sizes for two staples in April, which is an intervention the screen merely made obvious. Still, the direction is consistent across three months, and the waste bill, which the university pays by weight, fell by enough to be noticed by the finance office.

There is a design lesson in where the screen is placed. It faces the queue for the tray return, not the tables, so the number is seen by the person about to add to it. It updates within a second, which matters more than the absolute figure: a delayed number is a statistic, an immediate one is a response. And it shows a total rather than an individual weight, which avoids the shame of a personal score while keeping the social pressure of a shared one. None of these choices is accidental, and all of them are cheap.

The limits are worth naming. Feedback works on a population that has alternatives, and in a canteen the alternatives are limited: you can take less, but you cannot take something else. In a hospital or a school where the menu is fixed, the lever moves to the kitchen instead of the diner, and the scale becomes an instrument of procurement rather than behaviour. And the effect tends to decay. The literature on feedback interventions is full of results that fade after a few months, when the number becomes furniture.

For now the number is still moving, and the canteen has started publishing it weekly, which is the point at which a measurement becomes a policy. It is a modest thing: one scale, one screen, one unpleasant figure. But institutions are mostly made of such things, and the ones that change are usually the ones that found a way to see themselves.
