Ron DeSantis ended his presidential campaign on the afternoon of the twenty-first of January, in a video posted on social media, two days before the New Hampshire primary that he had been expected to lose badly. In the video he said the campaign had no clear path to the nomination, that he had signed a pledge to support the party's nominee, and that he was endorsing Donald Trump. The endorsement was delivered in the flat tone of a man closing an account. A campaign that had been launched eighteen months earlier as the most credible alternative to Trump ended on a Sunday afternoon, before a single primary vote had been cast.

The numbers explain the decision. DeSantis finished second in Iowa with 21.2 per cent against Trump's 51.1, after betting everything on the state, visiting all ninety-nine counties and moving most of his money and staff there. In New Hampshire he was polling in single digits, behind both Trump and Nikki Haley, in an electorate that is more moderate and less religious than Iowa's. His super PAC had raised and spent in the region of a hundred and fifty million dollars, much of it before he had formally declared, and the money bought an organization that could not convert itself into votes. When the last polls of the weekend showed no movement, the decision wrote itself.

The strategy is worth examining, because it was the most deliberate attempt anyone has made to beat Trump from the right. DeSantis ran as a governor who had turned Florida into a laboratory of conservative policy, on the argument that the party wanted someone who would fight and win rather than someone who would complain. He signed a six-week abortion ban, restricted what schools could teach about sexuality and gender, fought the Walt Disney Company over its special district, and sent planeloads of migrants to Martha's Vineyard. Each of those moves was popular with the primary electorate in theory and, it turned out, did not make him likable. The candidate who emerged from the ads was stiff in public, awkward with reporters, and unable to explain why the fight was worth having.

He also had the misfortune to be running against an incumbent in all but name. Trump's legal troubles, four indictments and a trial calendar, did not damage him with Republican voters, and each appearance in a courtroom was covered as a campaign event. The party's institutional apparatus had largely stopped resisting, and the donors who might have funded a challenger had watched 2016 and drawn conclusions. DeSantis's second place in Iowa was the best result any of them achieved, and it was not close.

Nikki Haley is now the only alternative standing, and New Hampshire on the twenty-third will decide how long she remains one. Her argument is that she can win the suburban voters and independents who decide general elections, and that the party should not throw those away. The polling in New Hampshire is closer than anywhere else, and a defeat by a wide margin would end her campaign as surely as Iowa ended his. What the party is discovering, in the space of eight days, is that the anti-Trump vote is large but divided, and that the divisions are easier to exploit than to heal. Two candidates have now tried, from different directions, and one of them has already gone home.
