Hearing loss has been on the list of modifiable risk factors for dementia for a few years. A pooled analysis published in *The Lancet Healthy Longevity* on 13 October adds a harder endpoint. Investigators combined individual participant data from population-based studies in Australia, Iceland and the United States, then replicated the result in a Norwegian cohort, and found that mortality rises with the severity of hearing loss, a gradient that survived the adjustments the investigators could make.

Design is the strength of the paper. Instead of pooling published estimates, the authors went back to the individual records, which lets them align the definition of hearing loss, adjust for the same covariates and treat age consistently across studies. That matters in a field where cohorts differ in how they measure hearing, in whom they enrol and in how long they follow people. Audiometry is not available everywhere, and studies frequently substitute a question about difficulty hearing in noise for a measured threshold.

Consistency after adjustment, and again in the replication cohort, is the check that separates a finding from a coincidence in one dataset. The association runs in the expected direction: mild loss carries less excess risk than moderate loss, and moderate less than severe, so the relationship behaves like a dose rather than a switch.

Hearing-aid use came out associated with lower mortality. That is the sentence the press office will quote, and it needs the caveats that come with any observational result about a device. People who wear hearing aids differ from people who do not in income, education, social contact and general engagement with health care, and a pooled cohort can adjust for some of these but not for all of them. A patient who buys an aid has already decided that hearing matters, and that decision is the kind of variable a cohort study records only indirectly.

Plausible mechanisms are social and clinical at once. Untreated hearing loss isolates people, and isolation is associated with worse outcomes across a range of conditions. Hearing loss also changes how a person accesses care: a patient who cannot follow a consultation misses instructions, and a patient who cannot hear a smoke alarm or a car horn is exposed to risks that have nothing to do with the ear.

What the analysis cannot answer is whether treating hearing loss changes mortality, because the device was not randomized. The trial that would answer it is feasible and has not been run, and until it is, the sensible reading is that hearing loss joins the list of conditions worth treating for their own sake, with mortality as a reason to take the referral seriously rather than a promise to make to a patient. Screening is cheap, referral is not, and the paper gives the referral a justification that is larger than speech intelligibility alone. What a health service does with that is a budget question, not a scientific one.
