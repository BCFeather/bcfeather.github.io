title: "Substation fire shuts Heathrow for a day"
date: "2025-03-21T09:20:00+08:00"

description: "A fire at an electricity substation in west London closed Heathrow for the whole of 21 March, cancelling more than a thousand flights and stranding passengers across the world, and the airport reopened late that evening."
---

Fire started at about eleven on the night of 20 March at a National Grid substation in North Hyde, a facility that supplies a local substation which in turn feeds the airport. Three supergrid transformers stood at the site, and two were destroyed or damaged beyond repair, while the third lost its connection because it shared a circuit with one of the others. Flames and black smoke were visible for miles, and about 150 people were evacuated from the surrounding streets.

Power to the airport failed for long enough that the backup systems could not carry the load. Heathrow closed about ninety minutes after the outage began and said it would stay shut until 23:59 on 21 March, and it told passengers not to travel to the airport. The closure of a hub is different from the closure of an airport, because a hub is where the long-haul fleet is based and where the crews and the connecting passengers are.

Numbers put the disruption in scale. The airport had been due to handle 1,351 flights on the Friday, carrying up to 291,000 passengers, and it handled almost none of them. About 120 flights were diverted to Gatwick, to Charles de Gaulle and to other airports in Britain and Europe, and many long-haul services turned around and returned to where they started. Airlines cancelled the rest, and the crews and aircraft ended up in the wrong places for the following week.

The recovery began in the evening. A limited number of flights operated late on Friday, mostly to move aircraft and crews into position, and the airport said that a full schedule would follow on the Saturday with some cancellations. Airlines warned that the effects would last for days, because an aircraft that is in the wrong city takes a day to reposition and a crew that has run out of duty hours takes a rest.

Cause is the part that regulators will examine. The substation was an old facility that supplied a critical load through a single path, and the airport's own backup generation was not able to island the site. The review that followed, published later in the year, found that the configuration had been known and that the resilience of a nationally critical piece of infrastructure had not been assessed against the failure of a single substation.

Political reaction was quick. The energy secretary called the incident a warning about the state of the grid, and the opposition pointed out that the investment in transmission had lagged behind the growth of demand from data centres and electrified heating. The airport's chief executive said that the lesson was about redundancy at the substation rather than about the airport.

What the episode demonstrated is the coupling that modern infrastructure carries. A transformer that failed in a field outside the perimeter took down a hub that handles 80 million passengers a year, and no amount of investment inside the terminals would have prevented it. The fix is a second supply path, and the question that the review left open is who pays for it, since the airport is a private company and the substation is a public one.
