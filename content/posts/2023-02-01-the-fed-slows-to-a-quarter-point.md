At two o'clock on 1 February the Federal Reserve raised its target range for the federal funds rate by a quarter point, to between 4.5 and 4.75 per cent. Six consecutive meetings had produced increases of three quarters of a point or half a point, and this one produced the smallest step since the campaign began in March 2022. The statement kept the phrase about ongoing increases, which the market read as a promise of at least one more. Officials repeated that they would take account of the lag with which policy works its way through the economy.

The reasoning was in the gap between two numbers. Headline inflation had fallen from 9.1 per cent in June to 6.5 per cent in December, and the monthly rate had been flat or negative in the two months before the meeting. Unemployment stood at 3.4 per cent, the lowest in more than fifty years, and payrolls were still growing by hundreds of thousands a month. A committee that had spent a year fighting an overheating economy was now arguing with itself about how much tightening was already in the pipeline.

Powell refused the invitation to declare a pause, and refused it in a way that sounded deliberate. He said that disinflation had begun in goods and had yet to begin in services, and that the labour market remained extremely tight by any measure he was willing to name. He also said that he would rather err on the side of doing too much than too little, a sentence that had been used before and that investors had stopped taking at face value.

Markets moved as if the peak were near. The two-year yield fell below 4.1 per cent, the dollar weakened against the euro, and the S&P 500 rose by about a per cent on the day. Futures priced a peak just above 5 per cent and a cut before the end of the year, which was a forecast the committee had explicitly rejected in December. The gap between what the Fed said and what the market heard had become the most reliable feature of the cycle.

Politics around the decision was quieter than the economics. Republicans on the banking committee argued that the Fed had started late and should keep going, and Democrats argued that the shelter component of the index was still measuring rents that had already peaked. Neither argument changed the vote, which was unanimous, and neither was addressed to the committee rather than to the gallery. Its own projections from December had assumed a mild recession in 2023, and the payroll data kept refusing to deliver one.

What the quarter point changed was the calendar rather than the destination. It put the next decision on 22 March, when the committee would meet eleven days after a bank in California had failed for reasons that had nothing to do with inflation. A tightening cycle that had been about prices would become a cycle about financial stability, and the February statement reads now as the last one written in the old frame.
