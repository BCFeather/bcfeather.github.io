Membrane fusion is a deformation before it is a joining, and the shapes that two vesicles adopt on the way to becoming one have been hard to watch. A paper in *eLife* on 23 October 2024 develops a preparation in which individual fusion events can be followed, and finds that the events fall into two modes. Large vesicles are a gift to the observer, since a structure that fills the field of view can be followed without super-resolution optics.

The cells are mouse yolk sac visceral endoderm, chosen because their endocytic vesicles are unusually large and therefore resolvable with ordinary optics. Size as a determinant is what the modelling contributes, and the fluctuation term is what allows a small vesicle to fuse at all.

Homotypic fusion, in which two late endosomes join, is fast. Heterotypic fusion, in which a late endosome joins a lysosome, is slow, and the difference is not a matter of size alone, since the modelling shows that size is a determinant and that membrane fluctuations can overcome it. Actin at the apposition site has been seen before in other systems, and the two-mode account is what makes the observation quantitative.

Actin is the switch. Filaments present at the apposition site are associated with one mode and absent from the other, and perturbing actin moves the balance between them rather than abolishing fusion. The distinction between sharing contents and being consumed is functional, and the mechanics now explain why the two should differ.

The result gives a physical account of a distinction that cell biologists have described for years. Two vesicles of the same kind merge to share contents, and a vesicle merges with a degradative organelle to be consumed, and the mechanics of the two events differ accordingly.
