For seven decades the A-15 superconductors meant metallic alloys of the form \(\mathrm{A}_3\mathrm{B}\), with a transition metal on one sublattice and a group III or IV element on the other, and the family produced the record: niobium germanium reached 23 kelvins, and niobium tin became the workhorse of every superconducting magnet built since.

A new member arrived in 2023, when a lanthanum hydride phase, \(\mathrm{La}_4\mathrm{H}_{23}\), was reported to superconduct at 81 kelvins under 118 gigapascals, a transition temperature far below the 250 kelvins of \(\mathrm{LaH}_{10}\) at 200 gigapascals and yet the highest recorded inside the A-15 sublattice. The result was subsequently confirmed by an independent group, which matters because the phase is made at pressure and recovered from a handful of diffraction peaks.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 29 February 2024 asks what kind of superconductor that is. The authors analyse the published experimental data rather than taking new measurements, and extract the quantities that a description of the pairing needs: grain size, crystalline strain, the electron-phonon coupling constant and the ratio of the Debye temperature to the Fermi temperature.

The numbers are unusual in combination. The material is nanograined, with crystallites between 5.5 and 35 nanometres across, the crystalline strain stays below 0.003, and the coupling constant lies between 1.5 and 2.55, which is strong even by the standards of the hydrides and well above the weak-coupling value that BCS theory assumes. The confirmation came from a different laboratory with a different pressure cell.

Nonadiabaticity comes out moderate. The ratio of the Debye temperature to the Fermi temperature and the ratio of the transition temperature to the Fermi temperature both land near the values derived for the cuprates, the iron pnictides and the near-room-temperature hydrides \(\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{S}\) and \(\mathrm{LaH}_{10}\), which places \(\mathrm{La}_4\mathrm{H}_{23}\), on the authors' reading, in the unconventional band of the Uemura plot rather than among the conventional alloys. That placement is the claim worth arguing with, since the Uemura plot relates the transition temperature to the superfluid density and separates families by the slope of their members.

The limits follow from the method. Grain size and strain come from line widths and peak shapes, the coupling constant is extracted from the transition temperature with a model for the phonon spectrum, and each step carries an assumption that a direct measurement would test; the range 1.5 to 2.55 is wide enough to hold very different pictures of the pairing.

What would settle it is a measurement of the superfluid density, through penetration depth or through a magnetic response, on the same samples that show the 81 kelvin transition. That would put the material on the Uemura plot directly, and it would also say whether the nanograined structure is intrinsic to the phase or a consequence of how the samples were made.
