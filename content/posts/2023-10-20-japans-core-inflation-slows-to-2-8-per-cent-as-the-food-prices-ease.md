Japanese consumer prices excluding fresh food rose 2.8 per cent in the year to September, the statistics bureau said on 20 October, down from 3.1 per cent in August. The measure excluding the energy as well was 4.2 per cent. The figure was in line with the forecast, and the headline measure including the fresh food was 3 per cent, the lowest since the spring of 2022.

Energy subsidies keep the headline low. The government has been paying part of the household bills since January, and the support is due to expire at the end of the year, which is the reason the economists expect the measure to rise in the spring. A subsidy that ends is an inflation that returns. The government has spent about 2 trillion yen on the support this year, and the finance ministry has said it will decide on the extension in December. The finance ministry has said the support will be extended in some form, and the budget for next year assumes about 1 trillion yen for the measure.

Food is slowing. The food prices rose by about 7.5 per cent, down from 8.7, and the vegetables and the meat came down as the supply recovered, which is the line that the households watch most closely. A grocery bill that stops rising is a mood that improves. The rice prices rose by about 9 per cent after the poor harvest, and the government has released about a fifth of its stock to the market.

Services are firm. The services rose by about 2.1 per cent, and the hotels and the restaurants were the strongest as the foreign visitors returned to about 85 per cent of their level of 2019. A visitor who pays the room rate is a price that the weak yen has brought. The number of the foreign visitors in September was about 2.2 million, and the spending per visitor has risen as the yen has weakened.

Wages are the next question. The base pay rose by about 2.5 per cent in the summer, and the unions have said they will ask for about 5 per cent in the spring talks. A wage that is agreed is a service price that follows. The unemployment rate is 2.6 per cent, and the ratio of the jobs to the applicants is about 1.3, which is the tightest in decades.

What the report shows is that the inflation has fallen to the target on the energy and the food and is above it on the services, and the services are the part the bank has said it wants to see. A core at 2.8 per cent is a core that the bank can call a success, and the success is what the exit has been waiting for. The bank meets at the end of the month, and the economists expect it to keep the policy while it waits for the wage data.
