Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia is a disease in which the immune system makes antibodies against the patient's own red cells, and the standard treatment is steroids followed by rituximab and then by immunosuppressants that suppress everything. Patients who fail all of it are left with recurrent transfusions and a life that contracts around hospital visits. The antibody-producing B cells are the source, and rituximab depletes only a subset of them, which is why relapses are common.

CD19-directed CAR T cells deplete B cells more completely, because the CD19 marker appears on plasmablasts and on the earlier B cells that rituximab misses. Eleven patients with primary multirefractory autoimmune haemolytic anaemia received a single infusion of autologous CD19 CAR T cells, five through a compassionate-use programme and six in a phase 1 study. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 14 January, with a median follow-up of 12.2 months.

Every patient had a complete response, defined as resolution of symptoms, a rise in haemoglobin and normalization of haemolysis markers. The median time to a complete response was 45 days, with a range from 21 to 153, and the median duration of drug-free remission was 11.5 months with a range from 6.8 to 21. The word drug-free is the important one, since these patients had been on continuous immunosuppression for years.

The safety picture was the familiar CAR T picture. Cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity are the expected complications, and the paper reports their incidence and severity across a small cohort in which one severe event would change the assessment. Eleven patients is enough to establish that the approach can work and not enough to establish how often it fails badly.

Relapse analysis is the paper's technical strength. The authors used flow cytometry, single-cell RNA sequencing and paired B cell receptor sequencing to ask where the returning B cells came from, and the answer distinguishes persistence of the original clone from the emergence of a new one. That distinction determines whether a second infusion would help, and it is the kind of question that separates a case series from a mechanistic study.

Cost and logistics limit the reach. CAR T therapy requires apheresis, a manufacturing wait of weeks and a centre capable of managing cytokine release, and autoimmune patients are not oncology patients with an established pathway into those beds. A disease that affects perhaps a few thousand people a year worldwide in its refractory form can support a handful of centres and not a broad rollout.

The wider significance is the direction of travel. CAR T against CD19 has now produced drug-free remissions in lupus, in myositis and in this anaemia, and the pattern suggests that the target is the B cell compartment rather than any single disease. Whether the remissions last beyond two years is the question that will decide if this is a treatment or a cure.
