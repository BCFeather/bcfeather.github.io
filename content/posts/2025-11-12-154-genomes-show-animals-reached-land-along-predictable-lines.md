Eleven independent moves from water to land look, at first glance, like eleven separate stories. Arthropods, vertebrates, rotifers, molluscs, annelids, nematodes, tardigrades and others each faced the same problem: how to keep water, handle a drier air, detoxify what a new diet contained, and sense a brighter world. Whether those solutions were found by chance or whether land imposes a set of answers that evolution keeps rediscovering has been hard to test, because no study has compared the genomes behind enough of those moves at once.

Jialin Wei, Jordi Paps and colleagues at the University of Bristol, with collaborators in Italy, report the comparison in *Nature*, 12 November 2025. Their dataset holds 154 genomes from 21 animal phyla and their outgroups. From those, they reconstructed the protein-coding content of the ancestral genomes associated with 11 terrestrialization events and placed the events on a timeline. The dataset is openly available, which matters because the claim rests on a reconstruction, which no repeated measurement can confirm directly.

The reconstruction yields distinct patterns of gene gain and loss for each lineage, and that is the contingency part of the answer. But the same biological functions recur: water retention and ion transport, stress responses and detoxification, immune system adjustments, sensory and neural expansion, and changes tied to metabolism and reproduction. Across the animal kingdom, adaptation to land looks predictable in broad outline, even when the genes doing the job are not the same ones.

Semi-terrestrial and fully terrestrial species answered the problem differently. The former show convergent functional patterns, while the latter followed more varied paths. Rotifers, nematodes and tardigrades, which need moisture even when living out of water, rely heavily on water-retention and cuticle functions that appear again and again. Insects, spiders and land vertebrates, which severed the need for standing water, used a wider set of routes, including changes in metabolism, sensory systems and reproductive strategy. That distinction is the study's most useful observation, because it suggests that a half-step onto land is solved repeatedly in a small number of ways, whereas a complete break with water leaves more room for lineage-specific solutions. The timeline identifies three temporal windows for animal colonization of land over the past 487 million years, each associated with particular ecological contexts.

Reconstruction is not observation. Ancestral genomes are inferred from modern sequences, and different models of gene gain, loss and phylogenetic relationships can move the boundaries of the reconstructed events. The 154 genomes are unevenly distributed across phyla, and some of the 11 events are represented by few species. Nor does a functional convergence prove that the genes in question caused the move to land: correlation between a gene family's expansion and a habitat is not the same as a demonstrated role in surviving on it.

What the study does not claim is that the same genes were recruited each time. Gene duplication and loss patterns differ from one lineage to the next, so the commonality is functional rather than sequence-level. Aquaporins and cytochrome P450 families are named in the report as examples of genes that recur, but recurrence across a dataset of this size is not proof of an indispensable role: a gene may expand for reasons unrelated to terrestrial life and merely correlate with the habitat.

A useful next step would compare the reconstructed gene sets with functional evidence from living semi-terrestrial species, instead of relying on reconstruction alone. If the same functions are found to be active during freshwater-to-land transitions in real time, and if their removal impairs survival out of water, the claim that land selects for a predictable genomic answer becomes substantially stronger than it is from comparative genomics by itself.
