description: "A three-month trial in 234 children and adolescents cut monthly migraine days by 2.5 against 1.4 on placebo, with 47.2 per cent of treated children achieving at least a halving of days."
---

Migraine prevention in adults has been transformed by antibodies against calcitonin gene-related peptide, and paediatric neurology has watched from a distance. Children with episodic migraine have few approved preventive options, and the drugs that exist were borrowed from adult practice with little paediatric evidence, which leaves clinicians prescribing on the basis of small open-label studies and their own experience. A properly powered trial in this population was overdue.

Fremanezumab is a monoclonal antibody against the calcitonin gene-related peptide ligand, given as a monthly subcutaneous injection. The trial randomised 237 participants aged 6 to 17 with episodic migraine, defined as migraine for at least six months and no more than fourteen headache days a month, to fremanezumab or placebo for three months. Dosing was 120 milligrams for children under 45 kilograms and 225 milligrams for those above, and 234 participants were included in the full analysis. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 14 January.

The effect was real and modest. Monthly migraine days fell by 2.5 on fremanezumab against 1.4 on placebo, a difference of 1.1 days, and days with headache of at least moderate severity fell by 2.6 against 1.5. The proportion of participants whose migraine days halved was 47.2 per cent against 27.0 per cent, and that responder measure is the one that matters to a family deciding whether an injection is worth it.

Tolerability was benign. Injection-site erythema occurred in 9.8 per cent of participants on the drug against 5.4 per cent on placebo, and no other adverse event distinguished the groups in a way the paper emphasises. A preventive treatment that a child will accept is a large part of the battle, and the absence of sedation and of the weight gain associated with older preventives is a genuine advantage.

A three-month trial cannot speak to durability or to the question that parents ask, which is whether a child who starts an antibody at twelve will still need it at twenty. Migraine in adolescence often remits, and the natural history means that some of the improvement attributed to the drug would have happened anyway. The placebo response of 1.4 days a month is a measure of that natural history and of the attention a trial provides.

Difference of 1.1 days is small in absolute terms and meaningful in a disease measured in days lost. Regulators and payers will weigh it differently, and the paper is careful to describe the result as a reduction rather than as a remission. For a child missing four days of school a month, a reduction of one is a real change and not a transformation.

The trial also establishes a dose and a safety database for a population that had neither, which is the unglamorous work that makes a drug usable. Longer follow-up is required, as the authors state, and the field will want to know whether efficacy persists beyond the blinded period and whether growth and development are unaffected.
