American aircraft struck more than eighty-five targets in Iraq and Syria on the second of February, in the first wave of retaliation for the drone attack on a base in Jordan that killed three soldiers and wounded dozens more five days earlier. Central Command said the targets included command centres, intelligence sites, rocket and drone storage, and logistics positions used by the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and the militias it supports. B-1 bombers flying from the United States took part, along with aircraft based in the region, and the command said more than a hundred and twenty-five precision munitions were used. The strikes lasted about thirty minutes and were spread across seven locations.

The attack they were answering had taken place on the twenty-eighth of January at Tower 22, a small logistics outpost in north-eastern Jordan near the Syrian border, where American troops have been stationed for years. A one-way drone hit the living quarters at night, killing three soldiers and injuring more than forty. It was the first attack since the war in Gaza began in which American service members were killed, and it turned an argument about deterrence into an argument about what a proportionate answer should look like. The administration spent five days choosing targets, and said publicly that it had decided not to strike inside Iran.

What the strikes did and did not do is the subject of the argument that followed. They hit the militia infrastructure in the places where it is stored and managed, and they hit it in a way that the Iranian government could not ignore and did not have to answer with its own forces. No American aircraft struck targets in Iran, and no Iranian official was killed, which was the point. The militias fired back within days, at bases in Syria and at shipping in the Red Sea, and the exchange settled into a pattern in which each side calibrates its response to the other's last one.

The politics in Washington were about scale. Republican members of Congress had spent the week demanding a direct strike on Iran, and some of them described the operation as too limited. Democratic critics asked whether the strikes would deter anything, given that the militias had been attacked repeatedly since October without stopping. The administration's answer was that a wider war was the outcome it was trying to avoid, and that the purpose of the operation was to restore a deterrent that had been damaged rather than to punish.

For Iraq, the strikes reopened a question that had been closed for a year. The government in Baghdad had been negotiating a timetable for the withdrawal of American troops, and the attacks on bases hosting them were used by the militias as evidence that the presence was a liability. Iraqi officials condemned the American operation and said it violated sovereignty, while also saying that they did not want the country used as a battlefield between Washington and Tehran. That is the position every Iraqi government has taken since 2003, and it has never been enough to stop any of the parties.
