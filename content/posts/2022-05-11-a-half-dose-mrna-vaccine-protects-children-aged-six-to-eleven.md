description: "Two 50-microgram doses in 4,016 children produced neutralizing titres of 1,610, above the 1,300 seen in young adults given 100 micrograms, and an efficacy of 88.0 per cent against Delta with no myocarditis reported."
---

Children were the last group to be vaccinated, and the argument for a reduced dose rests on the principle that an immune response scales with the dose until it saturates while reactogenicity keeps rising. The trial that established the dose for 6- to 11-year-olds was run in two parts for that reason.

Part 1 compared 50 and 100 micrograms in 751 children and selected the lower dose on safety and immunogenicity. Part 2 randomized 4,016 children in a 3:1 ratio to two 50-microgram doses 28 days apart or placebo, with a median follow-up of 82 days. The paper appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 11 May 2022. A trial that selects its dose in one part and tests it in another is the design that regulators asked for, because the dose for a child cannot be extrapolated from an adult by weight alone.

Neutralizing antibody titres one month after the second dose were 1,610 in the children given 50 micrograms and 1,300 in young adults given 100 micrograms, with seroconversion in at least 99.0 per cent of both groups. The comparison met the prespecified noninferiority criterion, which was the primary immunogenicity objective.

Efficacy against COVID from 14 days after the first dose was 88.0 per cent, with an interval from 70.0 to 95.8, during a period when Delta was dominant. The estimate comes from an interim analysis with a limited number of events, and the interval reflects that. An efficacy estimate of 88 per cent against a variant that was dominant during the trial is the number that a regulator uses, and the interval around it is the honest statement of how much the number could move.

Adverse events were mainly low grade and transient, with injection-site pain, headache and fatigue the commonest. No vaccine-related serious adverse events, multisystem inflammatory syndrome, myocarditis or pericarditis were reported as of the data cutoff, and the follow-up is short enough that the absence is not a demonstration of safety.

Myocarditis after mRNA vaccination is a rare event that clusters in adolescent boys after the second dose, and a trial of 4,016 children has no power to exclude it. The passive surveillance systems that detected the signal in older age groups remain the instrument for this question. A rare adverse event is exactly the kind of risk that a parent cannot have excluded by a trial, and the surveillance that follows authorization is the only instrument that can measure it.

A parent deciding whether to vaccinate a healthy 8-year-old is weighing a small absolute benefit against a small absolute risk, and the paper supplies the immunogenicity and efficacy numbers that make that conversation concrete. It does not supply the safety data that a longer follow-up would. A health system that vaccinates children to reduce transmission is buying a smaller benefit than one that vaccinates them to prevent severe disease, and the case for the policy depends on which of those goals is being pursued.
