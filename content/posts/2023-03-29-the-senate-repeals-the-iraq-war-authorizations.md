title: "The Senate repeals the Iraq war authorizations"
date: "2023-03-29T23:50:00+08:00"
categories: [news]
description: "The Senate voted 66 to 30 on 29 March 2023 to repeal the 1991 and 2002 authorizations for the use of military force against Iraq, the first time in more than five decades that Congress had voted to end such an authorization."
---

The vote was sixty-six to thirty, which is a margin that the Senate had not produced on a war powers question in twenty years. The bill repealed the authorization passed in 1991 before the Gulf war and the one passed in 2002 before the invasion, and it did so without replacing them. Twenty Democrats and ten Republicans had sponsored or supported it, and the two sponsors were an independent-minded Republican from Indiana and a Democrat from Virginia. The bill had been introduced in January and reported out of committee in February, and the floor debate took an afternoon.

Constitutional rather than strategic was the argument for repeal, and the members made it in the language of the founding document. The 2002 authorization had been cited by presidents of both parties to justify operations that Congress had never debated, and the 1991 one had been obsolete for three decades. Members said the vote was about restoring the power to declare war to the branch that the constitution gives it to, and they said so in a debate that lasted most of the afternoon.

Against repeal the argument was about signal and about precedent. Some senators said the repeal would encourage America's adversaries to believe that the country had lost its appetite for the fight, and others said that the authorizations might still be needed for operations that had not been named. The administration had supported the repeal in principle while asking that it be paired with a narrower authorization, and the pairing did not happen.

In the House the bill had not moved, and its future was therefore uncertain. A similar repeal had passed the House in 2021 and had died in the Senate, and this year the chambers had changed hands, which meant that the Senate's vote was a statement rather than a law. The sponsors said they would keep introducing the bill until it passed both chambers, which happened in June when the House took it up. The leadership said it would bring the measure to the floor before the recess, and the recess was four months away.

The timing was set by an anniversary rather than by a schedule. The vote came a week after the twentieth anniversary of the invasion, and the debate referred to it repeatedly, and the members who had voted for the war in 2002 were invited to explain themselves. Some of them did, and their explanations were about the intelligence that had turned out to be wrong, and the explanations were accepted as honest by members who had voted the other way.

What the vote changed was the legal basis for a set of operations that had been conducted under a resolution written for a war that had ended. The repeal did not end any deployment and it did not affect the fight against the Islamic State, which had its own authorization. It ended the claim that a president could act in Iraq because of a vote taken in 2002, and the ending of that claim was the point that its sponsors had been making for a decade. The repeal was signed in the summer, and the sponsors described the signing as the end of the longest authorization in the country's history.
