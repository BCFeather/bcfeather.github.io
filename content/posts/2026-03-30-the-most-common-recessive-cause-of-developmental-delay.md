Genetic diagnosis in neurodevelopmental disorders succeeds in perhaps half of cases, and the failures cluster where the tools do not look. Exome sequencing covers protein-coding exons, which is most of the genome's function and about one per cent of its sequence, and non-coding genes are covered incidentally if at all. A gene that produces an RNA and no protein is therefore easy to miss, which is why the discovery of a new one is worth attention.

*RNU2-2* encodes a small RNA that is part of the spliceosome, the machine that removes introns from messenger RNA. It is one of many copies of the U2 gene in the human genome, and it was treated as redundant, which is a reasonable assumption for a gene with dozens of paralogues and an unreasonable one for a gene that turns out to be the only copy a cell can use at scale.

Researchers at the University of Bristol, with collaborators at Mount Sinai and Stanford, found that biallelic variants in *RNU2-2* cause a neurodevelopmental disorder and that the variants result in a near-complete absence of the U2-2 RNA. The work appeared in *Nature Genetics* on 30 March, and the affected children shared hypotonia, developmental delay, limited speech and, in some, epilepsy and movement disorders.

The scale of the finding is what makes it notable. Variants in this gene account for around ten per cent of recessive neurodevelopmental disorders in which a cause has been identified, which turns a small non-coding gene into one of the most common recessive causes known. That is an unusually large share for a discovery from a study of this kind, and it is the sort of share that changes the order of a diagnostic pipeline.

Mechanistically the disorder is a splicing problem, which explains the phenotype. The spliceosome is required in every cell, and a partial loss of one of its components should be broadly disruptive. Neurons appear to be the tissue least able to tolerate it, which is the pattern seen in several splicing-factor diseases, and it is why the phenotype is neurological and not systemic.

Diagnosis is the immediate consequence. A gene that is non-coding and duplicated is not reported by standard exome pipelines, and variants in it are filtered out as intronic or as paralogous noise. Laboratories will have to add it explicitly, and the families who have spent years in undiagnosed clinics are the reason the addition matters.

Any therapeutic consequence is distant. A spliceosome component cannot be replaced with an enzyme infusion, and the possible routes are antisense oligonucleotides that boost the functional copy, or gene addition, both of which are speculative for this target. What the discovery provides is a diagnosis, and a diagnosis is the precondition for any of it. What the paper also demonstrates is a method. Short non-coding genes with many paralogues are a blind spot in the standard pipeline, and the blind spot is large. Finding the most common recessive cause of a common disorder inside it suggests the next discovery is a matter of looking and not of new technology.
