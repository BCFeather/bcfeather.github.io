description: "Solid electrolytes fail where the ceramic meets the metal, and the current density a cell can take depends on a pressure the pack has to supply."
---

A solid-state battery fails at its interfaces before it fails anywhere else, and the interface is a layer a few nanometres thick that nobody designed. The electrolyte is a ceramic or a polymer, the anode is a metal or a graphite, and the two are in contact over a surface that is rough at the scale of the grains. The contact is not intimate, because a hard ceramic does not deform to fill the valleys of a metal, and the places where the two touch carry all the current. The current density at those contacts is higher than the average by an order of magnitude, and the plating that happens there is the beginning of the failure. The contact is also where the chemistry begins, since a metal and a ceramic that are pressed together at room temperature are not in equilibrium at that interface. A metal that is polished to a mirror and a metal that is rolled in a mill give different contact areas under the same clamp.

Lithium metal plating is the failure mode that matters. When the cell is charged faster than the electrolyte can transport the ion, the metal deposits at the interface as dendrites and as a porous layer, and the layer grows into the ceramic along the grain boundaries. A dendrite that reaches the other electrode shorts the cell, and a porous layer that thickens raises the resistance until the cell cannot be charged at the rate it was sold at. The threshold current density at which plating starts is the number that is quoted for a new electrolyte, and it is measured in a cell that is not the cell the manufacturer will build. A cell that plates at a low current density and a cell that plates at a high one can have the same electrolyte, and the difference is the fixture and the surface finish of the metal. A plating threshold is usually quoted for a symmetric cell with two lithium electrodes, which is the easiest geometry to interpret and the furthest from a product.

The chemistry of the interface is the second problem. A sulfide electrolyte reacts with the metal and with the cathode, and the products of that reaction are a layer that conducts ions poorly and electrons well, which is the worst combination for a battery. An oxide electrolyte is stable against the metal and needs a high temperature to sinter it to the cathode, and the sintering step is where the two materials react. A polymer electrolyte is stable and conducts poorly at room temperature, which is why it is usually run hot or blended. A sulfide that is stable against the metal is often unstable against the cathode, and the coating that protects one side does not protect the other. A polymer that is blended with a ceramic also has a glass transition that sets the temperature above which the cell can be charged quickly.

Mechanics explains why pressure is part of the recipe. A stack that is clamped at high pressure keeps the contact intimate and suppresses the plating, and a stack that is not clamped has voids that grow with every cycle. The pressure is applied by a fixture that adds mass and cost to the pack, and the cells that are marketed without a fixture are cells that have been designed to tolerate a lower pressure. The trade between pressure and current density is the central design problem of the chemistry. A cell that is clamped at ten megapascals is a cell whose pack has to be built around the clamp, and the clamp is what the automotive engineers count as a cost. A stack that needs a clamp can still be used in a bus or a truck, where the pack is large and the mass is less critical.

Diagnostics have improved enough to make the failure visible. Operando X-ray tomography resolves the voids and the dendrites inside a ceramic while the cell cycles, and the images show the plating starting at the grain boundaries and spreading along them. Impedance spectroscopy separates the resistance of the bulk from the resistance of the interface, and the second number grows before the cell fails. The measurements have turned a failure that was described as a short into a process that can be followed, and a process that can be followed can be interrupted. The tomography also shows the voids that were already in the ceramic after sintering, which are the places where the plating begins. A diagnostic that resolves the interface while the cell runs is what the field needed, since a post-mortem on a shorted cell shows the end of the process.

Fixes being tried fall into three groups. Coatings put a thin layer between the electrolyte and the metal that conducts ions and blocks electrons, and the layer has to be thin enough not to add resistance. Interlayers of a soft metal deform under pressure and fill the voids, and the soft metal has to be stable against the lithium. Composite electrolytes mix a ceramic with a polymer so that the ceramic carries the current and the polymer fills the gaps, and the composite is neither as conductive as the ceramic nor as cheap as the polymer. A coating that is a few nanometres thick has to be deposited on a surface that is rough at the scale of its grains, and the coverage is the part that fails first. An interlayer of a soft metal that is too thick becomes a diffusion path for the lithium and a source of creep in the stack.

What would make the chemistry competitive is a current density that a fast charge demands at a pressure that a pack can supply. The cells that have been demonstrated at high current density need a fixture, and the cells that run without one are slower than the lithium-ion cells they are meant to replace. The gap is smaller than it was five years ago and it is still a gap, and the announcements that describe a solid-state cell usually describe one of the two numbers and omit the other. The number that would settle the question is a current density at a pressure that a pack can carry, and it has been approached in small cells and not in a module. A module has to be tested at the pressure it will see in a pack, and the pressure in a pack changes with the state of charge and with the temperature.
