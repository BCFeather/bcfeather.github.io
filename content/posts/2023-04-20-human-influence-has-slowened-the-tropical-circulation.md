---

The tropical circulation moves heat and moisture from the equator towards the subtropics, and the monsoon systems that billions of people depend on are its regional expressions. A slowdown of the circulation is a change in the water cycle's machinery.

The attribution study in *Nature*, April 2023 finds one. The authors analyze the strength of the Northern Hemisphere's tropical overturning circulation over recent decades and use a detection-and-attribution framework to ask whether the change is natural or forced.

The answer is forced. Natural variability, including volcanic eruptions and the Pacific's slow oscillations, cannot reproduce the observed trend, and the models' response to human drivers can.

The drivers are two. Greenhouse gases warm the troposphere and change its stability, and aerosols cool it unevenly, and the two forcings act on the circulation in different ways and in different regions.

The pattern is not uniform. Some monsoon regions have weakened and others have not, and the paper's regional decomposition is what makes the global statement usable.

The consequence for rainfall is the stake. A weaker overturning circulation shifts the zones where air rises, and a shift moves the rain that agriculture depends on.

The model's uncertainty is the caveat. Different models agree on the sign and disagree on the magnitude, and the spread is the range that a risk assessment has to carry.

The observational record is short. A circulation index derived from reanalysis depends on the reanalysis's assumptions, and the paper's use of multiple products is what makes the trend robust.

What the study cannot separate is the aerosol's contribution from the greenhouse contribution in the recent period, because the two have moved together in time.

For climate policy, the value is a variable that was treated as internal variability. A circulation that human influence has changed is a circulation that adaptation has to plan for, and a monsoon forecast that assumes a stationary circulation is a forecast with a bias.

The measurement is indirect. Circulation strength is inferred from winds, from sea-level pressure and from the water cycle, and each proxy has its own error. Agreement among the proxies is the paper's evidence.

The seasonal scale matters. A monsoon is a seasonal reversal, and a change in the annual mean may appear in one season and not another. The paper's seasonal analysis addresses that.
The circulation's role in the oceans is a further consequence. Trade winds drive the ocean's upwelling, and an upwelling that weakens reduces the nutrients that surface and the fisheries that depend on them. A change in the atmosphere is therefore a change in the sea.
The attribution method's assumption is that models reproduce internal variability. A model that generates too little variability will attribute more of a trend to forcing, and the paper's evaluation of that assumption is what separates a defensible claim from a bold one.
The regional detail is where the paper earns its keep. A global index is a summary, and the monsoon basins that show a weakening and those that do not are what a water authority needs to know before it plans a reservoir.
