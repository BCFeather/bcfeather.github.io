The tropical circulation moves heat and moisture from the equator towards the subtropics, and the monsoon systems that billions of people depend on are its regional expressions, so a slowdown is a change in the machinery of the water cycle itself.

In *Nature* in April 2023, an attribution study finds one. The authors analyse the strength of the Northern Hemisphere's tropical overturning circulation over recent decades and apply a detection-and-attribution framework to ask whether the change is natural or forced.

Forced is the answer. Natural variability, including volcanic eruptions and the Pacific's slow oscillations, cannot reproduce the observed trend, while the models' response to human drivers can. Two forcings are at work: greenhouse gases warm the troposphere and change its stability, and aerosols cool it unevenly, so the two act on the circulation in different ways and in different regions. Agreement among proxies carries the claim, since circulation strength is inferred from winds, from sea-level pressure and from the water cycle, each with its own error, and the convergence of the three is the evidence.

Because some monsoon regions have weakened and others have not, the regional decomposition is what makes the global statement usable, and it is where the paper earns its keep: a water authority planning a reservoir needs to know which basins are drying. A weaker overturning shifts the zones where air rises, and a shift moves the rain that agriculture depends on. The change reaches the ocean as well, since trade winds drive upwelling, a weaker upwelling brings fewer nutrients to the surface, and the fisheries that depend on them follow.

Uncertainty has two shapes. Different models agree on the sign and disagree on the magnitude, and the spread is the range a risk assessment has to carry; the attribution method also assumes that models reproduce internal variability, and a model generating too little of it will attribute more of a trend to forcing, which is why the paper evaluates that assumption. A short observational record adds the second constraint, because a circulation index derived from reanalysis depends on the reanalysis's assumptions, and multiple products are what make the trend robust.

Aerosols remain the unresolvable part. Their contribution cannot be separated from the greenhouse contribution in the recent period, because the two have moved together in time. What policy gains is a variable once treated as internal variability: a circulation that human influence has changed is one adaptation must plan for, and a monsoon forecast that assumes a stationary circulation carries a bias. The seasonal scale complicates any such forecast, because a monsoon is a seasonal reversal and a change in the annual mean may appear in one season and not another.

What the analysis cannot supply is a forecast for a single basin, because a hemispheric index averages regions whose rainfall responds on different timescales, and a water manager needs the regional number before the global one.
