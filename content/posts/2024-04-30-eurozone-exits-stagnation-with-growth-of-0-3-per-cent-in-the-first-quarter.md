Eurozone economy grew 0.3 per cent in the first quarter, ending a shallow contraction that had lasted since the middle of the previous year, and inflation held at 2.4 per cent in April. The four largest economies all expanded in the same quarter for the first time in a year. The figure was slightly better than the forecasts, and the revision to the previous quarter turned what had looked like a technical recession into a flat one.

Composition is uneven. Spain and Greece grew faster than the average, Germany returned to growth by a fraction, and the industrial heartland of the continent is still operating below its capacity. An average that is positive can conceal a member that is barely moving. Germany's growth came mostly from services and construction, while its factories continued to cut output, which is a recovery that does not restore the industry that the country built its model on. The divergence between the service economies and the industrial ones has widened in every quarter since the energy crisis, and the quarter did not reverse it.

Energy is the reason the recovery is shallow. Gas prices have fallen from their peak but remain above what the industry paid before the conflict, and the chemical and metal plants that closed during the crisis have not all reopened. A cost that is permanently higher is a cost that permanently reduces output. The share of the industry that has closed permanently is the number the economists argue about, and the answer decides whether the lost output ever comes back.

Labour market is the support. Unemployment is at a record low, and the wage increases that followed have kept consumption from collapsing even as real incomes fell. A labour market that is tight is a buffer that the economy has used instead of fiscal transfers. The wage increases have begun to outpace prices again, which means real incomes are rising and the consumption that supports the quarter can continue.

Inflation is the constraint on the response. The central bank has signalled a cut in June, and the quarter's growth is neither strong enough to justify a delay nor weak enough to demand a larger one. A number near the target is a number that gives the council room to move slowly. The council has said it will decide meeting by meeting, and the quarter's data give it a reason to move in June without having to explain a surprise.

What the quarter shows is that an economy can leave a stagnation without returning to its previous trend, and the difference between the two is the capacity that was lost while the energy price was high. A recovery of three tenths of a per cent is enough to change the direction of the statistics and not enough to change the experience of the households inside them. The next two quarters will show whether the recovery is a rebound from a low base or the start of a cycle, and the answer depends on the factories rather than on the beaches.
