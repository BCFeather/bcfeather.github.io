Three months of letters and threats ended on 22 July with a deal that both governments called historic and that both had to explain carefully, because a 15 per cent tariff is a tax on Japanese exports that Japan agreed to accept. The rate replaces the 25 per cent threatened in the spring and applies to cars, parts and most other goods, and the prime minister described it as the lowest rate negotiated by any country with a surplus.

Investment is the price of the rate. Japan committed 550 billion dollars to projects that the American government will select, in energy, semiconductors, critical minerals, pharmaceuticals and shipbuilding, and the arrangement says that 90 per cent of the profits from those investments accrue to the United States, which is an unusual division for a commercial deal.

Agriculture was the concession that mattered politically. Japan will buy more American rice within its minimum access scheme, increase purchases of corn, soybeans and fertiliser to about 8 billion dollars a year, and accept American cars and trucks without additional testing, which opens a market that Japanese regulation had kept largely closed. A project selected by the recipient government is a commercial investment or a transfer is a question that the framework does not answer, and the answer will be visible in the returns that the projects actually earn.

Autos were the reason the deal was worth doing for Tokyo. The 25 per cent rate on vehicles and parts that took effect in April is halved, and Japan secured a commitment that future tariffs on semiconductors and pharmaceuticals would be no worse than those applied to any other country, which is a most-favoured-nation promise written into a bilateral agreement.

Markets read it as relief. The Nikkei rose to a record, Toyota and Honda gained, and the yen stayed weak, which is the combination that a deal of this shape produces: exporters get a lower rate and the currency does not have to appreciate to absorb the improvement.

The politics are already written on both sides. The American administration can describe the investment as the largest ever secured, the Japanese government can describe the rate as a victory over a threat, and neither has to explain to its voters that the tariff is a tax and the investment is a promise whose projects do not yet exist. For an economy that exports about a fifth of its output and builds a quarter of the world's cars, a rate that is ten points lower than the threat is worth more than the investment is worth to the other side, which is why the deal was signed in the form it was.

What would settle the deal is whether the investment arrives as capital and not as announcements. A first tranche in energy and chips would make the framework credible, a year of studies would leave it as a communiqué, and the autumn implementation order is where the answer will be visible. Both governments will be judged on the price of rice in Tokyo and the price of a car in Detroit rather than on the framework document, and neither of those prices is set by the agreement alone.
