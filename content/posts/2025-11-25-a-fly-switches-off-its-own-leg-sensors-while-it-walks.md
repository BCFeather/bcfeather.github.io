title: "Proprioceptors go quiet while the fly walks"
date: "2025-11-25T09:35:00+08:00"

description: "A circuit in Drosophila suppresses movement-encoding proprioceptors during self-generated actions such as walking and grooming, so that the brain does not read its own motion as a signal from the world, the group reports in *Nature*."
---

An animal that moves is an animal that disturbs its own senses. Every step a fly takes bends the joints that carry proprioceptors, and those receptors fire in response, sending the brain a signal that looks exactly like an external perturbation. If the brain treated that signal as information about the world, walking would be a continuous confusion. The study in *Nature*, 13 November 2025 finds the circuit that prevents it. Leg proprioceptors in *Drosophila* are silenced presynaptically while the fly walks or grooms, and the silencing is selective: the receptors that encode movement are suppressed, while others keep reporting. The effect is that self-generated motion is removed from the signal before it reaches the circuits that compute posture.

Selectivity is the interesting part. A blanket reduction of sensory gain would deaden the leg as well as quiet it, and the fly would lose the feedback that keeps it upright. Suppressing only the movement-encoding channel leaves the position-encoding channel intact, so the animal can still tell where its leg is while ignoring how fast it is moving.

The mechanism is presynaptic inhibition, which acts on the transmission from the sensory neuron rather than on its firing. That is the least disruptive place to intervene: the receptor still responds, the spike still occurs, and the message is gated at the synapse where it would otherwise enter the nervous system. For a circuit, that is the equivalent of turning down a microphone rather than covering the ear.

The behavioural test is what makes the result convincing. When the suppressing circuit is disrupted, the fly's walking changes, and the change is consistent with a nervous system that is now hearing its own footsteps. Grooming, which involves coordinated leg movements of a different kind, recruits the same suppression, which suggests the circuit is tied to self-generated action rather than to walking specifically.

Comparative questions follow immediately. Vertebrates have proprioceptive systems with the same problem and different solutions, and the cerebellum's role in predicting the sensory consequences of movement is the classic example. Whether the fly's presynaptic gate has a counterpart in a mammalian spinal circuit, or whether it is an adaptation specific to an animal with six legs and a fast gait, is a question the genetics of *Drosophila* makes tractable.

A broader lesson concerns how nervous systems use prediction. Suppressing a predictable signal is cheaper than modelling it, and the fly appears to take the cheap route: instead of computing what its legs should feel, it turns off the channel that would report it. That is a strategy a robot controller could copy, and one that explains why the same principle appears in animals that evolved separately.

Experimental control is a comparison of the same receptor during movement and at rest. Signals recorded while the fly stood still show the full response, and signals recorded while it walked show the suppressed one, and the difference is the gate opening and closing. That pairing removes the possibility that the suppression is a property of the recording rather than of the circuit.
