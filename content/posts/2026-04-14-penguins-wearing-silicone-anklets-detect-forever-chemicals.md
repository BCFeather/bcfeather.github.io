Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances are called forever chemicals because the carbon-fluorine bond that defines them does not break in the environment, and because they accumulate in animals that live long enough to collect them. Measuring them is expensive, because it requires sampling water, sediment and tissue across a range that a ship cannot cover and a laboratory cannot afford to repeat.

An approach reported in *Earth: Environmental Sustainability* and described in *Nature* on 14 April turns the animals into the samplers. Researchers in Argentine Patagonia fitted Magellanic penguins, *Spheniscus magellanicus*, with silicone anklets, and silicone absorbs hydrophobic compounds from the surrounding medium in proportion to their concentration, which is the principle behind passive sampling devices used in water monitoring.

Penguins forage at sea for days at a time and return to the colony, and the band accumulates what the bird encounters. When the band is recovered, the analysis gives an integrated exposure over a foraging trip, which is the kind of measurement a water sample cannot provide, and it does so without drawing blood or taking tissue, which for a protected species is the difference between a study that a permit office will approve and one it will not.

First results show that the birds are carrying PFAS and that concentrations vary between individuals and between colonies. Variation is the useful part, because it points to differences in foraging area, in diet and in the depth of the water column a bird works, and a colony near a source of contamination shows a different profile from one that forages over open water.

Ethically the design needs justification and the researchers provide one. The band weighs a few grams, it is fitted at the leg where a ring is already used in bird research, and the birds are handled by experienced field workers under permit. The alternative, sampling tissue, requires killing birds, and the comparison is not close.

One limitation is that a passive sampler measures what it is exposed to and not what the animal is. Silicone absorbs a compound at a rate that depends on the compound's chemistry and on temperature, and the calibration between the band and the tissue of the bird is an assumption that has to be tested species by species.

Generalizability is the second limitation. A penguin is a marine predator with a defined foraging range, and a penguin sample describes a patch of ocean. For a global picture the method needs many species in many places, which is why the authors describe it as a monitoring tool that complements conventional sampling and does not replace it. What the study demonstrates is a principle that will outlive the specific result, and the principle is that an animal with a known range is an instrument with a known sampling footprint. Wild animals move through environments that instruments sample at points, and a device that turns an animal into an integrator closes the gap between the map of the ocean and the measurements taken in it.
