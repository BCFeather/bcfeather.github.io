The business model is different from charging. A charging station sells electricity; a swap station owns the batteries and rents them out. That means the operator carries the capital cost of the packs, and it means the packs have to be standardized across the vehicles that use the station. Fleets came first. Taxis in Beijing, buses in several cities, and heavy trucks in the coal and steel corridors have used swapping for years, because a fleet is a closed population that one company can equip. The highway network is the new stage. Stations are appearing at service areas on the main expressways, and their customers are long-distance drivers and, increasingly, the operators of electric heavy trucks. Economics depend on utilization. A station that swaps fifty packs a day is profitable; one that swaps five is not, and the difference is the density of compatible vehicles on that road. What the stations show is that the charging debate has a second answer. For a private car in a city, charging at home is cheaper and slower and nobody needs to swap.
