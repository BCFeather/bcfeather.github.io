Stars like the Sun end as white dwarfs, and the planets that outlive that transition are the only direct evidence of what a planetary system looks like after its star has died. Finding them is awkward, because a white dwarf is faint, small and quiet, and a planet close enough to transit is also close enough to have gone through the red-giant phase that should have destroyed it. WD 1856+534 b was the exception that made the problem concrete: a body of up to 13.8 Jupiter masses crossing a white dwarf 80 light-years away every 1.4 days.

Transit photometry found the planet in 2020, and the transit is also the only handle on its atmosphere, since a planet that blocks starlight leaves its chemical fingerprint in the light grazing its limb. A James Webb Space Telescope campaign observed the system across several transits, and the result appeared in *Nature* on 2 July 2026. The team searched the transmission spectrum for absorption by water, methane and carbon dioxide, and for the flat, featureless signature that clouds produce.

No secure molecular detection came back. What the data support is a limit: any hydrogen-dominated envelope has to be thin, and the planet is consistent with a stripped core, a rocky body with a modest secondary atmosphere, or a high-altitude haze that hides the chemistry below. The team also put an upper bound on the temperature contrast between the planet's day and night sides, which is the kind of number that separates a bare rock from a body wrapped in gas.

Survival is the puzzle the system was always about. A giant planet cannot have formed at 1.4 days from a white dwarf, so it must have migrated inward, most plausibly by scattering onto a high-eccentricity orbit and then being captured by tides. That history matters for the atmosphere, because a planet dragged through the red-giant envelope should have lost its outer layers, and the spectrum is the first chance to check whether anything survived the trip.

One object carries little weight. The signal is a few parts per million per transit, the white dwarf's own variability and star spots sit at a comparable level, and atmosphere models for a planet this hot and this irradiated are not well constrained. A non-detection is a real result, but it is a result about this planet on this orbit rather than a statement about white dwarf planets in general. A re-analysis of the same transits with a different reduction pipeline would be the cheapest check on the null result.

The programme that follows is a small sample observed the same way. Another handful of candidates, with masses and radii measured well enough to separate a core from an envelope, would turn a single limit into a distribution, and that distribution would say how much of a planetary system's atmosphere can survive the death of its star.
