Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva took the presidential sash on the first afternoon of 2023, and he took it from the president of the Senate rather than from the man he had defeated. Jair Bolsonaro had flown to Florida on 30 December and had said nothing in public since, so the ritual of the handover, which every incoming president had performed since 1985, went unperformed. Lula, seventy-seven, walked up the ramp of the Planalto palace beside his vice-president, Geraldo Alckmin, and a crowd that had been standing in the sun since morning. The ceremony began with the national anthem and a guard of honour that had been rehearsing since the previous week, and the temperature on the Esplanade was thirty degrees.

The arithmetic behind the ceremony was narrow. He had won the second round on 30 October by 50.9 per cent to 49.1, a margin of about two million votes out of more than 124 million cast. More than half the country had voted against him, and the half that had voted for him had waited two months for the ceremony to confirm it. That division shaped the speech, which was about rebuilding, about the poor, about national sovereignty, and about a country that had spent four years being governed for a part of itself.

Representatives of fifty-four countries attended, including heads of state from Latin America and from Europe. Alckmin, a former governor of Sao Paulo and once Lula's opponent, stood to his left as the visible proof that the new government had been assembled from a wider coalition than the one that had elected it. Ministers were sworn in afterwards in a long sequence in the palace. The oath was taken in the chamber of the Chamber of Deputies, and the crowd outside watched on screens that had been put up along the axis.

The absent sash carried more meaning than any of the speeches. Bolsonaro's supporters had camped outside army barracks since November, and the ceremony was the moment their claim that the election had been stolen lost its last official venue. The former president's silence from Florida left the ceremony with a hole in its centre, and the hole was the point. No foreign leader was invited to speak, which the organizers described as a question of protocol and the guests treated as a question about the former president.

Lula signed a first batch of decrees within hours, most of them revoking measures taken during the previous four years. He restored funds that had been emptied, reversed decrees on the sale of public assets, and reinstated a commission on climate policy that had been dissolved. The decrees were symbolic in substance, and they were meant to be.

Institutions are tested by what happens after the ceremony rather than during it. The question the day left open was whether the losing half of the electorate would accept a result that the losing candidate had never conceded. Eight days later the question was answered in the streets.
