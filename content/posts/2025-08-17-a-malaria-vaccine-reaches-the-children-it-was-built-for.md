First malaria vaccines approved for wide use have now been rolled out across a dozen African countries, and the results are being reported from routine clinics rather than from trials. The efficacy is moderate. Against clinical malaria the protection sits somewhere between half and three quarters in the first year, and it wanes, and a booster is required. Those numbers would be unimpressive for a vaccine against measles. For malaria they are the best result the field has produced in sixty years.

The parasite is the reason, and the immune system has been losing to it for a very long time. Malaria has a life cycle with several stages and each stage presents a different set of proteins to the immune system, so a vaccine that targets one stage leaves the others untouched. The two approved products target the same protein on the surface of the sporozoite, the form that the mosquito injects, and they differ in the adjuvant and in the manufacturing platform. A third generation of candidates targets other stages and is several years behind.

The logistics are the harder part. A child needs four doses by the age of two, and the schedule has to be fitted into a set of clinic visits that are already crowded with other immunizations. A dose that arrives at the wrong season protects less well, because the immunity wanes in the months when transmission is highest. The programmes that have done best are the ones that combined the vaccine with bed nets, seasonal chemoprevention and indoor spraying, and the combination is what the trials were never designed to measure.

The financing is a separate puzzle. The doses are bought through a global procurement mechanism, the price per dose has fallen with volume, and the demand from countries now exceeds the supply. A ministry of health that wants to add the vaccine to its schedule has to find room in a budget that is already funded largely by donors, and the donors have their own priorities.

What the rollout has produced, beyond the doses, is a body of operational knowledge that did not exist before. Which districts can hold the cold chain. Which months produce the worst dropout between the third and fourth dose. How much a community health worker can absorb in one visit. The answers are unglamorous and they are the difference between a licensed product and a protected child.

The mortality numbers will take a decade to read. The trials suggest that a full schedule reduces deaths from malaria by something close to a third in the age group that carries the burden, and a third of a very large number is a very large number. The clinics reporting now are seeing fewer severe cases in the under-two ward, which is the kind of evidence that arrives before the statistics do.

None of this ends malaria. The parasite has survived every intervention of the last century, including dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane, chloroquine and a hundred million bed nets. What has changed is that there is now a tool that works partially, at scale, in the places where the disease is worst, and the argument has moved from whether to use it to how to pay for the next hundred million doses.
