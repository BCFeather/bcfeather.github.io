An eruption in 1831 injected sulphate into the stratosphere, and the evidence for it is in polar ice and in written accounts. A paper in *PNAS* on 30 December 2024 identifies the source. A sulphate signal of 13 teragrams is a large event, and a large event is one that a climate reconstruction has to account for. A named eruption is a fixed point in a chronology, and fixed points are what other layers are dated against. A named layer is also a calibration point for volcanic forcing in a climate model, and forcing is what a model needs before it can reproduce a cold decade.

The signal is estimated at about 13 teragrams of sulphate, which places the event among the larger eruptions of the century. A signal of that size should have a source that can be found. Tephra fingerprinting is the standard attribution method, and it works when the candidate list is short and the composition is distinctive. Composition matching works best when the magma is distinctive, and a caldera that has erupted repeatedly supplies several fingerprints to choose from.

The search compared tephra from candidate volcanoes with the layer in the ice, and geochemistry supplied the fingerprint. A fingerprint that matches in composition and in age is what an attribution requires. Remoteness explains the delay, since a caldera without a settlement nearby leaves no account and no ash layer in a written record. A poor record and a large eruption are a combination that produces exactly this kind of mystery, and the mystery is now solved for one event.

Zavaritskii caldera on Simushir in the Kuril Islands is the match. The caldera is remote and its eruption history is poorly documented, which is why the event remained unidentified for two centuries. Radiative balance is the channel through which an eruption reaches the climate, and the size of the injection sets the size of the perturbation.

Attribution matters for climate, because an eruption of that size perturbs the radiative balance for several years. A temperature anomaly that has been attributed to something else can now be attributed to this event. An anomaly attributed to the wrong cause is an anomaly that misleads a reconstruction, and correction is the value of the identification.

For the same reason, the Kurils are an active arc with many calderas, and the paper suggests that other unidentified layers may have similar sources. A region that produces large eruptions and keeps poor records is a region that leaves mysteries. The Kuril arc is productive and under-monitored, and under-monitoring is what leaves chronologies with unnamed layers.

On the modelling side, the dating relies on ice-core chronologies, which have their own uncertainties, and a layer that spans several years cannot resolve a single event precisely. Ice-core chronology has annual layers in some cores and not in others, and the resolution of the dating depends on which core is used.
