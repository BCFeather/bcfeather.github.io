---

Registered reports invert the usual order of peer review. An author submits the design and analysis plan before collecting data; reviewers accept or reject the plan; acceptance is a commitment to publish whatever the data show, provided the plan is followed. A journal using this format reported this month that it had published its four hundredth such article, and the numbers behind the milestone are worth examining.

The format exists to solve publication bias. Studies that find nothing have historically been unpublishable, so the literature over-represents positive findings, and meta-analyses built on that literature overstate effects. A format that accepts before results removes the incentive to report something, anything, to justify the work.

Evidence on whether it works comes from the same journal. Comparing its registered reports with conventional submissions in the same field, the authors find that 41 per cent of registered reports reported a null result in the primary outcome, against 9 per cent of conventional articles. The difference is large and is not explained by design quality, since the two groups have similar sample sizes.

A second problem appears in the same data. Of the 400 reports, 63 per cent deviated from their analysis plan in the published article, mostly in the choice of covariates or the handling of missing values. Deviations are permissible under the format if they are labelled, and the journal reports that 71 per cent were declared. Even declared deviations weaken the protection the format offers, because the freedom to change an analysis after seeing the data is what publication bias exploited.

Incentives explain the deviation rate. The plan is written before the data exist, when the analyst does not know what the data will look like, and the first look at the data usually suggests a better specification. Career incentives have not changed: a null result still earns less attention than a positive one, even when both are published.

Adoption is the practical constraint. Fewer than 5 per cent of ecology journals offer the format, and the ones that do are small. Authors are reluctant because a registered report takes longer, since review happens before data collection and again after, and because a rejected plan costs the work already done on the design.

What this suggests is that the format fixes the selection problem and exposes the specification problem. Publishing null results changes the denominator of the literature; it does not by itself ensure that the analysis inside a paper was decided before the data were seen. Both problems are real, and the second is harder, because it requires reviewers to police a version of the paper that existed months earlier.

For a reader, the operational lesson is to check whether a study is a registered report and whether it reports deviations. A null result from a plan that was followed is informative; a null result from a plan that was reworked halfway through is a different object.
