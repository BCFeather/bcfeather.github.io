Jets from active galaxies are the largest structures in the universe and the least understood at their origin. A radio observatory network reported on 23 April in *Astronomy and Astrophysics* an image of the base of the jet in a blazar at 86 gigahertz, with a resolution of about 40 microarcseconds, which corresponds to a few hundred gravitational radii of the central black hole.

The measurement uses very long baseline interferometry, the technique of combining telescopes separated by thousands of kilometres so that they behave as one instrument with a baseline equal to the distance between them. At 86 gigahertz, atmospheric water vapour becomes the limiting error, and the team corrected for it with dual-frequency observations and water radiometers at each station.

What the image shows is a parabolic outflow. Close to the core, the jet's width grows with distance along the axis as a power law with an index near 0.5, which is the signature of a magnetically collimated flow, and further out the profile transitions to a cone. The transition happens at a distance the authors put at roughly 10^4 gravitational radii, and its location is the parameter that distinguishes between competing launch models.

The competing models differ in where the energy comes from. One extracts rotational energy from the black hole through the Blandford-Znajek process, in which magnetic field lines threading the horizon are twisted by the hole's spin. The other taps the accretion disc's rotation through a magnetocentrifugal wind. Both can produce a jet; they differ in the magnetization of the flow and in where collimation occurs, and the observed parabolic-to-conical transition is consistent with the first model at the mass and spin inferred for this source.

Brightness asymmetry is the second result. The jet is brighter on one side of the axis at small scales, and this is interpreted as Doppler boosting of emission from a flow moving towards the observer at a bulk Lorentz factor around 15, with the viewing angle a few degrees off the axis. That is consistent with the source's known variability, and the paper uses the asymmetry to constrain the angle rather than assuming it.

Limits are those of any single source. One blazar, one frequency, one epoch: the authors note that the transition distance could differ in a source with a different accretion rate, and that the image cannot separate the spin contribution from the disc contribution without a second source measured the same way. The next step is a survey at the same resolution, which requires more stations and more recorded bandwidth.

Frequency selection explains why this result arrived now. Resolution in interferometry scales with baseline length divided by observing wavelength, so 86 gigahertz gives the sharpest image available from Earth-based telescopes without resorting to space. The cost is sensitivity: atmospheric water vapour absorbs at these frequencies, so the observations must be scheduled in dry conditions and calibrated carefully, and the integration time per image runs to hours across several stations.

A second epoch would settle the remaining ambiguity. The parabolic-to-conical transition moves if the jet's power changes, and a source with a variable accretion rate should show the transition distance varying between observations. The team has scheduled a re-observation in eighteen months, which is a short interval for this kind of measurement and a reasonable one for a source that flares.

Why the result matters is scale-invariant. The same physics is invoked for the jets of stellar-mass black holes in X-ray binaries, where the scales are smaller by a factor of a billion and the observations are correspondingly worse. Getting the geometry right in a supermassive source constrains the models that are applied everywhere else.
