description: "Magnetic resonance images of the heart and the brain, paired with genetic data from a large cohort, reveal shared genetic influences on structure and function in both organs."
---

Cardiology and neurology have been separate disciplines, and their separation has been reinforced by anatomy. The heart is a pump and the brain is a network, and the diseases of each have seemed to have different causes.

In *Science* on 2 June 2023, a shared architecture appears underneath. The authors pair cardiac and cerebral magnetic resonance measurements from a large cohort, correlate them, and then use genetic data to ask whether those correlations have a common source.

Phenotypic correlations come first. Measures of cardiac structure such as ventricular volume and wall thickness correlate with measures of brain structure and with white matter integrity, and the correlations survive adjustment for body size and blood pressure.

Genetic analysis is the step that matters. A shared genetic factor influences both traits, and the overlap is estimated from the participants' genotypes rather than from family resemblance alone. Substantial overlap is the result, because several genomic regions influence both a cardiac measure and a brain measure, which is consistent with a common developmental and metabolic background.

Vascular link is the obvious candidate, since both organs depend on a blood supply that atherosclerosis affects and a factor shaping vessel walls would appear in both, which is why adjustment for vascular risk factors tests whether the signal survives that route. Development offers a second candidate, because heart and brain form early and in parallel, and a gene governing one organ's patterning can govern the other's, with the overlap's genomic location consistent with such genes. Interpretation stays cautious. A shared genetic influence does not mean one organ causes the other's state, and correlation is separated from causation explicitly. Imaging protocol constrains the comparison, because a cardiac sequence and a cerebral sequence acquire different quantities at different resolutions, so the correlation is between derived measures rather than between raw images.

Sample size is the method's strength, since a cohort large enough to estimate genetic overlap needs tens of thousands of participants with both modalities, and few datasets have them. Imaging sets the limitation, because a cardiac measure is a snapshot and a brain measure is structure rather than function, so the analysis speaks to anatomy more than to physiology. Mechanism stays unresolved, as a shared locus can act through a common pathway or through two independent ones, and the distinction needs experiments imaging cannot supply. Medicine gains a reason to look across the divide, because a cardiologist reading a brain image and a neurologist reading a cardiac one each see half of a shared picture, and these numbers argue for reading both.

Risk carries the clinical reading, since a patient with a cardiac condition has a brain sharing some of the same liabilities, and a shared factor is a target one intervention might address in both organs. A longitudinal design would be stronger than a cross-sectional one, since a shared genetic factor should predict change in both organs over time, and a single snapshot cannot distinguish a cause from a coincidence. A shared locus also invites a drug-target question, because a gene influencing both organs is a candidate for a therapy whose effects would have to be monitored in both.
