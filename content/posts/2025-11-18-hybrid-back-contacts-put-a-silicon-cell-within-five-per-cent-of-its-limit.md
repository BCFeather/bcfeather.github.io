Silicon solar cells have been close to their ceiling for a decade, and the remaining gains are measured in fractions of a point. The detailed-balance limit for a single junction sets the maximum, and every recombination path, every resistive loss and every parasitic absorption moves a cell away from it. The cells reported in *Nature*, 13 November 2025 close most of that distance with a contact design rather than a new material.

The design is a hybrid interdigitated back contact. Both electrodes sit on the back of the wafer, so nothing shades the front, and the fingers alternate across the rear surface. The hybrid part is that the two contacts are not made of the same stack: each polarity gets a contact tuned to it, one optimised for electrons and the other for holes, which lets the structure keep the low recombination that a single symmetric scheme forces it to trade away.

The numbers are relative to the limit, which is a more honest framing than an absolute efficiency. A power conversion efficiency approaching 95 per cent of the detailed-balance value and a fill factor approaching 98 per cent of its own limit together say that the cell is losing little to recombination and almost nothing to series resistance. Fill factor is where contact resistance shows up, so the second figure is the one that speaks to the back-contact scheme.

Back contacts are hard to make. The two polarities are interleaved at a scale of tens of micrometres, and any misalignment shorts the cell; the metallisation has to be patterned without damaging the passivation underneath. The paper's contribution is a process that keeps the interfaces clean enough for the electrical numbers to survive the patterning, which is an industrial problem as much as a physics one.

What the result does not say is how the design behaves in a module. A cell is measured under a spectrum and a temperature that a module cannot reproduce, and the encapsulation, the interconnection and the busbars all add losses that a cell record excludes. The efficiency of a module built from these cells is the number a manufacturer sells.

The other open question is cost. Hybrid contacts mean more process steps than a homogeneous back-contact scheme, and every step carries a yield penalty. Whether the gain of a point or two survives the economics of a line running millions of wafers is not settled by a laboratory result.

What the work does establish is that silicon is not finished. The gap to the limit has narrowed to a few per cent, and the route there runs through contact engineering rather than through a different absorber, which means the incumbents can adopt it. A technology that a running fab can retrofit is a technology that arrives.

The temperature coefficient is the third number a manufacturer reads. Silicon loses efficiency as it heats, and a cell that is excellent at 25 degrees can be ordinary at 60, which is where a module operates. Contact schemes change that coefficient, because the passivation that reduces recombination also changes how the cell responds to heat, and the paper's data at elevated temperature are part of what makes the result usable.
