title: "Hominin body size grew in steps, not a smooth ramp"

description: "A Bayesian analysis of 386 specimens across 21 hominin species finds body size moving in bursts tied to lineage splits."
---

Body size is the most basic fact about an animal and the hardest to reconstruct across a fossil record. A single skeleton gives a single estimate, but species are known from different numbers of specimens, from different bones, and from sites separated by hundreds of thousands of years. The temptation is to plot the estimates and draw a line, and the line then gets read as a trend. Reconstruction is the bottleneck, since a femur and a molar carry different amounts of information and a species known from both is rare.

A study in *PNAS* on 7 July 2026 replaces the line with a model. Jacob Gardner and colleagues applied Bayesian modelling of evolutionary lineages to 386 specimens from 21 hominin species, estimating body size along each branch of the tree rather than treating the record as a single series. The approach borrows strength across specimens and puts an interval on every estimate, which is what a fossil record of this unevenness requires. The model also estimates the uncertainty on each step's timing, which a plotted average cannot provide.

What emerges is a pattern of steps. Body size moves in bursts that coincide with lineage splits and with changes in dietary or locomotor strategy, and long periods of stasis sit between them. The smooth increase that a naive plot suggests is an artefact of comparing averages across species, and averaging hides the fact that most species do not change much during their existence. Steps of this kind are easier to see in a lineage with a dense fossil record, and the hominin record is dense only in a few regions.

Its strength lies in separating within-species variation from between-species differences, which the raw data confound. Its weakness is the model itself: the priors on branch lengths and on the rate of change are choices, and different choices shift the timing of the steps even when the overall pattern survives. Fossil completeness also varies by species, and a species known from three specimens gets a wider interval than one known from thirty.

What the finding changes is the story told about hominin evolution. A gradual ramp supports a narrative of steady selection for larger bodies; a stepped pattern supports a narrative of ecological events and adaptive shifts, with size following rather than leading. Neither narrative is settled by this paper, but the second now has a quantitative footing. The pattern also fits the observation that brain size, body size and diet do not shift together, which a single trend line cannot express.

The next test is independent. Body size estimates from teeth and from limb bones disagree, and a model that reconciles them should be checked against species known from both. Sites with dense stratigraphy, where a single lineage can be followed through time, are where a step should be visible directly rather than inferred. A record that spans two million years with gaps of a hundred thousand is still thin enough that a step can hide in a gap.
