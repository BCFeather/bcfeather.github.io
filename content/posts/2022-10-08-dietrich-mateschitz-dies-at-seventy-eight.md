Dietrich Mateschitz died on Saturday at seventy-eight, and Red Bull announced it in a statement that praised him as the man who turned a Thai tonic into a global brand. He had been ill for some time and had withdrawn from public appearances during the year. The company gave no cause of death. He was the richest person in Austria, with a fortune the Austrian press put at about twenty-five billion euros.

He was born in 1944 in Styria, the son of two schoolteachers, and he studied marketing in Vienna. He worked for a German toothpaste company and travelled to Thailand on business, where he met Chaleo Yoovidhya, who sold a syrup called Krating Daeng to lorry drivers and labourers. The two agreed in 1984 to sell a version of it in Europe with a Western taste, and Mateschitz took forty-nine per cent of the company. The syrup had been sold in Thailand for two decades, and the European version was adapted from it rather than copied. The Thai family kept the majority and the formula, and the arrangement held for nearly four decades.

The drink's advantage was marketing rather than chemistry. He sold it in small cans at a premium, sponsored student events and put it in the hands of people who did dangerous sports. The slogan about giving you wings arrived early and stayed. He insisted on one can size and one price, which made the product easy to pick out in a fridge. By 2021 the company was selling about nine billion cans a year and taking roughly seven billion euros in revenue, without a stock exchange listing and without much debt.

The sports empire grew out of the advertising budget. Red Bull owns two Formula One teams and has sponsored drivers and extreme athletes, and it owns football clubs in Leipzig and Salzburg. The clubs became a grievance for supporters in both countries, who objected to a brand owning a place in their leagues. The company also paid for a hangar of historic aircraft and for a record attempt in a balloon, and it bought a television station that broadcasts in Austria. The teams gave him a platform that advertising alone could not buy, and his drivers became the most photographed men in the sport.

His style was to buy attention rather than advertising space, and he described the spending as a marketing cost with an uncertain return. He gave few interviews, lived in a house he built near Salzburg and flew himself to meetings. Austrian politicians described him as a figure who had changed how the country was seen abroad, and the state gave him an award for services to the republic.

Ownership now passes to his son Mark, who is thirty, and to the Yoovidhya family, which holds the rest. Red Bull says the company will continue as it is, and the son has been involved in the business for several years. The question that remains is whether a brand built on one man's taste for spectacle keeps its character without him.
