title: "Congress passes fifty-two billion dollars of subsidies for American chip factories"
date: "2022-07-28T23:40:00+08:00"

description: "The House follows the Senate in a vote that is unusual for its bipartisanship, and the money comes with a condition about Chinese expansion."
---

The House of Representatives passed the bill that provides fifty-two billion dollars of subsidies for the makers of semiconductors on Thursday, a day after the Senate approved it. The package also includes a tax credit for the factories that is worth about twenty-four billion, and money for research and for the training of engineers. The vote was two hundred and forty-three to one hundred and eighty-seven and the majority included both parties. The president has said he will sign it in the next week.

More than a year of negotiation preceded Thursday's vote. The shortage of chips that began in 2020 shut down the car factories and raised the price of the vehicles, and the government began to look at the dependence on the factories in Taiwan and in South Korea. The first version was stalled in the Senate for months because the two parties could not agree on the social spending that was attached to it. The compromise dropped the social provisions and kept the industrial ones.

The companies are reading the conditions most closely. The firms that take the money will be barred from expanding their advanced capacity in China for ten years, and they will have to return the subsidies if they do. They will also have to share the profits above an agreed level and to use the money for the construction of plants in the United States rather than for buybacks. The lobby groups for the industry accepted the terms after the Senate vote.

Announcements of new factories have already begun. The largest maker has started the construction of a plant in Arizona and a second one in Ohio, and the other firms have announced plans in Texas and in New York. The subsidies will cover a fraction of the cost of each plant, and the companies have said they need the money to compete with the ones in Asia. The plants take three years to build and the equipment is made by a handful of firms in the Netherlands and in Japan.

Underneath the subsidies sits a strategic argument about concentration. About ninety per cent of the most advanced chips are made in one country, and the government has said that the dependence is a national security risk. The bill does not change that in the short term, and it does begin to build the capacity that would reduce it in the long term. The allies in Europe and in Japan have passed similar subsidies, and the competition for the engineers has begun.

Public money now goes into an industry the market had not built at home. The factories are expensive and the returns are slow, and the companies had chosen to invest where the costs were lower. The government has decided that the dependence is worth the price, and the conditions that it attached show that the policy is about the rivalry with China as much as about the shortage. The plants will open in the middle of the decade, and the shortages will be over or not by then.
