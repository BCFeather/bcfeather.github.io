The archive began as a hobby with a microphone and a notebook. A retired sound engineer started recording the noises of his neighbourhood in 2015, one Saturday at a time, and by 2019 he had several hundred hours of markets, bus queues, repair shops and rain on awnings. The city's cultural bureau took over the project in 2020, gave it a small budget and a server, and this month it published the first catalogue: eleven years, roughly four thousand recordings, indexed by street, time of day and type of sound.

Catalogue is more useful than the audio. Each entry carries a location, a date, a duration and a description, and the descriptions are the resource: a note saying the fishmonger on the corner called prices in a dialect that nobody under forty uses now, or that the sound of a particular tram bell was retired when the fleet was replaced in 2021. A recording without that note is an anonymous noise. The note is what turns it into evidence about a city.

Sound archives have a characteristic problem, which is that their subject disappears faster than their catalogues can be built. The sounds that people most want to preserve are often the ones attached to trades that are closing: the bicycle repair man, the coal stove, the hand-cranked grinder. Recording them is straightforward. Documenting them takes interviews, and the archive has only just begun to collect them, with a handful of sessions with retired workers this year.

Consent is the second problem. Street recordings pick up whoever is walking past, and a market recording catches a vendor's sales patter, which is both a public performance and a piece of personal expression. The archive's rule is that recordings made for publication are made with the knowledge of the people in them, and that anything recorded without that knowledge is restricted to research use. The rule is easier to state than to apply, since it is impossible to know in advance who will walk into the microphone's range.

The third problem is the one that archivists argue about at conferences: whether a recording preserves a sound or merely a moment. A market recorded in 2019 is not the market; it is one Tuesday, with a particular weather, a particular crowd and a particular microphone placement. The archive's answer is to be explicit: each entry records the conditions, and the catalogue publishes the number of recordings per location, so a user can see whether they are listening to a representative sample or a single fluke.

For the city, the practical value is already showing in two places. Urban planners have used recordings of crossings and intersections to argue about noise, and a theatre company has built a production out of the market recordings. Neither use was planned. Both are the ordinary afterlife of an archive that was published rather than kept, and they are the argument for publishing more of them.
