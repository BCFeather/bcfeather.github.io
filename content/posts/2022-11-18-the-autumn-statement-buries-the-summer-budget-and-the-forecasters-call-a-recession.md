The chancellor presented his autumn statement on Thursday and buried what was left of the budget that had brought down the previous government. Jeremy Hunt found about fifty-five billion pounds a year through a mixture of tax rises and slower spending, and he said the priority was to keep the markets calm. The energy price guarantee will be capped at a lower level from April and become targeted, which means the average household bill will rise again. The statement was accompanied by a forecast from the fiscal watchdog, and the forecast is the part that mattered.

Forecasters at the Office for Budget Responsibility expect output to fall by about one and a half per cent next year and to recover slowly after that, and they expect inflation to stay above seven per cent through 2023. It described the fall in living standards as the largest since records began, with real household incomes about seven per cent lower by the end of the period than they were before the pandemic. The watchdog also said the budget would meet the government's own rule of getting debt falling as a share of output within five years, though by a margin of about nine billion pounds.

The measures are familiar in their direction and painful in their detail. The threshold at which the forty-five per cent rate begins will fall from a hundred and fifty thousand pounds to a hundred and twenty-five, the freeze on income tax thresholds will last two more years, and the cap on council tax and on social rents will rise. The windfall tax on oil and gas producers goes up, and an equivalent charge is extended to electricity generators. Spending on public services will grow more slowly than inflation, which means cuts in real terms for most departments.

Markets took the statement better than they had taken the budget in September. The yield on ten-year gilts fell on the day, sterling rose, and the mortgage lenders that had withdrawn products in the autumn began to return. The improvement is relative: rates are still at their highest in fourteen years, and the cost of borrowing for the government is higher than it was a year ago. Investors said the statement had removed the risk of an unfunded promise, which was the risk that had caused the crisis.

Politics is harder than the arithmetic here. The prime minister had promised during the summer that he would not raise taxes, and the statement raises several. The opposition called it a budget of broken promises and pointed out that the target for the top rate has been lowered while the burden on working households grows. The party's own benches include members who campaigned on tax cuts and who now have to vote for increases, and the lobby afterwards was full of people explaining that the numbers had changed.

For households the statement is a calculation about the next two years. The energy cap protects until April, the payments to pensioners and benefit claimants continue, and the freeze on thresholds pulls more people into higher bands as wages rise. Nobody in the chamber pretended that the country would feel better soon, and the chancellor's own phrase was that these are difficult decisions. The forecasters agree on the direction, and they differ only on how long it lasts.
