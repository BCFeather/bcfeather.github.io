IgA nephropathy is the commonest primary glomerular disease in many countries, and its course is measured in decades of slowly declining kidney function. Protein in the urine is the variable that predicts that decline, so a drug that lowers proteinuria is judged on that number before anyone knows whether dialysis is delayed. That endpoint is what the field uses because complete remission is rare and partial remission still predicts a slower loss of filtration.

The phase 3 trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 13 May randomized 318 patients, 159 to telitacicept and 159 to placebo. At week 39 the 24-hour urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio fell 58.9 per cent with telitacicept and 8.8 per cent with placebo, a relative difference of 55.0 per cent.

Estimated glomerular filtration rate moved differently as well. It fell 1.0 per cent from baseline in the telitacicept group and 7.7 per cent in the placebo group, which is a much smaller decline over the same period and the kind of signal that supports a disease-modifying claim rather than a symptomatic one. A fall of that size is unusual in this disease, where the standard of care already includes renin-angiotensin blockade and, increasingly, a sodium-glucose cotransporter inhibitor.

Telitacicept is a fusion protein that blocks two cytokines involved in B-cell survival and activation, which is why it belongs to the same family of approaches as belimumab in lupus. The rationale in IgA nephropathy is that the disease is driven by galactose-deficient IgA1 and its immune complexes, and reducing B-cell output reduces the supply of the antibody that starts the chain. Two cytokines rather than one is a pharmacological bet that the pathway is redundant, and the trial's proteinuria result is the first clinical evidence that the bet pays.

Safety was acceptable rather than clean. Adverse events occurred in 89.3 per cent of treated patients and 78.6 per cent of controls. Infections are the expected cost of B-cell blockade, and a nephrology population already carries immunosuppression from steroids and from the disease itself. Long-term B-cell blockade carries the risk that vaccines stop working, and the trial's safety reporting will be read closely by nephrologists who already manage immunosuppressed patients.

The caveat is that proteinuria is a surrogate. The trial is an interim analysis, and the endpoint that regulators and patients care about is a composite of kidney failure, transplant and sustained loss of filtration, which takes years to accumulate. A 55 per cent reduction in protein is a strong predictor of benefit and not a substitute for it, and the follow-up that establishes the difference is the part still to come. Regulators have accepted proteinuria as a basis for accelerated approval in this disease, which means the surrogate may be enough for a conditional license and not enough for a permanent one. Until then the result is the strongest evidence yet that B-cell blockade works in this disease.
