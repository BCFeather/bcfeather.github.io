Video assistance was introduced to fix a specific problem: decisions that were obviously wrong, in a sport where one decision can decide a match. A decade on, the technology has been studied enough that it can be assessed as an institution rather than a gadget, and the assessment is more interesting than the argument it replaced.

Accuracy evidence is reasonably clear. Studies across several leagues find that the technology raises the share of correct decisions on the four categories it reviews, goals, penalties, red cards and mistaken identity, by a meaningful margin. The errors that remain are mostly judgment calls, offside margins measured in centimetres, and handball interpretations that changed with the technology rather than before it. The technology did not create those ambiguities; it made them auditable.

Time and flow carry the cost. A review stops the match, and the stoppage is longer than the decision it replaces. Leagues have tried to limit it with protocols, a clear-and-obvious threshold, and time limits for the review itself, and the effect has been a compromise: fewer wrong decisions, and a game that is interrupted at moments of maximum emotion. That trade is a design choice, and the designers did not model it.

Legitimacy is the second cost. A decision that is reviewed is a decision that is owned by a process rather than by a person, and when the process produces an outcome the crowd disagrees with, the disagreement has no human target. The stadium chants at the screen. That is a change in how authority works in the sport, and it is not obviously an improvement, even when the decision is correct.

Studies cannot measure the third cost: the effect on how players play. An attacker who knows that a marginal offside will be caught may hold the run a fraction longer; a defender who knows that a penalty-area contact will be reviewed may challenge differently. The game adapts to the instrument, and the instrument was calibrated on the game before the adaptation.

Governing bodies have responded with transparency, releasing public explanations of decisions and audio of the review conversation after matches. That is the right direction, and it is slow. A decision that comes with an explanation is more defensible than one that does not, even when the explanation is unsatisfying.

Semi-automated offside is the latest addition, using limb tracking from a set of cameras and a ball with an inertial sensor to place the moment of the pass. It removes the manual line-drawing that produced the notorious armpit offsides, and it introduces a different problem: a measurement precise to a few centimetres is now applied to a rule that was written when a linesman's eye was the instrument.

Technology in sport generally supplies the lesson. A tool that improves accuracy is not the same as a tool that improves the game, and the difference is decided by the protocol around the tool. The video assistant has made football more accurate and less continuous, and the argument about the balance is now a permanent part of the sport rather than a controversy to be resolved.
