Postpartum anaemia is common and undertreated. Blood loss at delivery is routine, the iron reserves of pregnancy are depleted by the fetus, and the result is a woman who is tired, breathless and less able to care for a newborn at exactly the moment when she has the least capacity to spare. Oral iron is the standard treatment in most of the world, and oral iron is slow, poorly tolerated and inconsistently absorbed.

A trial in Nigeria tested the alternative. The IVON-PP trial, led by Professor Bosede Afolabi at the University of Lagos with collaborators in the United Kingdom and funded by the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, randomised women with postpartum anaemia to a single intravenous infusion of ferric carboxymaltose or to oral ferrous sulphate twice daily for six weeks. The results appeared in *The Lancet Global Health* on 18 March.

Intravenous iron corrected haemoglobin faster and restored iron stores more completely. The women who received the infusion reached target haemoglobin sooner, their ferritin rose to levels that oral iron does not reach in the same period, and the effect was still present six months after delivery, which is the point at which the next pregnancy may already be being planned.

Safety was the question that mattered most, because intravenous iron has a history. Older formulations carried a risk of anaphylaxis, and the current agents, given as a large single dose, were introduced with a warning about hypersensitivity. The trial reports the infusion as tolerable and safe in this population, which is the statement a health ministry needs before it changes a protocol.

Implementation is the hard part, and the trial was designed with it in mind. A single infusion requires a clinic visit, a trained nurse and a cold chain, and it replaces six weeks of tablets that a woman may not take, may not tolerate and may not be able to afford to refill. Whether a health system prefers one visit to forty-two doses depends on which resource is scarcer, and in much of sub-Saharan Africa the answer is not obvious, because a clinic that can give an infusion is a clinic that already has a nurse and a refrigerator.

Applicability outside Nigeria is the constraint on generalising. The baseline prevalence of anaemia, the causes of blood loss and the local diet differ across the region, and a trial that works where hookworm and malaria are common is not automatically a trial that works in a setting where they are absent. The result is strong evidence for the setting studied, and a reason for other settings to run their own version and not assume the answer.

The broader argument is about how obstetric care is organised. Postpartum haemorrhage is treated as an emergency and postpartum anaemia as a follow-up problem, and a single-dose treatment turns the second into something that can be resolved before discharge. That is a change in the shape of care more than in the drug, and it is the kind of change that saves a woman's year, not only her life.
