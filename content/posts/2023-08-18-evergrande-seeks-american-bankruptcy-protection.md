China Evergrande filed for recognition under Chapter 15 of the American bankruptcy code on 17 August, a procedure that lets a foreign company's restructuring be acknowledged by an American court. The company, which reported liabilities of more than two trillion yuan in its last full accounts and owes offshore creditors more than three hundred billion dollars, has been negotiating with them for two years and has proposed a plan that would hand them a slice of its property management and electric vehicle units. Filing in Manhattan gives those creditors a forum in which a judge can police the process, and gives the company a stay against suits elsewhere.

Timing reflects a court date in the Cayman Islands. A winding-up petition brought by a creditor in Hong Kong was adjourned to allow the restructuring to proceed, and the offshore plan needs the approval of a majority of each class of debt. Some holders have objected that the terms value their claims at a fraction of what comparable developers have offered, and the company has warned that a failure to agree would leave them with whatever a liquidation produced, which is likely to be very little. A hearing in the Cayman court is scheduled for September.

The crisis is wider than one company. Country Garden, the largest developer by sales, missed a coupon payment in August and warned of losses, and several smaller builders have defaulted. New home sales have fallen for more than a year, investment in property has contracted every month since 2022, and local governments that relied on land sales to fund themselves have cut services. Construction accounts for a quarter of output once its supply chain is counted.

Policy has been cautious and local. Beijing has cut mortgage rates, eased down-payment rules in some cities and told banks to lend to selected projects, and it has declined to mount the kind of rescue that would guarantee the debts of private developers. The government has instead set up a fund to finish unfinished homes, financed by loans that local authorities must repay. Officials have said the aim is to protect buyers of unfinished homes and to consolidate the industry around state-backed firms, which is a plan for the aftermath rather than for the crisis.

Households feel the consequences directly. Chinese families hold most of their wealth in housing, and a market that falls for two years changes how they spend, which is part of why consumer prices are now falling. Local governments have borrowed through financing vehicles that were repaid by land sales, and those vehicles are now the second front in the same crisis.

A developer that once symbolized the country's construction boom, and at its peak employed tens of thousands of people across hundreds of projects, is asking a court in New York to supervise its unwinding, while its government asks the market to sort the industry out. The restructuring will decide who absorbs the losses, and the answer will shape how Chinese households treat property for a generation.
