American employers added 165,000 jobs in October, according to figures published on Friday, more than double the 65,000 that economists had expected and the third-largest monthly gain this year. Unemployment fell to 4.1 per cent from 4.2 per cent, the number of unemployed stood at 7.07 million, and the participation rate rose to 61.9 per cent from 61.8 per cent.

Average hourly earnings for private employees reached 38 dollars, up 0.4 per cent on the month, the sharpest monthly increase since November 2025, and 3.1 per cent higher than a year earlier. Production and non-supervisory staff earned 32.75 dollars an hour, also up 0.4 per cent, the average private workweek held at 34.4 hours, and the wider U6 measure of underemployment rose to 7.5 per cent, a reminder that the headline rate counts only part of the slack.

Revisions did much of the work. August payroll growth was raised to 186,000 from 133,000 and September to 60,000 from 29,000, a combined upgrade of 84,000 jobs, which turned a soft autumn into a firm one. October's gain compares with a twelve-month average of 48,000, and weekly claims for unemployment benefits fell to 189,000 at the end of the month against an October average of 195,000, the lowest reading of the year.

Private employers contributed 159,000 of the new positions and government 6,000. Professional and business services added 70,000, against a twelve-month average of 15,000 for that industry, transport and warehousing added 25,000, manufacturing 19,000 and financial activities 7,000. Construction shed 19,000, and leisure, hospitality, wholesale and retail were all softer than in September, so the strength was concentrated in offices rather than in shops, and the goods-producing side of the economy contributed almost nothing at all.

Traders moved quickly. The CME FedWatch tool put the probability of an increase at the December meeting at 76.8 per cent, nearly 10 points higher than a day earlier. Officials raised rates in September for the first time in more than three years and left them unchanged in October, and a report this strong removes much of the argument for a second pause. Peter Cardillo of Spartan Capital called it a good set of numbers, and the market agreed with him.

One caveat cuts the other way. Economists note that the labour force is growing slowly as immigration tightens and retirements mount, so a far smaller monthly gain now holds unemployment steady than was needed in 2024, when the figure was around 150,000; some estimates put the current breakeven nearer 40,000. Measured against that background, 165,000 is solid rather than spectacular, and three-month annualized wage growth of 3.9 per cent is the number that will occupy the policy meeting in mid-December.
