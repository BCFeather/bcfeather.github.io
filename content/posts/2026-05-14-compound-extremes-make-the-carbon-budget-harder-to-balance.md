description: "Hot-humid spells and drought-heat events grow more frequent with cumulative carbon dioxide emissions, and the severest combinations rise fastest, a modelling study in *Nature* finds."
---

Climate risk is usually counted one hazard at a time: a heatwave, a drought, a flood. The events that do damage tend to arrive together, and a paper in *Nature* on 14 May argues that the pairing has a direct relationship with how much carbon dioxide has been emitted in total.

The authors examined compound extremes, meaning combinations such as heat with high humidity and drought with heat, and compared their frequency against cumulative emissions rather than against the concentration in a single year. Cumulative emissions are the quantity that a carbon budget is supposed to constrain, so tying hazard frequency to them makes the physical risk and the policy accounting speak the same language. Concentration at a given moment and cumulative emissions are related but not interchangeable, and which one is used changes what a tonne of carbon is worth.

Frequency does not rise linearly. The study reports that the more severe the compound event, the faster its frequency rises as emissions accumulate, which means that the tail of the distribution moves first. A climate that looks tolerable in the mean can still become intolerable in the rare week that combines heat with moisture that makes sweating useless.

That distinction matters for adaptation. Heat action plans are built around temperature thresholds, and humidity changes what a given temperature does to a body. Drought planning is built around rainfall totals, and the arrival of heat on top of a dry soil changes both the fire risk and the crop loss. Two moderate hazards can produce an outcome that neither would produce alone, and the joint distribution is where the damage lives.

The policy implication the authors draw is that the 1.5 degree framing is not only about a global mean. If compound frequency scales with cumulative emissions, then every additional tonne carries a non-linear increment of joint risk, and the accounting used to set nationally determined contributions should reflect the combination rather than the average. Whether the models are right at the regional scale is the open question, and regional projections of joint extremes are exactly where the evidence is thinnest. A global relationship can be solid while a river basin's projection is unusable, and the paper's policy claim depends on the regional reading more than on the global one.

What the paper changes is the shape of the argument. The carbon budget has been treated as a quantity that sets a temperature; it also sets a rate at which the worst combinations of weather arrive. Framing the budget in those terms makes the cost of delay visible in a way that a mean temperature anomaly does not, and it gives adaptation planners a variable they can actually design for. Adaptation budgets, unlike emissions targets, are spent locally and by decade, and a hazard that scales with cumulative emissions gives local planners a reason to care about a global number.
