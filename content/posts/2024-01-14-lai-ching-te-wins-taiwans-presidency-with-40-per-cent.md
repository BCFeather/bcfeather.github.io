Voting closed at four in the afternoon on the thirteenth of January and the count was clear by the evening. Lai Ching-te, the candidate of the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), took about 40.05 per cent of the 'presidential' vote, with his running mate Hsiao Bi-khim. Hou Yu-ih of the Kuomintang (KMT) took 33.49 per cent and Ko Wen-je of the Taiwan People's Party took 26.46 per cent. In the simultaneous election for the Legislative Yuan, the result ran the other way. The KMT won 52 seats, the DPP 51, the People's Party 8, and independents 2, out of 113. For the first time since the DPP took the 'presidency' in 2016, the ruling party holds the executive and not the chamber.

The shape of the result is easy to read and hard to interpret. Lai's margin over Hou was about six and a half points, which is not close, and it was produced by holding most of the DPP's core vote while the opposition split. The two challengers between them took nearly sixty per cent, and the People's Party's twenty-six per cent came largely from young voters who wanted neither of the older options. That is a majority of the electorate that did not choose the winner, in a system where a plurality decides, and both of the losing camps have already begun to argue that the result is narrower than the number suggests.

The campaign turned on questions that were not entirely about the cross-strait relationship, which is the thing every outside observer watches for. Housing prices, wages for people in their twenties, the cost of childcare, the state of the semiconductor industry and the energy system were the issues that filled the television debates. Lai campaigned on continuity, promising to keep the informal arrangement with the United States and to avoid the provocations that Beijing has warned against. Hou promised to restore dialogue with the mainland and to reopen the tourist routes that closed during the pandemic. Ko promised to run the government like a business and to stop arguing about things that cannot be changed.

Governing is now the harder problem. A 'president' whose party does not control the legislature cannot pass a budget without the consent of the two opposition parties, and those two parties do not agree with each other on much beyond their dislike of the DPP. The People's Party has eight seats, which is enough to decide votes and not enough to set policy, and its leader has already said that he will support whatever is reasonable and oppose whatever is not. That is the language of a party that intends to be courted, and the new government will spend its first year being courted and blocked in roughly equal measure.

Beijing's response came within hours and was sharper than usual. The Taiwan Affairs Office said the result showed that the DPP did not represent the mainstream of opinion on the island, a formulation that appears to be preparing for the argument that the legislature's composition, rather than the 'presidential' vote, is the real expression of the electorate. Military pressure has continued at the same pace as before the election, with aircraft crossing the median line of the strait on most days. The next four years are likely to be defined by a 'president' who cannot legislate freely, an opposition that can block him, and a government across the water that will read every deadlock in Taipei as evidence of its own argument. That is a stable arrangement, and a combustible one.
