A virus that infects a tumour and kills it is an old idea that has repeatedly failed in the clinic, and the product that became the first approved oncolytic virus did so in a disease where nothing else works. The phase 2 trial behind that approval enrolled 19 adults, and an estimate from 19 patients is the kind of evidence that a desperate indication can accept.

Enrolment covered adults with residual or recurrent glioblastoma after standard therapy and administered G47Δ, a third-generation herpes simplex virus, intratumorally and repeatedly for up to six doses. The paper appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 21 July 2022. A third-generation herpes virus with deletions that remove its neurovirulence is a virus engineered to be safe in the brain, and the engineering is the reason it could be given repeatedly without the encephalitis that a wild-type herpes virus would risk.

Its primary endpoint, one-year survival after initiation, was 84.2 per cent, with 16 of 19 patients alive, and the prespecified endpoint was met, so the trial terminated early. Median overall survival was 20.2 months from initiation and 28.8 months from the first surgery. A one-year survival rate of 84 per cent against a historical expectation of roughly 50 is the comparison that a single-arm design has to rely on, and the comparison is historical and not concurrent.

Fever was the most common related adverse event, in 17 of 19, followed by vomiting, nausea, lymphocytopenia and leukopenia. Imaging showed enlargement of the target lesion with clearing of contrast enhancement after each administration, which is the radiological signature of an inflammatory response rather than of progression, and mistaking one for the other is the hazard that the trial had to manage.

Best overall response was a partial response in one patient and stable disease in 18, and biopsies showed rising CD4 and CD8 infiltration with persistently low Foxp3 cells. A survival benefit without shrinkage is the pattern that immunotherapy often produces, and the pattern is why overall survival became the endpoint rather than response rate.

A single-arm trial cannot separate the virus from the natural history of a selected population, and enrolment selects the patients well enough to be enrolled. The early termination means that the estimate rests on 19 patients, which is a small number for a survival claim. An imaging change that looks like progression and is not progression is the sort of ambiguity that a larger trial would have to resolve with a defined rule.

Approval in Japan is a regulatory decision on a single-arm study in a disease with no alternatives, and the decision is defensible on those terms. A randomized trial in a less desperate population is the study that would establish the size of the benefit, and the size is what a payer needs before a product enters routine practice, because a therapy that costs a great deal and adds months is a therapy that a health system has to justify.
