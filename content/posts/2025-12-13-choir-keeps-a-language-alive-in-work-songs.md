A language's survival is usually measured in speakers, and a choir measures something else: the settings in which the language is used. A choir of 30 singers, half of them over 70, has become the main place where one language is spoken daily, and its repertoire is a record of a working life.

Work songs make up the repertoire. They were sung to coordinate rowing, hauling nets and pounding grain, and each has a rhythm that matches the task rather than a musical phrase. The choir's conductor has transcribed 140 of them and recorded the task each belongs to.

Transcription is the technical problem. A work song's rhythm is not a fixed meter, and a metronome's grid flattens the phrasing. The conductor's notation records the task's cycle as the bar, which is a decision that the singers recognize as correct.

The language's grammar is preserved in the lyrics. The songs use verb forms and pronouns that younger speakers have replaced, and a linguist working with the choir has published a sketch grammar based on the corpus.

The arrangement works for a social reason. Rehearsal is weekly and in the language, and the singers who are fluent are the oldest. A younger singer learns by singing, which is the only setting in which an elder will correct a pronunciation without embarrassment.

A class and a choir teach different halves of a language. A class teaches vocabulary and grammar, and a choir teaches use with a rhythm that carries the words. The two are complements and the choir is the one that is scarce.

The repertoire's future is the open question. Two of the songs have been arranged for a school choir, and the conductor reports that the children sing the words accurately and do not know what they mean, which is a start and not a transmission.

A song fixes a phrase in a way a word list does not. A work song's rhythm carries the syllables and its repetition across a crew makes the words memorable, and a singer who learns the song has learned the phrase with its stress.

A recording is not a transmission. A tape preserves a pronunciation that no one uses, and a singer who learns from a tape reproduces the sound without the context. The choir's value is that an elder is present to correct, which is the part a recording lacks.

A choir's funding is a cultural budget line and its output is not a product. A grant that pays a conductor and a hall is a grant that buys rehearsals, and a rehearsal is the unit in which a language is transmitted. The arithmetic is unusual because the output is counted in hours of use.

A language's last domain is often a rehearsal room, and songs are what make it one. When a language leaves the workplace and the kitchen, a choir's rehearsal can be a domain that remains, and the songs are what makes it one.
