Sweden became the thirty-second member of NATO on the seventh of March, when its prime minister deposited the instrument of accession with the American government in Washington, which is the depositary for the treaty. Foreign minister signed the document in the presence of the secretary of state, and the flag was raised at the alliance's headquarters in Brussels the following Monday. Accession completed a process that had begun with an application in May 2022, three months after the Russian invasion of Ukraine.

Delay in the process came from two members with objections. Turkey held up ratification for twenty months, demanding action against Kurdish groups that it considers terrorist organizations and seeking approval for its own purchase of American fighter aircraft. Hungary delayed the vote until February, when its parliament finally ratified after the government of Viktor Orban dropped its opposition. Both countries extracted concessions that the Swedish government described as consistent with its laws and that critics described as unrelated to the alliance.

Military significance of the accession is mostly about geography. Sweden brings a coastline along the Baltic Sea, an air force with modern fighters and a defence industry that produces aircraft, missiles and radar. Its territory connects Norway and Finland to the rest of the alliance, and it turns the Baltic into a sea that is surrounded by members, which changes the calculations for the Russian fleet based at Kaliningrad and in the Gulf of Finland.

Non-alignment that Sweden is abandoning has a history of more than two hundred years. The country stayed out of the Napoleonic wars, both world wars and the cold war, and its policy was built on the argument that a small state could protect itself by avoiding alliances. That argument lost its force after February 2022, when the government concluded that the country could not defend itself alone and that the public had changed its mind. Support for membership rose from about thirty per cent to more than sixty within weeks of the invasion.

Terms on which Sweden enters are different from those of a founding member. It has no nuclear weapons and does not want them on its soil in peacetime, and it has said it will not host permanent NATO bases or troops. Those restrictions are political rather than legal, and the alliance has accepted them, which is the arrangement that Sweden negotiated to make membership acceptable to its voters.

What followed for the region is a north that is now a single strategic space. Finland joined in April 2023 and Sweden a year later, and the two countries have begun to plan their defence together, including the use of each other's airfields and ports. Russia has said it will respond by strengthening its forces in the north-west, and it has already announced the recreation of the Moscow and Leningrad military districts. What the seventh of March changed was the map of the alliance. Whether it makes the Baltic safer or simply moves the confrontation is a question that the next few years will answer.
