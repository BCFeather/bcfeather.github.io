description: "Aerosol, trace gas and meteorological records from the Amazon Tall Tower Observatory show that rainfall regularly triggers bursts of nanoparticles inside the forest canopy during the wet season."
---

Aerosol particles seed clouds, and over the Amazon rainforest in the wet season their origin has been uncertain. A paper in *Nature Geoscience* on 8 November 2024 attributes a regular source to rain falling inside the canopy. Wet-season aerosol over an intact forest is the background against which the bursts stand out, and a background is what makes a burst measurable at all. A burst is a short event against a steady background, and the sampling rate of the tower is what makes the two separable.

The measurements come from the Amazon Tall Tower Observatory, which records aerosol, trace gas and meteorological variables above and within the forest. A tall tower is what allows a signal to be assigned to a height. Rain that reaches the canopy releases compounds from leaves and soil, which is a chemistry a tower samples more easily than a chamber does.

Rainfall is followed by bursts of nanoparticles, and the bursts occur within the canopy rather than above it, which locates the source in the forest instead of in the free atmosphere. Nanoparticles that grow into cloud condensation nuclei are the link between the bursts and the clouds, and that link is what a climate model needs. Height is the discriminator here, since a signal that peaks inside the canopy cannot have been delivered from the cloud layer above.

Earlier work had suggested a downward flux of particles during precipitation, and the new record refines that picture by showing that the particles are produced where the rain lands. Regularity distinguishes a source from an event, and a parameterized source is what a model can incorporate while an event it cannot. Source and flux are different questions, and a paper that answers one of them has not answered the other.

The mechanism is not identified in detail. Rain wets surfaces, releases volatile compounds and creates local humidity excursions, and any of these could nucleate particles. The canopy is a reactor that the free atmosphere is not, so treating the forest as a flat surface is the approximation the result undermines. Humidity excursions are local and short, and a nucleation event that follows one is an event a chamber can reproduce.

Frequency is the notable result. The events are regular rather than exceptional, which makes them a candidate for the background aerosol that the wet-season forest maintains. A downward flux during precipitation had been inferred from outflow measurements, and the new record shows the flux and the source are different questions.

On the modelling side, the implication for climate is that a forest producing its own aerosol is a forest whose cloud cover depends on its biology, and that coupling is one that models represent poorly. Chamber work would separate the candidate mechanisms, while the tower supplies the boundary conditions that no chamber reproduces. A model that cannot represent the canopy as a source will underestimate wet-season aerosol and therefore cloud cover.
