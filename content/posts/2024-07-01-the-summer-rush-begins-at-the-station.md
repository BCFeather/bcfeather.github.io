Summer travel opens on the first of July. It runs for sixty-two days, until the end of August, and it covers the school holidays, the Olympic fortnight and the return to term. Railways add several hundred pairs of trains to the timetable for the period. The traffic is not evenly spread. First days carry students going home. Middle of the month carries families going to a coast or a mountain. Last week of August carries everyone back at once, which is the tightest part of the schedule.

Stations handle the crowd with moves rehearsed every year. Waiting hall is divided into zones by train number. Escalators are switched to run upward in the morning and downward in the evening. A temporary fence is put up to separate the queue for the security check from the queue for the ticket office. The ticket allocation is the constraint. A line that runs eight trains a day in March runs fourteen in July. Extra paths come from shortening the turnaround at each end. A train that arrives at ten past the hour leaves at twenty past, which gives the cleaners eight minutes.

Student tickets are a discount rather than a reservation. A student buys a half-price seat and the seat is taken from the same pool as everyone else's. Pool is what runs out first, which is why a family booking in June finds the morning trains full. The station at night is a different place. A train that leaves at one in the morning carries people who have worked a full day and who will sleep through the journey. Shops are shuttered. Lights are dimmed. Platform is quiet enough to hear the announcement twice.

Luggage is the visible sign of the season. A family of four with three suitcases, a folded pram and a bag of fruit moves through the hall at the speed of its slowest member. Staff at the gate have learned to let the group through in one block. The heat inside the hall is the part that the timetable does not address. A waiting room with ten thousand people in it is warmer than the street. Air conditioning runs at full capacity and the temperature still climbs through the afternoon.

Station staff work the season as overtime. A ticket inspector who normally checks two trains a shift checks five. A cleaner who has a fixed round is moved to the platform that the late train uses. Payments are made in August, and the staff who take them plan a holiday in September. The station also sells the journey. A shop that sells a neck pillow, a cup of instant noodles and a phone charger earns more in July than in the rest of the year, because the passenger who is about to sit for six hours buys all three.

Last student train ends the season. Extra paths are withdrawn. Temporary fence comes down, and the timetable returns to the shape it had in June. By the last week of August the platforms are quiet enough for an announcement to carry, and the staff who worked the peak take their holiday before the school term starts.
