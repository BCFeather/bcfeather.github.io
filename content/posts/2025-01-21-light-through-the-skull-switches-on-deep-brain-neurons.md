Deep brain stimulation is a treatment for Parkinson's disease, for essential tremor and for epilepsy, and it works by implanting electrodes in the brain and passing current through them. The operation is invasive, the hardware has to be replaced, and the stimulation is not selective: a electrode that reaches the subthalamic nucleus also affects the tissue around it, which is why the tuning of a device takes months.

Optogenetics offered selectivity and demanded genetics. A light-sensitive protein from a bacterium is expressed in a chosen population of neurons, and a fibre optic cable delivers light to them. The technique transformed neuroscience, and it has not transformed neurology, because inserting a gene into a patient's brain is a regulatory and ethical barrier that few programmes have crossed, and the ones that did were treating inherited blindness, where the alternative is nothing.

A paper in *Science Advances* on 21 January describes a middle path. The authors injected upconversion nanoparticles into the brain. These particles absorb near-infrared light, which penetrates skin, skull and tissue with little loss, and emit visible light at a wavelength that excites the neurons around them. The particle does the conversion that a gene would otherwise do.

In mice, the treatment produced stimulation of deep brain structures after near-infrared light was applied to the head, and the effect was reversible when the light was switched off. The authors tested the approach in a model of Parkinson's disease and reported an improvement in motor behaviour, which is the outcome that a clinical programme would need before it went further.

Advantages over electrodes are the ones the design intends: no craniotomy, no implanted hardware, no gene therapy, and a stimulation that can be aimed by aiming the light. The advantages over optogenetics are the same, minus the selectivity, because the particles stimulate whatever is near them and not a chosen cell type.

Questions are the ones that any new intracranial material raises. Nanoparticles have to be cleared or to stay without harm, and the paper reports no inflammatory response at the tested time points, which is short. The heating that the particles produce has to be small enough not to damage tissue and large enough to work, and the window between safe and effective heating is narrow, which makes it the parameter that a clinical device would have to hold across patients whose skull thickness, hair and head shape all differ.

Demonstration in mice is not a therapy, and the paper is careful about the difference. The distance from a mouse brain to a human one is a distance in scale, in skull thickness and in the regulatory path, and the authors present the work as a platform that others can build on. A platform is the right description, because the particles can be conjugated to targets and the light can be shaped, and those are the two dials that the next experiments will turn.
