The International Monetary Fund published its world economic outlook on 11 October, ahead of its annual meetings in Washington. It cut the forecast for global growth in 2023 to 2.7 per cent from 2.9, and it held the figure for 2022 at 3.2. The fund publishes two updates a year, in January and in April, and both revised the number upward.

Each of the three would take about a point off growth, and they were arriving at the same time. The fund said the outlook was unusually uncertain and that the balance of risks was to the downside. It described three shocks: the war in Ukraine, the energy crisis in Europe and the tightening of monetary policy everywhere. Advanced economies were forecast to grow 1.1 per cent in 2023, and the euro area 0.5. Germany was forecast to shrink 0.3 per cent, and Italy 0.2. The fund said a third of the world economy would experience two consecutive quarters of contraction, which is the common definition of a recession. Its chief economist said the three shocks reinforced each other rather than arriving separately.

America was forecast to grow 1 per cent in 2023, and the fund said the country would avoid a recession because of its tight labour market and its energy independence. The forecast was more optimistic than the market's, and the fund's chief economist said the chance of a soft landing was about one in three.

Emerging markets were mixed. China was forecast to grow 4.4 per cent, cut from 4.6, and the fund said the zero-covid policy was the main drag. India was forecast to grow 6.1, and Brazil 1. The fund said the dollar's strength was adding to the debt burden of countries that borrow in the American currency. Japan was forecast to grow 1.6 per cent, and Britain 0.3.

A chapter on the dollar and on the cost of the energy shock was included in the report. It estimated that the terms-of-trade loss for Europe was 3 per cent of output, which is larger than the oil shocks of the 1970s. It also said that the dollar's rise had pushed up import costs for emerging markets by 10 per cent in a year.
