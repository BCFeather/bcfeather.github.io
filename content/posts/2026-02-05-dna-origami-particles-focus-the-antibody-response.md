Vaccines that aim to elicit broadly neutralizing antibodies against HIV have a specific problem: the B cells that make such antibodies are rare, and the antigens that engage them are the ones that most B cells ignore. Presenting many copies of an antigen on a particle increases the chance that a rare B cell will encounter it, and the particles used so far have been made of protein, which is itself immunogenic.

Romanov and colleagues built the particle out of DNA instead. DNA origami folds a long single strand into a defined shape with addressable attachment points, so the number and spacing of antigens on the surface can be specified rather than approximated. They combined multiple copies of an HIV antigen with synthetic T cell epitopes and immunised animals, comparing the response with that produced by a conventional protein nanoparticle. The work appeared in *Science* on 5 February, with a Perspective by Bannard and Howarth.

Two results distinguish the DNA particle. It produced limited nonspecific B cell responses, unlike the protein nanoparticle, which generated antibodies against the scaffold as well as against the antigen. It also boosted antigen-specific B cells that produce broadly neutralizing antibodies to a greater extent, which is the outcome the design was aiming at. Both advantages come from the same property, which is that every position on the scaffold is addressable by design.

Scaffold immunogenicity is a familiar obstacle. A protein particle presents its own surface to the immune system, and a response against that surface consumes part of the response that the antigen was supposed to receive. A DNA scaffold is less immunogenic, and the reduction in off-target response is not a marginal refinement but a redirection of the entire response towards the target.

Precision of spacing is the second advantage and the more interesting one. B cell activation depends on how antigens are arranged, since a B cell receptor has to bind several copies at once to trigger, and DNA origami allows the spacing to be tuned and tested. The paper treats geometry as a design parameter, which is what distinguishes an engineering approach from an empirical one. Whether that tuning survives in a primate or a human immune system is the test that a mouse cannot supply.

Translation is a long way off. DNA nanostructures are not yet a licensed vaccine platform, they degrade in serum unless stabilized, and manufacturing at scale has been demonstrated for small batches and not for millions of doses. The immunology is promising and the chemistry is not yet industrial. Stabilization strategies exist, and each one adds a manufacturing step that a licensed product would have to validate.

The result does establish a principle that applies beyond HIV. If the scaffold can be made invisible to the immune system and the antigen array can be specified, then the immune response can be directed with a precision that protein particles do not allow. That is a platform claim, and platforms are judged over a decade.
