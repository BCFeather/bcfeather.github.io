Perovskite photovoltaics reached high efficiencies quickly because the absorber is forgiving: it tolerates defects, it can be deposited from solution, and its bandgap can be tuned. The efficiency record nevertheless stalled in the low twenties, and the reason had less to do with the absorber than with the layers that carry charge away from it. A cell that absorbs light well but cannot extract the current has a high open-circuit voltage and a poor fill factor. The same absorber that tolerates defects also tolerates the interfacial losses that a silicon cell would not survive, which is why the transport layers, not the perovskite, set the ceiling.

Fill factor is the ratio of the power a cell actually delivers to the product of its open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current. It falls when series resistance rises, and series resistance in a perovskite cell concentrates in the electron transport layer, a thin oxide that must conduct electrons while blocking holes. Improving that layer without damaging the perovskite beneath it is the problem the authors of a *Nature* paper set out to solve.

Their route was a reverse-doping process. Instead of adding dopants to titanium oxide after deposition, they grew nitrogen-doped titanium oxide in which the dopant was introduced during synthesis, and they report charge transport performance that exceeds the conventional material. The paper, published in *Nature*, 26 January 2022, puts the improvement into complete cells rather than into isolated films.

On 1-square-centimetre devices the fill factor exceeded 86 per cent, and the average across the batch was 85.3 per cent. A certified steady-state efficiency of 22.6 per cent was measured on a 1-square-centimetre cell, and a reverse current-voltage scan returned 23.33 per cent with a spread of 0.58 percentage points. The distinction between the steady-state and scan numbers matters, because perovskite cells can hold a transient charge that inflates a fast sweep. Certified measurement at a fixed operating point is the figure a manufacturer would quote, and the gap between 22.6 and 23.33 per cent is roughly the size of the hysteresis that certification removes.

Nitrogen substitution explains the transport gain in a straightforward way. Replacing some oxygen sites with nitrogen introduces states that donate electrons and raise the film's conductivity without the oxygen vacancies that usually accompany chemical doping, and a more conductive transport layer lowers the series resistance that limits the fill factor. The reverse process also avoids the post-treatment step that often damages the perovskite interface.

What the paper does not show is lifetime. A 1-square-centimetre cell is an improvement on the millimetre-scale devices that dominate the literature, and 22.6 per cent is below the best small cells, but the reported measurements cover initial performance rather than hundreds of hours of operation. Stability under illumination and heat is the test that perovskite technology keeps failing.

Scaling further is the next question. A centimetre is not a module, and the series resistance that limits a small cell behaves differently when the transparent contact must span 15 centimetres. The reverse-doping route is cheap and compatible with solution processing, which is why it will be tried at larger areas quickly.
