Ferdinand Marcos Jr won the Philippine presidency on 9 May with 31,629,783 votes, or 58.8 per cent of those cast, against 15,035,773 for his nearest rival, Vice-President Leni Robredo, who took 27.9 per cent. His running mate, Sara Duterte, the mayor of Davao and the daughter of the outgoing president, collected more than thirty-two million votes for the vice-presidency, which in a country where the two offices are elected separately meant that the second place on the ticket outpolled the first.

Congress proclaimed the result on 25 May after canvassing certificates from the provinces, and the inauguration was set for 30 June at the Quirino grandstand in Manila. The campaign had been built on a single word, unity, and on an organization that reached barangay level through local officials, while the candidate declined to appear in debates and left the arguing to surrogates. Turnout was high, with more than fifty-three million ballots cast in the presidential race, and the machinery that had twice delivered Rodrigo Duterte transferred its loyalty without much trouble.

The history is the part that cannot be set aside. Marcos's father ruled from 1965 until 1981 as president and then as a self-declared strongman until the family left Malacañang in a helicopter in February 1986, a period that included nine years of martial law, the suspension of the legislature, the closure of newspapers and thousands of killings and disappearances. The son has campaigned for years on a version of that period in which the country was orderly and the infrastructure was built, and on social media channels that repeated it to an audience that had no memory of the events themselves.

Robredo ran as the alternative, with volunteers instead of a party machine, pink instead of the blue and red of her rivals, and rallies in provincial cities that drew crowds on foot. The opposition that had united behind her in 2016 had fractured, several other candidates divided the anti-Marcos vote, and her campaign's final weeks were dominated by the question of whether a fragmented field could be consolidated overnight. It could not, and the margin was the largest in any presidential election since the return of competitive politics.

What the new president inherits is a country carrying debt accumulated during the pandemic, at about sixty per cent of gross domestic product, with inflation that had climbed to nearly five per cent, and with the most expensive fuel prices on record. Abroad he inherits a South China Sea in which Chinese coast guard vessels operate near disputed features and an alliance with Washington that the Duterte years had kept at arm's length, and he has said he intends to keep both relationships warm.

The Marcos name returns to the palace thirty-six years after it left, and the family returns with an apparatus a generation more sophisticated than the one that fell. The vice-president's father handed over to a successor he had once called his ally; the daughter now stands beside the son of the man his own father helped to remove. Philippine politics has a long memory and a short list of families, and both were on display in the count that finished on 25 May.
