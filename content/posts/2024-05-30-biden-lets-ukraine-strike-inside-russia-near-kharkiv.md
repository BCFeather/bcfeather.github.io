---American officials confirmed on the thirtieth of May that President Joe Biden had authorized Ukraine to use weapons supplied by the United States against military targets inside Russia, with the purpose limited to the defence of Kharkiv, the country's second city. Permission covers Russian forces massing near the border and artillery firing across it, and it does not extend to long-range missiles such as ATACMS, which remain restricted for strikes deep inside Russian territory.

Decision reversed a policy that had held since the start of the war. The administration had argued that allowing its weapons to be used against targets in Russia would escalate the conflict and invite retaliation against NATO territory, and it had refused repeated requests from Kyiv. Change was made after Russian forces opened a new front north of Kharkiv in early May and began shelling the city from positions just across the border, which Ukrainian commanders said they could see and could not hit.

Military logic of the shift is narrow and precise. Russian aircraft and artillery were operating from bases within a few dozen kilometres of the frontier, and Ukrainian forces were restricted to engaging them only after they crossed into Ukraine. Permission to strike those positions removes a sanctuary that had allowed Russia to mass forces without risk, and it applies to a strip of territory that is defined by the range of the weapons rather than by a line on a map.

Allies had been moving in the same direction. Britain and France had already said that their Storm Shadow and SCALP missiles could be used against targets inside Russia, and Germany remained opposed to sending its Taurus missiles. The American decision brought the largest supplier of weapons into line with the countries that had taken the step earlier, and it was presented as a change in the rules of engagement rather than in the strategy.

Russian response was a warning rather than a change on the ground. President Vladimir Putin said that NATO countries were playing with fire and that the use of Western weapons against Russian territory would have consequences, and he repeated that Russia could send long-range weapons to countries hostile to the West. Officials in Moscow said the decision would not change the outcome of the war, and the military continued its attacks on Kharkiv through the following weeks.

Officials also said the permission applied to counter-battery fire and to the destruction of launch sites, and that it did not extend to strikes on Russian air bases from which aircraft were dropping glide bombs on the city.

The Consequence of the decision was visible quickly. Ukrainian forces struck Russian positions and equipment inside the border region within days, and the pace of glide-bomb attacks on Kharkiv fell in June. The deeper question, whether Ukraine would be allowed to use long-range missiles against airfields and command posts further inside Russia, was left open, and it was still being debated in Washington when the autumn began. What changed on the thirtieth of May was a rule about where American weapons can be fired, and the war's front line moved by a few dozen kilometres as a result.
