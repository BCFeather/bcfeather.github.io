Tesla reported 386,810 deliveries for the first quarter on 2 April, down 8.7 per cent from the 422,875 it shipped a year earlier and short of what its own annual target implied. Shares fell sharply in the days that followed the release, and several analysts cut their forecasts for the year. Two consecutive quarters below the prior year is the pattern that turned a growth story into a debate about demand, and the debate is now about the second half.

Arithmetic is where the trouble starts. A company that has promised growth of roughly fifty per cent a year needs every quarter to beat the one before, and a first quarter below the previous year's is the first annual decline since 2020. A target that assumes acceleration is a target that one weak quarter can break, and the company has now missed it twice in succession. A company that misses its own target twice in a row has to explain the miss to the investors who priced the growth in, and that explanation is harder than the numbers.

Lineup explains most of the shortfall. Two models account for nearly all of the volume and both are several years old, while the newer model has been slow to ramp and carries a price that few buyers in the largest markets can reach. A range that has not been refreshed is a range that ends up competing on price rather than on product. The refresh that the company has promised for next year is the answer it has offered, and the market has heard that answer before.

Competition has changed the arithmetic of that price. Chinese manufacturers have cut repeatedly and now sell cars with comparable range for less, and the company's response, increases in China and in Europe, did not restore volume. A price cut that does not lift sales is a price cut that only costs margin. The increase in Europe was reversed within weeks, which suggests that the demand the company was trying to reach had already been taken by cheaper models.

Financing has been the cushion. Cheap loans and subsidized leases have moved inventory that the factories had already built, and the interest cost of those programmes sits with the manufacturer rather than with a bank. A discount that is buried in the financing is still a discount, and it appears in the accounts a quarter later. The residual value of a car bought on a subsidized lease is the number that decides whether the financing was a sale or a loan against a depreciating asset.

What the quarter shows is the limit of a delivery number as evidence. It counts cars handed to customers rather than orders taken, and the gap between production and delivery becomes the inventory that the next quarter has to absorb. What the next two quarters show will decide whether the shortfall was a pause in a long growth curve or the end of one.
