title: "Attacks in the Red Sea double the cost of moving a container"

description: "Freight rates on the Asia-Europe route have risen to more than twice their level in December, and the ships are going round Africa instead."
---

Container rates on the route from Asia to northern Europe have more than doubled since December, as the shipping lines have diverted their vessels around the Cape of Good Hope to avoid attacks in the Red Sea. The journey now takes about ten days longer. The index that the lines use for the spot rate rose to more than 3,000 dollars per container, and the contracts signed for the year are being renegotiated.

Diversion is the arithmetic of risk. A ship that goes round Africa burns more fuel and spends more time at sea, which reduces the capacity available for everything else, and the rate is the price of that scarcity. A longer route is a smaller fleet. The canal handled about a tenth of the world's trade, and the traffic through it has fallen by more than half since the attacks began in November. The canal was the shortest route between the factories of Asia and the shops of Europe, and the alternative adds about 3,500 nautical miles to the voyage.

Insurance is the second cost. The premiums for the war-risk cover have risen by several times, and some underwriters have stopped quoting the route altogether, which is why the lines describe the Red Sea as closed to them. A risk that cannot be insured is a route that cannot be used. The navies of several countries have been escorting convoys, and the lines say the protection is not enough to justify the risk to the crews.

Inventory is where the cost reaches the shops. The retailers that ordered for the spring have had to bring forward their orders or pay for air freight, and the lead times they planned around have become unreliable. A schedule that cannot be kept is a stock that has to be larger. The air freight market has tightened as the shippers switch, and the rates have risen by about a fifth on the routes from China to Europe. The importers that had moved to just-in-time ordering after the pandemic have gone back to holding more stock, and the warehouses have filled.

Capacity is finite. The ships that were idle have been put back into service, and the order book for new vessels is full until 2027, which means the market cannot add its way out of the disruption within the year. A shortage that cannot be fixed by building is a shortage that lasts. The shipyards are booked, and the vessels that were scrapped during the years of overcapacity cannot be replaced quickly. The lines have refused to sign long contracts at the spot rate, which means the shippers cannot lock in the cost of the year.

What the month shows is that a chokepoint can be closed by a militia with drones, and the cost of that closure is paid by every household that buys an imported good. A route that is closed for a year is a supply chain that is redesigned, and the redesign is what the importers are doing now.
