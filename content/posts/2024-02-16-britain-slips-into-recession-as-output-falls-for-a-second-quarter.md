British output fell 0.3 per cent in the fourth quarter of 2023, according to the figures published on 15 February, after a decline of 0.1 per cent in the previous quarter. Two consecutive quarters of contraction are the technical definition of a recession. The figure was weaker than the forecast, and the revision to the previous quarter turned the summer into a contraction rather than a stagnation.

Services carried most of the fall. The retail, the hospitality and the health sectors all shrank, and the manufacturing was flat, which is the pattern of a consumer-led slowdown rather than an industrial one. A recession that starts in the shops is a recession that the households feel first. Growth for the year was 0.1 per cent, the weakest since 2009 outside the pandemic, and output per person has not recovered to its level before the financial crisis. Ministers pointed to the monthly figures, which showed a rise in December, and the opposition pointed to the level of the tax burden. A recession in an election year is an argument that both sides make from the same numbers. The budget in March is expected to cut the taxes on the payroll, and the economists say the effect will be small relative to the size of the fall.

Monetary policy is the constraint on the recovery. The rate is the highest in sixteen years, and the mortgages that were fixed at low rates are still being repriced, which means the effect on the households is not yet complete. A household that has not yet repriced is a household that has not yet cut. The committee has said it expects the growth to return in the spring, and the forecast it publishes in March will be the test of that expectation.

What the quarter shows is that an economy built on consumption cannot recover while the consumer is paying more for the debt, and the rate that fights the inflation is the rate that deepens the fall. A recession that is shallow and long is the one the forecasters fear, because it does not force the policy to change and it does not let the households recover. The forecasters have cut their growth estimate for this year to about half a per cent, which is the level the country has lived with since 2008.
