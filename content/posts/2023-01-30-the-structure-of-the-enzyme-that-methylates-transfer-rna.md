Transfer RNA carries a set of chemical modifications, and one of them sits at the wobble position of the anticodon or in the variable loop. Methylation at guanine N7 is added by a two-protein complex, and the complex is conserved from yeast to humans.

The structures in *Nature*, 4 January 2023 resolve that complex. Victor Ruiz-Arroyo, Rishi Raj, Yunsun Nam and colleagues used cryo-electron microscopy to image METTL1, the catalytic subunit, with WDR4, the regulatory one, bound to tRNA, and a companion paper from Jiazhi Li, Longfei Wang and Richard Gregory reports complementary structures.

The catalytic problem is access. The target guanine is buried in a folded tRNA, and a methyltransferase has to flip it into an active site without unfolding the whole molecule. The structures show the tRNA docked so that the target base is extruded, with the enzyme's loops holding the rest of the substrate in place.

WDR4's role is the second puzzle. The regulatory subunit has no catalytic activity, and its contribution is structural: it stabilizes METTL1 and positions the tRNA. Disease mutations in WDR4 cause a syndrome with growth and neurological features, which is the evidence that the complex matters in development.

The companion papers agree on the architecture and differ in emphasis, one on substrate recognition and one on catalytic regulation. Read together, they define the states of a reaction cycle: tRNA binding, base flipping, methyl transfer and release.

The modification's function is translational. m7G at a defined position affects the codon-anticodon interaction and the stability of the tRNA, and loss of the modification changes the proteome, with effects on selenoprotein and on transcripts that depend on specific tRNAs.

Cancer is the third context. METTL1 is overexpressed in several tumours, and the modification is required for the translation of oncogenic transcripts. A structure that shows the active site is a starting point for an inhibitor, and the field has been looking for one.

The inhibitor's difficulty is the cofactor. The methyl donor, S-adenosylmethionine, binds a conserved pocket, and a competitive inhibitor would hit every methyltransferase in the cell. A molecule that binds the tRNA interface would be more selective, and the structures show that interface.

What remains is the dynamics. Cryo-electron microscopy captures stable states, and the flipping of the base is a fast motion that the structures infer rather than observe. Time-resolved experiments would test the inferred pathway.

The clinical angle is the syndrome. If WDR4 mutations destabilize the complex, then a chaperone that restores its assembly would be a treatment, and the structures define the interface a chaperone would have to protect.

The complex's assembly is a further question. METTL1 alone is unstable, and WDR4 keeps it folded; the structures show the interface as a set of contacts between the two proteins, and the disease mutations map onto it, which is how a structural observation becomes a mechanistic explanation.
