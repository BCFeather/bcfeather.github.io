On the morning of the seventeenth of January, the National Bureau of Statistics released the year's full accounts, and the headline number was the one the government had been promising for twelve months. Gross domestic product for 2023 came in at 126.06 trillion yuan, about 17.7 trillion dollars at the year's average exchange rate, an increase of 5.2 per cent at constant prices and above the target of about five per cent that had been set a year earlier. Growth in the fourth quarter was also 5.2 per cent, following 4.9 in the third. The number was accurate and it was uncomfortable, because a 5.2 per cent year that depends on a strong first half, and on a comparison with a weak 2022, tells a different story from the one the headline suggests.

The components were where the difficulty showed. Retail sales grew 7.2 per cent for the year, but the growth was concentrated in services and in the reopening of travel and dining, and the December figures for goods were soft. Fixed-asset investment rose 3.0 per cent, and within that number property development investment fell 9.6 per cent, the second consecutive year of contraction in the sector that had supplied a quarter of the economy. Exports fell 4.6 per cent in dollar terms. Industrial output grew 4.6 per cent. Consumer prices ended the year slightly below where they began, with December inflation at minus 0.3 per cent, which in a country that has spent thirty years worrying about inflation is a novelty that nobody in the statistical system enjoys explaining.

Population is the number that will outlast all the others. At the end of 2023 the country had 1,409.67 million people, 2.08 million fewer than a year earlier, the second annual decline since the figures began to be published in their current form. Births were 9.02 million, deaths 11.10 million. The decline is small in percentage terms and large in meaning, because it has begun earlier and faster than the official projections assumed, and because it is happening in a country whose pension and health systems were designed for a population that grows. Local governments that have spent a decade borrowing against future land sales are now also facing a future with fewer taxpayers.

What the government does with the number is the question that the year will answer. The Politburo has spoken of a proactive fiscal policy and a prudent monetary policy, the language it uses when it intends to do something modest. Several provinces have set growth targets for 2024 below the national one, which is a signal that the centre's ambitions are being read as ambitions rather than instructions. Economists outside the system have argued for direct transfers to households, for a bailout of local government finances, and for a restructuring of the property sector that would allow prices to fall to where buyers are. None of those is cheap, and each of them has a political cost that the current arrangement avoids by doing less.

There is a comparison worth keeping in mind. In 2009, a 5 per cent growth rate would have been a disaster, and the government responded with a stimulus equal to twelve per cent of GDP. In 2024, 5 per cent is the target, the stimulus is a fraction of that, and the growth is coming from a base that is smaller relative to the debt that supports it. A mature economy growing at five per cent with deflation and a falling population is not a failing economy, but it is one that has to be managed differently from the one that the institutions were built for. The statistics bureau published the accounts on a Wednesday morning. The argument about what they mean will run for the rest of the year.
