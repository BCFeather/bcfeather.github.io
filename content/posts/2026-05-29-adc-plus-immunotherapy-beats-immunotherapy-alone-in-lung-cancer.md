Immunotherapy alone sets the floor for first-line treatment of advanced non-small-cell lung cancer without a targetable driver. Pembrolizumab helps a minority durably, and chemotherapy added to it improves the average at the cost of tolerability. A third ingredient that lifts the average without that cost has been the goal of a large trial programme. The trial was run in China, which matters for the size of the population and for how quickly the result will be tested in other health systems with different PD-L1 testing practice.

OptiTROP-Lung05, published in *The Lancet* on 29 May, tested sacituzumab tirumotecan, a TROP2-directed antibody-drug conjugate, added to pembrolizumab in patients with PD-L1-positive, driver-negative disease. TROP2 is expressed broadly in lung adenocarcinoma and squamous carcinoma, which makes it a convenient address for a cytotoxic payload. Sacituzumab tirumotecan has been developed in China and the trial's population is therefore a specific one, which is both a strength and a reason to repeat it elsewhere.

The trial is reported as the first randomized phase 3 evidence that an antibody-drug conjugate combined with immunotherapy beats immunotherapy alone in this setting. Progression-free survival improved with a hazard ratio of 0.35, a 65 per cent reduction in the risk of progression or death, and the magnitude is what the field has been waiting for since the chemotherapy-plus-immunotherapy trials. A hazard ratio of 0.35 in first line is unusual in this disease, and the number invites the question of whether the control arm performed as expected rather than whether the experimental arm overperformed.

Combining the two mechanisms has a biological rationale. Antibody-drug conjugates kill tumour cells and the dying cells release antigens, which can prime the immune response that checkpoint blockade is supposed to unleash. Chemotherapy does something similar and has never produced an effect of this size in the same population. Chemotherapy plus immunotherapy set the previous benchmark at a hazard ratio near 0.6, and the improvement from adding an antibody-drug conjugate is measured against that.

The toxicity question is the one that decides uptake. Antibody-drug conjugates carry payload-specific risks, and TROP2 conjugates have been associated with stomatitis and with interstitial lung disease in other tumour types. Adding one to an immune checkpoint inhibitor also raises the possibility of overlapping immune effects, and the trial's adverse event table will be read as closely as its survival curve.

Regulatory and pricing decisions will follow quickly, because first-line lung cancer is the largest oncology market there is. The clinical question that remains is sequencing and selection: whether all PD-L1-positive patients should receive the combination, or whether a TROP2 expression assay can identify the ones who gain most, and whether the regimen holds up in the squamous and non-smoking subsets where the biology differs. Squamous histology has fewer options and more TROP2 expression in some series, and a subgroup analysis will decide whether the combination is offered uniformly or selectively.
