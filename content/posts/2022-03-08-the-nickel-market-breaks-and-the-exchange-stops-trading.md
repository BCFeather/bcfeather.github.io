Nickel on the London Metal Exchange doubled on 8 March, passing 100,000 dollars a tonne from about 48,000 the day before, and the exchange suspended trading before the market opened in the afternoon. The move was the largest in the history of a contract that had traded since 1877, and it came in a metal that most investors never think about. When the exchange reopened on 16 March, it did so under rules that had been written in the intervening week.

The position behind the squeeze belonged to Tsingshan Holding Group, the Chinese company that is the world's largest producer of stainless steel and a large owner of nickel assets in Indonesia. Tsingshan had sold the contract forward, a hedge that becomes a liability when the price rises, and as the price rose its brokers demanded more margin against it. The company's founder, Xiang Guangda, was reported to have covered part of the position at a loss, and the size of the loss was never disclosed.

Nickel matters because of two industries. About 70 per cent of it goes into stainless steel, and a growing share goes into the cathodes of electric car batteries, which need a class of nickel that is chemically pure. Exchange stocks had fallen to a few days of consumption before the war, and Russia, through Norilsk Nickel, supplied a large part of the battery-grade material that the rest of the world bought. A market with thin inventories and one dominant seller is a market where a large short position can become a national problem.

The exchange's response created a second controversy. It cancelled all trades executed on 8 March, worth about 3.9 billion dollars, on the ground that they had been made in a disorderly market, and it postponed the delivery obligations that would have followed. Hedge funds that had bought the metal at the high price found their profits erased, and one of them, Elliott, later sued the exchange for 456 million dollars. The exchange argued that a default by a clearing member would have damaged the market more than the cancellation did.

Trading resumed with a 15 per cent limit on daily moves and a new rule that allowed the exchange to call for delivery of a position. The restart was itself disorderly: the price opened below the limit, some trades executed outside the permitted range, and the exchange had to annul another batch. Tsingshan reached a standstill agreement with its banks, and the position was unwound over months rather than days.

What the episode exposed was a structural fact about commodity exchanges. A price is a claim about the next trade, and a contract is a claim about a metal that has to be loaded onto a ship, and the two separate when the stock of deliverable metal falls below the size of the positions that reference it. The LME is a private company that performs a public function, and after March its rulebook carried a clause about orderly markets that its members had never expected to be used.
