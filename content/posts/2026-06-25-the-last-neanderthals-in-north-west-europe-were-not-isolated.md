description: "Genetic analysis of at least eleven individuals younger than 52,500 years finds a large, closely connected population in north-west Europe, isolated from other Neanderthals but not from each other."
---

Standard accounts of the Neanderthal endgame has small bands retreating into refuges, losing diversity as they shrink. A paper in *Nature* on 25 June complicates that picture with genomes from at least eleven individuals who lived in north-west Europe later than 52,500 years ago. Genomes from this period are rare because warm, wet cave sediments destroy DNA, and the sites that preserve it are exceptions.

These individuals are genetically close to one another, which describes a population that was large enough to avoid the inbreeding that small groups accumulate, and connected enough that genes travelled between its members. The same genomes sit apart from other Neanderthals and from early modern humans, which describes a population that was also bounded. A population that is bounded but internally connected is what a geographical barrier produces, and the barrier in this case was probably the ice and the rivers rather than a competitor.

Those two findings together are the interesting part. Isolation from the wider Neanderthal world and close connection within a regional group is a pattern that shows up in island populations and in species with a limited range, and it suggests geography rather than decline set the boundary. The region is small enough that the group's connections are plausible to model.

The timing places them in the period when modern humans were arriving in Europe. Whether the two groups met, and what came of it, is the question that genetic data can answer only if both are sampled from the same region and the same window, and the paper's contribution is to establish the Neanderthal side of that comparison properly. Sampling both sides from the same region and the same window is what would let a geneticist ask whether the two groups exchanged anything at all.

DNA from old bone is a fragile material, and the sample size here is the achievement as much as the result. Eleven individuals from a region and a period that has produced few such specimens means the analysis can distinguish a population structure from an artefact of one badly preserved genome. A connected population also implies that the individuals were not the last of their kind locally, since gene flow requires contemporaries to exchange with. That is a testable prediction for future sampling.

The implication for the extinction story is that the last populations were not necessarily small and marginal. A population that is connected and genetically healthy can still disappear, and if it does, the cause is more likely to be competition, disease or violence than the slow erosion that a small gene pool would imply. That reframing also fits the archaeological record of the period, in which the last Neanderthal toolkits look neither impoverished nor marginal. It also implies that the cause of extinction has to be looked for outside the population, and the candidates are competitors, pathogens and climate rather than genetics.
