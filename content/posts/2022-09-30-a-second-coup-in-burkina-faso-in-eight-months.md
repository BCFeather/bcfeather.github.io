Captain Ibrahim Traoré appeared on state television on Friday evening and announced that he had removed the officer who had seized power eight months earlier. The broadcast said the government had been dissolved, the constitution suspended and the borders closed, and a curfew was declared from nine at night. Soldiers in the capital had surrounded the presidential palace and the broadcaster through the afternoon, and shots were heard in the city after dark. The deposed leader, Lieutenant Colonel Paul-Henri Damiba, was said to have agreed to step down after negotiations at a military camp.

It is the second coup in the same year, and both were justified in the same language. Traoré said the previous government had failed to defeat the armed groups that have been fighting in the north and the east for a decade, and he promised a new approach. Damiba had used the same argument in January when he removed the elected president, who had also failed to stop the insurgency. The pattern is one that the region knows: a government that cannot deliver security is replaced by officers who promise it, and the promise expires within a year.

Burkina Faso is one of the poorest countries in the world and one of the most affected by the violence that has spread across the Sahel. About two million people have been displaced inside the country, and the areas under government control have shrunk each year. The army has been expanded and equipped, and the attacks have grown in number as it has grown. Analysts say the problem lies in the way the force is commanded and supplied rather than in its size, and the officers who replace each other keep saying the same thing.

The international reaction was cautious. The regional bloc condemned the seizure and said it would suspend the country, which is what it did in January before lifting the suspension. The former colonial power said it was following the situation and that it had suspended its development aid earlier in the year. The Russian government, which has been cultivating contacts in the region, said the events were an internal matter. Nobody described the new leader as legitimate.

Practical effects are already visible. The borders with Mali, Niger, Ghana and Ivory Coast were closed, and the supply routes for fuel and food run through them. The price of petrol rose in the capital within a day, and the markets that depend on imports from Ghana began to run short. The schools that had reopened in the safer districts closed again, and the flights out of Ouagadougou filled up.

What the coup shows is a state that cannot hold its own territory and a population that has lost confidence in the institutions that are meant to defend it. Traoré is thirty-four and was a member of the same movement that took power in January, and his programme is a promise rather than a plan. The armed groups do not care who governs the capital, and the next government will be judged by a single test. The test is whether the roads to the north reopen, and no government in the last five years has passed it.
