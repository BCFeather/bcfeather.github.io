Genetic risk scores are built from studies, and studies are built from the people who volunteer for them. The consequence is that most polygenic scores were calibrated on Europeans, and their performance falls when applied to anyone else. The failure is not a small correction: a score that predicts well in one population can lose much of its discrimination in another, because the variants it uses are in different combinations and the allele frequencies differ.

The Taiwan Precision Medicine Initiative, reported in *Nature*, 15 October 2025, is an attempt to give one such population its own reference. Hsin-Chou Yang, Pui-Yan Kwok, Jer-Yuarn Wu and colleagues recruited and genotyped more than half a million people, almost all of Han Chinese ancestry, and linked the genotypes to medical records.

The scale is what makes the cohort useful. A score needs thousands of cases to be estimated and tens of thousands to be validated, and a national programme can supply both where a single hospital cannot. The paper reports genome-wide analyses across many conditions rather than a single disease, which turns the cohort into a platform.

The population question is the delicate one. Han Chinese ancestry is not a single genetic population, and the cohort reflects Taiwan's history, which includes waves of migration and a period of isolation. A score built here transfers imperfectly to mainland China, to Singapore, and to the Chinese diaspora, and the paper is explicit about the reference population rather than claiming universality.

Privacy is the constraint that shapes the design. Linking genomes to medical records is the same operation as linking them to individuals, and the governance of the cohort decides what research can be done and what cannot. The paper describes an approach built around consent and institutional control, and the details matter because a national biobank is a permanent institution.

What the cohort enables beyond risk scores is the study of variants that are common here and rare elsewhere. A variant that appears in one per cent of Europeans may appear in ten per cent of this population, and its effect can only be measured where it is common. That asymmetry is the reason diversity in genomics is a scientific argument and not only an equity one.

The clinical step is the hard one. A risk score that stratifies a population is not yet a test that a doctor can order, because the thresholds, the communication and the evidence of benefit all have to be established. The cohort provides the first of those and makes the other two possible.

For the field, the contribution is a reminder that reference populations are infrastructure. A score without a cohort behind it is an estimate; a score with one is a measurement that a health system can use, and the difference is what this cohort supplies.

Consent and return of results are the parts a cohort cannot retrofit. Participants who give a sample and a record expect to learn something from it, and a programme that never returns a finding loses the trust that makes the next cohort possible. The paper's governance section is as important as its genetics.
