A chromosome is copied from many starting points, and where those points sit is a decision the cell makes every cycle. The mechanism is better understood in yeast than in animals. Work reported in *Science* in September 2023 takes the animal version, mapping the origins that fire in a cycle and correlating their positions with chromatin marks and with the binding of a licensing factor.

Correlation is the finding. An origin's activity tracks an open chromatin state and a bound licensing complex, and together the two predict which sites fire early. Licensing supplies the mechanism's core, because a complex loading a helicase onto DNA marks a site as a candidate, and a candidate sitting in an accessible region is one that fires.

Chromatin explains the variation. A region compacted in one cell type and open in another carries different origins, so a cell's identity is written into its replication. Timing is the second layer, since early and late origins sit in different chromatin environments and the ordering is consistent with accessibility setting the schedule. Disease connects plausibly. A cell firing from the wrong origins accumulates rearrangements, and comparison with a cancer line shows a shifted map.

Resolution sets the limitation, because a population of cells averages the origins and one cell's choice may differ. The trigger is what the paper does not supply, since a licensed origin still needs a kinase to fire and that transition is a separate step. Molecular biology gains a map of a decision, since the genome's copying has been studied as a machine and this describes where the machine starts.

Origin's definition is functional, because a site where a helicase loads and a polymerase begins is an origin while a licensed site that never fires is a candidate the cell declined. Efficiency is the measured quantity, since an origin firing in every cell differs from one firing in a tenth of them. Yeast offers the comparison, because its origins are defined by a short sequence while animal ones are defined by context, and the two teach different lessons about specificity. Stability is the stake, since too few origins leave gaps and too many cause collisions.

A single-cell assay that read the origins fired in individual nuclei would show whether the population average hides a choice made differently from cell to cell, and a spread of choices would mean the map describes a probability rather than a schedule. A degradation of the licensing factor at a chosen time would also test whether late origins simply lose their licence before the fork arrives, which is a cheaper explanation than a chromatin switch.

The relation between firing order and gene expression is the hypothesis the map supports, because a region the cell transcribes early is one it may need to copy early as well. A comparison of the origin map in a differentiated cell and in its progenitor would show how quickly the pattern is rewritten when a cell changes identity, and a slow rewrite would mean the map is set early and inherited.
