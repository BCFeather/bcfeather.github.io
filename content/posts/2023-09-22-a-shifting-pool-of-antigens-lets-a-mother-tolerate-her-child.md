The fetus is half paternal, and the maternal immune system does not reject it. Explanations have come as a list of separate mechanisms, and a paper integrating them into a dynamic picture is a step towards understanding why the arrangement fails. Studied in *Science* on 22 September 2023, the picture is built from measurements of the antigens the maternal immune system encounters, and the pool's composition turns out to shift as the pregnancy advances. Shift carries the finding. An early pregnancy presents one set of antigens and a late pregnancy another, so tolerance is a moving target rather than a state fixed at implantation.

Presentation supplies the mechanism. A fetal antigen displayed by a maternal cell is one the immune system learns to accept, and repeated exposure reinforces that learning. Regulatory cells are the effectors, because a population of regulatory T cells expands during pregnancy and tracking its specificity is what connects the shift to the tolerance itself.

Failure is the pathology. A pregnancy that loses tolerance ends in a disorder, and comparing affected pregnancies with healthy ones is the clinical link the authors draw. Paternal antigen is the variable. A first pregnancy and a second present different paternal antigens, and that difference is a candidate explanation for the change in risk with parity.

Model sets the limitation, since a mouse's pregnancy is short and its immune system differs while the human data remain correlative. A therapy is the claim not made, because a tolerance depending on a shifting pool is harder to induce than a single switch, and the mechanism suggests a schedule rather than a drug.

Immunology gains a dynamic account of a classical paradox, since the fetus's survival has been explained by barriers and by suppression and this account includes time. Placenta supplies the interface, because trophoblast cells present antigens at the maternal boundary and that boundary is where the immune system meets the fetus; a peptide on a maternal cell's surface is the signal a T cell reads, and the context of that cell decides the outcome.

Memory explains persistence. A tolerance surviving one pregnancy is one a second inherits, and comparing parity is the evidence; hormones form the background, because progesterone and its metabolites shift the immune balance and the antigen pool's change is layered on top of that.

A depletion experiment that removed the regulatory population would show whether the tolerance collapses without it, and a collapse would make the expanding cells the mechanism rather than a correlate. A sampling schedule across all three trimesters would also show whether the antigen pool changes smoothly or in steps, and a step would point to a placental event that a smooth drift would not. Therapeutic tolerance is the distant goal, because a transplant a patient's immune system accepts would be a cure, and pregnancy is the natural experiment the field has been reading for decades. A comparison of the antigen pool between a pregnancy that ended in pre-eclampsia and one that did not would show whether the failure is a missing shift or a shift to the wrong set, and the two readings imply different interventions.
