A polymer in thermal equilibrium explores its surroundings symmetrically, because the noise that pushes it is the same in every direction. Put the chain in a bath of self-propelled particles and that symmetry goes: the swimmers exert forces that stay correlated for a finite time, the chain feels a persistent push rather than a random kick, and the effective dynamics acquire a drift that equilibrium does not have.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 9 March 2024 asks what happens when the activity itself varies in space. The authors take an ideal Rouse chain, the bead-and-spring model in which each bead feels friction, elastic coupling to its neighbours and the noise of the bath, and let it sit in a bath whose density of swimmers changes from place to place. The swimmers are treated as point particles whose orientation relaxes on a finite timescale, and that timescale is the one ingredient that makes the chain's response unlike a thermal one.

Coarse-graining gives the answer in closed form. By integrating out the internal modes, the authors derive an effective equation for the centre of mass alone, and the drift in that equation depends on the gradient of the activity field and on the relaxation time of the chain. The resulting equation has the shape of a diffusion in an effective potential, with the potential built from the activity gradient and the chain's own stiffness.

The direction of the drift depends on parameters. For some values the chain migrates towards regions of high activity and localizes there; for others the effective force reverses and the chain settles where the bath is quiet, which is a counter-intuitive outcome that follows from the competition between the persistent forcing and the friction that the swimmers also modify.

Separation follows from the drift. Because the relaxation time of a Rouse chain scales with its length and with its connectivity, chains of different length respond to the same gradient with different speeds, and a bath that is non-uniform in space therefore sorts a mixture of polymers by size. Rate and resolution pull against each other here: a steep gradient sorts quickly but spreads the chains apart before the sorting is complete, and a shallow one takes longer than the swimmers stay oriented.

What the model does not include is hydrodynamic interaction. The swimmers push the solvent as well as the beads, and a chain in a crowded active suspension feels the flow that its neighbours generate, which the Rouse model omits and which would change both the drift and the sorting rate.

The proposal is practical. A patterned active bath, made by lighting a photoactive swimmer suspension through a mask, would produce a steady activity landscape, and the calculation says what landscape would separate two given polymer lengths, which is the kind of prediction that an experiment on active sorting can test directly. A first experiment would only need to show that two lengths drift at different speeds in a fixed landscape, and that is a measurement of displacement rather than of anything harder to control.
