Rugby's international calendar is set by the unions that own the tournaments, and the clubs that employ the players have no vote. That asymmetry has a price, and a study published this month in a sports medicine journal puts a number on it: the six-week Six Nations window costs the professional clubs in the two largest leagues about 11 million pounds per season in player availability and medical costs.

Method is a cohort study. The authors tracked 412 players from eight clubs over three seasons, recorded matches, training load and injury diagnoses, and compared injury incidence in the weeks during and after the international window with the rest of the season. Incidence during the window was 2.4 times the baseline for players released to national squads.

Mechanism is load, not violence. A player who plays five international matches in six weeks, at a higher intensity than club rugby, returns to a club side that is playing its own league matches and enters a schedule with no recovery week. The injury risk concentrates in the two weeks after return, which is where the study finds most of the excess.

The financial estimate uses squad cost rather than salary. The authors value a player-week of unavailability at the club's cost per player-week, and multiply by the excess weeks attributable to the window. The 11 million figure is that product for the two leagues, with a stated uncertainty of plus or minus three million.

Counterarguments exist and are reasonable. The unions argue that international revenue funds the game below the top tier, and that a shorter window would reduce the number of matches and therefore the money that flows to grassroots programmes. The study does not contest this; it prices one side of the ledger.

Comparisons with other sports are instructive. Football's international windows are shorter and more frequent, and the club-versus-country dispute has been managed through a compensation scheme agreed with the continental body. Rugby has no equivalent mechanism, which is why the cost sits where it lands.

Proposals on the table include a longer window with fewer matches, a mandatory rest week after international duty, and a compensation payment from unions to clubs. Each has an obstacle: the first reduces revenue, the second shortens the season, and the third requires an agreement between parties whose interests do not align.

What the study makes visible is that calendar design is a health policy. The number of matches, the interval between them and the intensity of the competition determine injury rates, and those are decisions taken by committees that do not carry the medical cost.

A reader following the tournament will notice the effect without the study: players who return from international duty are rested for a week or two, and the clubs that lose them most are the ones with the smallest squads. The 11 million pounds is distributed unevenly, which is why the argument keeps returning.
