Revenue growth at this scale stops being a company story and becomes a demand indicator for an entire industry, which is why a quarterly filing is read by utilities and construction firms as well as by investors. NVIDIA reported second-quarter revenue of 96.2 billion dollars on 26 August, up 18 per cent on the previous quarter and 106 per cent on the year, with net income of 59.7 billion and a gross margin of 75 per cent. A gross margin of 75 per cent is the number that tells you how tight supply still is, because a company with spare capacity does not hold that line for a year.

Data centre was the whole of the story. Revenue from that segment reached 89 billion dollars, up 117 per cent year on year, which means the rest of the company, gaming, professional visualization, automotive, grew slowly by comparison. Guidance for the third quarter is 108 billion dollars plus or minus two per cent, against a consensus of about 104 billion, and the forecast assumes no data centre compute revenue from China. Segment concentration cuts both ways, since it makes the forecast legible and makes any softening in one customer group visible immediately.

Excluding China is a statement about policy rather than about demand. Export controls have made the largest market for accelerators partially inaccessible, and a company that guides without it is telling investors the number does not depend on a resolution. Whether that is prudence or a permanent write-down of an addressable market is the question the guidance leaves open. Excluding China also removes the most price-sensitive part of the market, where local alternatives have been gaining share on cost rather than performance.

Capital returns ran alongside the expansion. The company returned about 26 billion dollars through buybacks and dividends in the quarter, still holds 99 billion under its authorization, and declared a quarterly dividend of 0.25 dollars per share payable on 1 October. A 75 per cent gross margin is what funds that, and it is also what attracts competitors who would accept a lower one. Buyback authorisation of that size is a statement that management sees no better use for the cash than returning it, which is not what a company expecting a capacity crunch usually says.

The commentary from the chief executive framed the quarter as demand acceleration rather than supply relief, describing several frontier labs scaling in parallel, an open model ecosystem and physical AI coming online, with the Vera Rubin platform now in full production. That framing is deliberate, since the bear case for the sector is that demand is concentrated in a handful of buyers whose own financing depends on the same vendors. Physical AI is the part of the story with the longest lead time, because robotics demand depends on customers outside the data centre industry finding useful work for the models.

What to watch next is the gap between revenue and the capital that supports it. Vendor financing arrangements, guarantees and leases have become part of how capacity is built, and a supplier whose growth depends on underwriting its customers' construction has an exposure that does not appear in the income statement until demand turns. The next two quarters will show whether the backlog is customer demand or vendor-funded capacity.
