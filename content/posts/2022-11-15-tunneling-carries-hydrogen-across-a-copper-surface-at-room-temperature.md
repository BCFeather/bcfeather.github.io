Dissociation of a hydrogen molecule on a metal surface is the first step of many catalytic reactions, and the standard picture is a barrier that thermal energy has to clear. The barrier for copper is computed to be about 0.59 electronvolts, which is large compared with room temperature and small compared with the energies at which a molecule would simply break apart in the gas. The intermediate size of the barrier is what makes tunneling a candidate, since a barrier that is too high or too low takes the question out of range.

Work posted to arXiv on 11 November 2022 computes the barrier from first principles and then asks what happens underneath it. The authors identify two stationary states near the height at which the bond breaks, one molecular and one dissociated, and they follow the minimum energy path between them with a transfer matrix method that carries the quantum amplitude through the barrier. A transfer matrix propagates a solution from one side to the other in slices, and the transmission it returns is the tunneling probability, with the shape of the barrier resolved along the path instead of approximated by a single width.

Tunneling turns out to matter. At room temperature and below, the transfer through the barrier is significant, so a fraction of the molecules dissociate without acquiring the thermal energy the classical picture requires. The effect is not a curiosity at low temperature; it is a contribution at the temperature where the reaction is run, which is the finding that changes how a rate should be computed, since a rate computed from the barrier alone omits the channel that carries the current.

The contribution can be split. Translational motion of the molecule along the surface and vibrational motion of the bond both feed the tunneling amplitude, and the paper quantifies each across a range of temperatures. Which motion dominates depends on the temperature, since the populations of the two kinds of state change at different rates. A reader can therefore read the paper's table as a map of which channel to include at a given temperature.

Electron transfer is what makes the barrier what it is. Charge moving from the copper into the antibonding orbital of the molecule weakens the bond before the atoms arrive, and the authors trace that transfer along the path, which connects the potential energy surface to the electronic structure that produced it.

What the calculation does not include is the surface's own motion. Phonons broaden the barrier and can assist tunneling, and a static lattice is an approximation that a reader should keep in mind when comparing with an experiment. Phonon assistance is usually included as a correction, and its size for this system is not estimated here.

A measurement of the dissociation rate on a cold surface would isolate the tunneling term, and the temperature dependence the paper predicts is the signature such an experiment would look for.
