Loneliness entered health policy through its association with mortality, depression and cardiovascular disease, and the association is strong enough that governments commissioned loneliness strategies. The strategies were written on the assumption that the size of the problem was known.

A systematic review tested that assumption by collecting every nationally representative survey that used a validated loneliness instrument between 2000 and 2019. The authors searched four databases and grey literature to September 2021, appraised studies with the Joanna Briggs checklist, and pooled estimates by instrument, age group and World Health Organization region where methods were homogeneous. The review appeared in *The BMJ*, 9 February 2022. The review restricted itself to studies with at least 292 participants, which excludes small community surveys but includes the national health surveys that policy relies on.

Data existed for 113 countries from 57 studies. Adolescents were covered in 77 countries, young adults in 30, middle-aged adults in 32 and older adults in 40, and outside Europe the coverage for adults was close to nil. Meta-analysis of 24 studies produced 212 estimates for 106 countries. The gap is uneven in a way that matters, because countries with ageing populations and no survey are precisely those where the burden is likely to be rising fastest.

The pooled prevalence for adolescents ranged from 9.2 per cent in South-East Asia, with an interval from 6.8 to 12.4, to 14.4 per cent in the Eastern Mediterranean, with an interval from 12.2 to 17.1. Within Europe the pattern was consistent across ages: the lowest figures sat in the north, at 2.7 per cent for middle-aged adults, and the highest in the east, at 21.3 per cent for people aged 60 or older. The adolescent figures are the only ones that can be compared across regions, and even there the instruments used in Asia and in the Americas are not identical.

That east-west gradient is the result worth carrying away. It survives stratification by instrument and age, which suggests that it reflects something about households, migration, widowhood and social contact rather than about how the question was translated. Eastern European countries combine high widowhood, low household income and emigration of younger adults, and each of those raises the chance that an older person spends the day alone.

The equity problem is the review's main message. The countries with the least data are the ones where ageing is fastest and where extended family structures are changing, so the global burden is probably undercounted and certainly unmeasured. Temporal trends could not be assessed because repeated surveys were too few. A country that has never asked the question cannot know whether its loneliness strategy is aimed at the right group, and the review cannot tell it.

Methodological heterogeneity limits what the pooled numbers mean. Instruments differ in whether they ask about feelings or about social contacts, and the threshold for a case differs with them, so a comparison between a northern European country and an eastern one carries more confidence than a comparison between continents. Pooling was possible only where instruments, age bands and regions matched, which is why the meta-analysis covers 24 studies out of the 57 that were included.
