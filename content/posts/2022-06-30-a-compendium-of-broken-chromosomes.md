Cancer genomes are damaged in patterns, and a paper in *Nature* on 30 June argues that the patterns can be catalogued the way mutations are. A team led by Ruben Drews and colleagues at Cambridge and elsewhere analysed seven thousand eight hundred and eighty tumours covering thirty-three cancer types and sorted the copy number changes they found into seventeen signatures. Each signature is a characteristic way in which chromosomes gain, lose or rearrange pieces of themselves.

Chromosomal instability is the condition that produces those changes. A cell that loses the machinery for accurate division ends up with the wrong number of chromosomes, with fragments that have detached and rejoined elsewhere, and with stretches of DNA copied many times over. This kind of damage is present in most cancers and had been described case by case, with no common system for measuring it or for comparing one tumour with another.

Method borrowed from the study of point mutations. Mutational signatures, catalogued a decade earlier, describe the patterns of single-letter changes that different chemical and physical processes leave behind, and the same logic applies to larger events: a broken chromosome repaired badly leaves a particular arrangement of copy number changes that reflects how it broke. The team assembled signatures from two independent collections of cancer genomes and combined them into one compendium, then tested the classification on simulated genomes with known causes.

Causes identified include defects in homologous recombination, the pathway that repairs double-strand breaks and that is impaired in tumours with BRCA mutations. That defect produces a signature the authors describe as a fingerprint of the pathway, and the fingerprint matters clinically because tumours with it respond to drugs that exploit the weakness, including PARP inhibitors. The compendium also associated signatures with resistance to common treatments and proposed targets that had not been considered.

The clinical value claimed lies in prediction rather than diagnosis. The signatures predicted drug response in datasets where the outcome was already known, and the authors argue that a tumour's pattern of chromosomal damage could be read from a routine sequencing test and used to choose a therapy. Validation in trials is the next step, and the paper is explicit that the predictions are associations rather than mechanisms.

Limits come from the data. The analysis used bulk sequencing, which averages over millions of cells and cannot say whether a pattern describes one clone or several, and the resolution is too coarse to catch rearrangements shorter than a few thousand bases. Most of the tumours came from a small number of countries and from patients who had access to sequencing, which affects how the signatures should be interpreted elsewhere. What the paper establishes is a vocabulary. A field that had described chromosomal chaos as a symptom now has a list of named patterns, each with a proposed cause and a clinical consequence, and a tumour can be given a score rather than a shrug; whether the vocabulary survives contact with prospective trials is the question the next few years will answer.
