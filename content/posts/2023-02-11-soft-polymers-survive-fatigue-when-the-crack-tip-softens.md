Soft materials fail in two different ways. A single large load tears them, and a repeated small load grows a crack until it does. Toughness answers the first, fatigue the second, and the two are governed by different physics.

In *PNAS* on 7 February 2023, the second is addressed. Binhong Liu, Tenghao Yin, Shaoxing Qu, Guangmin Zhou and colleagues design polymer networks in which the material at a crack tip softens as the crack advances, and the softening blunts the stress concentration that drives growth.

A phase transition supplies the mechanism. A polymer network near its swelling limit softens when a critical stress is reached, and a softened zone at the tip spreads the load over a larger area, so a crack that cannot concentrate stress cannot grow, even under many cycles.

Because a tough material absorbs energy in a large process zone while a fatigue-resistant one keeps the zone small under small loads, the distinction from toughening is the paper's point, and the standard strategies, such as adding a second network, raise the first and leave the second unchanged. Selectivity is what makes the idea useful rather than merely interesting, since the softening is built into the network's chemistry so that it happens at the stress a crack tip produces and not at the stress a bulk sample produces.

Threshold is the measured quantity, taken from a cyclic test that grows a crack from a notch and reports the energy release rate below which growth stops, and these materials show a threshold far above comparable networks without the softening. Natural materials offer a parallel, since tendons and arteries resist fatigue through a combination of stiff fibres and a soft matrix, and crack-tip softening is the synthetic version of that strategy. The threshold is not the only number that matters, because a material that resists growth below it can still fail when a defect exceeds it, and the design must keep manufacturing flaws below the critical size.

Softening carries a cost, since a material that softens at a crack tip can soften elsewhere under a different stress, and a component that is dented or cut may lose the property that protects it. Wet environments are untested, because a hydrogel in the body exchanges water and its network relaxes, and the transition may depend on swelling state rather than on stress alone. What the design serves is soft robotics and medical devices, where a hydrogel surviving a million cycles is what an artificial muscle or a flexible implant needs, and materials tough enough for a single load have not been fatigue-resistant. The broader lesson is about how failure is designed against: toughness is a property of a material, fatigue a property of a structure, and a material that controls the crack tip is doing structural work at the molecular scale.

Field conditions are the final unknown, since a device flexing a million times at body temperature adds variables that a bench test at room temperature leaves out.
