TSMC told customers at its symposium in Hsinchu on 6 May that the A16 process has entered risk production, the stage at which a new manufacturing flow stops being an engineering exercise and starts running designs that customers intend to sell. The company describes A16 as a 1.6-nanometre-class node, though the number is a marketing convention rather than a measurement, and the more consequential change sits underneath the transistors: power delivery has moved from the front of the wafer to the back.

Backside power delivery is the structural break. Since the first integrated circuits, the wires that supply current have shared the front surface with the wires that carry signals, competing with them for the same handful of routing layers. Moving the supply network to the reverse side of the wafer frees those layers for signal routing, reduces the voltage drop between the package and the transistor, and allows the power rails to be wider without blocking the interconnect. TSMC calls its version Super Power Rail, and the company says the combination with its gate-all-around transistors yields a ten per cent speed gain at the same power or a twenty per cent power reduction at the same speed, with a small increase in logic density.

Risk production is a term of art that hides a great deal. It means the process is stable enough to accept customer tape-outs, that the design rules are frozen, and that yields are being measured on real product rather than on test structures. It does not mean the node is manufacturable at the volume and the yield that a phone launch requires, and the gap between a working first silicon and a profitable ramp has swallowed more than one promising process. The customers whose designs are now running are not being named, which is normal at this stage, and the company said volume production remains scheduled for the second half of next year.

The competitive context is unusually crowded. Intel has staked its turnaround on a comparable shift to backside power in its 18A process, and Samsung is pursuing a similar architecture while trying to recover customers it lost in the previous generation. All three are now making the same structural bet, which means the differentiator over the next two years will be the unglamorous business of yield, defect density and how quickly a design can be moved from a mask set to a shipped product. The customers who choose a foundry on the strength of a data sheet will be disappointed; the ones who choose it on the strength of a ramp will be rewarded.

There is a broader point in the timing. For a decade the industry sold each node as a smaller number, and the gains came from shrinking the transistor. That era is closing, and the improvements now come from packaging the wafer differently, stacking the memory closer, and moving the plumbing to the other side of the silicon. A16 is the first node where a leading manufacturer has said out loud that the wiring, rather than the transistor, is the thing being redesigned. Risk production is the least photogenic milestone in the semiconductor calendar, and it is the one that decides whether the next two years of products exist.
