Brown adipose tissue burns fuel to produce heat, and its existence in adult humans was doubted for decades before imaging confirmed it. Once it was confirmed, a second question followed: people with more brown fat tend to have better glucose control, and the improvement is larger than the amount of heat produced would explain. Something about brown fat affects metabolism through a route other than calories.

Neri and colleagues addressed the route by looking at the nerves that supply it. Interscapular brown adipose tissue receives sympathetic innervation from the stellate ganglion, and the new work shows that two distinct subcircuits within that ganglion innervate the same tissue and do different jobs. One subcircuit controls thermogenesis, the heat-producing function that the tissue is named for. The other controls glucose tolerance, an effect that appears without a comparable change in heat. The study appeared in *Nature Metabolism*, with a News and Views commentary on 20 January. The tissue sits between the shoulder blades in rodents and in a more distributed pattern in people, and its total mass in an adult human is a few hundred grams at most.

Separating the two is an anatomical and functional achievement. Sympathetic fibres are small, interleaved and difficult to trace individually, and the authors combined tracing with selective manipulation to show that the populations are distinct and that silencing one leaves the other working. Demonstrating independence is harder than demonstrating an effect, and it is what makes the result a mechanism rather than a correlation.

Clinical implications are speculative and pointed. If glucose handling can be improved by activating a nerve circuit that does not increase heat production, then a drug that targets that circuit would avoid the unwanted consequence of raising metabolic rate, which includes sweating, tachycardia and the discomfort that has sunk most thermogenic drug programmes. The obvious target is the circuit, not the tissue.

Translating anatomy across species is the weak point. The work is done in mice, and the innervation of human brown fat differs in its distribution and in the amount of tissue involved. A subcircuit found in a mouse ganglion may have no counterpart in a human, and the paper cannot say whether it does.

Brown fat research has produced a series of promising mechanisms that failed to become therapies, largely because increasing energy expenditure is poorly tolerated. The value of this result is that it separates the metabolic benefit from the thermogenic cost, which is the specific problem that has blocked the field. That history is worth remembering when a new mechanism is described as the one that finally separates the benefits from the costs.

Whether the separation can be exploited is a question for pharmacology. A circuit is a target in principle and a difficult one in practice, since the fibres that carry the signal are not easily addressed by a pill, and the alternative of delivering a drug to the ganglion is not a realistic outpatient therapy. Delivering a drug to a ganglion is done in pain medicine, and doing it chronically in healthy people is not a reasonable proposition with the tools available.
