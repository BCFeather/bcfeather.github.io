Iron-sulfur clusters are among the oldest cofactors in biology, and they sit at the active sites of enzymes that move electrons, fix nitrogen and reduce carbon dioxide. Their assembly and repair are processes that cells perform carefully, and the chemistry involved has been difficult to observe.

The study in *PNAS*, 7 February 2023 observes it in a synthetic cluster. Niklas Thompson, Gil Namkoong, Brighton Skeel, Daniel Suess and colleagues show that every iron-sulfur bond in a cuboidal Fe4S4 cluster can be broken and re-formed, a flexibility that had been assumed for parts of the cluster rather than for all of it.

The cubane is a cube of iron and sulfur atoms, with each iron coordinated by three sulfurs and by a ligand. The cluster's function depends on its ability to accept and donate electrons, and that ability depends on the bonding within the cube.

The paper's method is spectroscopy and synthesis. A cluster with a defined ligand set is prepared, and a reagent that cleaves a bond is introduced, and the resulting states are characterized. The sequence shows a cluster that opens and closes rather than one that either holds together or falls apart.

The implication for biology is about assembly. A cluster that can be disassembled bond by bond can be built bond by bond, and the proteins that assemble Fe-S clusters in cells may be doing exactly that: presenting a scaffold and feeding iron and sulfur in a controlled order.

The implication for repair is the other half. An enzyme whose cluster is damaged by oxygen has to be repaired, and a cluster that can lose one bond and regain it is repairable without being replaced. The flexibility the paper demonstrates is the chemical basis for that.

The caveat is the ligand environment. A synthetic cluster with a specific ligand set is not the same as a cluster buried in a protein, where the surrounding residues constrain the geometry and the access of reagents.

What the paper does not measure is the energetics. Breaking four bonds in sequence costs energy, and the pathway that the reagents take may be unavailable in a cell. The demonstration establishes a possibility, and the biological mechanism may use a narrower route.

The synthetic value is a handle. A cluster that can be opened is a cluster whose pieces can be substituted, and a substitution is how a label or a probe is installed at an active site.

For the field of bioinorganic chemistry, the result is a reminder that cofactors are not static objects. A cluster that breathes is a cluster that can be maintained, and maintenance is what a cell spends its effort on.

The cluster's electronic states change as bonds break, and the paper tracks them by spectroscopy. A cluster that loses a sulfur becomes a different oxidant, and the sequence of states is a sequence of redox potentials that a protein could exploit.
The synthetic result also bears on the origin of the cofactor. A cluster that assembles from iron and sulfide without a protein is a cluster that could have formed in the early ocean, and the flexibility the paper shows is what allowed a mineral to become an enzyme.
