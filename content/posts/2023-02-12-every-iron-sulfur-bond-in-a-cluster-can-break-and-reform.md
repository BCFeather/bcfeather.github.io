Iron-sulfur clusters are among the oldest cofactors in biology, and they sit at the active sites of enzymes that move electrons, fix nitrogen and reduce carbon dioxide. Their assembly and repair are processes cells perform carefully, and the chemistry involved has been difficult to observe.

In *PNAS* on 7 February 2023, the chemistry is observed in a synthetic cluster. Niklas Thompson, Gil Namkoong, Brighton Skeel, Daniel Suess and colleagues show that every iron-sulfur bond in a cuboidal Fe4S4 cluster can be broken and re-formed, a flexibility assumed for parts of the cluster rather than for all of it.

The cubane is a cube of iron and sulfur atoms, each iron coordinated by three sulfurs and by a ligand. Its function depends on the ability to accept and donate electrons, and that ability depends on the bonding within the cube, so the sequence of states the paper tracks is a sequence of redox potentials a protein could exploit.

Spectroscopy and synthesis supply the method. A cluster with a defined ligand set is prepared, a reagent that cleaves a bond is introduced, and the resulting states are characterized, which yields a cluster that opens and closes rather than one that either holds together or falls apart.

Because a cluster that can be disassembled bond by bond can be built bond by bond, assembly is where the result bears on biology: the proteins that build Fe-S clusters in cells may present a scaffold and feed iron and sulfur in a controlled order. Repair is the other half, since an enzyme whose cluster is damaged by oxygen has to be restored, and a cluster that can lose one bond and regain it is repairable without being replaced.

Ligands are the caveat, because a synthetic cluster with a chosen ligand set is not a cluster buried in a protein, where surrounding residues constrain the geometry and the access of reagents. Energetics stay unmeasured, since breaking four bonds in sequence costs energy and the pathway the reagents take may be unavailable in a cell, so the demonstration establishes a possibility while the biological mechanism may use a narrower route. What the chemistry offers is a handle, because a cluster that can be opened is one whose pieces can be substituted, and substitution is how a label or a probe reaches an active site. Cofactors are not static objects here: a cluster that breathes is a cluster that can be maintained, and maintenance is where a cell spends its effort. The origin of the cofactor follows as well, since a cluster assembling from iron and sulfide without a protein could have formed in the early ocean, and this flexibility is what let a mineral become an enzyme.

Whether the same sequence runs inside a protein is the question the synthetic system poses, because a cluster held by residues may open only along the route its ligands allow.
