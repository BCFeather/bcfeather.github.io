She died at her home in New York on 30 December, at ninety-three, and the announcement came from her publicist. She had retired in 2014, after a career that began in the 1950s and that had been conducted mostly in front of a camera. In 1976 she became the first woman to anchor a network evening news programme, at a salary that the newspapers reported at a million dollars a year. The number was repeated for decades, because it was the first time such a number had been paid to a woman in television.

Interviews are what she is remembered for. She asked heads of state and film stars the questions that their handlers had forbidden, and she did it in a voice that sounded interested rather than hostile. Her conversation with a prime minister of India, and her joint interview with a president and his wife, were watched by audiences that had not seen anything like them. She prepared obsessively, and the preparation is the part of the craft that her colleagues described most often. She had begun as a writer for a morning programme, and the camera work came later, when a producer noticed that she asked better questions than the men who were paid to ask them.

She founded a programme of interviews with women in the late 1970s, at a time when the networks were sure such a thing would not find an audience. It ran for years, and its subjects included the wives of presidents and the first women in several professions. The programme made the personal story a legitimate subject for news, which is a change that is still being argued about. She also spent two decades on a weekly news magazine, where she conducted the interviews that made the programme famous.

Her manner was parodied, and she knew it. The parodies made her more famous, and she said in interviews that being imitated was a form of being noticed. Younger journalists described her as the person who proved that a woman could hold a conversation with power on equal terms. The generation that followed her into the studios said the same thing in different words. Her interviews with foreign leaders were arranged months in advance, and the conditions she accepted were fewer than the ones her rivals demanded.

Tributes came from people she had interviewed and from people who had watched the interviews on television with their parents. A network changed its schedule to run a tribute the same evening, and the obituaries used the same few clips. Her daughter spoke about the hours her mother worked, and about the evenings when the work came home. The interviews were sold to stations abroad, and in several countries they were the first American programme of the kind that viewers there had seen.

Television has changed in ways that make her career look like a single long moment. The interview is now a podcast and a video clip, and the anchor desk is no longer the only place from which news is read. What she did was to insist that a conversation could be news, and the industry has spent the years since agreeing with her. The archive of those interviews is held by a university, and the tapes are still used to teach the craft.
