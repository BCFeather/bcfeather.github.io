Photosynthesis begins with an antenna that absorbs light and passes the excitation to a reaction centre. In red algae the antenna is a phycobilisome, a soluble complex of phycobiliproteins sitting on the thylakoid membrane, and where its energy goes has been inferred rather than seen.

In *Nature* on 15 March 2023, that arrangement is shown. The authors use cryo-electron tomography on chloroplast membranes to resolve the phycobilisome in situ, together with photosystem II, photosystem I and the light-harvesting complex, and the assembly that emerges is a megacomplex with defined connections.

Coupling is the key observation. The phycobilisome contacts photosystem II directly and reaches photosystem I through other components of the membrane, so one antenna can feed both centres, and the geometry of the contact sets the ratio of energy that each receives.

Because a complex purified in detergent loses the membrane's organisation and an antenna detached from its partners reports its own structure rather than its wiring, the word that matters is in situ, and tomography keeps the wiring. Distance supplies the parameter that transfer depends on, since a few nanometres of gap changes the coupling, and the measurements put a number on a gap previously estimated from spectroscopy.

Regulation follows from the architecture. A cell that can shift the antenna's output between two photosystems can balance the two halves of the electron transport chain, and the paper supplies the structural basis for that balance. Preparation is the technical achievement, as a chloroplast membrane has to be vitrified without ice crystals and the phycobilisome has to stay attached through the process, and preserving both is what makes the in situ claim credible.

Evolution gives the context. Chloroplasts descend from a cyanobacterial endosymbiont, and the phycobilisome is a cyanobacterial invention that land plants lost, so a red alga retains the ancestral arrangement, which is why its structure is informative about the origin. Retention raises its own question, because a large soluble antenna must be anchored and maintained while a membrane-spanning one is cheaper to build, and the persistence suggests an advantage in the light regimes that red algae inhabit.

Resolution sets the limit. Tomography of a membrane in a vitrified cell reaches a few nanometres, enough to place complexes and not enough to see the pigments, which makes the maps architectural rather than atomic. Dynamics remain open, since energy transfer happens in picoseconds and a static structure shows possible routes rather than actual fluxes. Plant science gains a correction, because the antenna has been drawn as a funnel to photosystem II and these connections make it a switchboard with two outputs, while artificial photosynthesis gains a design, since a system that routes energy between two catalysts is what a photoelectrochemical cell needs and no engineered version has matched it. A second question is whether the switchboard is dynamic, since a cell under changing light may rewire the ratio rather than only exploit a fixed geometry.
