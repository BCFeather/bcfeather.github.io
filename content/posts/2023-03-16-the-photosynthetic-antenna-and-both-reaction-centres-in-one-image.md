Photosynthesis begins with an antenna that absorbs light and passes the excitation to a reaction centre. In red algae the antenna is a phycobilisome, a soluble complex of phycobiliproteins that sits on the thylakoid membrane, and where its energy goes has been inferred rather than seen.

The structure published in *Nature* on 15 March 2023 shows it. The authors use cryo-electron tomography on chloroplast membranes to resolve the phycobilisome in situ, together with photosystem II, photosystem I and the light-harvesting complex, and the assembly that emerges is a megacomplex with defined connections.

The key observation is the coupling. The phycobilisome contacts photosystem II directly and reaches photosystem I through the membrane's other components, so that one antenna can feed both centres. The geometry of the contact sets the ratio of energy that each receives.

In situ is the word that matters. A complex purified in detergent loses the membrane's organization, and an antenna that is detached from its partners tells you its own structure and not its wiring. Tomography keeps the wiring.

The energy-transfer implication is about regulation. A cell that can shift the antenna's output between two photosystems can balance the electron transport chain's two halves, and the structural basis for that balance is what the paper provides.

The red algal lineage is the evolutionary context. Chloroplasts descend from a cyanobacterial endosymbiont, and the phycobilisome is a cyanobacterial invention that land plants lost. A red alga retains the ancestral arrangement, which is why its structure is informative about the origin.

The technique's limit is resolution. Tomography of a membrane in a vitrified cell gives a few nanometres, enough to place complexes and not enough to see the pigments. The paper's maps are architectural rather than atomic.

What remains is the dynamics. Energy transfer happens in picoseconds, and a static structure shows the possible routes rather than the actual fluxes. Spectroscopy on the same preparation is the complementary measurement.

The application is artificial photosynthesis. A system that routes energy between two catalysts is what a photoelectrochemical cell needs, and a natural megacomplex is a design that engineers have not matched.

For plant science, the result corrects a model. The antenna has been drawn as a funnel to photosystem II, and the paper's connections make it a switchboard with two outputs.

The sample preparation is the paper's technical achievement. A chloroplast membrane has to be vitrified without ice crystals, and the phycobilisome has to remain attached through the process. The authors' protocol preserves both, which is what makes the in situ claim credible.

The distance between the antenna's core and the reaction centre is the parameter that energy transfer depends on. A few nanometres of gap changes the coupling, and the tomography's measurements put a number on a distance that had been estimated from spectroscopy.
The evolutionary question is why red algae kept an arrangement that other lineages replaced. A phycobilisome is a large, soluble antenna that must be anchored and maintained, and a membrane-spanning antenna is cheaper to build. The retention suggests an advantage in the light regimes that red algae inhabit.
