How much of Africa is ploughed, planted and harvested is a question that satellite records answer more honestly than national statistics. Ground surveys are expensive, sparse and irregular from country to country, so the usual comparisons rest on modelled estimates that can differ by half. A study using daily satellite imagery combined with more than 150 ground-truth sites reports that the cultivated area across the continent is 36 per cent larger than the figure previously in use, and that the difference is concentrated in places where the farming is done by hand.

The measurement is built from 154 genomes, but the real innovation is the comparison. The team sequenced whole genomes from 154 species across five continents and three habitat transitions: from sea to land, from water to land in amphibians, from forest to grassland, and from cold to temperate zones. Each lineage was then matched against its closest relative that did not make the same move, so the signal of adaptation could be separated from the background of ordinary divergence. Convergence showed up at the level of protein-coding sequences and regulatory regions, and it did so in the same genes repeatedly.

What the numbers show is that the same changes appear in independent lineages. In the transition from water to land, for example, the same set of genes involved in skin barrier function and limb development carried the adaptive signal in multiple amphibian lineages, and the overlap was far higher than would be expected by chance. Skin and barrier function account for a disproportionate share of the changes, which is consistent with desiccation being the immediate selective pressure at the water's edge. Limb development appears in a separate cluster, pointing to a second pressure rather than a single coordinated programme.

Repetition is the result's strength and its limit. The same genes reappearing across unrelated lineages mean that the paths available to evolution are not unlimited: a land vertebrate has to solve a particular set of physical problems, and the molecular toolbox is finite. That is not the same as saying evolution is deterministic in detail. The study identifies the genes that are accessible, not the circumstances that make one lineage take the path while another stays in the water.

What remains unsettled is whether the pattern holds when the habitat changes are more subtle. The four transitions studied are large and well defined, which makes the signal easy to detect; a move from one forest type to another, or a shift in rainfall within the same biome, may not produce the same clean overlap. Sample size also varies sharply by lineage, and the 154 genomes are not evenly distributed across the tree.

The case for predictability is strongest at the level of physiology rather than form. Skin, respiration and water balance are constrained by physics, so the same molecular solutions recur; body shape and behaviour have many more degrees of freedom, and the same convergence should not be expected there. A second question is whether the regulatory changes the study finds are causal or merely markers of shared ancestry that the matching procedure did not fully remove.

Evolutionary convergence has often been illustrated with examples, the eye and the wing among them, rather than measured across a dataset. A record of 154 genomes gives the claim a denominator, and the denominator matters: convergence is common, but only within a narrow set of problems, and the constraints that produce it are physical before they are genetic.
