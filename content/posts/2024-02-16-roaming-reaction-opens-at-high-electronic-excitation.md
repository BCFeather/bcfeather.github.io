Textbook kinetics assumes that a reaction walks over the lowest saddle on the energy landscape, and that the products follow the minimum-energy path down the other side. A roaming reaction does something else. The bond that should break stays intact while the departing atom circles the fragment, then abstracts a hydrogen or recombines from an unexpected angle, and the products carry vibrational energy that the minimum-energy path never supplies. The phenomenon was found in the early 2000s, and for two decades it was studied only in low-lying electronic states, because no bright tunable source could put a molecule high enough to test the rest of the landscape.

A free-electron laser in Dalian changed that. It is an extreme-ultraviolet free-electron laser, the only user facility operating in that band, and Yang Xueming and Yuan Kaijun of the Dalian Institute of Chemical Physics used it with the theory group of Zhang Donghui and Fu Bina to prepare sulfur dioxide in a highly excited state. The result appeared in *Science* on 16 February 2024.

Photolysis near 133 nanometres produced excited oxygen in two distinct vibrational distributions. The cold one follows the minimum-energy path. The hot one does not, and experiment and quantum-state-resolved dynamics on a machine-learned potential energy surface agree that it comes from a roaming channel. That channel accounts for close to half of the excited oxygen formed, which makes it a main road rather than a side path.

Chemistry gives the result a planetary edge. Sulfur dioxide is the dominant sulfur gas in volcanic plumes, and its extreme-ultraviolet photolysis is one candidate source of oxygen in the anoxic atmosphere of the early Earth. A channel that yields vibrationally hot oxygen changes the downstream network, since hot products can clear barriers that cold ones cannot.

Rotational and vibrational state distributions are the fingerprint that separates the two paths, and the experiment resolved them. A model that reproduces the cold distribution alone would have passed a looser test.

High excitation matters because the accessible states are dense there, and a molecule can leave the minimum-energy route in more places than one. That density is exactly what a low-lying-state experiment cannot probe. Sulfur dioxide is small enough that the surface can be mapped to spectroscopic accuracy, which is why it made the test possible at all. For theory the paper is a check on assumptions. If roaming operates at high excitation, then models built on transition-state theory alone will miss a fraction of the products in photochemistry, combustion and atmospheric chemistry. The repair is not a correction term but a broader search of the potential surface, with the machine-learned fit doing the work that a fitted analytic surface cannot.

Both groups also report the same branching ratio, close to one half, which is a strong agreement for a fit this flexible. Put plainly, the claim is that roaming is general. Roaming is general, and the exception was the state of the experiments, not the state of the molecule.
