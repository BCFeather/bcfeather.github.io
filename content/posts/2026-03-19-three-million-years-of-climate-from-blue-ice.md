Continuous ice cores reach back about eight hundred thousand years, which covers the recent glacial cycles and misses the transitions that set them up. Older ice exists, but it is not in a continuous column, because the layers have been folded and thinned by flow. At a few places in Antarctica, ice that old is exposed at the surface, brought up by the flow and ablated by the wind, and the Allan Hills blue ice area is one of those places.

Seven years of field work went into recovering the material, and two papers in *Nature* on 19 March analysed it. The ice preserves bubbles of ancient air and the isotopic composition of the water itself, which together record temperature and the composition of the atmosphere at the time the ice formed. The record extends back roughly three million years, into the period when the Northern Hemisphere ice sheets first appeared.

The headline conclusion is about what drove the transitions. The dominant influence on the major climate reorganizations of the last three million years appears to have been changes in ocean temperature, with greenhouse gas changes playing a supporting role. This inverts the emphasis of much model-based work, which treats carbon dioxide as the master variable and the ocean as a feedback.

Two transitions anchor the record. Around 2.6 million years ago, ice sheets formed across the Northern Hemisphere and glacial cycles settled into a rhythm of about forty-one thousand years, which matches the obliquity of the Earth's axis. Around 1.2 million years ago, the rhythm lengthened to a hundred thousand years, and the ice sheets grew larger, in a shift that has never been explained to general satisfaction.

Ocean temperature as the driver makes the mid-Pleistocene transition easier to think about. A colder ocean can hold more heat before ice sheets begin to grow and more heat before they retreat, which lengthens the cycle, and a change in ocean circulation can change the temperature of the deep sea without changing the atmosphere much. The ice core gives the deep-sea temperature directly, which is what models have had to reconstruct.

Sampling is the constraint that every reader should hold in mind. Blue ice records are assembled from many discrete samples, and the age scale depends on stratigraphic correlation and on dating of volcanic ash where it exists. A three-million-year curve built this way is a triumph of field work and a curve with gaps, and the intervals where the record is thinnest are exactly the intervals where a transition might hide.

What the papers change is the hierarchy of causes. Carbon dioxide is still a control knob, and the record does not say otherwise; it says that the ocean was the variable that moved first and furthest in the transitions that matter. For a planet now warming because of carbon dioxide, that is a distinction worth keeping straight, because the ocean's response is where the consequences accumulate.
