At twenty to six the kettle goes on, and the fridge door opens before the kitchen light does. A sheet of paper is taped to it, blue biro, dates out of sequence, two of them ringed in red because those cannot be moved.

The paper holds the household's next six weeks. A dental appointment for a boy of nine, a residence permit that expires on a Thursday, an eye clinic for a grandfather who will need someone to read the signs, a cousin's birthday that nobody has mentioned to the cousin in four years. The items look administrative, and they are. Each one sits inside a larger piece of knowledge: which entrance of the hospital has the ramp, which card the clinic accepts, how long the queue takes on a Monday, and what to say at the desk when the clerk asks for a document that no longer exists.

Keeping that knowledge is a job with no title and no hours. It is done in the gaps of other work, on the bus, in the ten minutes before a shift, at the moment a message arrives saying the appointment has been moved to Thursday week. Economists fold it into a category called unpaid care, alongside cooking and cleaning. The fold is honest, as far as it goes. The labour is real, it substitutes for services somebody would otherwise be paid to provide, and it never appears on an invoice.

Time-use surveys in most wealthy countries record these hours under housework and care, in categories broad enough to hold an entire life. The totals differ from country to country and from household to household, and the pattern inside them does not: the person who keeps the dates is usually the person who also keeps the feelings, and in most homes that is still a woman. The statistic is dull. What it describes is not.

A Tuesday in that job runs like this. Up at 5:40 to make food that will be eaten at noon by someone else. A stop at the pharmacy where the prescription has one item missing and the queue has eleven people. A form at the school office with the same twelve questions as last year, answered again in the same handwriting. A call to the insurance line that plays music for nineteen minutes before a voice explains that the claim was filed under the wrong policy number. Then the shopping, then the boy's dinner, and the pans.

None of it produces an object anyone can point at. The house looks the same on the days this work is done and on the days it is not, for about three weeks. That lag is why the work is invisible from inside a family. It succeeds by leaving no trace, and a trace appears only when something has been missed.

A child reads all of it as weather. The shoes by the door in the evening were simply there. The thermos was filled because thermoses are filled. Somebody stood at the school gate in the rain with a second umbrella, and the umbrella was part of the gate.

Sleep comes late in this flat, and the work continues in a lower key. The corridor light stays on until the last door closes. A cough from the back room is heard through a wall and answered with a glass of water, and neither the cough nor the water is discussed in the morning. Somebody checks the back door, checks the boy's shoes are dry for tomorrow, checks the phone charging by the bed of a person who will call at two if the blood pressure is wrong.

At nineteen, in a first rented room, a person learns what weather costs. The electricity account is final-notice before it is anything else, the clinic will not answer on a Saturday, and the appointment that was missed has to be rebooked in eight weeks. The realization arrives without drama, usually while standing in a queue with a number in hand. The thing that held the household up was a person, and that person had a name.

Reminders continue after the child leaves, and they arrive as small irritations: the deadline for a document, the name of a clinic, a message asking whether the deposit was paid. For two years these are answered late. In the third year they are answered on time, and the child starts writing the dates down, out of sequence, on a sheet taped to a fridge in another city.

Somewhere under the list and the forms sits a first exchange that nobody remembers making. Someone held you before you could hold anything back. A hand on a back at three in the morning, a corridor walked until the crying stopped, the weight of a child who would not sleep, and the arm that would not let go. Everything afterwards repeats that transaction with less sleep and more paperwork.

Kinship is the love that arrives already assigned. Nobody chose the grandfather, the cousin, the sister with the difficult temper; they were there when memory began, and the love comes bundled with an obligation to answer the phone. That bundle is what makes it durable. It is also what makes it expensive, since the obligation cannot be renegotiated on a bad afternoon the way a friendship can.

A habit like this is inherited before it is understood. The mother who keeps the dates learned it in a house where her own mother kept them, in a village where the clinic opened at eight and the bus came twice a day. She never learned to swim, because the swimming lessons fell on Saturdays and Saturdays belonged to her grandmother. She does not describe the lessons as a sacrifice. She says the family did not do that.

Food is where the feeling gets spoken, because the words are unreliable. 'Have you eaten?' arrives at eleven at night and expects a photograph of a plate. A soup crosses the city in a container that has to be returned. A recipe is copied out by hand although the book is on the shelf, because a copy can be held.

Money enters quietly. A parent who disapproves of a grown child's choices still pays the deposit and still drives four hours to a city he dislikes, because the alternative is a two-day wait for a train. He does not say the word love during any of it. He says the traffic was bad.

Care can also be a cage, and the households that pretend otherwise are the ones that suffer longest. A mother keeps every date and resents every date. A ledger is kept in silence, itemized and never produced. The control arrives dressed as concern, asking where you were and why the phone was off. Love that will not let a person leave is still love, and it is also the thing they have to escape.

The grandmother's handwriting appears on the older forms in the drawer, the ones from 1998 and 2004, squared and careful. She stopped filling them in the year her eyes went, and for two years afterwards the forms were filled for her by a daughter who had learned her signature from watching. The reversal is not announced. It happens in the small print, in the line where the patient's name is written by another hand.

Reversal comes slowly, then all at once. A man of forty-five learns to cook the six dishes his mother made, badly at first, and the failure is specific: too much salt, the pan too hot, the onions cut too thick. A daughter learns the password to a portal that holds her father's scans, and finds that the portal was last opened by him, in a month when he could still read.

Receiving is harder than giving, and the household has no practice for it. When the son finally cooks the dinner, his mother stands in the doorway and says the onions are cut too thick, then eats all of it and washes the pan before he can. The comment is how the thanks arrives, and nobody in the room says so.

Then one winter the keeper of dates stops. It happens between one Thursday and the next, in a hospital that had been on the list for years, without the speech or the reconciliation the films would have supplied. The calendar on the fridge is still there, unread, with a date in red that nobody now needs.

What follows is arithmetic. Whose turn it is to book the follow-up, call the bank, sit with the aunt, collect the death certificate from an office that opens at nine and closes at noon. The siblings divide it in the first week and discover in the third that the mother had been doing all of it in about ninety minutes a day, and that ninety minutes was what the household had been standing on.

Letters arrive addressed to her for eight months. The bank sends a card replacement. A clinic sends a reminder about a check-up that will not happen. Somebody calls the landline and asks for her by her first name, and the person who answers has to say the sentence again, and then listen to the silence that follows it.

Disagreement survives inside this love. Two brothers do not speak for a year over a house, and one of them still drives the other to the airport at five in the morning. Nobody in the car mentions the house. The drive belongs to an older contract than the argument, and it is why the argument never quite ends the relationship.

Some relatives do not come. They send the message, the transfer, the flowers, and they stay where they are, and the household learns to count them among the people who are absent without malice. That absence has a texture of its own, softer than enmity and harder to name, and it takes up space at the table on the days that matter.

Birthdays sit at the bottom of the list, below the permits and the clinics, and they are the entries nobody can quite remember adding. A date for a great-aunt in another province, a friend of the family who moved away in 2011, a child's classmate whose party was attended once. They stay on the paper for years because removing them would be a decision, and nobody wants to make it.

Written now in another flat, the list carries the parents' names: a scan for the father, a repeat prescription for the mother, an anniversary that used to be theirs to keep. The transfer has no ceremony. It happens because a form asks for a next of kin, and the pen is in a different hand.

By March a second hand has added two dates to the sheet on the fridge, in black ink, slightly crooked. One is a vaccination, one is a birthday. Nobody comments on the change of pen. The kettle goes on at twenty to six, the list is read by someone new, and the household carries on with the arrangement that no one chose and no one has yet been able to leave.
