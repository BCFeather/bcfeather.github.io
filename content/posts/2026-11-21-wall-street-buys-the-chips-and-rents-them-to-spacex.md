---

Apollo Global Management and Goldman Sachs priced about 44 billion dollars of senior and subordinated notes on 20 November, and the collateral behind them is a fleet of graphics processors. The entities that raised the money will buy the chips and related equipment and lease them to SpaceX, according to the announcement, and the deal was oversubscribed. BlackRock, Goldman's asset management arm and funds managed by KKR took part alongside the two leads.

The structure is a financing lease rather than a purchase. An Apollo-managed entity buys the hardware, and SpaceX pays rent for it over terms of roughly five years to five years and three months for each batch. Deliveries are scheduled to begin in the first half of 2027, which means the notes are raised against equipment that does not yet exist in a building, and the rental income starts only after the last of it is installed.

That structure spreads the purchase and keeps it off the balance sheet. It lets a company that has never been short of ambition acquire computing without adding the full purchase price to its own balance sheet, and it gives the lenders a claim on physical assets that can be re-leased if the tenant fails. The trade-off is cost: a lease embeds the financier's return, so the same chips cost more over their life than they would bought outright, and the obligation runs for years whatever happens to demand.

The timing is not accidental. Demand for accelerators has outrun the cash that the companies using them generate, and the financing has moved from equity to debt to, in this case, a rental market with Wall Street as landlord. Elon Musk said over the same weekend that the AI industry may face a serious funding shortfall next year, arguing that powering, cooling and running large computing installations reliably is far harder than buying them, and that if revenue lags the cost of compute by even a few months the working capital gap runs into hundreds of billions of dollars.

The equipment side is tightening too. Dell, Hewlett Packard Enterprise, Lenovo and Supermicro have reportedly sought price increases of as much as 45 per cent on AI servers, including on orders signed months earlier, which pushes the cost of a build-out up after the budget has been approved. When hardware prices rise and financing costs rise together, the arithmetic of a data-centre programme changes twice.

For SpaceX the chips are a cost of running whatever computing its launch and satellite businesses need, and the lease spreads that cost. For the noteholders the question is counterparty risk in a company with a heavy capital programme of its own, secured by equipment whose resale value depends on how quickly the next generation arrives. Both sides have priced that in, and the oversubscription says the market currently likes the trade.

What to watch is dull and decisive: whether the batches arrive on the 2027 schedule, what rate the notes carry relative to investment-grade debt, and whether other operators copy the lease. If they do, the constraint on the AI build-out stops being the supply of chips and becomes the supply of tenants with long enough contracts to justify the borrowing.
