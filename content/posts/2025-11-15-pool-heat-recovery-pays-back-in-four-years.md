A swimming pool is an energy problem in three parts: heating the water, heating the hall and dehumidifying the air. A municipal pool installed a heat recovery system this year and reported a 60 per cent cut in gas consumption, and the design's interest is that it collects heat from three sources at once.

The sources are the pool's overflow drain, the showers' greywater and the hall's exhaust air. Each is between 25 and 32 degrees, which is too cool for a boiler and warm enough for a heat pump's evaporator.

Coefficient of performance is 4.2, which means 4.2 kilowatts of heat per kilowatt of electricity. A gas boiler at 90 per cent efficiency delivers 0.9, and the comparison is where the saving comes from.

Dehumidification is the second change. A pool hall's air carries evaporated water, and the conventional approach is to heat and ventilate. The new system condenses the moisture on the heat pump's evaporator, which recovers the latent heat and dehumidifies in one step.

Capital cost was 380,000 dollars against an annual gas bill of 140,000, and the payback is four years at current prices. A pool with a smaller gas bill would wait longer, which is why the case is about a big pool.

The control problem is the operational risk. A heat pump's output falls with the source temperature, and a winter's drain temperature of 20 degrees reduces the capacity by a third. The system keeps a gas boiler as a backup and runs it 40 hours a year.

A district heating connection is the policy alternative. A pool near a waste incinerator would do better to buy heat than to recover its own, and the city's plan for the next pool is a connection rather than a heat pump.

A pool's energy loss is mostly evaporation. Water at 28 degrees in a hall at 26 loses heat through the surface, and a dehumidifier that condenses the moisture recovers it as latent heat. A heat pump that lifts that heat to 45 degrees supplies the showers.

The control problem is real. A pool's demand peaks in the morning and its shower load peaks in the evening, and a buffer tank sized for four hours of demand is what makes the two curves meet. A system without storage runs its heat pump at the wrong time.

A public pool's case is a municipal one. A council that owns the building and pays the energy bill captures the saving directly, and a council that leases the building to an operator captures it only through a lease's terms. The payback's four years assume the first arrangement.

Heat below 40 degrees used to be worthless, and a heat pump is what changed the valuation. Waste heat below 40 degrees used to be worthless, and a device with a coefficient of performance of 4 makes it a resource, which changes what a building's drains are worth.
