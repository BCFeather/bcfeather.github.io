Liz Truss resigned as prime minister on Thursday afternoon, forty-four days after taking office, which makes her the shortest-serving holder of the job in British history. She spoke outside Number 10 and said she had been chosen by the parliamentary party and that she could no longer command its confidence. A leadership contest will finish within a week, and she will stay in office until a successor is chosen. The Conservative Party has to choose a prime minister for the third time this year.

Her government fell because of a budget. On 23 September the chancellor, Kwasi Kwarteng, announced forty-five billion pounds of unfunded tax cuts together with a cap on energy bills, and the markets read the combination as a sign that the arithmetic had been done in a hurry. The pound fell to its lowest level against the dollar on record, gilt yields jumped and pension funds that hedge with interest-rate swaps were forced to sell assets to meet collateral calls. The Bank of England bought gilts for thirteen days to stop the spiral, and the chancellor was dismissed on 14 October.

His successor, Jeremy Hunt, reversed almost every measure within four days and moved the fiscal statement forward to 31 October. The home secretary, Suella Braverman, resigned on Wednesday after sending an official document from a private address, and her letter contained a paragraph about the government's direction that her colleagues read as an attack. The whips were reported to have told the prime minister on Wednesday evening that her position had gone. She spent the night in talks and announced the decision at half past one.

Her mandate came from a ballot of party members in the summer, when she beat Rishi Sunak with 57 per cent of the vote. That contest had been fought on a promise of tax cuts that the markets then refused to fund, and the promise became the reason her government lasted six weeks. The 1922 Committee met on Thursday morning and set a threshold of a hundred nominations, with the aim of finishing the contest by Friday of the following week. If two candidates remain, the members will vote again.

Markets welcomed the news, which is the one thing the government had asked of them for a month. The yield on thirty-year gilts fell and sterling rose, and several mortgage lenders that had pulled products from sale began to return. The opposition called for a general election, and the party has said there will be none. The Commons will meet on Monday, and the new chancellor is due to present the fiscal plan on the date that was already booked.

What remains is the problem that started the crisis, which is an economy with inflation above ten per cent and a budget that needs either cuts or taxes. Every candidate in the coming contest has promised to explain where the money will come from, and none of them has yet said which promise will be broken. The country has had three prime ministers in four years, and its allies are asking whether the fourth will last longer than the third.
