description: "Global modelling finds that putting photovoltaics on building façades would generate substantial electricity, cut building energy use and reduce emissions, and that the option is largely unexploited."
---

Building-integrated photovoltaics are usually imagined on roofs, and roofs are the obvious place: they face the sky, they are unshaded for most of the day and they are already part of the building envelope. The constraint is area, because a roof has the footprint of the building and a façade has its perimeter multiplied by its height, which for a tower is the larger number by an order of magnitude.

A modelling study reported as a research briefing in *Nature Climate Change* on 3 April takes the global building stock and asks what would happen if vertical surfaces carried photovoltaics as well. The analysis combines insolation on façades at different latitudes and orientations with a model of building energy demand, and it estimates electricity generation, the reduction in cooling and heating loads, and the resulting emissions.

The results are large enough to matter at the scale of a national grid. Façade photovoltaics in the aggregate could supply a significant share of global building electricity demand, and the buildings that would benefit most are the ones in dense cities, where roofs are few, land is expensive and the grid connection is already there.

The energy balance is more subtle than generation alone. A photovoltaic panel on a wall shades the wall, which reduces solar gain in summer and therefore cooling demand, and it also insulates the envelope, which reduces heat loss in winter. Both effects are small per square metre and large over a city, and the study includes them, which is what separates an engineering estimate from a sales pitch.

Orientation is the limitation that matters most. A south-facing façade at a mid latitude receives useful irradiation, an east or west-facing façade receives it at the wrong time of day relative to demand, and a north-facing façade in the northern hemisphere receives almost nothing. The global potential is therefore concentrated in buildings whose geometry happens to point the right way, and a warehouse on an industrial estate is a better candidate than an office tower whose glazing faces north for the whole of its working day.

Cost is the second limitation. Façade photovoltaics compete with architectural glazing, and a panel that replaces a window changes the light inside the building, which is the part of the design that architects defend. The technology is available and the economics depend on whether the alternative is glass and whether the electricity is valued at retail or at wholesale.

What the study establishes is that the resource exists and that the models can quantify it. What it does not establish is that developers will install it, because a façade is a design decision made once and a photovoltaic array is an investment recovered over decades, and the two decisions sit with different people in the same project, one of whom is paid for the building and the other of whom pays the electricity bill.
