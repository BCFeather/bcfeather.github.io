Thrombectomy transformed large-vessel stroke, and the natural temptation was to extend it to smaller vessels. Occlusions in the M2 and M3 segments, and in the distal branches beyond them, affect a large share of patients, and many of those patients are left with disability even when the clot dissolves. Trials of the question have accumulated slowly, and the results have not matched the enthusiasm. The extension also raised a practical question: if the procedure helps only a subset, which subset, and at what cost in time to reperfusion for everyone else.

DISTAL randomised 543 adults with acute ischaemic stroke and medium or distal vessel occlusion at 55 hospitals in Europe and the Middle East, comparing endovascular treatment plus best medical therapy with best medical therapy alone. The 90-day result has already been reported, and the 12-month follow-up appeared in *The Lancet* on 9 July 2026. Median age was 77, most occlusions were M2 or M3, and 65 per cent of participants received intravenous thrombolysis. The trial was stopped early for futility at the 90-day analysis, which is why the one-year follow-up is reported separately rather than as a pre-specified endpoint.

At 12 months, the median modified Rankin score was 2 in both groups, with no significant difference in functional outcome or in overall survival. The finding reproduces the 90-day result and rules out the possibility that a benefit emerged slowly, which was the main hope behind following patients for a year. The authors conclude that routine endovascular treatment is not supported for mild to moderate medium or distal occlusion. Subgroup analyses by occlusion site did not identify a group with a clear benefit, and the confidence intervals were wide enough to be uninformative in the smallest strata.

Why the procedure fails here is a matter of anatomy. A large-vessel occlusion threatens a large territory, and removing the clot saves enough tissue to change a patient's life. A distal occlusion threatens a small territory, the procedure takes time and carries a risk of perforation, and the tissue at stake is often not worth the journey to the angiography suite.

Limits come from the population. Patients with severe deficits from a distal occlusion, or with a large perfusion mismatch, were underrepresented, and the trial cannot say whether a selected subgroup would benefit. The open-label design also means that treating physicians knew the assignment, which affects ancillary care even when outcomes are assessed blind. A negative trial of this size is also a useful benchmark for future devices, since any new catheter will have to beat medical therapy rather than an older procedure.

Practice will change by default, not by prohibition. Distal occlusions should be treated medically unless a patient's deficit is disproportionate to the imaging, and the burden of proof has shifted to the intervention. The next trial worth running would enrich for severe deficits and for imaging evidence of salvageable tissue, since that is where the physiology still argues for opening the vessel.
