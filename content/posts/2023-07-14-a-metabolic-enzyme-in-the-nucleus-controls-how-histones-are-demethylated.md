---

Histone demethylases remove a methyl mark from a histone, and they need alpha-ketoglutarate as a cofactor. Supply of that cofactor has been assumed to come from the cytoplasm's metabolism. In *Science* on 14 July 2023, a local source is found. The authors show that a form of alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase localizes to the nucleus, produces the cofactor there, and controls demethylation of histones.

Localization is the finding. The enzyme's canonical role sits in the mitochondrial Krebs cycle, and a nuclear pool of the same activity is a second function for one protein. A dehydrogenase producing the cofactor in one compartment and consuming it in another is a protein whose location decides its function.

Chromatin consequence is direct. A demethylase limited by cofactor availability is one whose activity tracks the nucleus's metabolism, and the marks change when the nuclear enzyme is perturbed. Design of that perturbation carries the evidence, since removing a nuclear localization signal leaves mitochondrial activity intact while the chromatin change follows the nuclear loss.

Link to metabolism is the significance. Metabolism and chromatin have been connected through cytoplasmic pools, and a nuclear source makes the connection local and fast, so a cell's metabolic state can be read by its chromatin without a signal travelling from the mitochondrion. Supply chain frames it, because a cofactor diffusing in from the cytoplasm would arrive slowly and at a concentration set elsewhere.

Chemistry explains the cofactor's role. Alpha-ketoglutarate is consumed by the demethylase in a reaction also using oxygen and iron, and its concentration sets the rate. Cancer connects plausibly, since a tumour's metabolic state shapes its chromatin, and a nuclear enzyme is a node where the two meet.

Method is the combination. A genetic perturbation of the nuclear form, a measurement of the cofactor in the nucleus and a readout of histone marks together establish the chain. Resolution sets the limitation, as a nucleus's metabolite pool is small and a bulk assay cannot separate the compartments, so the pool is inferred from enzyme activity.

Extent is what the paper does not establish, because one demethylase family is affected and whether the nuclear pool controls others stays open. Epigenetics gains a compartment's metabolism, since the nucleus was treated as a compartment without one, and this paper gives it one. Isocitrate dehydrogenase offers the comparison, because its mutations produce an oncometabolite inhibiting demethylases, and a nuclear cofactor source would modulate that effect. A metabolite sensor targeted to the nucleus would measure the pool directly rather than infer it, and that measurement is the missing link between the enzyme's presence and the cofactor's concentration at the histone. Whether the nuclear pool responds to the cell's nutritional state is the physiological question, and a nucleus that makes its own cofactor would buffer chromatin against a fluctuating cytoplasm. A comparison across tissues would show whether the nuclear enzyme is a general feature of dividing cells or a peculiarity of the lines the authors study, and that survey is the cheapest breadth the result can buy.
