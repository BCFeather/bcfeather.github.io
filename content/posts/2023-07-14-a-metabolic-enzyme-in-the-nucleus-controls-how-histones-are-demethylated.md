---

Histone demethylases are enzymes that remove a methyl mark from a histone, and they need alpha-ketoglutarate as a cofactor. The cofactor's supply has been assumed to come from the cytoplasm's metabolism.

The work in *Science*, 14 July 2023 finds a local source. The authors show that a form of alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase localizes to the nucleus, produces the cofactor there, and controls the demethylation of histones.

The localization is the finding. The enzyme's canonical role is in the mitochondrial Krebs cycle, and a nuclear pool of the same activity is a second function for one protein.

The chromatin consequence is direct. A demethylase that is limited by its cofactor's availability is one whose activity tracks the nucleus's metabolism, and the authors' measurements show the marks changing when the nuclear enzyme is perturbed.

The link is the significance. Metabolism and chromatin have been connected through the cytoplasm's pools, and a nuclear source makes the connection local and fast.

The developmental reading is that a cell's metabolic state can be read by its chromatin without a signal traveling from the mitochondrion.

The method is the combination. A genetic perturbation of the nuclear form, a measurement of the cofactor in the nucleus, and a readout of histone marks together establish the chain.

The limitation is the measurement's resolution. A nucleus's metabolite pool is small, and a bulk assay of a cell's metabolites cannot separate the compartments.

What the paper does not establish is the extent. One demethylase family is affected, and whether the nuclear pool controls the others is open.

For epigenetics, the value is a compartment's metabolism. The nucleus has been treated as a compartment without its own metabolism, and the paper gives it one.

The cofactor's chemistry is the link. Alpha-ketoglutarate is consumed by the demethylase in a reaction that also uses oxygen and iron, and its concentration sets the rate.

The enzyme's dual role is the surprise. A dehydrogenase that produces the cofactor in one compartment and consumes it in another is a protein whose location determines its function.

The perturbation's design is the evidence. A nuclear-localization signal removed from the enzyme leaves the mitochondrial activity intact, and the chromatin change follows the nuclear loss.

The comparison with isocitrate dehydrogenase is instructive. That enzyme's mutations produce an oncometabolite that inhibits demethylases, and a nuclear source of the cofactor would modulate that effect.

The cancer connection is plausible. A tumour's metabolic state shapes its chromatin, and a nuclear enzyme is a node where the two meet.

The technical limit is the metabolite's turnover. A pool that is small and fast is hard to measure, and the authors' approach infers the pool from the enzyme's activity.

The generality is the question. Whether other cofactors are also produced locally is the wider implication of the paper's result.
The compartment's supply chain is the paper's frame. A cofactor that diffuses from the cytoplasm would arrive slowly and at a concentration set elsewhere, and a local source is one that responds to the nucleus's own demand.
