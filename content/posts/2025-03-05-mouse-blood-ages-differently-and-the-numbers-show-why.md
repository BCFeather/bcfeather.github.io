description: "A study of blood stem cells in mice published in *Nature* finds that the animals accumulate about 45 mutations a year in their blood-forming cells, only three times the human rate despite a lifespan thirty times shorter."
---

Blood is the tissue that geneticists can read most precisely, because a stem cell that acquires a mutation passes it to every descendant, and the descendants can be sequenced. A phylogenetic tree built from those sequences reconstructs the history of a stem cell population: how many cells founded it, when they divided and which clones expanded.

A paper in *Nature* on 5 March applies the method to mice and reports a mutation rate of about 45 per year in stem and progenitor cells, against roughly 15 per year in humans. The comparison is the striking part, because a mouse lives two or three years and a human eighty, and a naive expectation would be that the short-lived animal accumulates mutations at a much higher rate per year.

Explanation is metabolic and structural. A mouse cell divides more often and lives in a higher-oxygen, higher-temperature environment with a faster heartbeat, and each division is an opportunity for a copying error. A rate that is three times the human one despite a lifespan that is a thirtieth as long means that a mouse accumulates about a tenth of the mutations that a human does over a lifetime, which is why mouse cancers are mostly driven by other things, and why a laboratory mouse is a poor model for the mutation burden that human ageing carries.

Architecture of the population is the second result. The stem cell pool is established during embryogenesis and then expands steadily through life to about 70,000 cells, with each cell renewing roughly every six weeks. Human stem cells behave differently: the pool turns over more slowly and the clones that dominate old age are the product of decades of selection.

Aged mice did not lose clonal diversity. In humans, old blood becomes dominated by a handful of clones that carry mutations in genes such as *DNMT3A* and *TET2*, a condition called clonal haematopoiesis that raises the risk of blood cancer and of cardiovascular disease. The mice kept a broad pool and developed only small expanded clones, and those clones grew larger after an injury to the blood system.

Selection landscape is nevertheless conserved. The genes that were mutated in the mouse clones are the genes that appear in human clonal haematopoiesis, and the order in which the mutations accumulated followed a similar path. A short-lived animal that reproduces the same selection with a tenth of the mutations is an animal in which the experiment can be run quickly, and that is the practical value of the result.

Limits are the ones a model organism always carries. A mouse is not a small human, the telomeres are longer, the tumour suppressors differ and the immune system has a different architecture. What the paper provides is a baseline against which a perturbation can be measured, and the studies that knock out a gene and watch a clone expand are the ones that will use it first.
