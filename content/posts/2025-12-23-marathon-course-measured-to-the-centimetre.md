A marathon is 42,195 metres, and the number is a measurement rather than a definition. A course's certification rests on a calibrated bicycle, a steel tape and a surveyor's protocol, and the uncertainty of 0.1 per cent is 42 metres, which is the distance a record can be worth.

The instrument is a Jones counter, a device that counts a bicycle wheel's revolutions and is calibrated on a measured kilometre. A measurer rides the course on a line 30 centimetres from the corners, which is the shortest path a runner can take.

Calibration is the accuracy chain. A calibration course is laid with a steel tape that is itself calibrated against a national standard, and the counter's constant is measured four times and averaged.

Certification expires in ten years or when a course changes, and a record on an expired certificate needs a re-measurement. That is what happened to the record in dispute this autumn, and the re-measurement found the course 0.08 per cent long. A measurer's notes are the record a protest is answered with, and a race that keeps them can show where a turn's radius was taken.

Altimetry changed the protocol. A modern survey uses a GPS receiver with a fixed baseline to check elevation, because a course's climb and descent must be within a tolerance for a record to stand on a looped course.

Geometry and statistics are the two ways to measure a race. A track's 400 metres is surveyed once and its accuracy is a millimetre, because the loop is short and the measurement is geometric. A road course is long and its measurement is statistical.

Race directors bear the cost. A certification costs 500 dollars in fees and a measurer's day, and a race that changes its route annually pays it every year, which is why some races keep a route for a decade.

A course's short-course risk is one-sided. A measure that is 0.1 per cent long costs every runner a few seconds; one that is short makes the record invalid. Measurers therefore aim for a course at the long end of the tolerance, which is why certified courses are consistently slightly over distance.

A city's construction is the reason certificates expire. A road resurfaced or a bridge replaced changes a course's length by metres, and a race that does not remeasure after a works program is running a course that no longer exists.

A course's certification is a public document and a record's foundation. A race publishes its certificate's number, and a record's ratification cites it. A race that cannot produce the certificate is a race whose fast times are anecdotes.

A record depends on a bicycle, and that dependence is the subject. A time is a measurement of a runner on a course, and a course is a measurement of a city's streets, and the second is where a record becomes provisional.
