The 2022 mpox outbreak created an urgent question and a ready answer. Tecovirimat was approved for smallpox on the basis of animal models, was available through a protocol, and was given to thousands of patients because a pandemic does not wait for a trial. The trial ran alongside the outbreak, and its result settles whether the drug does what the field hoped.

STOMP randomised 412 participants with laboratory-confirmed clade II mpox to oral tecovirimat or placebo, of whom 336 had active skin or mucosal lesions and entered the primary analysis. The endpoint was the cumulative incidence of clinical resolution by day 29, assessed as a competing risk because healing and other events compete. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 25 February and were funded by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases.

Resolution by day 29 was 83 per cent on tecovirimat against 84 per cent on placebo, a hazard ratio of 0.98 with a confidence interval from 0.74 to 1.31 and a *P* value of 0.89. Pain among participants with severe pain fell by a difference of 0.1 point, complete lesion healing had a hazard ratio of 0.97, and viral DNA clearance did not differ. Every endpoint points in the same direction, which is the direction of no effect at all, and the confidence intervals are narrow enough to exclude a large one.

Safety was unremarkable, with a grade 3 or higher adverse events in 4 per cent of the treated group against 3 per cent of the placebo group. That is the pattern of a drug that is well tolerated and inactive against the condition being treated, and it removes the argument that the dose was limited by toxicity.

Its own limitation is the population studied. Clade II mpox in this cohort was mostly a painful skin illness in adults who were not severely immunocompromised, and the participants who died or needed intensive care were largely outside the randomised comparison. A drug that does nothing for a self-limiting illness may still do something for a severe one, and the trial was not designed to answer that question for clade I disease.

That distinction matters for the next outbreak. Clade I mpox has higher mortality and affects children more often, and tecovirimat has been used there on the strength of the same animal data. The STOMP result does not support the inference that clade I will respond, and it does not forbid a trial that asks; it does mean that the burden of proof has shifted.

The lesson is about how medicine uses a plausible mechanism. Tecovirimat blocks a viral protein that the virus needs to leave a cell, the mechanism is elegant and the drug is safe, and none of that produced a benefit in a well-conducted trial. Plausibility is a hypothesis, and a hypothesis is now answered.
