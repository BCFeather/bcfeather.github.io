description: "Ferroelectric nematic liquid crystals built from achiral molecules turn out to have a chiral ground state, with left and right handed twists of polarisation that lower the electrostatic energy at a cost in elastic energy."
---

A ferroelectric nematic liquid crystal is made of molecules that carry a large dipole and that are not chiral, yet the phase has a polar orientational order, with the dipoles pointing the same way. The standard description of that phase is unidirectional, a single polar axis fixed through the sample. Whether the axis can twist is the question the paper addresses. A single fixed axis is an assumption about the ground state, and an assumption of that kind is what the paper tests.

Work posted to arXiv on 18 January 2024 shows that the ground state of the phase, when nothing external fixes an alignment direction, is chiral. The polarisation forms left and right handed helices, and domains of opposite handedness are separated by walls. Chirality is not available to such a phase in the usual description, which is what makes the observation worth reporting. The container is not imposing a direction, so the sample itself has to select a handedness.

Twisting costs elastic energy, and the authors are explicit that the helicoidal deformation and the defect walls both raise that cost. What the twist buys is a reduction in electrostatic energy, and the balance between the two is what selects a helical ground state rather than a uniform one. Both terms are bulk energies, so the argument holds for any sample large enough to ignore its boundaries. A family of allowed deformations is what the calculation minimises over, and the result is a helicoid whose pitch follows from the two bulk energies.

Doping is the test. When the material is doped with ions, the twist weakens, which is the signature of an electrostatic mechanism, since mobile charges screen the field that the twist is avoiding. Since a coincidence would not produce a doping dependence, the mechanism follows from the data. Screening length is the measurable quantity here, and a doping series is how a laboratory would test the claim.

The result matters for the description of the phase. A material with no chiral centres has been described as achiral by construction, and here the chirality appears from the polar order itself rather than from the shape of any molecule. A phase needs a description before it can be used, and a chiral ground state changes that description.

What the paper does not do is give a microscopic model of the twist. The argument is macroscopic, built on elastic and electrostatic energies, and a reader who wants the molecular mechanism will not find it here. Macroscopic arguments are the right first step for a new phase, and a reader can check them without a microscopic model.

The practical consequence is that a ferroelectric nematic is a soft material in which chirality can be switched by doping, which is the kind of handle a device would want. A handle is what a device engineer asks for, and doping is the cheapest one available.
