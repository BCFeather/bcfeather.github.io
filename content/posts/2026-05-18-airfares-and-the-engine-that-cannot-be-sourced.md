description: "Airfares on short-haul routes have stopped falling, and the reason is a shortage of engine overhaul capacity rather than of demand. Shop visits now take 120 days against 60, and the fleet is flying less."
---

Airfares are set by capacity and cost, and a shortage that raises both is visible in a carrier's results. A low-cost carrier reported this month that its capacity would grow 2 per cent this year rather than 8, and attributed the shortfall to engine shop-visit turnaround, which has risen from 60 days to 120.

The engine is a geared turbofan with a known durability issue in a hot-section component, and the manufacturer has issued service bulletins that require inspection at shorter intervals. Every aircraft in the fleet now needs a shop visit within four years rather than six.

MRO capacity is the constraint. The manufacturer's own shops and two licensed partners handle the fleet, and their combined capacity is 380 visits a year against a requirement of 520. A new shop opens next year and adds 120.

Cost per visit has risen with the duration. A shop visit costs 3.5 million dollars, and the aircraft earns nothing while it waits, so the effective cost per available seat-kilometre has risen 6 per cent in two years.

Fares have followed. Short-haul fares on the carrier's routes are 9 per cent above last year at the same stage of booking, and the carrier's load factor is 92 per cent, which means the market is absorbing the increase.

The comparison with the previous generation is instructive. The older engine needed a shop visit every six years and had two overhaul providers; the new engine is more efficient in cruise and more constrained in maintenance. That trade is visible only after a fleet has aged.

New entrants are affected more. A carrier with 30 aircraft cannot negotiate a maintenance contract with priority, and two start-ups have delayed launch by a year because no shop would commit to a slot.

Lessors are the third party in the constraint. An aircraft's lease requires a maintenance reserve payment per flight hour, and an aircraft parked for a shop visit earns nothing while the reserve accrues. Two lessors have agreed to defer reserves for the affected fleet, which is a concession that keeps airlines solvent through the wait.

The engine's durability fix is in test. A redesigned turbine blade coating is running in 12 engines, and the manufacturer expects a 40 per cent life extension. That would move the constraint from the shop to the order book.

The route mix explains why fares rose where they did. The affected engine powers a narrowbody that flies short sectors, and a carrier with a long-haul fleet is unaffected. The fare increase is therefore concentrated on regional routes, which have fewer substitutes.

The manufacturer's own liability is part of the story. It has agreed to fund the shop visits above the contracted interval, which is an admission that the durability was below specification. That liability is why a fix is being tested rather than deferred.

For a reader, the mechanism is that an airline's capacity is set by its maintenance plan as much as by its order book. When a component's durability falls, the constraint moves from the factory to the shop, and the fare follows.
