Insulin is a protein, and proteins do not cross intact skin: the stratum corneum blocks molecules above roughly 500 daltons, which puts insulin, at about 5,800 daltons, well outside the range. Injections work but impose a daily burden that explains much of the gap between prescribed and actual dosing, and the alternatives each have a cost of their own. Oral insulin is destroyed in the gut unless heavily modified, and inhaled insulin is efficient at the clinic but sensitive to refrigeration and handling in places where the cold chain is unreliable.

A polymer that loosens the barrier reversibly takes a different route. Applied as a cream to unbroken skin, it inserts itself over about an hour into the lipid bilayers of the stratum corneum, creating temporary pathways wide enough for a macromolecule to pass, and the effect wears off within roughly six hours in tests with no irritation or thinning of the surface. Insulin is then delivered by a separate patch placed over the treated area, so the two steps can be adjusted independently rather than formulated into one product.

The result that matters is the dose, measured against a clinical standard. In diabetic mice, the formulation reached a bioavailability of 116 units per kilogram, against 29 units per kilogram for a leading commercial penetration enhancer and 15 for insulin applied in saline. Blood glucose fell to within the target range and stayed there for about eight hours, a profile that matches once-daily basal insulin rather than the two or four injections many patients are prescribed. Glycated haemoglobin, measured after four weeks of daily application, fell by 1.8 percentage points.

Safety is the harder test for a barrier that has been deliberately opened. In the mouse studies the skin recovered fully within six hours and showed no thinning or irritation, and a four-week repeated-dose study found no change in transepidermal water loss, a standard measure of barrier integrity. Human skin equivalents gave the same recovery time. Whether a human epidermis, which is thicker and subject to a lifetime of sun exposure, behaves identically is the question that animal data cannot answer.

What the work does not establish is the variability between individuals. Barrier thickness varies with age, site and condition, and a formulation tuned to an average may be ineffective at one end of that range and excessive at the other. The study also reports glucose control, not long-term vascular outcomes, and the eight-hour profile leaves open whether the formulation can cover the overnight period without a second application.

The comparison with other non-injectable routes is instructive. Oral delivery avoids the skin entirely but requires chemical modification that can alter the dose; inhaled delivery reaches the bloodstream quickly but depends on a device and on refrigerated supply; a topical route needs neither, at the price of a two-step application and an hour's wait before dosing.

A barrier that can be opened and closed to order changes the economics of a daily medicine as much as the experience of taking it. The reversibility is the property that makes the approach plausible rather than merely potent: a permeability enhancer that does not recover would be a wound, not a formulation, and the six-hour return to baseline is the result that separates the two.
