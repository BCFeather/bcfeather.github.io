Skin is a good barrier, which is the whole difficulty. The stratum corneum admits water and small molecules and turns back proteins, so insulin, at about 5,800 daltons, has never crossed intact skin in a quantity that matters. Injections handle the problem at the cost of a needle every day, and the alternatives each carry a penalty: oral insulin is digested, inhaled insulin needs a device and a cold chain. A polymer that the skin lets through, carrying insulin with it, is the route a team in Hangzhou has taken, reported in *Nature* on 20 November.

The polymer is a polyzwitterion, built so that its charge depends on where it is. In the acidic sebum and the fatty-acid lipids between corneocytes it is protonated and cationic, which draws it into the barrier and lets it diffuse along the lipid lamellae. Below the stratum corneum, at the pH of living tissue, it turns neutral and migrates across cell membranes through the epidermis and dermis, ending in the dermal lymphatics and then the blood.

Conjugated to insulin, the polymer carries the hormone with it. In mice with type 1 diabetes, a transdermal dose of 116 units per kilogram brought blood glucose into the normal range within an hour, an effect the authors report as comparable to subcutaneous insulin, and it lasted more than twelve hours without driving glucose below normal. Binding assays put the conjugate's affinity for the insulin receptor close to that of unmodified insulin, which is the point that decides whether the hormone still works after being chemically tethered.

Pigs are the harder and more relevant test, because their skin is closer in thickness and structure to a person's. In diabetic minipigs a transdermal dose of 29 units per kilogram normalised blood glucose, roughly a quarter of the mouse dose per kilogram, which is what a thicker barrier and a larger animal would predict if the route works at all. Skin area grows with body surface, so a formulation that works in a mouse has to be re-derived rather than rescaled for an animal forty times heavier.

What the study does not answer is the efficiency of the transfer. A dose applied to the skin is not a dose delivered to the blood, and the fraction that completes the journey is the number that would settle how much polymer and insulin a patch has to contain. Repeated application, barrier integrity over weeks and the behaviour of human skin under sun and age are questions that animal work cannot close, and the paper reports the pharmacology rather than a clinical programme.

The wider claim is about a class of drugs, not one hormone. Proteins and peptides are the fastest-growing part of the pharmacopoeia and almost all of them are injected, so a polymer that crosses the barrier without puncturing it would change the delivery problem for antibodies, growth factors and vaccines as much as for insulin. Whether that promise survives the move from a pig to a person, and from a laboratory conjugate to a manufacturable one, is the work the result now sets up.
