Sign languages are full languages with their own grammar, and they have been poorly served by dictionaries because a sign has three simultaneously produced parts: a handshape, a location and a movement. A project published 40,000 signs from 12 languages this month, and its contribution is a notation that makes the entries comparable.

The notation describes each component on an independent axis. Handshape, palm orientation, location, movement and non-manual marker are coded separately, which lets a search for a handshape return signs from any of the 12 languages and lets a comparison find where they diverge. The substantive result is a finding about grammar. Two languages that share a handshape for a number use it in different syntactic positions, and the dictionary's tagging makes that visible for the first time at this scale. Behind the entries sits 900 hours of video, recorded with deaf researchers in each language community and annotated by deaf annotators. That choice is not decorative: a hearing annotator misses non-manual markers that carry negation and question structure. Inter-annotator agreement is the corpus's quality measure, and the project reports 0.9 for handshape and 0.7 for movement.

Orthography is the political question. Each community has its own writing system or none, and the project presents the notation as a lookup layer rather than as a proposed writing system, which is the position a linguist can defend and a community can accept.

Access is the practical test. The interface accepts a handshape drawn on a phone and returns candidates, and the project reports 78 per cent accuracy on a held-out set. A dictionary that requires a notation's alphabet has no users outside a seminar. Scale separates a signed dictionary from a spoken one. A spoken language's dictionary can be compiled from text; a signed language's must be compiled from video, and the annotation is the cost. The project's 40,000 entries took seven years. Comparability is the point of a notation. A sign language's phonology is a set of contrasts in handshape, location, movement and orientation, and a notation that records each on an independent axis makes two languages' inventories comparable in the way that two spoken languages' vowel systems are.

Annotation cost is the bottleneck. A minute of video takes 40 minutes to annotate by a trained deaf annotator, and a corpus of 900 hours is a decade of work by a team of six. That is why the notation's adoption will be slow even where it is wanted.

A notation's test is whether a reader can reproduce a sign from it. A deaf reader who has never seen a sign, given the notation alone, should produce something a native signer recognizes, and the corpus's annotation protocol includes a check of that kind on a sample.

A dictionary's job differs when the language's medium is the body. The entries are a record, and the notation is the instrument that turns a record into a comparison.
