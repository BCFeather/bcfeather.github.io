The Penghu 1 mandible was trawled from the seafloor of the Penghu Channel between Taiwan and the mainland, and for a decade it has been the oldest hominin fossil found in Taiwan with no secure identity. Its teeth are large, its jaw is thick, and its shape resembles the Xiahe mandible from the Tibetan plateau, which was identified as Denisovan by protein analysis in 2019. Attempts to extract ancient DNA from Penghu 1 failed, which is the usual outcome for a fossil that has spent millennia in warm, wet conditions.

Sequencing moved to the proteins that survive where DNA does not. Researchers from Japan, Taiwan and Denmark sampled about 25 milligrams of bone powder and sequenced the surviving collagen and enamel proteins by mass spectrometry, work reported in *Science* on 10 April 2025. They recovered sequence information for 4,241 amino acid residues from 51 proteins. Ancient protein degrades more slowly than DNA and carries enough variation to place a fossil in a lineage, which is what the study needed.

Two positions did the identifying. One variant in the recovered sequences is specific to Denisovans and one is semi-specific, and together they place Penghu 1 on the same branch as the Denisovan from Denisova Cave in Siberia. A third signal settled the sex: peptides from amelogenin Y, the enamel protein encoded on the Y chromosome, covered 48.9 per cent of their sequence, which makes the individual male. Sex is a small fact with a large consequence, because it means the specimen can be compared with male and female fossils without a caveat.

Geography is the point of the paper. Denisovans were known from a finger bone and a few teeth in Siberia, from the Xiahe jaw on the Tibetan plateau and from sediments in caves that carry their DNA and nothing else. A mandible from a shallow strait off Taiwan extends the range into subtropical eastern Asia, and it does so with a specimen that was found without any archaeological context, which is the hardest kind of fossil to interpret.

Proteins are not DNA. The resolution of a proteome is coarser, the number of informative positions is smaller, and a single individual cannot describe a population. The age of Penghu 1 is also uncertain, placed somewhere in the Middle or Late Pleistocene on morphological grounds rather than measured directly, so the fossil tells us where the lineage was and only roughly when. Those limits are the price of a specimen that has no collagen left to radiocarbon.

The result fits a pattern that has been building since 2019. Proteins have identified a Denisovan jaw in Tibet, a Homo antecessor individual in Spain and now a mandible from the Taiwan Strait, each time from a fossil whose DNA was gone. Palaeoproteomics has become the routine instrument for the fossils that matter most in eastern Asia, where warm caves destroy the molecules that palaeogenetics depends on.

What would settle the rest is more of the same, plus a date. A second specimen from the same region with preserved protein, a direct measurement of the mandible's age, and a phylogenetic analysis that includes the growing set of Asian hominin fossils would turn a single identification into a population history. For now the jaw has a species, a sex and a place, and that is more than it had a month ago.
