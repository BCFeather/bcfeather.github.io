An outbreak of hepatitis in young children appeared in the spring of 2022 in a country that had just emerged from a pandemic, and the question was whether a common virus had become dangerous or whether a rare complication had become visible. The case series from a single transplant centre is the clinical description that the question needs. A disease that appears in a country which has just emerged from a pandemic invites the hypothesis that the pandemic changed something, and the hypothesis is not the same as the evidence.

A review covered children aged ten or younger referred to a paediatric liver transplant centre in the United Kingdom between 1 January and 11 April 2022 who met the confirmed case definition of acute hepatitis that was not hepatitis A to E and had no metabolic, inherited, congenital or mechanical cause, with aminotransferases above 500 international units per litre. The paper appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 13 July 2022. A case definition that excludes hepatitis A to E is the definition that makes the series a description of the unexplained cases, and the exclusion is what gives the series its specificity.

Forty-four children met the definition, and most had been previously healthy. The median age was four years, and the presenting features were jaundice in 93 per cent, vomiting in 54 per cent and diarrhoea in 32 per cent. A median age of four years is younger than the typical age for adenovirus hepatitis, and the youth of the cases is part of what made the outbreak notable.

Of the 30 patients tested for human adenovirus, 27 were positive. Fulminant liver failure developed in six, and all six received a transplant, and every child including the six was discharged home. A transplant centre is the place where the severe cases concentrate, and the concentration is what makes the series a description of the worst end of the distribution.

Concentration of the cases in a transplant centre is a referral bias, because a child with mild hepatitis is never referred, and the denominator is therefore the severe end of a distribution that nobody has measured. The bias explains why the series reads as severe. A common childhood virus that appears in a child with liver failure is a virus that might be a bystander, and the authors say so rather than assuming causation.

Adenovirus is a common childhood infection that usually causes a cold, and its presence in a child with hepatitis is not proof that it caused the hepatitis. The authors say so, and the sequence of infection followed by liver injury in a small fraction of children is the hypothesis rather than the finding. A virus that changed, a population whose immune experience changed, and a surveillance system that improved are three explanations that a case series cannot separate.

An open question is is whether the virus changed, whether the children's immune systems changed after two years of reduced exposure, or whether the surveillance simply improved. A series cannot separate the three, and the answer will come from the virological and the epidemiological work that follows. A series of 44 children is small enough that the estimates are descriptive, and the description is what a clinician needs before the mechanism is known.
