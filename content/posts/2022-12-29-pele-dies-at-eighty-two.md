He died on 29 December in a hospital in São Paulo, where he had been treated for colon cancer and for the infections that followed. He was eighty-two, and his family had gathered at the hospital through Christmas. The government declared three days of mourning, and the football confederation changed the colour of its emblem within hours. His daughter posted a photograph of the family holding him, and the caption said that everything they were had come from him. The hospital did not say what had caused the failure of his organs, and his family asked the press to leave the ward quietly.

Records are the part that can be counted. He was the only player to win three World Cups, in 1958, in 1962 and in 1970, and he scored in the first of them at seventeen. He played most of his club football for Santos, and he finished his career in the United States, at a time when the country was learning the game. The tally of his goals is disputed, because it includes matches against local teams and friendlies, and the number that his admirers use is one thousand two hundred and eighty-three.

Mexico City in 1970 is the image that survives. Brazil beat Italy four goals to one, and the fourth goal was the one that everyone remembers, a move that went through half the team before Pelé, who had no need to shoot, rolled the ball to the right for Carlos Alberto. He had been marked throughout the tournament and kicked in most matches, and the team he played in was the first that the world agreed was beautiful.

His career had a political shadow that he did not escape. The military government that ruled Brazil from 1964 used his fame, and he was criticized for not speaking against it. He said in later years that he had been naive, and that a footballer of his time had no encouragement to hold opinions. The country that made him a national symbol also asked him, decades later, to explain what he had done with the symbol.

Tributes came from the people who had played against him and from the ones who had only seen the recordings. The current captain of Brazil dedicated a goal to him the following week, and the club in Santos opened its stadium for the queue that wanted to leave flowers. A funeral was held in the city where he had played, and the cortège went past the ground where the stands are named for him. The queue outside that ground lasted two days.

Numbers will be produced to settle the argument about who was the greatest, and the numbers will not settle it. What is certain is that the shirt with the number ten on it, which had been a shirt before he wore it, became a position afterwards. Every player who has worn it since has been measured against him, and most of them have said so. The arguments about him have always been arguments about what a footballer is allowed to be, and he was allowed to be more than the game had room for.
