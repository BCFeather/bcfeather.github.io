Thirty leaders met in Brussels on 24 March for an emergency summit that lasted a morning, and the statement they issued was longer on commitments than on deadlines. Four new battlegroups would be sent to Bulgaria, Hungary, Romania and Slovakia, which would bring the total on the eastern flank to eight and the troops under NATO command in the region to about 40,000. The leaders also agreed to help Ukraine with equipment against chemical, biological and nuclear attack, and to increase the stockpiles of ammunition and fuel that the alliance keeps.

This was the second gathering of the leaders in a month, called by the secretary general after the invasion. The first, in February, had been a scheduled session that the war turned into a demonstration of unity; the second was held to agree on the next phase, and it produced a communique that named Russia as the threat and described the alliance's posture as a reset for the long term. The leaders said they would spend at least 2 per cent of their national income on defence, a target that most of them had already promised and few had met.

The question that every leader was asked was about a no-fly zone. President Zelensky addressed the summit by video and asked for one, and he asked for aircraft and for air defence systems, and he asked for the alliance to close the sky over his country. The leaders refused, as they had refused since the first week, on the ground that a no-fly zone would mean shooting down Russian aircraft and that a nuclear power would treat that as an act of war. The refusal appeared nowhere in the statement, and it was the only subject that the journalists wanted to discuss.

President Biden used the summit to hold a series of meetings on the margins, with the leaders of the countries on the eastern flank and with the secretary general, and then travelled to Poland. He visited the troops at Rzeszow, near the border, met the president and the prime minister in Warsaw, and spoke to Ukrainian refugees in a stadium that had been turned into a reception centre. The White House said the visit was intended to show the commitment to article five, and the Polish government said the visit was about the troops that it wanted permanently stationed on its soil.

The alliance's problem was the gap between the promise and the timeline. A battlegroup of a few thousand soldiers can be ordered to deploy in a week, and the equipment that makes it a fighting formation takes months, and the ammunition that a war of attrition consumes takes longer than that. The eastern members asked for permanent bases and for pre-positioned stocks, and the older members asked for a plan that would not provoke Russia further, and the communique settled on the language of a reinforced presence rather than on a number.

What the summit established was that the war had given the alliance a purpose that it had spent a decade looking for. Two decades of missions outside the treaty area had ended in Afghanistan, and the threat that the planners had written about since 2014 had arrived on the border of a member state. The leaders left Brussels with a statement, a set of deployments and no answer to the question about the sky, and the war went on at the pace that both sides had settled into.
