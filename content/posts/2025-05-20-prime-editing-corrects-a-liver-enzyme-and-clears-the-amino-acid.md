Phenylketonuria is caused by a defective enzyme in the liver, and the treatment is a diet so restrictive that patients describe it as a second job. A gene therapy that corrected even a fraction of liver cells would allow a normal diet, and the obstacle has always been delivery: an editing nuclease that stays active long enough to cut many cells also cuts the ones it should not.

A paper in *Nature Biomedical Engineering* on 20 May 2025 described a way around that. The authors put the guide RNA into adeno-associated virus vectors, which express it continuously, and delivered the prime editor itself as nucleoside-modified mRNA in lipid nanoparticles, so the editor is present for a few days and then disappears. Editing happens while the protein is there, and no DNA sequence for the editor is left behind.

Efficiency was dose-dependent and large for a single administration. At the *Dnmt1* locus, one dose of 2 milligrams per kilogram produced 26.2 per cent editing with the PEmax editor and 47.4 per cent with PE7. Those are the numbers that matter for a liver target, because hepatocytes turn over slowly and a corrected fraction persists.

In a mouse model of phenylketonuria carrying the *Pah* enu2 mutation, three doses corrected 4.3 per cent of alleles with PEmax and 20.7 per cent with PE7. Blood phenylalanine, which started above 1,500 micromoles per litre, fell below the therapeutic threshold of 360, which is the outcome the diet is trying to achieve by other means.

Simplified version is the one that would scale. Co-delivering PE7 mRNA with synthetic guide RNAs in the same lipid nanoparticle gave 35.9 per cent editing after two doses at *Dnmt1* and 8.0 per cent after three at *Pah*, again bringing the amino acid under the threshold. One formulation and not two vectors is a manufacturing argument, and manufacturing is what decides whether a therapy reaches patients: a single lipid nanoparticle is one fill-finish line, and two vectors are two.

What the paper does not show is durability or safety over a lifetime. Mice were followed for weeks, not years, and off-target editing was assessed by the methods available rather than by whole-genome sequencing of every treated animal. A transient editor reduces the risk of random integration, and it does not eliminate the risk of editing the wrong base, which for a liver enzyme means a second mutation in the same gene.

Next steps are a larger animal, a dose that a clinician can give repeatedly, and an immune response study, because a lipid nanoparticle carrying a bacterial-derived editor will be seen by the immune system more than once, and the second dose is where platform therapies usually fail, because antibodies against the delivery vehicle appear after the first exposure and clear the second one before it reaches the liver. A platform that corrects a metabolic disease in mice is a platform, and platforms become therapies only after those three questions are answered.
