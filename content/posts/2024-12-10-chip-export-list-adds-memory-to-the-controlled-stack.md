description: "New rules widened the set of memory and equipment that requires a licence, and the counter-measures came within a day."
---

American export controls were extended in early December to cover high-bandwidth memory, a further set of manufacturing tools and a hundred and forty companies, and the effect is to push the restriction from the logic chip into the stack around it. A rule that controls only the processor leaves a buyer free to obtain the memory that the processor needs, and the new list closes that gap. A list that grows by a hundred and forty names in one round is a list that compliance teams cannot keep up with.

Memory is the part that mattered. High-bandwidth memory is made by a small number of firms, and the stacking and testing are concentrated in a few places. A buyer that can obtain logic but not memory cannot build a system, and the control therefore works through the bill of materials rather than through a single product. A stack that is twelve dies high is a stack whose test is as valuable as its fabrication.

Equipment list is broader than the product list. Machines that deposit films, that etch deep features and that measure the result are made by a handful of suppliers, and a rule that restricts the tools restricts the fab that a country can build. A country that can buy a design but not the equipment to make it is a country that depends on an import for every generation. A tool that is restricted is a tool that a fab can buy second-hand, which is why the rule also covers servicing.

Counter-measures were quick. China restricted exports of gallium, germanium and antimony to the United States, three metals that are used in compound semiconductors and in munitions, and it opened an investigation into a subsidiary of an American chipmaker. A tit for tat in critical materials is a tit for tat that raises the cost of the whole supply chain. A metal that is used in a munition is a metal that a defence ministry will notice.

Enforcement problem is the same as before. A rule that names a company is a rule that a company can work around through a subsidiary in a third country, and the list has grown to the point where compliance departments cannot map their own supply chains. A control that nobody can fully comply with is a control that is applied unevenly. A subsidiary in a third country is a subsidiary that the rule was written to catch and probably does not.

What the round shows is that the technology competition has moved from a single choke point to a stack. A policy that picks one node is a policy that the other side routes around, and each round of rules adds nodes until the whole system is regulated. A policy that regulates a stack is a policy that has to be rewritten every time the stack changes. A list that reaches the whole stack is a list that the industry will treat as a licensing regime rather than as an exception.
