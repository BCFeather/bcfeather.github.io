description: "A lithography program has found that the defect density of masks for high-numerical-aperture extreme ultraviolet is set by the protective film rather than by the patterning, which changes where the effort has to go."
---

Next-generation extreme ultraviolet lithography uses a wider numerical aperture, which halves the printable feature size and shrinks the depth of focus to tens of nanometres. A program reviewing mask performance reported this month that the limiting defect source is the pellicle, the thin membrane that protects the mask from particles, and not the patterning step everyone has optimized.

Mask blanks for this generation are made with a multilayer reflector of 40 to 50 layers, and each layer's thickness must be controlled to a fraction of a nanometre. The blanks available now have fewer than 0.02 defects per square centimetre at the critical size, which is adequate.

Pellicle trouble starts at the exposure's heat. A membrane thin enough to transmit 90 per cent of the light at 13.5 nanometres is 2 nanometres thick, and it must survive the heat that the exposure's power deposits. The membranes tested so far develop pinholes after 40 hours of exposure, and a pinhole prints as a defect on every die.

One mitigation is a thicker membrane with a lower transmission. A membrane at 3 nanometres transmits 82 per cent, and the loss of dose costs throughput. The program's model says the trade is acceptable below a wafer throughput of 100 per hour, which is the current target.

Inspection is the second constraint. A mask inspection tool must find 20-nanometre defects on a surface that reflects rather than transmits, and the available tools work at half the required sensitivity. The program has funded a new inspection design.

Economics concentrate at the mask. A single mask costs 400,000 dollars and a layer set for a logic product costs 8 million, so a defect that forces a mask's replacement is a large number. The mask's share of a product's development cost has risen from 5 to 12 per cent in two generations.

A pellicle is a membrane held a few millimetres above the mask, and its job is to keep particles out of the focal plane. At a wider aperture the light passes through it at an angle, and a membrane that absorbs 3 per cent of the light heats by 20 degrees and distorts.

Throughput pays for the fix. A mask without a pellicle must be inspected every few wafers, and an inspection costs 10 minutes of a tool whose hour is worth 300 dollars. A membrane that cuts the inspection rate by half pays for itself in a month.

A defect count is a yield model's input rather than a quality score. A mask with 0.1 defects per square centimetre at a 30-nanometre pitch produces a die failure rate that a fab multiplies by its wafer value, and the same count at a mature node is tolerable. The pellicle's value rises with the pitch's shrink.

A manufacturing chain moves its limit to its least interesting component, and this case shows how. The patterning, the optics and the resist all have roadmaps; the pellicle is a sheet of material whose failure mode is a hole, and it is where the schedule will be decided.
