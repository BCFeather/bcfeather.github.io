A ribosome that stalls on a broken message is a ribosome that a cell has to rescue, and the rescue is done by a small protein that looks like a transfer RNA. The message is the mRNA, and a ribosome reads it three bases at a time until it reaches a stop codon or the end, and a message that has lost its stop codon leaves the ribosome stuck with a peptidyl-tRNA in the P site and an empty A site. The cell cannot afford to lose its ribosomes, so every organism has systems that recognise the stalled state and split the ribosome into its subunits. A cell has a fixed number of ribosomes and a fixed rate of protein synthesis, and a ribosome that is stuck is a ribosome that costs the cell a fraction of its capacity. A bacterium that loses its rescue factors grows poorly on a rich medium and dies on a minimal one.

The recognition is structural. A rescue factor binds the empty A site and the mRNA channel at once, and its shape mimics the tRNA that would normally occupy those sites. The mimicry is what allows a single protein to read the state of the ribosome without reading the sequence of the message, since the state is the same whatever the message was. The factors that have been characterised fall into families, and the bacterial and the eukaryotic ones are related in function and not in sequence. A factor that binds the empty site has to avoid the occupied one, and the discrimination is made by the geometry of the two sites. A factor that reads the state of the site rather than the sequence of the message is a factor that works on any message.

Rescue comes in two flavours. One class of factor splits the ribosome, releasing the subunits for the next round of initiation, and the peptidyl-tRNA that was in the P site is handed to a protease or a hydrolase for degradation. The other class releases the incomplete peptide and lets the ribosome continue, which is what happens when the stall is caused by a rare codon and the message is otherwise intact. A cell uses both, and the choice between them is made by which factor reaches the ribosome first. A factor that splits the ribosome releases the message as well, and the message is degraded by the same machinery that degrades a defective transcript. A cell that loses a rescue factor accumulates ribosomes on a single message, and the accumulation is visible in a sucrose gradient.

The quality-control side is what the recent work has clarified. A stalled ribosome exposes a part of its surface that is normally buried, and the exposure is a signal that recruits the degradation machinery. The nascent chain that is still attached is a substrate for a quality-control ligase, and the chain carries a tag that marks it for destruction. The system therefore couples the rescue of the ribosome to the disposal of the protein, which is the arrangement a cell needs, since a truncated protein is usually not useful. A ligase that tags the nascent chain recognises the length and the exposure of the chain, and the two are coupled. A tag that is added to the chain is added to a lysine near the point at which the ribosome stopped, and the position is set by the geometry of the tunnel.

Therapeutic interest comes from the bacterial factors. A factor that is essential in bacteria and absent in humans is a target, and the structures of the bacterial rescue factors have been solved with ligands bound. The ligands that have been found are not antibiotics yet, since a compound that binds a factor in a crystal does not necessarily kill a cell. The target is attractive because the pathway is essential and because the binding site is unlike the sites that existing antibiotics use. A ligand that binds the factor in a crystal has to reach the factor in the cytoplasm of a bacterium, and the envelope is the obstacle. A compound that binds the factor also has to reach the cytoplasm of the bacterium, and an efflux pump can remove it before it does.

What the field has not settled is how the cell chooses among the rescue routes. A ribosome that is stalled on a damaged message and a ribosome that is stalled on a rare codon look the same to a rescue factor, and the cell responds differently to the two. The difference must be carried by something else, and the candidates are the length of the nascent chain, the identity of the last codons, and the proteins that are already bound to the ribosome. A system that reads all three is a system that a single structure cannot explain. A single structure cannot explain a choice, and the field has moved to kinetics and to single-molecule measurements to see the decision happen. A measurement that shows two routes competing is a measurement that has to resolve the same ribosome twice, and that is what the newer assays do.
