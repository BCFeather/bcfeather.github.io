Japan's cabinet published a fifteen-year investment plan worth 370 trillion yen this week, and by 9 July the yen had slid past 162.80 to the dollar, a level it had not reached in nearly forty years. The plan covers ports, rail, data centres and a new industrial base for semiconductors, and it sets out spending commitments that stretch beyond the term of any government that will have to pay for them. Traders treated the document as a signal about the direction of fiscal policy rather than as a schedule of projects.

Financing is the part the plan leaves out. Japanese newspapers and the opposition parties noted that the plan identifies no tax source and no bond programme, and that its arithmetic assumes growth which the country's demography makes unlikely. The finance ministry has been running a primary deficit for years, and the cost of servicing existing debt has risen as the central bank has allowed its holdings to shrink. The plan also assumes that private companies will supply two thirds of the total, which is the assumption economists questioned most.

Currency has become the visible scoreboard. The yen has lost about a tenth of its value against the dollar over twelve months, and Japanese exporters have not benefited as much as expected because their suppliers and their energy costs are priced in the same currency. Households feel the loss in imported food and fuel, and the government's own popularity has fallen with the price of rice.

Intervention record explains why the market tests the ministry. The government and the central bank bought yen on three occasions in the spring, spending 11.73 trillion yen, and the effect lasted weeks rather than months. Officials have said they will act against what they call disorderly moves, and the market has learned to read that phrase as a warning about speed rather than about level. Rice prices had risen after a poor harvest and a restriction on imports, and the government released stock from its reserves in June.

Policy mix is the underlying problem. A central bank that wants to raise rates to defend the currency and a government that wants low rates to finance its borrowing have been pulling in opposite directions, and the plan published this week made the second preference explicit. Investors have responded by shortening the duration they hold in Japanese bonds, which pushes yields up without lifting the currency.

Plans of this size are usually judged by what they build, and this one will be judged first by what it costs. A weaker currency raises the price of the imports that the projects need, so the same 370 trillion yen buys less infrastructure with every month that the exchange rate slides. The government has chosen to spend now and to explain later, which is a choice a rich country can afford for a while and not indefinitely. The central bank has said it will review policy in the autumn, and the market has begun to price a rise it did not expect in the spring.
