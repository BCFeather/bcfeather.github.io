Agonists of the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor changed the treatment of type 2 diabetes by combining glucose lowering with weight loss, and the next generation of molecules has been chasing two pathways at once. Amylin is the other satiety hormone co-secreted with insulin, and a molecule that engages both receptors can plausibly do more than a selective agonist at the same dose, which is the premise behind a class of unimolecular dual agonists.

Zenagamtide is one of those molecules, given as a weekly subcutaneous injection, and the phase 2 trial reported in *The Lancet* on 15 August randomised 262 participants to one of six doses from 0.4 to 40 milligrams or to placebo. Mean baseline HbA1c was 7.8 per cent, treatment ran for 36 weeks, and the primary comparison was the change from baseline against placebo. Screening ran over four months and enrolled about a quarter of those assessed, which fits a class where gastrointestinal tolerability decides who stays on treatment.

The fall in HbA1c ranged from 0.9 percentage points at the lowest dose to 1.7 at the highest. Against placebo the estimated differences were 0.77 points at 0.4 milligrams, with an interval from 1.26 to 0.28, and 1.56 points at 40 milligrams, with an interval from 2.05 to 1.07, both statistically significant. The dose response is monotonic, which is the cleanest internal check that the effect belongs to the drug.

Gastrointestinal adverse events dominated the safety profile, and most were mild to moderate. Twenty-one of 261 treated participants, 8 per cent, reported a serious adverse event, distributed across every dose group and three in the placebo arm, and no deaths occurred. The titration schedule, weekly steps rather than a jump to the target dose, is what keeps nausea manageable in this class. Serious events spread evenly across doses suggests the profile is driven by background risk rather than by escalation, though the numbers are too small to exclude a dose effect.

A 36-week trial with 262 participants can establish a dose response and a tolerability profile, not an outcome. Weight, cardiovascular events, kidney outcomes and durability all sit beyond the horizon of this design, and the placebo group of 37 is too small to characterise rare events. Approval will depend on a phase 3 programme with thousands of patients and at least a year of exposure. A trial that ends at 36 weeks cannot see the endpoint that regulators care most about, which for this class is whether the weight loss persists after the drug is stopped.

The interesting comparison is not against placebo but against the incretin co-agonists already in late development. A dual amylin and GLP-1 molecule may trade some of the weight loss of a GIP co-agonist for better tolerability, and whether that trade is worth making depends on the outcome that the field has agreed to measure, which is still mostly weight.
