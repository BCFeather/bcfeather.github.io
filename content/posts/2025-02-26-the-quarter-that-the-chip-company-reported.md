NVIDIA reported its quarterly results after the close on the twenty-sixth of February, and the numbers were the largest in the company's history. Revenue was about thirty-nine billion dollars for the quarter, the data centre business supplied the great majority of it, and the full-year revenue passed a hundred and thirty billion.

The growth is still fast and it is slower than it was. The quarterly revenue rose by about three quarters compared with the same period a year earlier, which is a rate that few large companies have ever sustained, and the rate is lower than the doubling that the previous quarters showed. The market read the deceleration as the beginning of a normal curve rather than as a warning.

The margin is the number that moved the shares. The gross margin that the company guided for the coming quarter was slightly below the level of the quarter just reported, and the explanation was the cost of ramping a new generation of chips. A margin that falls by two points on a revenue base of that size is a large amount of money, and the analysts who model the company recalculated within the hour.

The product transition is the reason. The company was moving its customers from one architecture to the next, and a new chip is more expensive to build in the first quarters because the yield is lower and the packaging is new. The transition is a technical event and it appears in the accounts as a margin, which is why the two are reported together.

The demand is the part that the company cannot control. The customers are a small number of large buyers who are building data centres, and their spending plans are set by their own businesses rather than by the chip maker. A quarter of that size depends on a handful of purchase orders, and the concentration is a risk that the filing lists.

The shares fell by several per cent on the following day, and the fall is the market's judgment about the margin rather than about the revenue. The company's revenue beat the consensus and the guidance for the next quarter was in line, and the shares still fell, which tells you what the buyers had already priced in.

What the quarter shows is that a company at the centre of a boom is measured on its second derivative. The growth was enormous and the question that the market asked was whether it was accelerating, and the answer was that it was not. The number that the company reported was a record, and the record was the reason the shares fell.

The next test is the supply. The company has committed to a production plan that depends on the packaging capacity that its partners are building, and the quarter that follows will show whether that capacity arrives on schedule. The guidance that the company gave is a promise about the second half of the year, and the second half is where the analysts have placed their optimism.
