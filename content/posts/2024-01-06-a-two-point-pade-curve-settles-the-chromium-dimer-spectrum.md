Chromium dimer is two chromium atoms bonded to each other, and its ground state has puzzled quantum chemistry for four decades. The difficulty is that the six electrons of the chromium \(3d\) shell are strongly correlated, so neither a single determinant nor a perturbative expansion describes the bond, and different methods disagree by amounts that matter for the spectrum. A bond between two transition metal atoms is the hardest case for electronic structure theory, and chromium is the standard example of that difficulty.

Work posted to arXiv on 6 January 2024 takes a different route. Instead of computing the electronic structure, the authors build the potential energy curve from the two limits in which theory is reliable, and they join the two with an analytic form. Two limits and a fit is an old strategy in spectroscopy, and what is new here is that both limits are used with their own asymptotics rather than as starting guesses.

Short range is where perturbation theory applies, since the atoms are close enough for the expansion in the internuclear distance to converge. Long range is where the multipole expansion applies, since the atoms are far apart and the interaction is a dispersion series. Both limits are asymptotic, and neither is valid in the region where the bond actually lives. An asymptotic series is useful only where its terms decrease, and the two regions where that happens sit far apart on the distance axis.

Joining them is the technical step. The authors use a two-point Padé approximant, which is a rational function fixed by the coefficients of both expansions, and they add a few turning points extracted from the experimental data of Casey and Leopold in 1993. The result is a single analytic curve valid over the whole range of distances. A rational function fixed by two expansions is the minimal object that can respect both, and the turning points anchor it in the region where neither applies.

The curve is accurate. It reproduces 29 experimental vibrational energies to three or four decimal digits, which is the accuracy a spectroscopist needs in order to assign lines rather than merely to fit them. Line assignment is the test, and four digits is the accuracy that an experimental fit has to beat before it can claim to predict.

A large spectrum is what follows. The potential supports 19,694 rovibrational states, with a maximum vibrational quantum number of 104 at zero angular momentum and a maximum rotational quantum number of 312 above an energy of \(10^{-4}\) hartree, plus 218 weakly bound states below that energy. Counting states is what a spectroscopic potential is for, and the numbers here are the input to a partition function.

What the construction does not do is explain the bond. A curve fitted to data and to two asymptotics is a spectroscopic object, and it says nothing about why the chromium dimer is difficult. A reader who wants the electronic structure will not find it here, and the authors say so. Spectroscopy and electronic structure are different questions, and the paper answers the first one thoroughly.
