Ski jumping has a measurement problem that most sports would envy and few would want. A jump's distance depends on wind, and wind at the take-off table changes between athletes in the same round. Before the current rule, a jumper who caught a headwind gained metres and a jumper who caught a tailwind lost them, and the competition was partly a lottery of conditions.

The rule in force since the 2024 season compensates in two ways. The gate is moved between rounds to normalize conditions, and a per-jump deduction or credit is applied by a formula that converts wind speed along the flight path into a distance equivalent, using a coefficient calibrated on the hill's geometry. A tailwind of one metre per second costs roughly 4.5 metres on a large hill and is deducted at 0.4 points per metre, which is close to the value of one metre of distance.

Implementation depends on instrumentation. Each hill carries anemometers along the in-run table, at the take-off and at three points in the landing area, sampled at ten hertz, and the software integrates the wind component along the jump's direction over the flight. A jumper's score therefore depends on data that is collected before the landing, which is why the system is reviewed by a technical delegate independent of the jury.

Effect on results is measurable. In the three seasons since the rule, the standard deviation of distance within a round has fallen by about 20 per cent, and the correlation between an athlete's start position in the round and final rank has weakened. The competition has become more repeatable, which is what a winter sport with a diminishing calendar needs.

The rule has its critics, and their argument is technical. Wind measured at fixed points is not wind experienced by the jumper, who flies a curved path through air that varies with height, and the correction assumes a linear relationship between wind and distance that holds only for small perturbations. A crosswind, which the formula does not compensate, can help a jumper who handles it well and hurt one who does not.

Adaptation has become a skill in itself. Coaches now teach jumpers to read the flag at the take-off and adjust the flight posture, and several national teams employ a meteorologist who tracks the wind profile in the hour before the round. That is a professionalization of a variable the sport had previously called luck.

What the change reveals is a general problem in judged and measured sports. Any compensation formula converts an environmental variable into a score adjustment, and the formula's coefficients are calibrated on historical data that may not describe the current hill. Fairness improves at the cost of transparency, since no spectator can verify a deduction of 3.2 points in real time.

For a reader, the practical consequence is that the leaderboard after a round is not simply the order of distances. A jumper in fifth may have flown further than the leader and lost on the correction, which is visible on the results sheet and invisible on the broadcast.
