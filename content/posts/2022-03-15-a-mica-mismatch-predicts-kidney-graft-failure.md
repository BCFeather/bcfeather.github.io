title: "A mismatch outside HLA predicts kidney graft failure"
date: "2022-03-15T10:40:00+08:00"

description: "In 1,356 kidney transplants, mismatches at MICA were associated with a hazard ratio of 2.12 for graft loss, and anti-MICA antibodies raised the risk of antibody-mediated rejection by nearly fourfold."
---

Kidney allocation matches donor and recipient at the human leukocyte antigen loci, and a proportion of grafts still fail for reasons that the matching scheme cannot explain. Antibody-mediated rejection in a patient with no donor-specific HLA antibodies is the clearest of those unexplained failures, and it points to a histocompatibility locus that the standard panel does not cover. Matching at HLA reduces rejection and does not eliminate it, and the residual failures have been attributed to non-adherence, to ischaemic injury and to loci that the panel does not type.

MICA is a major histocompatibility complex class I gene that is highly polymorphic, expressed on epithelial and endothelial cells, and not included in routine matching. A French multicentre cohort of 1,356 transplants tested whether mismatches and antibodies at this locus carry the risk that the immunology predicts. The study appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 14 March 2022.

MICA mismatches were associated with worse graft survival, with a hazard ratio of 2.12 and an interval from 1.45 to 3.11. Anti-MICA donor-specific antibodies were associated with antibody-mediated rejection before transplantation, with a hazard ratio of 3.79, and after transplantation, with a hazard ratio of 9.92. Antibodies against MICA are not detected by the standard crossmatch, so a patient can be sensitized and appear unsensitized on the test that the transplant team relies on.

The combination of anti-MICA and anti-HLA antibodies was worse than either alone. Before transplantation the hazard ratio for rejection was 25.68 when both types were present, and after transplantation it was 82.67, which is the kind of synergy that suggests two independent pathways converging on the same graft. A hazard ratio above eighty describes a combination that is rare in practice, since few patients carry both antibody types at high titre, and its value is in showing that the two pathways reinforce each other.

De novo anti-MICA antibodies were the most damageing category, because they appeared after the transplant and were associated with reduced graft survival in addition to rejection. A patient who develops them has no matching strategy to fall back on and no established treatment for the antibody. The synergy is biologically coherent, since MICA and HLA are both expressed on the graft endothelium and an antibody response to one does not exclude a response to the other.

An independent cohort of 168 patients with antibody-mediated rejection confirmed the effect on graft survival, with a hazard ratio of 1.71, which is the replication that a single-centre finding needs before it changes practice.

The finding argues for adding MICA to the matching algorithm and to the antibody screen, and the practical obstacle is that both are more expensive and slower than the HLA equivalents. A centre that adopted them would identify a minority of patients at high risk and would need a strategy for what to do with that information. A centre would need a validated assay, a threshold for unacceptable mismatch, and a plan for desensitization, and none of those exist for MICA in the way they exist for HLA.
