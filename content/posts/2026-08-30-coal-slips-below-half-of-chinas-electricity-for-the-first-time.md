Half-year figures released in late August put coal's share of Chinese electricity generation at 49.7 per cent, the first time in the modern statistical record that it has fallen below half. Coal plants produced about 2.5 trillion kilowatt-hours. Renewables, including hydro, wind, solar and biomass, reached 41.2 per cent of the total. The figures came from the half-year reports of the grid and the statistical bureau, and they were reported together with a set of industrial numbers that rarely travel with them. Installed coal capacity has continued to grow even as its share falls, because new plants are built to run fewer hours as a reserve for a grid that depends on weather.

The crossing of the halfway mark will be reported as a milestone, and its significance depends on the baseline. Coal has supplied the majority of Chinese electricity since the 1990s, and a fall of one or two points would be noise in a country that added a hundred gigawatts of solar in a single year. What makes this reading unusual is that demand also rose: total generation grew, and coal's absolute output grew with it. A share that falls while output rises is the signature of a system adding demand faster than it adds cleaner supply, and that is the pattern in most of the provincial data. The milestone therefore measures composition rather than decline, and the difference matters for anyone who reads it as the beginning of the end for coal.

The other half of the energy story is industrial. Revenue in the digital sector reached 20.71 trillion yuan in the first six months, up 13.6 per cent, and the build-out continues: 5.102 million 5G base stations, and computing capacity of 2,185 EFLOPS, an increase of 177 per cent in a year. Those numbers describe electricity demand as much as they describe an industry. Computing capacity of that size is a new kind of load: it runs all day, it cannot be shifted to the night, and it sits in provinces that already import power. Data centres have become a planning problem for the grid, and several provinces have begun to require that new ones build or buy their own generation.

July set a record for the grid. Electricity consumption reached 1.04 trillion kilowatt-hours in the month, the highest ever recorded, as a hot summer pushed air-conditioning load to a peak. The peak also shows where the system remains fragile, because the hours after sunset are supplied by whatever can ramp quickly, and that is still mostly coal.

The constraint is the grid rather than the generation. Solar and wind produce when the weather allows, and the transmission and storage that turn their output into firm capacity are being built more slowly than the plants. Coal remains the fallback that keeps the lights on, which is why its capacity has continued to grow even as its share falls. Storage is being built at a scale that would have seemed implausible five years ago, and it remains small relative to the daily swing in solar output. A market that pays for flexibility is the reform that would make the next twenty points possible, and it is the one that has moved slowest.
