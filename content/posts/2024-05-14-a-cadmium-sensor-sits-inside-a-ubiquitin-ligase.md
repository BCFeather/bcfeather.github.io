---

Ubiquitin ligases are the machinery that decides which proteins get destroyed, and the largest family of them is built on a shared scaffold. Skp1, cullin 1 and an F-box protein assemble into an SCF complex, and the F-box domain has had exactly one job in the textbooks: it grips Skp1 and holds the substrate receptor in place. A paper in *Nature Communications* on 14 May 2024 gives that domain a second job, as a sensor for a toxic metal.

The work concerns Met30, the F-box protein of the SCF complex that controls sulfur metabolism in budding yeast. When cadmium enters the cell, the F-box domain binds it, and several highly conserved cysteine residues take part. The measured dissociation constant, \(K_\mathrm{D}\), is about 8 µM, a value in the range that a cellular stress response can use.

Binding does something structural. The domain changes shape in a way that licenses autoubiquitylation of Met30 itself, and the ubiquitin chain that results is recognized by Cdc48, the segregase also known as p97 or VCP. That ATPase pulls the modified protein out of the complex, and the SCF assembly comes apart.

The consequence is inactivation, and inactivation is the point. With the ligase disassembled, the substrates it would otherwise mark for degradation survive, and the transcriptional programme that handles cadmium is allowed to run. The authors show that this protects cells, which turns a structural curiosity into a resistance mechanism. Whether the switch is reversible is another open point. Disassembly by a segregase is usually followed by reassembly once the chain is trimmed, and a cell that has cleared the metal needs its sulfur pathway back.

Cadmium enters cells through transporters meant for zinc and calcium, and it damages proteins by displacing the metals those proteins need. A sensor built into a ligase is therefore a sensible design, since the same machine that responds to the damage can also be switched off while the response runs.

Seen from the outside, the finding rearranges a family tree. F-box domains have been catalogued by the sequences they recognize in substrates, and the assumption has been that the domain itself is inert scaffolding. A domain that binds a metal and undergoes a conformational switch is a different kind of part, and there are hundreds of F-box proteins whose domains have never been tested for binding anything but Skp1.

Cadmium is the specific ligand here, and the paper does not claim that other F-box domains sense other metals, though the conserved cysteines invite the question. Nor does the work identify the kinase or ligase that writes the chain on Met30, which the model needs in order to close. What it does establish is a mechanism with a clean logic. A cell that wants to switch off a degradation pathway can do it by disassembling the machine from within, using the same domain that normally holds the machine together.
