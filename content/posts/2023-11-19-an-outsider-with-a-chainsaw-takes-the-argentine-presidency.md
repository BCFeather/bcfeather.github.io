Javier Milei won Argentina's presidential runoff on 19 November with 55.7 per cent of the vote against 44.3 per cent for the economy minister, Sergio Massa, according to the provisional count published by the national electoral authority. With almost all ballots counted, the margin was close to three million votes, and the result was the highest share won by any candidate since the country returned to democracy in 1983. Massa conceded within an hour of the first official figures.

Milei is an economist and former television commentator who entered politics three years ago and has never held executive office. His campaign promised to dollarize the economy, shut the central bank, cut spending by fifteen percentage points of gross domestic product, and remove most ministries. He carried a chainsaw to rallies as a symbol of the cuts, and he described the political class with a vocabulary that Argentine television had never broadcast from a presidential candidate.

Voters were rejecting the government Massa served more than the man himself. Inflation was running above 140 per cent a year, the currency had lost most of its value, and reserves at the central bank were negative on a net basis. Prices had been held down by controls that had created shortages and a parallel exchange rate. Nearly four in ten Argentines were below the poverty line, and the economy was heading into a recession that the outgoing government had tried to postpone until after the election.

His coalition is thin. His party, La Libertad Avanza, has seven seats in the Chamber of Deputies and none in the Senate, and the parties that supported him in the runoff, including the former president Mauricio Macri's, expect to be paid in appointments. His vice president, Victoria Villarruel, comes from a different faction of the right, and the two have already disagreed on issues from human rights prosecutions to the role of the state.

His programme faces a test in its first hundred days. Ending subsidies on energy and transport will raise prices, and lifting exchange controls will change the value of everyone's savings. Milei has said he will move quickly and has warned that the adjustment will be painful, a message that voters accepted in the runoff and may find harder to accept in January. The social movements that picketed his campaign headquarters on election night will be back.

For the region, the result adds Argentina to the list of countries where outsiders have won on anti-incumbent sentiment, alongside Brazil in 2018 and Chile in 2021. The difference is the programme: Milei's is the most radical economic platform ever offered to Argentine voters by a winning candidate, and it will be tested against a Congress that he does not control and a society that has lived through six defaults. Cabinet appointments announced in the following weeks drew on the Macri government and on a small circle of libertarian economists, a sign of how thin the bench of loyal appointees with executive experience really is.
