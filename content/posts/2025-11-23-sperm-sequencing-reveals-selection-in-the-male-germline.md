description: "Whole-genome and exome nanorate sequencing of sperm measures mutation rates directly and finds extensive positive selection among the genes that shape sperm production, a study in *Nature* reports."
---

The mutations a child inherits from its father are not a random sample of the father's cells. They are the output of a lineage that has been dividing since before the father was born, and the cells that reach the sperm are the ones that survived a long competition. Sequencing sperm is therefore a way of reading that competition directly, and it has been impractical because sperm DNA is scarce and mutation rates are low.

The study in *Nature*, 13 November 2025 applies nanorate sequencing to sperm, combining whole-genome sequencing with deep exome and targeted panels. The error rate of the method is low enough that the mutations counted are real, and the counts can be compared between donors of different ages and between genes.

The rate is the first result: mutations accumulate in the male germline with age, at a pace that has consequences for the children of older fathers. The second result is selection. A set of genes shows mutation counts far above what the local rate predicts, which means the mutations in those genes made the cells that carry them divide more often or survive longer.

The selected genes are, unsurprisingly, genes about sperm production and about the signalling that governs it. A mutation that speeds a spermatogonium's division is an advantage to the clone and a risk to the child, because the same pathway that drives expansion in the germline drives growth in a tumour. The overlap between germline selection and cancer is the reason this line of work attracts attention beyond reproduction.

What the method cannot see is the tissue behind the sperm. A clone that expands and then dies out leaves no trace in the ejaculate, and the counts are a snapshot of the lineages that were producing sperm at the time of donation. Longitudinal sampling from the same donor would show whether a clone persists, which is the difference between an expansion and a coincidence.

The clinical question is whether the selected genes can be read as risk. A father's sperm carrying many mutations in a growth pathway is a fact; whether it predicts a child's disease is a question for cohorts with outcomes, and those cohorts are only now being assembled.

The methodological point stands on its own. Direct measurement of the male germline's mutation rate, at population scale, replaces an estimate derived from disease incidence with a number read from the cells themselves. For genetic counselling and for the study of ageing, that is a better foundation than the one in use.

Donor variation is part of the result. Two men of the same age do not carry the same number of mutations, and the spread between them is as informative as the trend across ages. Some of that spread is exposure, some is the size of the stem-cell pool, and separating the two is what would make the measurement a personal estimate rather than a population average.

Ageing is the axis the field has measured before, and this study adds the gene-level view to it. The rate rises with age in every donor, but the selected genes differ between donors, which suggests that the competition is local and stochastic rather than a programme that runs on a schedule.
