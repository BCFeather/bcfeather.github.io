She entered the courtroom in the morning and sat with her lawyers while the judge read the reasoning behind the sentence. The count was one hundred and thirty-five months, or eleven years and three months, followed by three years of supervised release. The judge, Edward Davila, had presided over a trial that ran for nearly four months and had heard from patients, doctors and former employees. Sentence was announced on 18 November, in the federal courthouse in San Jose, more than three years after the company had dissolved and its assets had been sold for a fraction of what they cost.

The case rested on a simple claim that turned out to be false. Theranos said its machines could run hundreds of tests on a drop of blood taken from a finger, and the company raised hundreds of millions of dollars from investors on the strength of that claim. The machines could run a handful of tests, and they produced results that were wrong often enough to endanger patients. Two people who had worked inside the company told a journalist what they had seen, and their account began the unraveling. The board, which included two former secretaries of state, had been kept in the dark for years.

Jurors convicted her in January on four counts of fraud, having acquitted her on four others, and the verdict was unanimous on the count concerning patients. Each count carried a maximum of twenty years, and the guidelines pointed to a range well above the sentence she received. Her defence argued for probation and home detention, citing her two young children and the shame she had described in court. The prosecution asked for fifteen years and a fine that would have reached into the hundreds of millions.

Letters were submitted to the court by people who had known her, including a former secretary of state and a senator, and the judge read them before he ruled. He said that the picture they gave of her generosity had to be weighed against the conduct the jury had found. He also ordered restitution, and said that the amount would be decided at a later hearing, because the losses were spread across many investors and could take months to work out. Some of those investors had put their savings into the company in its early years.

Before the sentence was read she spoke for the first time in the proceedings at length. She said she was devastated by her failures, that she loved the company and what she had wanted it to do, and that she had made mistakes for which she was deeply ashamed. Her parents sat a few rows behind her, and reporters described her voice breaking more than once during the ten minutes she spoke.

The sentence was stayed until a date in the spring, because an appeal was filed within hours and the judge agreed that she could remain free on a bond while it was heard. The company had once valued itself at nine billion dollars, and its founder had been the youngest self-made billionaire on the lists that newspapers publish. On the day of the sentence, the only number that mattered was one hundred and thirty-five.
