Berkshire Hathaway reported an operating profit of 10.76 billion dollars for the third quarter on 4 November, up from 7.8 billion a year earlier, and the cash and the Treasury bills reached 157.2 billion. The figure was the highest for any third quarter, and the revenue rose by about 20 per cent as the insurance premiums climbed.

Interest is the new income. The company holds about 120 billion dollars of the short-term Treasury bills, and the yield on them has risen to about 5 per cent, which adds about 1.5 billion dollars a quarter. A bill that pays 5 per cent is a cash pile that grows on its own. The company has been buying the bills rather than the longer bonds, and the maturity is kept short because the managers want the flexibility. The company said it would keep the maturity short, and the finance chief has said the long bonds are not attractive at these yields.

Insurance was the base. The underwriting profit was about 2.4 billion dollars, and the property and the casualty business had a quiet quarter for the catastrophes. A quarter without a hurricane is a margin that is wide. The reinsurance business took less of the catastrophe losses than in the previous year, and the premiums have been rising for three years. The insurance float is about 170 billion dollars, and the cost of it has turned negative as the premiums grow faster than the claims.

Investments lost money on paper. The portfolio that includes Apple and Bank of America fell by about 24 billion dollars in the quarter as the bond yields rose, and the accounting rule puts the change through the net income. A paper loss is a number that the shareholders have learned to ignore. The company reported a net loss of 12.8 billion dollars for the quarter because of the portfolio, and the operating profit is the number the shareholders watch.

Buybacks slowed. The company spent about 1.1 billion dollars on its own shares, the lowest in two years, because the price was near the record and the valuation was full. A buyback that slows is a manager who thinks the shares are fair. The company has about 40 billion dollars more cash than at the start of the year, and the managers have said the deals are expensive.

What the quarter shows is that the cash has become a profitable asset and the company is waiting for the price of the businesses it wants, and the waiting is what the pile is for. A company that earns 5 per cent on its cash is a company that does not need to buy anything, and the not needing is the discipline. The company holds more cash than most of the funds it competes with, and the size is the constraint on the returns.
