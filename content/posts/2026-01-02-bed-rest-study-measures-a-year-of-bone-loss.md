A year-long bed-rest study reported this month put twelve healthy men in six-degree head-down tilt for 360 days, in a metabolic ward with controlled diet and no standing. Bone mineral density fell 8 per cent at the hip over the year and 2 per cent at the spine, and the pattern of loss is the finding rather than the total.

Design carries the result. A second group of twelve lay in the same ward for the same period but performed 30 minutes of resistive exercise every other day, and lost 3 per cent at the hip. Mechanical loading, not the horizontal posture, accounts for most of the difference, which is the first clean separation of the two effects in a controlled setting.

Cellular mechanism explains the site-specific pattern. Bone is maintained by osteoblasts building matrix and osteoclasts resorbing it, and unloading tilts the balance towards resorption within weeks. Trabecular bone loses faster than cortical bone because its surface-to-volume ratio is higher, which is why the femoral head and the vertebral body are the sites that move first.

Biomarkers give a timeline the density scans cannot. Serum CTX, a resorption marker, rose 40 per cent in the first month and stayed elevated. P1NP, a formation marker, fell over three months. That asymmetry explains why astronauts regain bone more slowly than they lose it, and why a twelve-month mission leaves a deficit that a twelve-month recovery does not close.

Countermeasures were tested as a third arm. A subgroup received a bisphosphonate for the final four months, and their hip density stopped falling at the level already reached. The drug is a floor, not a repair; nothing in the protocol rebuilt bone while the men were horizontal.

Comparing with orbit is the point of the analogue. Astronauts lose about 1.5 per cent per month at the hip, faster than the bed-rest rate, because unloading combines with fluid shifts, reduced ultraviolet exposure and a different mechanical loading profile. The study's value is that it isolates one variable cleanly.

Exercise arm is the part that matters for mission planning. Two hours daily on a resistance device is the current orbital protocol, and the ground analogue shows it recovers two-thirds of the loss at best. A three-year Mars mission on that protocol would arrive with hip density in osteoporotic territory.

Muscle follows a parallel curve and a different shape. Lean mass at the thigh fell 14 per cent over the year and did not respond to the resistance protocol, which recovered 4 points of the 14. The paper's authors note that the same unloading produces a larger relative loss in muscle than in bone, and that neither returns without loading.

The ward's diet was controlled at 2,600 kilocalories with 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram, so the loss is not a nutritional effect. That control is what allows the study to attribute the change to unloading rather than to the reduced intake that usually accompanies immobility.

For a reader, the study quietly reframes the question. Bone loss in space is not a mystery of microgravity but a well-understood response to unloading, and the interventions that work on Earth, loading and drugs, work in orbit to the extent they can be applied. The unsettled part is how much loading a crew will actually perform on a long transit.
