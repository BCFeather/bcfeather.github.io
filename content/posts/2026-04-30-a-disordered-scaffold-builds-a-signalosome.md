Signalling pathways are drawn as chains of molecules, and the drawing hides the fact that many of the links are made by scaffold proteins that hold the components in one place. A scaffold is supposed to be a passive fixture, and the linker for activation of T cells, LAT, is one of the best-studied examples in immunology.

LAT is anchored to the membrane and, when a T cell receptor is engaged, it is phosphorylated and recruits a series of proteins through small modular domains. The textbook picture is a set of independent docking sites, each binding one partner, with the scaffold providing proximity and nothing else, and a scaffold that did more than that would be a scaffold that contradicts the drawing.

A study in *Science* in late April by Rubin and colleagues tested the picture by mutating individual sequence elements and measuring what happened to the rest. The result was not modular: mutations that blocked the interaction with one partner also disrupted the binding and downstream signalling of others.

The interpretation is that LAT assembles a signalosome, a complex held together by its members as much as by the scaffold, in which the scaffold initiates assembly and the partners maintain it. Each recruited protein makes contacts with its neighbours, and those contacts stabilize the whole, so that removing one produces a loss of stability and not a loss of one function.

The claim carries an immunological consequence. If the complex is cooperative, then partial inhibition of any component can produce a disproportionate loss of signalling, and that changes how a drug targeting one node in the pathway should be dosed, and how resistance to it should be expected to appear in a clinic over a period of years.

An evolutionary reading follows from the same data, and it is the more interesting one. A disordered protein that binds many partners is a protein whose sequence is not constrained to a single fold, and the disorder allows the complex to assemble in more than one order. Cooperativity of that kind may be what makes a signalling pathway tunable and not merely switchable.

Methodologically, the result comes from a systematic mutational scan across the disordered region combined with measurements of binding and signalling for every partner, which is the kind of experiment that a modular model never required and that a collective model demands, and the difference is a generation of graduate students. The paper's contribution is as much in the technique as in the conclusion. Generalization across the family of scaffolds is the open question. Other scaffolds with disordered regions exist in other pathways, and whether they assemble collectives as well is a question that can now be asked with the same method. If they do, the textbook drawing of signalling chains is due for a revision, and the revision would be a drawing with more contacts and fewer arrows.
