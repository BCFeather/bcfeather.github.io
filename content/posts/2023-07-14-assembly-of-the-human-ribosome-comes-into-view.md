A ribosome is built from RNA and proteins, and its assembly in a cell is a process with dozens of factors. The bacterial assembly has been studied for decades, and the human version is more elaborate.

The snapshots in *Science*, 14 July 2023 resolve the human process. The authors purify assembling particles from cells, classify them by their composition, and determine structures that place each factor at a stage.

The order is the result. A particle's structure shows which proteins are present and which processing factors are bound, and the series places the steps in sequence.

The difference from bacteria is the finding. The human large subunit's assembly involves factors that have no bacterial counterpart, and the additional steps are where the eukaryotic process diverges.

The RNA processing is a step the bacterial system lacks. A eukaryotic ribosomal RNA is made as a precursor and trimmed, and the trimming is coupled to the assembly of the particle's domains.

The factor's role is often a placeholder. A protein that binds a domain and is later removed is one that keeps the domain from folding prematurely, and the structures show which factors occupy which domains.

The disease connection is the motivation. Mutations in assembly factors cause bone marrow failure syndromes, and a structure that places the factor is a basis for understanding the mutation.

The method is the advance. A heterogeneous population of particles is separated by gradient and imaged by cryo-electron microscopy, and the classification is what turns a mixture into a series.

The limitation is the resolution's coverage. A particle that is flexible produces a blurred map, and the earliest stages are the least ordered.

What the paper does not show is the kinetics. A series of structures is a set of states, and the rates between them need a time-resolved experiment.

For cell biology, the value is an assembly line described in molecular detail. The ribosome's biogenesis is one of the cell's largest manufacturing operations, and the snapshots are its blueprint.

The processing factor's identity is the clue to the order. A nuclease that trims a specific helix must act before the helix's proteins bind, and the structures show the trimming and the binding in the states that follow.

The compartment's geography is part of the story. The assembly begins in the nucleolus and finishes in the nucleoplasm, and the structures are from particles caught at each stage.

The protein's stoichiometry is the classification's basis. A particle with a given set of proteins is a stage, and a mass measurement plus a structure assigns each particle to one.

The comparison with yeast is the useful one. The yeast's assembly is genetically tractable and the human's is not, and a structure that matches the yeast's genetics is a bridge between the two systems.

The assembly's energy is unmeasured. A factor that uses ATP is a step with a cost, and the structures do not report the energy that the process consumes.

The quality control is the last step. A particle that fails to assemble is degraded, and the structures of the discarded intermediates are the ones that the survey does not capture.
