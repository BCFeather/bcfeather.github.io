A ribosome is built from RNA and proteins, and its assembly runs through dozens of factors. Bacterial assembly has been studied for decades, and the human version is more elaborate. In *Science* on 14 July 2023, the human process is resolved. The authors purify assembling particles from cells, classify them by composition, and determine structures placing each factor at a stage.

Order is the result. A particle's structure shows which proteins are present and which processing factors are bound, and the series puts the steps in sequence. Divergence from bacteria is the finding. The human large subunit's assembly involves factors with no bacterial counterpart, and those additional steps are where the eukaryotic process departs. RNA processing adds a step the bacterial system lacks. Eukaryotic ribosomal RNA is made as a precursor and trimmed, and that trimming is coupled to folding of the particle's domains.

Placeholder roles explain many factors. A protein binding a domain and later removed is one keeping that domain from folding prematurely, and the structures show which factors occupy which domains; identity of the processing factor is the clue to order, since a nuclease trimming a specific helix must act before the helix's proteins bind.

Geography is part of the story, because assembly begins in the nucleolus and finishes in the nucleoplasm, and the structures come from particles caught at each stage. Stoichiometry underlies the classification, since a particle with a given set of proteins is a stage, and a mass measurement plus a structure assigns each particle to one.

Disease supplies the motivation. Mutations in assembly factors cause bone marrow failure syndromes, and a structure placing the factor is a basis for understanding the mutation. Method is the advance, as a heterogeneous population is separated by gradient and imaged by cryo-electron microscopy, and classification turns a mixture into a series. Coverage limits the resolution. A flexible particle produces a blurred map, and the earliest stages are the least ordered. Kinetics is what the paper does not show, because a series of structures is a set of states and the rates between them need a time-resolved experiment.

Cell biology gains an assembly line described in molecular detail, since ribosome biogenesis is one of the cell's largest manufacturing operations and these snapshots are its blueprint. Yeast offers the comparison, because its assembly is genetically tractable while the human one is not, and a structure matching yeast genetics bridges the two systems. Quality control closes the story, as a particle failing to assemble is degraded, and those discarded intermediates are what the survey does not capture.

A comparison of the human series with the yeast one at the same resolution would show which factors are conserved placeholders and which are additions, and that alignment is what would make the two literatures comparable. An in vitro reconstruction of a single step would test whether the factor's presence is sufficient, since a structure shows occupancy rather than causality, and the reconstitution is the experiment the snapshots invite.
