A festival jury has to choose between films that are good and films that matter, and this year it chose one that is both. The 78th Cannes Film Festival closed on 24 May with the Palme d'Or going to Jafar Panahi for *It Was Just an Accident*, a film about five people who believe they have recognized the prosecutor who had them tortured, and who cannot be sure because they were blindfolded.

Story is drawn from the director's own imprisonment. Panahi was arrested in 2010, convicted of propaganda against the regime, banned from filmmaking for twenty years, and kept working anyway, smuggling *This Is Not a Film* to Cannes on a flash drive in 2011. A travel ban of fifteen years was lifted in 2023, and this was his first return to the festival in two decades.

Award is a vindication with a commercial edge. Neon, the American distributor, has now won six consecutive Palmes, from *Parasite* in 2019 through *Titane*, *Triangle of Sadness*, *Anatomy of a Fall* and *Anora*, and it will release this one in the United States. A distributor's streak is not an artistic judgment, and it is a fact about how a small company has learned to buy the winners.

Rest of the list was French. Seven of the eight prizes went to French productions or co-productions, including the Palme, which was made with Les Films Pelleas and Arte France, and the Grand Prix, which went to Joachim Trier's *Sentimental Value*, a Norwegian film starring Renate Reinsve, Elle Fanning and Stellan Skarsgard. Twenty-two films competed, and the jury was chaired by Juliette Binoche.

Competition had been shaped by politics before the jury met. Films from Iran, Palestine and Ukraine were in the selection, and the festival's decision to program them is itself a statement in a year when several governments were restricting what their directors could say. A prize to a director who was jailed for his work is the clearest version of that statement.

Panahi's speech was about his country and not about cinema. He asked Iranians with different opinions to put their differences aside, and said the most important thing was the country and its freedom. A festival that usually rewards formal invention rewarded a sentence, and the audience stood for it, which is the only review that matters on a closing night.

What the prize does to the film is what prizes do. A Palme adds a distributor, a marketing budget and an audience that would not have found an Iranian drama about a blindfold, and it also fixes the film as a political document and not as a piece of craft. Panahi has spent fifteen years being both, and he accepted the smaller of the two descriptions. A director who was forbidden to make films for fifteen years has now made the one that a jury could not ignore. He has already said he will keep working in the country that banned him, which is the sentence the prize was for.
