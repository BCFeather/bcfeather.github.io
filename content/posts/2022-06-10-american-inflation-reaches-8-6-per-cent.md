Consumer prices rose 8.6 per cent in the year to May, the Labor Department reported on 10 June. The figure was above the 8.3 forecast and it was the highest since December 1981. Core prices rose 6 per cent, and they had risen 6.2 in April. The monthly gain was 1 per cent, which was the largest since 2005. The index had risen 8.3 per cent in April, and the forecast was for the same figure.

Energy was the largest part. Petrol rose 4.1 per cent in the month and 50 per cent in the year, and the national average reached 5 dollars a gallon. Electricity rose 12 per cent, and natural gas 30. Food rose 10 per cent, and the price of eggs 30 because of the bird flu outbreak. Shelter was the part that mattered for the future. Rents rose 5.2 per cent in the year, and they had been rising 4.5 in January. The index measures rents with a lag of 12 months, and economists said the increase would keep core inflation high into 2023.

Used cars fell 1.5 per cent in the month, and they had fallen for three months. The price of new cars rose 1.1, and it was 12.6 higher than a year earlier. Apparel rose 0.7, and airline fares rose 12.6 in the month, which was the largest increase on record. The report changed the market's expectations. Futures priced a 75 basis point rise at the June meeting, and they had priced 50 before. The two-year yield rose 30 basis points, and the ten-year yield rose 20. The stock market fell 3 per cent, and the dollar rose. Housing was 40 per cent of the index, and it was the largest single component.

Fed officials had said they would raise by 50 points in June and in July. It raised by 75 in June, which was the largest increase since 1994, and by 75 in July. The chairman said the committee would do whatever it took to bring inflation down, and he said the pace would depend on the data. The S&P 500 fell 3 per cent, and the Nasdaq 4.

Average hourly earnings rose 5.2 per cent in the year, and they had fallen behind prices. The wage data did not support the fear of a spiral. Economists said real wages had fallen 3 per cent, and that households were using savings and credit to keep spending.

Inflation peaked at 9.1 per cent in June and fell to 8.5 in July. The Fed raised by 75 points four times in the year, and the rate reached 4.5 per cent. The index fell to 6.5 per cent in December and to 3 per cent in June 2023, and it reached the target range in 2024. The rate reached 4.5 per cent by December, and it stayed there until March 2023.
