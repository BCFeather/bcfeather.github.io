President Biden announced on 8 March that the United States would ban imports of Russian crude, refined products, natural gas and coal, and he did it without waiting for the Europeans. The order covered about 700,000 barrels a day of crude and products, roughly 8 per cent of American imports and 3 per cent of consumption, and it was the first measure that reached the revenue that funds the Russian army. Britain said it would phase out Russian oil by the end of the year, and the European Union did not follow.

The arithmetic explains the split. The United States took a small share of its oil from Russia and had domestic production to fall back on. Germany imported about a third of its gas and a quarter of its crude from Russia, and its refineries in Schwedt and Leuna were built to run on Urals crude delivered by the Druzhba pipeline. A ban that Washington could announce in a morning was for Berlin a decision about whether industry would run in the winter.

Prices moved at once. Brent settled above 127 dollars on the day of the announcement, the American average for petrol reached 4.17 dollars a gallon, a record in data that went back to 1990, and diesel in Europe rose further than crude. The president blamed the Russian government for the rise, called it Putin's price hike, and released more oil from the strategic reserve, which had already fallen to its lowest level since 1984.

The ban created a new trade rather than ending one. Russian crude found buyers in India and China at discounts that reached 30 dollars a barrel, and Indian refiners, which had processed little Russian oil before the war, took about a million barrels a day by the summer and exported refined products back into the markets that had banned the crude. Ship-to-ship transfers off Greece and Malta, the shadow fleet of tankers bought to carry the trade, and the insurance arrangements around it became an industry of their own within a year.

Washington had to choose what the measure was for. Announced as a punishment for the invasion, it also raised the price that American drivers paid, for a president whose approval was already falling over inflation. The administration argued that the revenue forgone by Russia would exceed the cost at home, and the argument held only if the discount and the rerouting did not restore most of the volume. By the summer Russia's oil revenue was higher than it had been before the war, because the price had risen faster than the volume had fallen.

What the announcement established was the sequence that every energy sanction followed afterwards. A ban on imports is easy to declare and hard to enforce, because the barrel that one country refuses is bought by another and refined into a product that the first country then imports. The price cap that the allies agreed in the autumn was an attempt to fix the problem that the ban of March had created: to cut the revenue without cutting the barrel.
