Spacecraft in low Earth orbit are sandblasted all year by sporadic meteoroids, and that background flux sets the shielding rules. Meteor showers add spikes on top, lasting hours or days, which matter because a crew outside cannot be brought in at short notice. A shower that delivers its particles over ten hours and one that delivers the same mass in a single hour leave an operator with very different amounts of warning.

NASA's Meteoroid Environment Office publishes annual shower forecasts for exactly that purpose, naming the periods of elevated risk. The list has grown by accretion: newly discovered showers were added, old parameters were refined, and nothing was taken away. Each addition was locally reasonable and collectively unmanageable.

That approach has run out of room. More than a thousand showers have been reported in the literature, most of them extremely minor, several unconfirmed, and many missing the quantities a forecast needs, such as the particle flux at Earth, the speed distribution, and the position of the radiant. Showers recovered from single-camera video are the weakest entries of all, since a radiant and a speed cannot be separated from one viewpoint.

Moorhead, Cooke, Brown and Campbell-Brown replace accretion with a test. They set out a quantitative criterion for a shower that could plausibly matter to a spacecraft, apply it to the established shower list, and admit the survivors to the forecast. The criterion has to encode the physics the forecasts already describe: how many particles a shower delivers, how fast they arrive, and from which direction relative to the spacecraft's motion. Direction matters because shielding is directional, and a stream arriving from behind a panel counts for less than one arriving head-on.

Speed does much of the work. Impact energy scales with the square of velocity, so a slow stream of debris and a fast stream of the same mass loading are not the same hazard, and a shower's velocity distribution is among the parameters most often missing. None of the three can be guessed from a shower's name or from its parent body, which is why the list grew without being checked. A forecast that lists everything predicts nothing in particular.

What the test buys is a forecast that can be defended. A shower dropped from the list becomes a judgement somebody made and can be re-examined; a list of a thousand entries, ranked by nothing, is a judgement nobody can check.

The uncertain middle stays where it belongs, with showers whose parameters are incomplete. Those need observation before they need a policy, and the criterion at least makes plain which measurement would settle the question. It also shortens the forecast to entries that can be counted and rechecked, which is exactly what keeps the document readable before a spacewalk rather than after one. The paper was accepted by *Advances in Space Research*.
