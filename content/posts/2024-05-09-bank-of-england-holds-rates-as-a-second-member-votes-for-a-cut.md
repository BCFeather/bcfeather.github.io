Bank of England left its bank rate at 5.25 per cent on 9 May, the sixth meeting in a row without a change, and two of the nine members of the committee voted to cut. It was the first vote in favour of a reduction since the tightening began. The governor said the committee would need to see more than one month of data before it acted, which is the language that precedes a cut rather than the language that announces one.

Split is the signal. A committee that votes unanimously to hold is a committee that has not yet seen what it needs, and a committee that splits is one where the argument has moved from whether to when. A dissent of two is small and it is the direction that matters. The two members who voted for a reduction argued that the real level of the bank rate had risen as inflation fell, which is a technical point that carries a political one.

Inflation is nearly at the target. Headline inflation had fallen to 3.2 per cent and the forecast published with the decision showed it reaching two per cent within a year, which is faster than the committee expected in February. A forecast that is revised down is a justification that the doves can use. The forecast assumes the market's path for rates, and the path has the bank rate falling to around four per cent within two years, which is why the projections look benign.

Services inflation is the obstacle. Prices in the service sector were still rising at around six per cent, and the committee has said that number reflects domestic wage pressure rather than imported costs. A number that falls slowly is the reason the majority still wants to wait. Wages in the private sector were growing at six per cent, and the committee has said the rate consistent with its target is closer to three, which is a gap that closes slowly.

Growth picture is the other side. The economy had just left a shallow recession and the forecasts were raised, which removes the argument that policy is unnecessarily tight. A recovery that is real is a recovery that gives the committee room to be patient. The government has been pressing the bank publicly for relief, and the governor has been careful to describe the relationship as one of respectful distance.

What the meeting shows is that the first cut in a cycle is decided by the composition of inflation rather than by its level, and the members who voted for it are betting that the composition is already turning. A first cut in August rather than in June would leave the committee with one meeting before the autumn, and the timing is now the whole of the argument. The next meeting in June is the one the market has circled, and the vote in May is the reason the market now expects a change in the summer rather than in the autumn.
