A vegetable that costs about thirty rupees a kilogram in a normal July cost more than two hundred in Delhi, Mumbai and Chennai in the third week of the month, and the queue outside a government stall in the capital ran around a city block. Tomatoes are the cheapest and most perishable of India's staple vegetables, and their price is watched more closely than any other food item because it moves first and fastest. When it quadruples, the government hears about it.

The immediate causes are weather and biology. Heat in the main growing districts of Maharashtra, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh cut yields, and the whitefly-borne virus that farmers call leaf curl spread through fields weakened by drought and then by unseasonal rain. Tomato crops are harvested within weeks and cannot be stored, so a shortfall in one region becomes a national price within days, because the supply chain that moves them is thin and informal.

Political arithmetic does the rest. India votes in a general election next year, and food prices are the measure by which most households judge the government. The Bharatiya Janata Party has spent a decade arguing that its management of the economy is superior, and an onion or tomato at four times its normal price is a counter-argument that everyone can see. State governments have opened subsidized stalls, capped prices in some markets, and ordered supplies from the hill states where the crop was still arriving. Truckloads were escorted into the capital's wholesale markets at dawn, which for a day made the price a question of policing rather than of farming.

Federal intervention has been improvised and visible. The consumer affairs ministry asked the cooperative Nafed to buy tomatoes and sell them at a discount, arranged extra trains from Himachal Pradesh and Karnataka, and urged states to crack down on hoarding. Imports from Nepal were discussed, which is an unusual step for a perishable crop, and the agriculture ministry published data showing that the area under cultivation had risen. None of it changes the fact that a shortage of a perishable good cannot be fixed by a subsidized delivery that arrives a week late.

Behind the price is a structural problem. Farmers plant tomatoes when the previous year's price was high, which guarantees a glut and a crash the following season, and they have no cold storage, no grading, no futures market and no insurance that pays quickly. The middlemen who move the crop from field to city take a large share of the retail price, and the consumers who pay it have no way of knowing who took what. A crop that reaches the city within a day of harvest passes through four or five hands, each taking a cut that nobody records.

Prices began to fall by the end of the month, as they always do, and the government claimed credit for the relief. The lesson that farmers and consumers take from a season like this is that the state can intervene at the moment of a crisis and cannot prevent the next one, which is a lesson they have learned before.
