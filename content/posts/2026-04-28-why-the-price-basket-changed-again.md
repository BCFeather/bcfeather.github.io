description: "A statistical agency reweighted its consumer price basket this month, cutting food's share and raising services. Reweighting changes the measured inflation rate without changing any price, and the direction of the change is worth knowing before the next reading."
---

Statistical agencies revise consumer price baskets on a schedule, and the revision published this month moved three numbers that matter: food's weight fell by 2.4 percentage points, housing services rose by 1.9, and communications fell by 0.8. No price changed. The inflation rate measured from May onwards will differ from the one measured in April, and the difference comes entirely from what the index chooses to look at.

Weights come from household expenditure surveys, which lag reality by two to three years because collecting and cleaning the diaries takes that long. A basket that reflects spending patterns from 2023 is measuring a 2026 economy, and the gap widens when relative prices move sharply, as they have. Households shift towards whatever became cheaper, so a fixed basket overstates the cost of the items whose prices rose and understates the share of the items whose prices fell.

The direction of this revision is towards services. Food's share in urban households has been declining for a decade, and the survey data caught up. Housing services, which include rents and imputed owner-occupancy costs, rose in weight because rents did. Communications fell because bundled plans got cheaper and households bought more of them, so expenditure share dropped even as consumption rose.

Measurement effects follow quickly. Core inflation, which excludes food and energy, gains weight in the overall index, so a shock in services now moves the headline more than it did last month. A reader comparing year-on-year rates across the revision will see a step change that is arithmetic, not economic.

Statistical agencies handle this in different ways. Some chain-link the index and publish a series that is continuous across revisions; others publish the new basket and a one-off estimate of the difference. This agency did the second, and the published estimate is that the new basket would have read 0.2 percentage points higher over the past twelve months than the old one.

That number is small and consequential. A central bank targeting a range with a 0.2 point measurement shift has less room than it thought, and the communication problem is that the shift cannot be explained in a headline. The honest description is that the target is unchanged and the ruler has been recalibrated.

Two caveats deserve space. First, imputed owner-occupancy costs are a construct: they estimate what an owner would pay to rent their own home, and their weight in a basket determines how much of a housing market enters the inflation measure. Second, substitution within categories, the practice of assuming households buy more of a cheaper variety when prices diverge, dampens measured inflation mechanically and is built into most index formulae.

Formula choice matters as much as weight. A Laspeyres index, which most consumer price indices use, prices a fixed basket and therefore overstates the cost of living when consumers substitute away from items that become expensive. A chained index updates weights annually and corrects for that substitution, at the cost of requiring a continuous expenditure survey and a revision cycle that publishes numbers twice. This agency uses the fixed basket, so the reweighting is the only periodic correction available to it.

Practical consequence for a reader: the twelve-month rate published after the revision is not comparable with last year's rate published before it. Statistical offices usually publish a linking factor for exactly this purpose, and the one issued with this revision converts the old series to the new basis, which is the number to use when comparing across the change.

Anyone reading monthly inflation for investment decisions should note which basket produced the number. Comparing a post-revision reading with a pre-revision forecast is comparing two different measurements, and the error is systematic rather than random.
