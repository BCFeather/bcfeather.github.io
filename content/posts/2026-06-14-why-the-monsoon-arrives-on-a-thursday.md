There is no start date for the monsoon, only a circulation that switches on. In the weeks before onset, the land surface of South Asia heats, the temperature contrast with the ocean grows, and the cross-equatorial flow in the lower atmosphere strengthens until, at some point, deep convection organizes over the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea and the rain begins in earnest. That transition can happen in three days, and the difference between an onset on 5 June and an onset on 15 June is, for a farmer, the difference between a crop and a failed planting.

Forecasting that transition has been the goal of a programme that reported its latest results this month. The skill is now around ten to fifteen days ahead for the date of onset over Kerala, the southern state where the monsoon is conventionally declared to have arrived. That is not a long forecast by the standards of weather prediction, and it is a large improvement on the two or three days that were routine a decade ago.

Identifying the precursor accounts for the improvement. A group of researchers has shown that the onset is preceded by a specific pattern in the tropical atmosphere: a slow build-up of moisture in a layer between three and six kilometres over the Arabian Sea, visible in satellite humidity retrievals, combined with a weakening of the subtropical jet over the Tibetan plateau. Neither signal alone is reliable. Together, they appear to give a probabilistic forecast with useful skill, because the build-up is a physical constraint: the atmosphere cannot produce the deep convection of onset until it has enough moisture in that layer.

Ocean temperature is the other ingredient. Sea surface temperatures in the Arabian Sea have been running above average in recent years, and the air-sea flux that moistens the atmosphere depends on them. Warmer water means more evaporation and a faster build-up, which shifts the probability of an early onset even if the large-scale circulation is unchanged. The forecasts have started to include that explicitly, and the effect on the timing is measurable.

Why a fourteen-day forecast is valuable despite its uncertainty is a matter of decisions rather than physics. Sowing dates, irrigation scheduling, the release of reservoir water and the purchase of seed are all decisions that are made weeks ahead, and a probabilistic onset forecast lets those decisions be hedged. Farmers who know there is a two-thirds chance of a late onset can choose a shorter-duration variety; water managers can hold reservoir levels. The value of a forecast is set by the decision it changes, not by its accuracy alone.

Limits are the familiar ones. The onset is a threshold event in a chaotic system, and a forecast that gives a sixty per cent chance of onset in a given week is wrong forty per cent of the time by construction. The programme's evaluations are honest about this, and about the regional variation: skill over Kerala is better than skill over the Bay of Bengal, where the convection is more sensitive to transient disturbances.

Whether the same precursor logic applies to the withdrawal in September, and to the breaks that matter more than the onset for agriculture, is the deeper question the work raises. Those are harder problems, because they involve the same circulation but without a clear threshold. The onset, for all its drama, is the part of the monsoon that a forecast can actually get right.
