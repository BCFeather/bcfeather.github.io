title: "Erosion rates put dates on the sedimentary rock of Mars"
date: "2022-11-05T10:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A two-parameter model that fits crater counts and a constant erosion rate separates old from young sedimentary deposits on Mars, and flags sites where shallow ice may survive."
---

Crater counting dates a surface by assuming that craters accumulate and nothing removes them. On an eroded surface the assumption fails, and a one-parameter fit returns an age that is too young by an amount nobody can bound, so a wind-scoured deposit can look recent when it is ancient, and the size of that error cannot be estimated from the count alone.

Medusae Fossae, a formation near the Martian equator, is exactly such a surface. Its origin is disputed, its absolute age is unknown, and wind erosion has removed enough material that the crater record is a distorted version of the original.

Work posted to arXiv on 1 November 2022 fits a two-parameter model instead. Cratering is treated as a Poisson process and erosion as a constant rate that removes small craters faster than large ones, and the authors fit age and rate together to new crater counts from Context Camera imagery, which resolves craters small enough to constrain the rate.

The fit splits the formation in two. The central part formed more than 1.5 billion years ago and erodes at under 650 nanometres per year, while the eastern parts and Zephyria Planum formed less than 1.5 billion years ago and erode faster than 740 nanometres per year, a factor of about two in age and a factor of about two in rate. Craters on the top of Aeolis Mons in Gale Crater point to a young surface and a slow rate.

Slow erosion has a consequence for what lies beneath. Fast erosion exposes shallow ice to loss, while a slow rate leaves it stable, and the authors use their fitted rates to nominate sites where near-surface equatorial ice could persist and be detected by gamma-ray or neutron spectroscopy. Zephyria Planum appears on the list, which is the paper's most concrete prediction.

A likelihood over crater diameters is what the fit maximizes, and each size bin carries an expected count that depends on both age and erosion, so the shape of the distribution separates the two parameters where a total cannot. The cost of that separation is a wider confidence interval: two parameters fitted to one distribution leave more room for error than one, and the paper's error bars are correspondingly larger than a single-age estimate would give. Limits follow from the model: a constant rate is an idealisation, since a surface that eroded quickly and then slowly is fitted by an average, and the imagery's resolution fixes the smallest crater the fit can use, which for a slow-eroding surface is also the bin that carries most of the signal.

Sampling one of the nominated sites would test the inference, and the sites named in the paper are the ones an instrument team would choose first. A neutron spectrometer reading at Zephyria Planum would either confirm the erosion-based argument or show that the constant-rate assumption fails where the ice is.
