Thwaites Glacier drains a basin the size of a large country, and its grounding line, where the ice lifts off the seabed and floats, is the hinge on which West Antarctic mass loss turns. The line has retreated inland for decades, and the rate has been inferred from satellites that see the surface rather than the base.

The survey reported in *Nature*, 16 February 2023 looks at the base directly. The authors combine sonar and radar measurements of the ice-ocean boundary with ocean data, and the picture that emerges is of a boundary that melts unevenly rather than as a uniform front.

Heterogeneity is the result. Some stretches of the grounding line lose ice at a rate far higher than the average, and those stretches coincide with the paths by which warm water reaches the ice. A model that assumes a uniform melt rate along the line will underpredict the places where retreat actually happens.

The mechanism is the ocean's access. Warm, salty water is denser than the cold meltwater it displaces, and it intrudes along the seabed to the deepest part of the grounding line. Where the bed slopes inland, the intrusion can reach further, and the melt concentrates where the geometry allows it.

The measurements matter for the same reason that all ice-sheet fieldwork matters: models are calibrated on the parameters that observations supply. A basal melt rate that varies by an order of magnitude along a single glacier changes the predicted retreat by decades.

The geometry is the other variable. A grounding line on a retrograde slope, where the bed deepens inland, is unstable in the sense that retreat exposes more ice to the same warm water. Thwaites has such a slope in its inner reaches, and the survey's map of the boundary is an input to the stability calculation.

What the survey cannot see is the time series. A snapshot of the boundary tells where melt is concentrated now, and the rate of change requires repeated measurements, which are expensive and logistically difficult in Antarctic waters.

The projection consequence is a spread rather than a number. Sea-level estimates from West Antarctica depend on how fast the grounding line retreats, and a heterogeneous melt pattern makes the fast sectors faster and the slow ones slower, which widens the range rather than shifting the mean.

For the observational programme, the lesson is that the grounding line is a place, not a line. It has topography, and the topography decides where the ocean does its work.

Sonar from beneath the ice shelf is the technique that made the survey possible. A vehicle lowered through a borehole maps the ice's underside and the seabed in the same pass, and the two surfaces together define the cavity through which the ocean circulates.
The cavity's shape is the mechanism's other half. A narrow cavity restricts the exchange of water and traps the meltwater, while a wide one lets warm water replace it. The survey's bathymetry shows which stretches are wide, and those are the stretches where melt is fastest.
Fresh meltwater is the buoyancy source that drives the circulation. It rises along the ice base and draws warmer water in behind it, and the strength of that plume depends on the melt rate it feeds. The feedback is what makes the system sensitive to small changes in ocean temperature.
