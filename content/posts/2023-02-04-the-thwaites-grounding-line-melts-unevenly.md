Thwaites Glacier drains a basin the size of a large country, and its grounding line, where the ice lifts off the seabed and floats, is the hinge on which West Antarctic mass loss turns. The line has retreated inland for decades, but the rate has been inferred from satellites that see the surface rather than the base.

In *Nature* on 16 February 2023, a survey looks at the base directly. Sonar and radar measurements of the ice-ocean boundary are combined with ocean data, and the picture is of a boundary that melts unevenly rather than as a uniform front; a vehicle lowered through a borehole maps the ice's underside and the seabed in one pass, and the two surfaces together define the cavity through which the ocean circulates.

Heterogeneity is the result. Some stretches of the line lose ice at a rate far above the average, and those stretches coincide with the paths by which warm water reaches the ice, so a model that assumes a uniform melt rate will underpredict the places where retreat actually happens.

Because warm, salty water is denser than the cold meltwater it displaces, it intrudes along the seabed to the deepest part of the grounding line, and where the bed slopes inland the intrusion reaches further, so melt concentrates where the geometry allows it. The cavity's shape governs the exchange: a narrow one restricts it and traps the meltwater, a wide one lets warm water replace it, and the bathymetry shows which stretches are wide and therefore fast. Fresh meltwater drives the circulation as well, rising along the ice base and drawing warmer water in behind, and the plume's strength depends on the melt it feeds, which makes the system sensitive to small changes in ocean temperature.

Geometry is the other variable. A grounding line on a retrograde slope, where the bed deepens inland, is unstable in the sense that retreat exposes more ice to the same warm water, and Thwaites has such a slope in its inner reaches, so the survey's map of the boundary is an input to the stability calculation. Models are calibrated on the parameters observations supply, and a basal melt rate that varies by an order of magnitude along one glacier changes the predicted retreat by decades.

What the survey cannot see is the time series, since a snapshot tells where melt is concentrated now and the rate of change needs repeated measurements, expensive and logistically difficult in Antarctic waters. Projections therefore get a spread rather than a number, because West Antarctic sea-level estimates depend on how fast the line retreats, and a heterogeneous pattern makes the fast sectors faster and the slow ones slower, widening the range instead of shifting the mean. The lesson for the observational programme is that the grounding line is a place rather than a line, with a topography that decides where the ocean does its work.
