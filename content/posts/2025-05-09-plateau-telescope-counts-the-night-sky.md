Lenghu sits on a basin floor in Qinghai at about 4,200 metres, in a landscape of eroded sandstone that looks like a film set for another planet. In the past five years it has become the site of a cluster of optical telescopes, built by Chinese universities and institutes, and the reason is the air. The site was identified by a survey that took five years and measured cloud cover from satellite data before anyone built anything there, which is how astronomy sites are chosen now.

Astronomical observing depends on three things: how clear the sky is, how stable the air is, and how dark the site is. Lenghu scores well on all three. The plateau is dry, with annual precipitation below 100 millimetres, and the prevailing wind is steady enough that the turbulence above the site is low. The nearest city is far away, and the local government has passed an ordinance restricting outdoor lighting.

Statistics from this site are unusual for China. Astronomers measure seeing in arcseconds, and Lenghu reports median values around 0.7 to 0.8 arcseconds, comparable to the best sites in the Chilean Andes. Cloud-free nights exceed 300 a year at some of the stations on the mountain, which is a number that observatories in the east of the country can only dream about. Among existing Chinese sites, only Gaomeigu in Yunnan comes close, and it is lower and wetter in summer.

The telescopes being built there are not the largest in the world, and they are not meant to be. A 2.5-metre telescope with a wide field is more useful for survey work than a 10-metre telescope with a narrow one, and China's astronomy community has been short of mid-sized instruments with good sites for a generation. Lenghu gives those instruments a home.

The practical problems are the ones that come with altitude. Staff work in shifts and rotate down to lower ground; the domes need heating that does not disturb the air; and the roads close in winter. The electricity comes from a small grid that has been reinforced, and the fibre link to Xining is the bottleneck for transferring data, which is why the observatories process their images on site. Each night produces terabytes, and the software that reduces them runs on a cluster in a container beside the dome, with the results sent down in the morning.

What the site means for the field is capacity. A country that wants to do time-domain astronomy, watching things that change from night to night, needs many telescopes pointed at the same sky rather than one enormous one. Lenghu is the first Chinese site that can host that kind of network, and the network is being assembled telescope by telescope. The next additions are a wide-field survey instrument and a pair of 1-metre telescopes for monitoring, both funded by provincial and national programmes. The site's master plan lists domes for a dozen instruments, which would make it the largest concentration of optical astronomy in China by 2030.
