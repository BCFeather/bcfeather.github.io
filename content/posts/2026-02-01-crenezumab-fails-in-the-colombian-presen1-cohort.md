The Colombian kindred that carries the *PSEN1* Glu280Ala variant is the best-studied example of autosomal dominant Alzheimer's disease, with a large extended family, a predictable age of onset and a mutation that can be tested in childhood. A prevention trial in this population can start before any symptom and can ask whether a drug delays the onset, which is a question that a trial in sporadic disease cannot answer cleanly.

Crenezumab is a monoclonal antibody against amyloid beta that had already failed in symptomatic sporadic disease, and the Colombian prevention trial was designed to test whether earlier treatment works when the disease process has not yet produced symptoms. Participants were cognitively unimpaired carriers who received the drug or placebo for five to eight years, which is a longer exposure than any previous Alzheimer's prevention trial. The results appeared in *The Lancet Neurology* in the February issue.

The primary outcome did not move. The annualised rate of change on the API ADAD composite was minus 1.10 in the crenezumab group and minus 1.43 in the placebo group, a between-group difference of 0.33 with a confidence interval from minus 0.48 to 1.13 and a *P* value of 0.43. The free and cued selective reminding test showed a difference of 0.01 with a *P* value of 0.16. Neither is a signal.

A trial this small cannot exclude a small effect, and the confidence interval includes differences that a larger study would detect. The design was powered for a larger effect size, and the result is best read as a failure to demonstrate benefit rather than as a demonstration of absence. That distinction matters for a field in which every negative prevention trial is cited as proof that amyloid is the wrong target.

Biomarker data are the more informative part. A drug that engages its target should change amyloid burden in the cerebrospinal fluid or on imaging, and the trial reports those measurements alongside the clinical result, which allows the question of whether the drug did anything at all to be separated from the question of whether doing it helped.

Its cohort is a scientific asset in its own right. A family with a known mutation, a reliable onset age and a willingness to enrol for eight years has produced a resource that outlives a single drug, and the natural history data from the placebo arm are what any future prevention trial will be designed against, which is a contribution that no drug trial can make on its own.

Where the field goes is toward earlier and more potent intervention. Lecanemab and donanemab remove amyloid in symptomatic patients and slow decline modestly, and the prevention hypothesis is that starting earlier would do more. That hypothesis has now survived one test and failed another, and the next trial will be designed with the lessons of both. Eight years of follow-up in a family cohort is a dataset that no pharmaceutical company would fund twice, and it now sits in the public literature.
