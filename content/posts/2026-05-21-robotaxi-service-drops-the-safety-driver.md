---A robotaxi service has operated with a safety driver behind the wheel since it launched, and the driver's presence is both a legal condition and a data collection method. An operator removed the driver from 200 vehicles this month after 18 months of supervised service, and its safety report is the evidence a regulator has to weigh.

Disengagement rate is the headline number. The fleet drove 4.2 million kilometres in supervision with 0.8 disengagements per 1,000 kilometres, of which 0.2 were the driver intervening for safety and 0.6 were the system handing control back for a planned manoeuvre.

The Comparison with a human baseline is the regulator's frame. A human driver in the same city has about 0.05 police-reported crashes per million kilometres, and the supervised fleet had 0.03, with the caveat that a safety driver can prevent a crash that the system would have caused.

The removal is conditional. Vehicles operate in a geofenced area of 40 square kilometres, in daylight and dry weather, and the remote operations centre can send a command that stops the vehicle. Those conditions are written into the permit.

Insurance repriced the risk. The operator's premium fell 30 per cent when the driver was removed, because the largest liability in a supervised operation is the driver's own error, and the policy now covers the system alone.

Public response was measured and mixed. A survey of 2,000 residents found 54 per cent comfortable with a driverless taxi, against 38 per cent two years ago, and the discomfort concentrated among people over 60.

The comparison with other cities is the regulatory variable. Two other cities permit driverless operation with a reporting requirement and one requires a safety driver, and the operator's expansion plan follows the permits rather than the technology.

Remote assistance is the substitute for a driver, and its limits are in the permit. Two remote operators monitor 200 vehicles, and a vehicle that cannot resolve a situation stops and waits. The permit caps the wait at four minutes and requires the vehicle to pull to the kerb, which is a behaviour the software had to learn.

Accessibility is an unresolved requirement. A wheelchair user needs assistance with a ramp, and with no driver the service asks the rider to wait for a human who may take ten minutes. Two disability groups have filed comments against expansion.

The comparison with a human driver's cost is the operator's arithmetic. A safety driver costs 60,000 dollars a year per vehicle, and 200 vehicles cost 12 million. The removal is a saving that pays for the remote operations centre four times over.

For a reader, the useful data point is the ratio of disengagements to kilometres. A system that intervenes 0.2 times per 1,000 kilometres for safety is not perfect, and the regulator's decision is whether a remote monitor plus a geofence is an acceptable substitute for a person in the seat.
