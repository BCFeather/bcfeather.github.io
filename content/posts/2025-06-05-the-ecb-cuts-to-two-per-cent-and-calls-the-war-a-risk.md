A central bank that has been cutting for a year is a central bank looking for a place to stop, and on 5 June the European Central Bank found one that looked like the target. The Governing Council cut all three key rates by 25 basis points, taking the deposit rate to 2 per cent, the main refinancing rate to 2.15 per cent and the marginal lending rate to 2.40 per cent, with effect from 11 June.

Projections explain the decision. Eurosystem staff put headline inflation at 2.0 per cent this year, 1.6 per cent in 2026 and 2.0 per cent in 2027, which is a downward revision of three tenths for both of the first two years and mostly the consequence of lower energy prices and a stronger euro. Core inflation was left almost unchanged at 2.4 per cent this year and 1.9 per cent after that.

Growth is the weaker half of the story. Staff kept 2025 at 0.9 per cent and raised the later years to 1.1 and 1.3 per cent, which is a recovery that depends on public investment, real wages and a labour market that has held up better than the surveys suggested. A first quarter flattered by exporters front-running American tariffs makes the second half look worse.

The council's language moved with the data. Its statement said most measures of underlying inflation point to a sustained return to 2 per cent, that wage growth is still elevated but moderating visibly, and that profits are absorbing part of the cost. A bank that says the job is nearly done is a bank preparing to stop, and the deposit rate at 2 per cent is roughly where economists put the neutral level.

Lagarde named the uncertainty rather than the number. Tariff policy, energy prices and a euro that has strengthened against the dollar are the three variables that could move the path, and she said the council would decide meeting by meeting. Markets read the pause into that phrase and moved on.

The asymmetry is the interesting part. A bank that has cut seven times has spent most of its insurance against a shock it has not yet seen, and the next move in a world of 10 per cent American tariffs, a Chinese surplus and a euro that has risen 8 per cent against the dollar is more likely to be a hold than a cut. The 2027 return to 2 per cent in the projections is the promise that keeps the option open.

What would settle the year is whether the 1.6 per cent forecast for 2026 survives the autumn. A cheaper euro would raise it, a cheaper oil price would lower it, and a trade war that cuts European exports would do both to growth and neither to prices. A pause is not a reversal, and the council has been careful to say so at every press conference since March.
