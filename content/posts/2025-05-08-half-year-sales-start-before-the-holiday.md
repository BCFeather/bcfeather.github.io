Pre-sales for the mid-year shopping festival opened in the second week of May, three weeks before the date the name refers to. The shift is now a decade old: the festival has migrated from a single day in June to a month and a half of pre-sales, deposits, deposits expanding into balances, and a final payment window that the platforms call the climax.

The mechanics are worth understanding because they explain the numbers. A buyer puts down a small deposit to lock a price, the platform counts that as demand, and the balance is paid on a fixed date. The reported gross merchandise value therefore includes orders that will be cancelled, and the cancellation rate has risen every year since the practice began. Platforms rarely publish the figure, but merchants can see it in their own settlement statements, and it now runs at a fifth to a quarter of deposit orders in apparel.

This year the discount language is quieter. The platforms are leaning on the trade-in subsidy that the government pays for appliances, phones and cars, which is a fiscal transfer rather than a merchant discount, and on the coupons that platforms fund themselves. A phone that is eligible for both can be 15 per cent cheaper without the retailer giving up margin, which is why the category has grown faster than the rest of the sale. A subsidy is also easier to advertise than a discount, because the number on the poster does not depend on which of a dozen coupons a buyer happens to hold.

Merchants have mixed feelings. A subsidy brings buyers who would not otherwise have bought, which is good, but it also pulls forward purchases that would have happened later, which flattens the rest of the year. Appliance retailers reported exactly that pattern in the first quarter: a strong January to March, then a quiet April, then a June that depended on the next round of coupons. That rhythm is hard on inventory planning: a warehouse that fills for March and empties in April has to refill for June with less certainty about what will sell.

The logistics side has adapted. Warehouses pre-position stock in regional hubs weeks in advance, and the couriers hire temporary staff for the payment window rather than for the whole month. The cost of a parcel falls when the volume is predictable, and the platforms have become good at making it predictable. The couriers' pay is now tied to a forecast rather than to the actual day, which shifts the risk onto the platform and off the rider.

What the festival is for has changed. It began as a way to create a demand spike in a month with no holiday; it is now a way to distribute a subsidy at scale. That is a different business, with a different margin structure, and the platforms that understand the difference are the ones reporting growth this year.
