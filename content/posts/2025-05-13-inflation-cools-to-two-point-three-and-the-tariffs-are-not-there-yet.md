title: "Inflation cools to 2.3 per cent and the tariffs are not there yet"

description: "American consumer prices rose 2.3 per cent in the year to April and core prices 2.8 per cent, a third month of cooling in which the new tariffs had barely appeared."
---

April's consumer price report, published on 13 May, was the first full month of data after the tariff announcement of 2 April, and it contained almost no tariff. Headline prices rose 0.2 per cent on the month and 2.3 per cent on the year, core prices rose 0.2 and 2.8, and both annual rates were the lowest since March 2021. The monthly core gain of 0.2 per cent is an annual pace near 2.4, which is close enough to target to make the report unremarkable.

Shelter did most of the work. The index for shelter rose 0.3 per cent on the month and remained the largest single contributor to the annual rate, while energy rose 0.7 per cent as natural gas and electricity offset a fall in petrol, and food fell 0.1 per cent as egg prices came down from their winter peak. Shelter alone contributed more than half of the monthly increase.

Core services carried the monthly increase, and medical care was the largest positive within it. Core goods, which is where a tariff on imports should appear first, showed little. That timing is not a mystery: a tariff applies when goods enter the country, importers hold inventories, and retailers change shelf prices on a schedule that lags the border by months.

The number beat expectations of 2.4 per cent on the headline, and the reaction in rates markets was to reduce the probability of a June cut. A central bank that sees inflation at 2.3 with unemployment at 4.2 has the room to wait, and the report removed the argument that the wait was costing something.

Distribution matters for what comes next. America imports a large share of its clothing, footwear, furniture and toys, and those categories are the ones where a 30 per cent tariff is a shelf price rather than a margin. The April report cannot show that pass-through because the goods had been ordered before the rate changed, and the inventories were still being sold at the old cost.

Business surveys in the same weeks told a different story from the official statistics. Firms reported plans to raise prices, consumers reported expectations of higher prices, and both cited tariffs. Plans and expectations are not prices, and the gap between them is the thing a data-dependent central bank is trying to measure. A firm that plans a price rise and then does not carry it out is a firm that feared demand more than cost, and the balance between those two fears is the whole question for the second half.

What would settle it is the June and July reports, when inventories bought ahead of the rate have been sold and replacement stock arrives at the new duty. A jump in core goods inflation would confirm the pass-through; another month at 2.8 core would suggest that firms absorbed the cost in margins, which is what a weak demand environment allows, and the two outcomes imply very different paths for rates.
