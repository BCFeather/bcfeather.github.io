description: "A grocery chain removed self-checkout from 40 stores after shrink rose 1.4 percentage points, and the reversal is a case study in how a labour-saving device became a loss-prevention cost."
---

Self-checkout was sold as a labour saving: a bank of eight machines replaces three cashiers. A grocery chain that installed them across 200 stores has removed them from 40, and the reason in its disclosure is shrink, the industry term for inventory loss, which rose 1.4 percentage points in the stores that had them.

The arithmetic is simple and the numbers are not. A self-checkout bank costs 120,000 dollars and saves 90,000 a year in labour. A 1.4 point rise in shrink on a store with 18 million dollars of sales is 250,000 dollars, which reverses the case immediately.

Mechanism is partly deliberate and partly accidental. Scanning errors, produce codes entered for cheaper items and unscanned items in the bagging area account for most of the loss, and the chain's audit found that 60 per cent of the losses came from 8 per cent of transactions.

Technology responses have limits. Weight checks and computer vision help, and the chain's data show they recover half the loss at a cost of 20,000 dollars per store per year plus the friction of false alarms, which reduce throughput.

Staffing is the counter-intuitive finding. The stores that kept self-checkout but added a dedicated attendant at the bank had shrink 0.5 points above baseline rather than 1.4, because the attendant deters the deliberate cases. That cost is 45,000 dollars a year, which halves the labour saving.

Customer response is not uniform. Self-checkout is faster for a basket of ten items and slower for a trolley, and the chain's transaction data show average basket size 22 per cent smaller at self-checkout. That reduction in basket size is a revenue effect the labour calculation ignores.

The comparison with other chains is mixed. Two competitors have expanded self-checkout and report shrink within normal range, which suggests the outcome depends on store format, product mix and the proportion of high-theft categories.

Category mix explains the variation between stores. A store with a high share of alcohol, cosmetics and fresh meat loses more at self-checkout, because those items are expensive per unit and easy to mis-scan. The chain's worst store lost 3.1 points and its best 0.4.

The accounting question is where the cost sits. Shrink is a cost of goods line and labour is an operating line, and a store manager judged on labour hours has an incentive to install the machines whatever the shrink does. The chain has changed its bonus metric to include shrink, which is the control that matters.

The intervention with the best record is a receipt check at the exit, which recovers 0.4 points at a labor cost of one employee per four machines. That is the same arithmetic as a cashier, which is why the chain's decision is a choice about service rather than about theft.

For a reader, the case is about a technology whose cost lands in a different line from its benefit. Labour sits in store operations and shrink sits in cost of goods, and a decision made on the first line will be reversed when the second line reports, which is roughly what happened here.
