The Carter Center announced on 18 February that the former president had decided to spend his remaining time at home in Plains, Georgia, under hospice care rather than continue to receive hospital treatment. He was ninety-eight, the oldest former president in the country's history, and he had been in declining health for several months. The statement asked for privacy and said that the family was grateful for the support it had received.

His doctors described the decision as a choice about the kind of care rather than about its quantity. Hospice in the American system means comfort measures and the withdrawal of curative treatment, and it is usually chosen when a patient's prognosis is measured in months. Carter had survived a brain cancer diagnosis in 2015 and a series of falls in 2019, and he had spoken publicly about his willingness to accept the limits of his age.

His presidency lasted one term and is remembered for its failures and for its aftermath. He took office in 1977 after Watergate, brokered the Camp David accords between Israel and Egypt, and lost in 1980 to Ronald Reagan amid inflation, an oil shock and the hostage crisis in Iran. He was defeated by a margin that his own party described as a rejection, and he spent the following year building the foundation that would define the rest of his life. He lost forty-four states in 1980, and the defeat was complete enough that his own party spent a decade pretending he had never been nominated.

The work after the presidency was the part that changed how he was judged. The Carter Center monitored elections in more than a hundred countries, led a campaign that reduced guinea worm disease from millions of cases to a few dozen, and built houses with Habitat for Humanity into his nineties. He won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2002, and he taught Sunday school in Plains until the year before his death.

Both parties responded with the kind of unanimity reserved for people who are leaving. Presidents of both parties, including the one who had defeated him, issued statements that described his integrity, and the tributes were notably free of the arguments that had attached to his administration. The country had spent forty years deciding what it thought of him, and the announcement suspended the argument for a few days. It was the longest retirement any president had been given, and he used most of it.

What the decision illustrated was a difference between two kinds of public life. The one that is measured in elections ended in 1981 and was judged harshly, and the one that is measured in institutions ran for four decades and was judged generously. Carter had said more than once that he had been a better ex-president than president, which was a sentence that his admirers quoted and his critics found convenient. The distinction mattered to him less than it mattered to the people who wrote about him, and he said so in interviews after he had stopped giving them.
