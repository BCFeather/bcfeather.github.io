Greece's parliament voted on the fifteenth of February to open civil marriage to same-sex couples, by 176 votes to 76, with two abstentions and forty-six members absent. The president signed the bill the following day. Greece is the first Orthodox Christian country to take the step and the sixteenth member of the European Union. Law gives same-sex couples the right to marry and to adopt, and it does not extend surrogacy, which remains available only to heterosexual couples and only under medical supervision. Debate in the chamber lasted two days and crossed party lines in both directions.

Measure was carried by a coalition of the prime minister's New Democracy party and the opposition Syriza and PASOK parties, and it was opposed by a large part of New Democracy's own parliamentary group. Kyriakos Mitsotakis, who had promised the reform during his 2023 campaign, allowed his members a free vote, which is unusual on a government bill. The result was that the bill passed with more votes from the opposition than from his own party, and the political cost was absorbed by the prime minister himself rather than by the party.

Orthodox Church of Greece opposed the bill and issued a statement describing it as a step that would damage the family, and the Church's leader asked the government to withdraw it. Opposition inside the church was loud and opposition in the country was quiet, which is the pattern of most social reforms in Greece. Polling before the vote showed a narrow majority in favour and a large minority strongly against, and the issue did not dominate the campaign or the news for more than a week. What the debate did produce was a large demonstration in Athens on the day of the vote, and a smaller one of supporters.

For the couples who will marry under the law, the change is practical rather than symbolic. Until February, a Greek same-sex couple could not register a marriage, could not be recognized as a family in dealings with the state, and could not adopt jointly, although single people could adopt. All three change with the new law, which also sets out parental rights for children already being raised by same-sex couples, the part that the courts had been dealing with case by case for years. First marriages are expected in the spring, after the civil registry procedures are adjusted.

Regional significance is what the government emphasized. Greece is the first country in south-eastern Europe to legislate on the question, and the government presented the vote as evidence that the country is a modern European state rather than a Balkan one with an Orthodox inheritance. That framing is political, and it is also accurate about the direction of travel: of the sixteen EU members that now recognize same-sex marriage, eight joined the union after 2004, and most of the recent moves have come from the east and the south rather than from the west.

Implementation is now the test. The law requires the civil registry to record marriages without a distinction between couples, and it requires courts to treat existing families equally in custody and inheritance cases. Lawyers have said the practical work will take months, and the first test cases will probably involve couples who married abroad and who now want their union recognized at home. Conservative members of the government have said they will not support further changes, which sets a boundary that the next debate will run into.
