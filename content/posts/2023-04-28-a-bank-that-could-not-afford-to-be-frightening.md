---

First Republic reported its first-quarter results on 24 April and the number that mattered was the deposit line. Customers had withdrawn 40.8 per cent of the bank's deposits since the end of December, leaving 104.5 billion dollars, and that figure included 30 billion that eleven of the largest American banks had placed with it in March as a rescue. Wall Street had expected about 145 billion, and the shortfall was read as evidence that the withdrawals continued after the rescue. The bank also reported that insured deposits had fallen less than uninsured ones, which told customers exactly which accounts to move.

Its problem was a balance sheet built for a different interest rate. It had lent long at low rates, mostly as jumbo mortgages to wealthy customers, and it funded those loans with deposits that were insured only up to 250,000 dollars. When rates rose, the loans lost value and the depositors who could leave did leave, and the two facts reinforced each other in a way that no announcement could interrupt.

Management spent the week trying to describe a recovery. Executives said that deposits had stabilized in April, that they were reducing borrowings from the Federal Reserve and that they would sell assets and cut staff. The bank had borrowed from the discount window and from a new facility created in March, and the interest on those borrowings was higher than the yield on its loans, which meant that survival would cost money every quarter. The gap between the two rates was about two percentage points, which on a hundred billion dollars is two billion a year.

The share price published the arithmetic. The stock had lost most of its value since the start of March, and it fell further in the days after the report, at one point by more than a third in a single session. Analysts who had covered the bank for years wrote notes saying that the franchise was intact and that the funding was the problem, which is a distinction that does not help a bank whose business is funding.

Regulators had been watching since March. The Federal Reserve's financial stability report, published in May, described the outflow between 10 and 16 March as a run and noted that the equity price fell further after the earnings call. The bank's advisers had been looking for a buyer since the middle of April, and the buyers wanted a guarantee that the government would absorb the losses on the loan book.

The end came on 1 May, when the California regulator took possession and sold the bank to JPMorgan, the largest American bank, in a transaction arranged over a weekend. First Republic had 229 billion dollars of assets, the second largest failure in the country's history, and it failed eleven days after telling investors that its deposits had stabilized. The month of April is the record of how a bank that was too large to fail quietly was allowed to fail loudly instead.
