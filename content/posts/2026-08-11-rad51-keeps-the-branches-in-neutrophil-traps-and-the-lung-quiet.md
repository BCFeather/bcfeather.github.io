Neutrophils defend tissue by releasing web-like structures of chromatin and antimicrobial protein, and those webs are useful precisely because they stay where they were cast. Their chromatin is branched and dense, a conformation that holds the fibres together and keeps the inflammatory cargo local. What maintains that branching, and what happens when it fails, is a question about structure as much as about immunity. A trap is a physical object with a shape, and the shape determines whether the antimicrobial proteins inside it meet a pathogen or a plasma enzyme.

RAD51 is the recombinase that mediates strand exchange during homologous repair, generating branched DNA intermediates called Holliday junctions. A team writing in *Science* in August asked whether the same activity shapes the chromatin of a neutrophil trap, using imaging, functional assays, inflammatory profiling in experimental models and samples from patients with fungal airway disease. The choice of assays matters here, because junction-resolving enzymes cut specific structures, so sensitivity to GEN1 and RuvC is evidence about what the fibres actually contain.

Knocking down RAD51 in human and mouse neutrophils produced traps with fewer branches, and those traps were unstable, breaking up faster and proving more susceptible to plasma endonucleases and to the junction-resolving enzymes GEN1 and RuvC. Different inducers raised RAD51 to different degrees, which implies the stability of a trap is tuned by the signal that provoked it rather than fixed by the cell type. Measuring RAD51 across inducers is the experiment that turns a knockdown result into a mechanism, since the same protein rising with the stimulus implies a regulated step. The same branching is also what makes the structure visible under a microscope, since a branched network scatters differently from a straight fibre.

The prediction the authors tested was that destabilizing the traps would reduce inflammation, since the pathogen would no longer be caught in a persistent web. The opposite happened. During challenge with *Aspergillus fumigatus*, the unstable traps were cleared quickly and the chromatin fragments accumulated in circulation, where they activated monocytes and induced interleukin-6.

That shift reorganized the response rather than dimming it. Pulmonary cytokines such as interleukin-1 beta and interleukin-5 fell, while systemic interleukin-6 rose, and the result was stronger Th2 and Th17 inflammation, eosinophilia, more mucus production and worse airflow obstruction. Plasma from patients with aspergillosis showed the same pattern, with cell-free DNA elevated and correlating with interleukin-6. Whether the circulating chromatin is a marker or a cause is the next experiment, and blocking interleukin-6 would separate the two. A follow-up that tracks chromatin fragments in patients would test whether the mechanism operates at clinical scale rather than only in a challenge model.

Structure, on this evidence, is a containment mechanism. The therapeutic implication runs against the intuition that dissolving a trap is anti-inflammatory, and any drug that targets NET stability would have to be tested for where the debris goes rather than only for how much inflammation remains in the lung. A trap that dissolves is not a trap that has been switched off, which is the confusion the paper is trying to name.
