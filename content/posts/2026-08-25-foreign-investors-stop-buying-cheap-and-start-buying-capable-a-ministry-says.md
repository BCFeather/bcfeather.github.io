The Ministry of Commerce used a press conference on 25 August to describe what it calls three changes in the way foreign companies invest in China. The first is a shift from a cost advantage to a value advantage, and the second moves from filling gaps in a supply chain to building particular strengths. The third moves from operating alone to developing products and markets with Chinese partners. The ministry packaged the observation as a compliment to Chinese industry and as an invitation to foreign firms, and both readings are accurate. Officials also used the occasion to preview the next round of measures on services and on pilot free-trade zones, without giving a timetable.

Bureaucratic phrasing aside, the observation matters more than the words. Companies that once came for cheap labour now come for engineering talent, suppliers that can iterate quickly and a market large enough to justify designing a product for it. That is a different kind of investment, and it treats China as a place to make things well rather than a place to make things cheaply. Wage growth has eroded the old advantage for labour-intensive assembly, and the companies that have stayed are the ones that found something else to compete on. A firm that builds a research centre in Suzhou is making a bet on engineers, and that bet is harder to reverse than a lease on a warehouse.

The second shift shows up in the sectors that attract the most capital this year: electric vehicles, batteries, medical devices, chemicals and industrial software. A factory that only assembles imports is less interesting to a foreign firm than a research centre attached to a Chinese supply chain that can build the whole product within a hundred kilometres. Medical devices and industrial software attract capital because the domestic market is large and the regulatory pathway, while slow, is now familiar. Chemicals illustrate the second shift best: a foreign group that once imported a compound now builds a plant next to a Chinese partner's downstream units.

A third shift changes the politics. Joint development means joint intellectual property, joint risk and joint access to third markets, and foreign firms have negotiated those arrangements with growing sophistication since the negative list for manufacturing was shortened. Chinese officials describe the pattern as mutual benefit; foreign executives describe it as the price of entry, and both descriptions fit. Joint ventures of this kind have produced products that neither parent could have sold alone, and they have also produced disputes over who owns the resulting patents. The shortened negative list for manufacturing removed formal barriers, and the informal ones have proved more durable.

The ministry also noted the services sector and the pilot free-trade zones as the areas where foreign money is growing fastest. Services were the weak spot in the old model, because they need lawyers, accountants and data flows rather than land and electricity, and liberalization there has been slower than in manufacturing. Services liberalization has advanced in the pilot zones faster than nationally, which gives foreign firms a map of where to test a model before scaling it. Data rules remain the binding constraint for anything involving customer records, and the ministry's own officials acknowledge that the negotiations on them are slow.

Whether the description survives a difficult autumn is the open question. Investment decisions run on a ten-year view, and tariff schedules run on a two-year cycle, and a firm that commits to a research centre this year will be reading next year's trade news with more than usual interest. A firm that commits capital this year will be reading next year's tariff news, next year's election results and next year's security reviews at the same time. The three shifts describe a relationship that has become more technological and therefore more political, which is why the ministry's language is studied abroad.
