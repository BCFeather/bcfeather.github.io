title: "Biodiversity turns over faster when the temperature moves"
date: "2025-02-27T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A global analysis of 42,225 community time series in *Nature* finds that sites which warmed or cooled quickly changed their species composition faster, with turnover in some terrestrial communities reaching 48 per cent a decade."
---

Ecologists describe change in a community in two ways. The first is the number of species, which is what a red list counts; the second is the composition, which is which species are present. A site can keep its species count while losing half of its inhabitants to others, and the second measure is the one that captures the rearrangement that warming produces.

The analysis assembled time series of species abundances from sites across the world, from plots in forests and grasslands to sampling stations in lakes and oceans, and paired each series with the temperature record for its location. The total of 42,225 series is the largest collection of its kind, and the breadth is the reason the result can be stated as a global pattern rather than as a collection of local observations.

Where the temperature moved quickly, in either direction, the composition changed quickly. The relationship held for terrestrial and for marine communities, and the rates were highest in the terrestrial ones, where the fastest sites turned over nearly half of their species in a decade. That figure is the one that matters for conservation, because a community that replaces half of its members in ten years is a community whose functions are being rebuilt continuously.

Cooling produced turnover as well. The intuition that warming is the driver is natural, because the climate is warming, but the data show that the rate depends on the speed of the change, not on its direction, and a site that cooled quickly rearranged as thoroughly as one that warmed. The mechanism is the same: a shift in temperature moves the competitive balance, and the species that gain are the ones whose tolerances fit the new regime.

Turnover is not extinction, and the paper is careful about the difference. A species that leaves a plot has moved and has not vanished, and the global count can be stable while the local composition is transformed. The consequence is that a protected area designed around a species list may fail to protect the species in it, because the list will not be the list in twenty years.

The pattern also gives a way to forecast. A site whose temperature is projected to move quickly is a site where the composition will change quickly, and the projection can be made from the climate model without waiting for the turnover to happen. That is the kind of result that a manager can use, and the resolution of the data is coarse enough that the forecast is regional, not local.

What the paper cannot say is which species will leave. Turnover is an aggregate, and the identity of the winners and losers depends on traits, on dispersal and on the connectivity of the landscape. The next step for the field is to pair the aggregate record with trait data, which is what a set of follow-up analyses is already doing.
