On 9 September, Apple held its annual launch event and announced the iPhone 16, and the most striking thing about the presentation was how little of it was about the phone. Hardware was there in the usual way. Two new chips, larger screens on the Pro models, a camera-control button on the side. But the event's centre of gravity was unmistakably software, specifically Apple Intelligence, the company's suite of generative-AI features, which will arrive as a free update in October and which the presentation treated, without apology, as the reason to buy the new phone at all. It was the first time in years that Apple has sold a phone as a vessel for something else.

Feature set is carefully chosen to be legible. Writing tools that rewrite and summarize text across the system; notification summaries that compress the day's interruptions; a redesigned Siri that draws on personal context and can move between apps; an image playground for generating pictures; and a system-wide integration that lets the software see, in a controlled way, what is on the screen. None of this is revolutionary in the abstract, and most of it has been previewed by competitors, but Apple's version carries the two properties the company has always sold: integration, the sense that the features are native to the device rather than bolted on, and privacy, the promise that processing happens on the device or in Apple's private-cloud servers, where data is not stored or used to train models. Promise matters, because it is the one argument Apple can make that the rest of the industry cannot.

Timing is the story beneath the story. Apple is late to the generative-AI party, by its own standards of punctuality: the competitors shipped their assistants and their chatbots a year and more ago, and the market's early judgement was that Apple had missed the wave. An answer arrived in the form of a claim about what the wave actually is. Intelligence is woven into the operating system rather than bolted on as a chatbot, which is a game Apple has never lost. Claim is partly marketing. It is not only marketing, and the difference shows in details the company treats as its brand, the on-device models, the permission prompts, the refusal to ship features before they are ready.

Harder questions sit beside the launch, mostly unasked. Apple Intelligence will not be available, at launch, in the European Union or in China, and the reasons, the DMA's interoperability demands on the one hand and the regulatory requirements of Beijing on the other, are the clearest demonstrations yet that the era of the global phone, the same device sold everywhere with the same software, is ending. iPhone has become a device whose capabilities vary by jurisdiction. Apple built its fortune on a single product for a single world and is now asked to make a product that differs everywhere. How that reshapes the company's economics is a question the launch event did not touch.

What the event did establish is that Apple has chosen its answer to the moment. A company that was famously reluctant to admit the existence of artificial intelligence has made AI the entire reason to upgrade. The stake is the whole ecosystem, repositioned as the surface on which the intelligence runs. Failure is possible if the features disappoint or the rollout stumbles. Success is possible if the integration story holds and the privacy argument draws the users it is aimed at. Either way, the event marked a change in what Apple believes it sells: not devices, but intelligence, delivered through devices. Hardware was announced on 9 September; the argument was about the decade.
