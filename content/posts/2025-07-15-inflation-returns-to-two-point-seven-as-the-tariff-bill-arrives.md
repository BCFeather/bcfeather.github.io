A tariff is a tax on an import and a price on a shelf, and the gap between those two things is the interval that June's inflation report measured. Consumer prices rose 0.3 per cent on the month and 2.7 per cent on the year, up from 2.4 per cent, and core prices excluding food and energy rose 2.9 per cent, which is the first monthly print in which the higher duties appear in the goods that carry them.

Goods did the work. Core goods prices rose after months of decline, and the categories that moved were the ones with a Chinese or a Vietnamese supply chain: furniture, apparel, toys, small appliances and the parts that go into them. A category that sat still for a year and then rises in the month the duties took effect is a category where the pass-through is visible and not yet complete, and where the next two prints will show whether the first instalment was a quarter of the bill or half of it.

Shelter stayed calm. Rent and owners' equivalent rent rose modestly, and the services measure that the Federal Reserve watches for domestication rose in line with the spring, which means the report shows a goods shock and not a broad one. A shock confined to goods is a shock the central bank can look through, and a shock that reaches services is one it cannot, which is why the services line matters more than the headline in every report this autumn.

Energy was the other half. Petrol prices fell through June as crude retreated from the spring peak, and that subtraction is what kept the headline at 2.7 rather than above three, which is why a report with a traded-goods core and a market-driven energy line can look mild at the top and firm underneath.

Base effects are the arithmetic that will decide the summer. The comparison is against a low month in 2024, so a monthly rate of 0.3 per cent now translates into a higher annual rate in the autumn even if nothing accelerates, and the committee knows that a tariff and a base effect can produce a 3 per cent print without any wage-price spiral, which is a situation that the textbooks do not describe well and that the press will describe badly.

Firms have told surveyors that they intend to pass the duties on over two or three quarters and not in one jump, because a single jump invites a customer to look elsewhere and a gradual one does not, and that intention is why the goods line should keep rising into the autumn while the annual rate tells only part of the story.

What would settle the question is whether the services measure stays near its current pace for two more quarters while goods keep climbing. A services rate that holds and a goods rate that rises is a tariff story, and a services rate that accelerates is the story that forces the committee to choose between its employment mandate and its inflation one.
