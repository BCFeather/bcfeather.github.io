description: "A comparison across primate genomes finds incomplete lineage sorting so widespread that the branching tree cannot represent the order's early history."
---

A gene tree and a species tree are supposed to agree, and where they disagree the cause is usually incomplete lineage sorting: an ancestral polymorphism that persists through a speciation event and is resolved differently in each descendant.

In *Science* on 2 June 2023, the extent of that process is measured. The authors compare gene trees across the primate genomes, count the regions whose history contradicts the species tree, and estimate the ancestral population sizes that would produce the observed discordance.

Amount is the finding. Discordance is common rather than exceptional, and the order's early branches are where it is most extensive. Cause lies in population size, because a large ancestral population maintains diversity for longer, and longer persistence makes a speciation event more likely to split a polymorphism than to fix it.

Time adds the other factor. Two speciation events close together leave little time for sorting, and the interval between the primate order's early splits was short, which is the condition that produces this kind of tangle. History supplies the unit of work, since a gene tree inferred from many genes is a set of histories, and each one is evidence about the population that contained it.

Selection adds a second result. Some discordant regions are not random, and the authors identify genes whose histories suggest selection rather than drift kept an allele alive across a speciation. Hybridisation raises a follow-on question, because discordance can also come from gene flow after speciation, and separating the two causes is what makes the sorting estimate meaningful. Method carries a warning. A phylogeny built from one gene can be that gene's history rather than the species', and a concatenated analysis can hide the discordance measured here. Coverage constrains it, since a genome-wide estimate needs many genes from many species, and the primate collection provides the breadth.

Inference sets the limitation, because ancestral population size is estimated from discordance and the estimate depends on assumptions about recombination and generation time. Functional consequence stays unshown, as a discordant gene is one whose history is complicated, and whether that matters for the animal is a case-by-case question. Systematics gains honesty about the tree, since a tree is a summary and a summary hiding the sorting misleads about how species form. Conservation reads it indirectly, because a species carrying a deep polymorphism holds diversity older than itself, and that is what a breeding programme should protect. Teaching needs a different diagram, since a single line per branch is a simplification students take literally, and dashed lines for discordant regions would be truer. A simulation would help here, since the expected discordance under a given history can be computed, and the observed amount can then be compared with what the model predicts rather than with a rule of thumb. A dated fossil calibration would tie the short interval to a number, and the primate radiation's timing is the parameter the analysis borrows rather than measures.
