description: "A comparison across primate genomes finds incomplete lineage sorting so widespread that the branching tree cannot represent the order's early history, according to work published in *Science* on 2 June 2023."
---

A gene tree and a species tree are supposed to agree, and where they disagree the cause is usually incomplete lineage sorting: an ancestral polymorphism that persists through a speciation event and is resolved differently in each descendant.

The analysis in *Science*, 2 June 2023 measures how much of that has happened. The authors compare gene trees across the primate genomes, count the regions whose history contradicts the species tree, and estimate the ancestral population sizes that would produce the observed discordance.

The amount is the finding. Discordance is common rather than exceptional, and the primate order's early branches are the regions where it is most extensive.

The cause is the population size. A large ancestral population maintains diversity for longer, and a longer persistence means that a speciation event is more likely to split a polymorphism rather than to fix it.

The time is the other factor. Two speciation events close together leave little time for sorting, and the interval between the primate order's early splits was short.

The selection signature is the second result. Some discordant regions are not random, and the authors identify genes whose histories suggest that selection, rather than drift, kept an allele alive across a speciation.

The methodological consequence is a warning. A phylogeny built from one gene can be a gene's history rather than a species', and a concatenated analysis can hide the discordance that the paper measures.

The conservation reading is indirect. A species whose genome carries a deep polymorphism is a species whose diversity is older than the species itself, and that diversity is what a breeding programme should protect.

The limitation is the inference. The ancestral population size is estimated from the discordance, and the estimate depends on assumptions about recombination and about generation time.

What the paper does not show is a functional consequence. A discordant gene is a gene whose history is complicated, and whether the complication matters for the animal's biology is a case-by-case question.

For systematics, the value is honesty about the tree. A tree is a summary, and a summary that hides the sorting is a summary that misleads about how species form.

The gene's own history is the unit the authors work with. A gene tree inferred from many genes is a set of histories, and the paper's approach treats each one as evidence about the population that contained it.

The speciation interval is the key parameter. A short interval between splits is the condition that produces discordance, and the primate order's radiation is a case where the interval was short.

The hybridization question follows. Discordance can also come from gene flow after speciation, and the authors' separation of the two causes is what makes the estimate of sorting meaningful.

The sample's coverage is the constraint. A genome-wide estimate needs many genes from many species, and the primate collection provides the breadth that the analysis requires.

The teaching consequence is a change of diagram. A tree with a single line per branch is a simplification that a student takes literally, and a diagram with dashed lines for discordant regions would be more honest.
