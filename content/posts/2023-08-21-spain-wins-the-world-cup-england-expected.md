Olga Carmona scored in the twenty-ninth minute, running onto a pass from the left and striking low across the goalkeeper, and Spain held the lead for an hour in front of 75,784 people in Stadium Australia, a record for a football match in the country. England pressed, hit the crossbar through Lauren Hemp, and had a penalty saved after a video review in the second half. The final whistle made Spain world champions for the first time, eight years after the country's women's team played its first knockout match at a major tournament and sixteen years after the federation began paying its players a salary.

The victory belongs to a generation that learned the game inside Barcelona's academy. Seven of the starting eleven came through La Masia or the club's women's structure, and the passing style that carried them through the tournament is the same style the men's team has tried to sustain for fifteen years. Aitana Bonmati, who won the Golden Ball, is twenty-five and has spent her career at Barcelona, and the coach, Jorge Vilda, kept his job through a year in which fifteen players asked not to be called up.

England's tournament was a triumph until the last hour, and it was the country's first final in the competition's history. The team reached the final without losing a match, beat Australia in the semi-final in front of a home crowd in Sydney, and drew audiences at home that broke records for women's football. Sarina Wiegman, who had won the European Championship a year earlier, made changes in the final that did not work, and the players who might have changed the game came on too late. The country had spent a month preparing a celebration that it now had to postpone, and the newspapers that had commissioned victory editions printed them anyway.

The aftermath was dominated by a man who did not play. Luis Rubiales, the president of the Spanish federation, kissed the forward Jenni Hermoso on the lips during the medal ceremony, and she said afterwards that she had not consented. He refused to resign, told a meeting of the federation that he would fight, and was suspended by FIFA at the end of the month pending an investigation. Several players said they would not play while he remained in post.

Money and attention followed the football, as they always do after a tournament that people watch. FIFA reported record ticket sales and broadcast audiences, and sponsors who had ignored the women's game for years announced new deals within days. The tournament also showed how thin the structures beneath the top teams remain: several squads trained on borrowed facilities, and federations in Africa and Latin America complained about funding before the first whistle.

Spain won a World Cup with a squad that had spent the previous year in conflict with its own federation, and the celebration was interrupted by its president. The football was the best the tournament has seen; the argument that followed it will decide whether the next one is better funded.
