description: "Drugs that clear senescent cells have moved into trials for specific diseases, and the broader claim about ageing is still a claim about a mechanism."
---

A senescent cell is a cell that has stopped dividing and has not died, and it releases a mixture of inflammatory signals that damage the tissue around it. The accumulation of those cells with age is one of the hallmarks that ageing research has organized itself around, and the hypothesis that follows is that removing them would restore some function to the tissue. A cell that has been damaged or has divided too many times enters the state, and the state is a program rather than a failure.

Drugs that do it are called senolytics, and the first combination was found by screening for compounds that kill senescent cells and spare normal ones. Dasatinib and quercetin was the first pair tested in humans, in a small trial of patients with diabetic kidney disease, and the results were modest and the trial was short. A first trial of a combination in humans enrolled fourteen patients, which is enough to establish safety and not efficacy.

Clinical strategy has shifted from ageing to disease. A regulator will not approve a drug for ageing, because ageing is not a disease, and a trial that measures a functional endpoint in a specific condition is a trial a regulator can assess. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, osteoarthritis and frailty are the conditions where the trials have been run, and the endpoints are walk distance and lung function. A trial in fibrosis measures lung capacity, and a trial in osteoarthritis measures pain, and neither is a measure of ageing. A trial that shows a functional gain in one organ is a trial that supports a hypothesis about one organ.

Measurement is the hardest part. A senolytic that works will reduce the number of senescent cells, and there is no reliable blood test for that number in a living patient. The trials therefore rely on downstream markers of inflammation, and a marker that falls is not proof that the mechanism worked. A biopsy of skin or of fat can be stained for markers, and a biopsy is not a diagnostic test.

Risk is real. A cell that has stopped dividing is a cell that cannot become a tumor, and a drug that kills senescent cells may remove a safeguard. The trials have reported manageable toxicity, and the long-term question is what happens when a patient takes a senolytic for years. A drug that clears senescent cells in a mouse extends lifespan in some models and does nothing in others.

What the field has is a mechanism with a plausible story and a set of small trials. A mechanism that explains ageing is not the same as a therapy that reverses it, and the gap between the two is measured in decades of trials rather than in publications. A therapy that works for one disease of ageing would be a therapy that regulators can approve, and that is the route the field is taking.
