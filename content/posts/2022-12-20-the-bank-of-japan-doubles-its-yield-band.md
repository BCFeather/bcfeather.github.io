The decision came at the end of a two-day meeting that most analysts had expected to pass without a change. The policy rate stayed at minus one tenth of one per cent, and the bank kept buying bonds, so the headline setting did not move. What changed was the band around the ten-year yield, which widened from a quarter of a percentage point to half of one. The bank described it as an improvement in the way the policy works rather than a tightening, and the markets read it as a tightening anyway. Officials had said for months that the band was a tool rather than a target, and the statement repeated that phrase verbatim.

Reaction was violent by the standards of a currency market. The yen, which had been falling all year, strengthened by several per cent within minutes of the announcement. The ten-year yield rose towards the top of the new band, and the bank had to buy bonds on both of the following days to keep it there. Japanese equities fell, and the shares of the banks rose, which is the pattern that appears when a long period of low rates is expected to end.

Governor Haruhiko Kuroda spent the press conference explaining that the change was technical. He said the wider band would make the bank's bond purchases more effective, and that the policy of low rates remained in place. Reporters asked whether the bank was preparing to raise rates, and he denied it, in a sentence that the market had heard before. His term was due to end in the spring, and the question of who would replace him became part of the story by that evening.

Behind the decision lay a year of divergence. The Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank had raised rates at the fastest pace in decades, and the Bank of Japan had held its policy rate below zero. That gap pulled capital out of Japan and pushed the yen to its weakest level in thirty years, which raised the price of imported energy and food. Widening the band was a way of letting the currency find some support without abandoning the policy.

Households and companies had to work out what the move meant for them. Mortgages tied to short rates would not change, because the policy rate had not. Long-term borrowing costs would rise, and the government's own debt, which is the largest in the world relative to the size of the economy, would cost more to refinance. A tenth of a per cent on that stock is a large number, and the finance ministry knew it.

By the following week the yield had settled near the middle of the new band, and the currency had given back part of its gain. The bank had demonstrated that a band can be widened without a rate rise and that a market can be moved by a technical paragraph. What it had not demonstrated was how long it could keep buying bonds in a world where every other central bank had stopped.
