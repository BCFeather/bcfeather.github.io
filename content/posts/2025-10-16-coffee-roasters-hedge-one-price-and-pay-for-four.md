description: "A specialty roaster's margin depends less on the price of coffee than on the gap between the futures contract it hedges with and the physical beans it actually buys, and this year that gap widened enough to close two small roasters."
---

Roasters talk about the price of coffee as a single number, and their accounts use at least four. The futures contract, the physical differential, the freight and the currency each move, and a roaster who hedges only the first is exposed to the other three. This year's widening in the differential is the reason two small roasters have closed and one has been acquired.

Differential means the premium a physical lot commands over the futures price, and it reflects quality, origin and freight. For a washed arabica from one origin it rose from 18 cents a pound above the contract to 45, and the roaster's hedge left that increase uncovered.

Hedging mechanics explain why. A roaster buys futures to fix a cost and sells physical beans to roast, and the futures position covers the contract price. The differential is priced at the time of purchase, and a roaster with a fixed retail price absorbs it.

Currency is the second uncovered exposure. Beans are priced in dollars and a roaster in Europe pays in euros, and the euro's 8 per cent move over the year cost more than the differential for a roaster with a small volume.

Three things kept the surviving roasters alive. They raised retail prices by 6 per cent, they shortened their forward contracts from 12 months to 6, and they added a second origin to their blend so a single differential could not dominate.

Consumer response was inelastic at the low end. A 6 per cent rise on a 12-dollar bag did not change purchase frequency in the roaster's own data, though the mix shifted towards the cheaper single origins.

Scale is the structural reading. A roaster with 20 tonnes a year cannot hedge the differential without a counterparty, and the green coffee trade's smaller merchants have consolidated into three houses that can. That consolidation is what an input market looks like when volatility rises.

A roaster's exposure is not one price but four. The futures price is the smallest part: a green coffee's differential, the freight rate, the currency and the roasting margin each move independently, and a hedge on the futures contract leaves three of them open.

Contract terms transmit the risk. A retail buyer with a fixed shelf price and a 12-month contract passes the roaster's unhedged exposure back to the roaster, and a roaster that cannot renegotiate absorbs it. That is the mechanism by which a commodity's volatility reaches a supermarket's shelf.

A roaster's blend is the second exposure. A blend's recipe is a set of ratios between origins, and a shortage in one origin forces a substitution that changes the cup. A hedge on a futures contract protects the price of a commodity that the roaster may not be buying.

What a hedge actually covers is the argument this case makes. A futures contract fixes one of four prices, and a business that treats it as the whole cost is hedging its accounts rather than its risk.
