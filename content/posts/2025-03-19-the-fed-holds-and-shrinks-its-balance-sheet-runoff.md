description: "The Federal Reserve kept its policy rate at 4.25 to 4.5 per cent on 19 March and slowed the pace at which it is running down its bond holdings, a decision that separates the rate path from the balance sheet."

Committee members had expected to hold, and the question before the meeting was how to describe an economy in which unemployment was low, inflation was near 3 per cent and a trade policy was being written by the week. The statement that they issued said that uncertainty around the outlook had increased, a phrase that had not appeared in the previous one, and it said that the committee was attending to the risks on both sides of its mandate.

Forecasts in the summary of economic projections moved in a way that is hard to read. Growth for 2025 was cut to 1.7 per cent from 2.1, unemployment was raised to 4.4 per cent, and core inflation was raised to 2.8 per cent, a combination that describes an economy slowing while prices stay high. The median dot still showed two quarter-point cuts before the end of the year, which is fewer than the market had priced in January and more than the inflation forecast would justify on its own.

The balance sheet decision was technical and consequential. From April the committee would redeem at most 5 billion dollars of Treasury securities each month, down from 25 billion, while leaving the 35 billion cap on mortgage-backed securities untouched. The change is not a stimulus, because the holdings still shrink. It is a recognition that the Treasury market had become fragile enough that a large monthly redemption was a risk the committee did not want to keep taking.

Fragility is the right frame. A runoff that removes 25 billion a month from a market whose dealers hold limited inventory can produce the kind of spike in yields that the committee noticed in 2019 and again in 2024, and the repo market had shown signs of strain in the preceding weeks. Slowing the runoff is a way of keeping the balance sheet large without cutting rates, which is the option that a committee facing an inflation risk prefers.

The communication problem was the one that has dogged the institution for two years. A statement that mentions uncertainty and a projection that cuts growth while raising inflation describes stagflation without using the word, and the chair was asked repeatedly whether the committee would choose between its two mandates. His answer was that the committee sees no conflict at present because unemployment remains low, and that answer holds only while the labour market holds.

Markets took the combination as a modest dovish shift. Yields fell across the curve, equities rose and the dollar weakened, and the price of a cut by June moved above even. The move was small because the rate decision itself had not changed, and the reader should be careful about attributing it to the balance sheet when the projections had already softened the tone.

What followed in the spring tested the forecast. Growth slowed, the tariff measures raised goods prices, and the committee held again at each of its next meetings, which made the two cuts in the median dot a forecast that the year did not deliver. A projection is a statement about what the committee would do if its forecast were right, and 2025 was a year in which the forecast was mostly wrong.
