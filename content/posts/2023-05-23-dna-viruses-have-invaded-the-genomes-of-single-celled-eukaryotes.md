---

Viral sequences are a component of every genome, and the fraction varies. In animals, endogenous retroviruses are common and DNA viruses are rare, and the assumption has been that DNA viruses do not integrate efficiently.

The survey in *PNAS*, 23 May 2023 tests that assumption. The authors search the genomes of unicellular eukaryotes for sequences from DNA viruses and find them widely, in many lineages and in many copies.

The scale is the surprise. Integration is not an occasional accident; it is a recurring event, and the genomes of some organisms carry viral sequence in proportions that rival the transposon content of animals.

The mechanism is unclear. A DNA virus that integrates needs a way to insert into the host's genome, and the paper's analysis of the inserted sequences suggests that the viruses' own recombination machinery is involved.

The evolutionary consequence is a source of novelty. An integrated viral gene can be co-opted, and the survey identifies host genes with viral ancestry, which is evidence that the process has contributed to the hosts' biology.

The immunity consequence is the other side. An integrated sequence can act as a record of past infection, and some hosts use it to recognize the virus, a mechanism that the paper's data support.

The comparison with animal genomes is the frame. Animals carry few DNA virus insertions and many retroviral ones, and the difference suggests that the two host types have different defences or different exposures.

The limitation is the detection. A sequence that has diverged far from its viral ancestor is hard to recognize, and a survey that relies on similarity misses the oldest insertions.

What the paper does not establish is the frequency over time. A snapshot of extant genomes cannot show whether integration was more common in the past, and the phylogeny of the inserted sequences is the proxy.

For virology, the value is a shift in the host range of the phenomenon. Integration has been studied in animals and in plants, and the unicellular eukaryotes are a third case that changes the general picture.

The policy use is a warning. A lake that is shrinking is a lake whose water rights were allocated in a wetter decade, and a basin plan that assumes the old level is a plan that will fail.

The uncertainty is in the extrapolation. A satellite measures area and a model converts area to volume, and the conversion's error is the largest term in the estimate. The authors' validation against gauges is what bounds it.
The host range is broad. The survey covers algae, protozoa and fungi, and the presence of insertions across such distant relatives suggests that the phenomenon is ancient rather than recent.
The biotechnological reading is speculative. A virus that integrates at a defined site is a tool, and the survey's catalogue of insertion patterns is a list of candidates for a genome-editing system that nature has already built.
