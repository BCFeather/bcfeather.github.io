---

Viral sequences are a component of every genome, and the fraction varies. In animals, endogenous retroviruses are common and DNA viruses are rare, and the assumption has been that DNA viruses do not integrate efficiently.

In *PNAS* on 23 May 2023, that assumption is tested. The authors search the genomes of unicellular eukaryotes for sequences from DNA viruses and find them widely, in many lineages and in many copies.

Scale is the surprise. Integration is not an occasional accident but a recurring event, and the genomes of some organisms carry viral sequence in proportions rivaling the transposon content of animals. Host range is broad, since the survey covers algae, protozoa and fungi, and insertions appearing across such distant relatives suggest the phenomenon is ancient rather than recent.

Mechanism stays unclear. A DNA virus integrating needs a way into the host's genome, and the analysis of inserted sequences points to the viruses' own recombination machinery. Novelty follows from the evolution, because an integrated viral gene can be co-opted, and host genes with viral ancestry are evidence that the process has contributed to the hosts' biology.

Immunity is the other side. An integrated sequence can act as a record of past infection, and some hosts use it to recognize the virus, a mechanism the data support. Animals supply the frame of comparison, since they carry few DNA virus insertions and many retroviral ones, and the difference suggests two host types with different defences or different exposures.

Detection sets the limitation, because a sequence diverged far from its viral ancestor is hard to recognize, and a survey relying on similarity misses the oldest insertions. Frequency over time stays unestablished, since a snapshot of extant genomes cannot show whether integration was commoner in the past, and the phylogeny of the inserted sequences is only a proxy. Virology gains a shift in the phenomenon's host range, because integration has been studied in animals and plants and unicellular eukaryotes are a third case that changes the general picture. Biotechnology reads it speculatively, since a virus integrating at a defined site is a tool, and the catalogue of insertion patterns is a list of candidates for a genome-editing system nature has already built.

What the survey cannot do is date an insertion, and an undated insertion is a fact about the present rather than about the history that produced it. Dating would need a molecular clock that runs on the viral sequences themselves, and the divergence of the inserted copies is the only handle the data offer. A catalogue of insertions is also a catalogue of ancient infections, and an infection recorded in a genome is one that no museum specimen preserves. Whether the insertions are still active is the other open question, since a sequence that retains its recombination genes can keep moving and one that has lost them is a fossil. A host that keeps the sequence and uses it is a host that has turned an infection into a function.
