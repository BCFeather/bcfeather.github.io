Dravet syndrome begins in infancy with prolonged seizures and progresses to refractory epilepsy and developmental delay. The cause is usually a variant in *SCN1A*, which encodes a sodium channel, and the resulting defect is conventionally described as a loss of GABAergic interneuron function. The first years are exactly when the brain is assembling the circuits that seizures later disrupt. Most cases present before the first birthday, which puts the relevant developmental window before most families have a diagnosis.

A paper in *eLife* on 27 August 2024 asks what happens before that loss. The authors derived induced pluripotent stem cells from patients and from healthy donors and pushed both through GABAergic differentiation, sampling chromatin accessibility at day 0, day 19, day 35 and day 65. Chromatin is sampled because accessibility is the layer at which a developmental programme is scheduled.

The developmental trajectory differs. Dravet cells open chromatin earlier than control cells, and the difference is already visible at day 19, which places the abnormality early in the programme rather than at its end. Day 19 is early enough that the difference is regulatory rather than a downstream consequence of repeated seizures.

Acceleration is the pattern. A programme that runs ahead of schedule produces interneurons that may be present in the right number but assembled at the wrong time, and timing is what a developing circuit uses to establish connectivity. An interneuron that arrives early may find its niche unoccupied, and the connection it forms may not be the one the circuit needs. Whether the accelerated programme produces fewer interneurons, or merely differently connected ones, is not resolved by accessibility data alone.

Valproic acid, a standard anti-seizure drug, changes accessibility as well, and the effect depends on when the drug is applied. That finding turns a clinical question into a chromatin question, since the drug is usually given after seizures begin. A drug that changes accessibility has an epigenetic effect alongside its ion-channel effect, whether or not that was intended.

The study does not link the accessibility changes to a specific seizure phenotype, and iPSC-derived interneurons are not the same as those in a child's brain. The four time points give a trajectory rather than a mechanism. Patient cells carry the variant but not the seizure history, so the model isolates the genetic part of the disease.

For therapy the implication is that an epigenetic intervention would have a window, and the window may close before the first seizure brings the child to a clinic. Screening for the chromatin signature earlier is the practical corollary. What the paper establishes is that Dravet is not only a channelopathy. The regulatory programme that builds the interneuron is itself altered, and that alteration precedes the functional defect. An intervention delivered at day 19 in a dish would correspond to a much earlier point in a pregnancy than any clinic could reach. That reconciliation, between a channel defect and a scheduling defect, is where the next experiments begin.
