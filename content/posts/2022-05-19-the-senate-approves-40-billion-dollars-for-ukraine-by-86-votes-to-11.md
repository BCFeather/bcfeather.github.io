Senators passed a forty billion dollar package of military, economic and humanitarian aid for Ukraine on 19 May, by 86 votes to 11, a week after the House had approved a slightly larger version. The bill provided 40.1 billion dollars, bringing the total authorized since February to about fifty-four billion, and the president signed it on 21 May in Seoul, at the start of a trip to Asia. Eleven Republican senators voted against it, and the rest of the party joined the Democratic majority.

Money in the bill is divided among purposes that do not run at the same speed. Nearly half is for weapons and ammunition, drawn from existing stocks and from contracts that take months or years to deliver, and a large block is economic support for a government whose budget deficit runs at about five billion dollars a month. The rest covers food aid, refugee programmes, intelligence sharing and the training of Ukrainian forces, and a slice pays for American troops deployed to allied countries on NATO's eastern flank. Part of that money replenishes stockpiles the Pentagon had already drawn down, which turns some of the aid into an order book for American factories.

Passage was delayed by matters that had nothing to do with Ukraine. The Senate spent a week on an immigration dispute over the pandemic-era public health order known as Title 42, which the administration wanted to end and which some senators wanted to keep, and the Ukraine package waited while that argument ran its course. A single senator, Rand Paul, held up the bill for a day demanding that an inspector general be appointed to audit the spending, and the leadership eventually accepted a version of that demand, adding a requirement that the inspector general report to Congress every quarter.

The size of the number invited a question about absorptive capacity. Ukraine's army was burning through artillery at a rate that Western factories could not match, its air force was flying Soviet-era aircraft, and the training of new brigades happened on allied bases outside the country. Money can buy shells, but it cannot make a production line move faster than its machine tools, and the Pentagon was already acknowledging that some categories of ammunition would take years to replenish.

Reaction from Moscow was contemptuous. The foreign ministry said the package would prolong the war and warned of consequences, and Russian state television described it as evidence that Washington was fighting to the last Ukrainian. That framing has its own logic: the aid does not change the balance of forces on any given day, but it determines whether Ukraine can keep fighting at all through the summer and beyond.

What the vote bought was time, and time is the one commodity a war of attrition consumes fastest. The bill's durability past November depended on a Congress that might change hands, and the administration's decision to sign it in Asia rather than in Washington was a reminder that the war had become one line item in a foreign policy that now spanned the Pacific as well.
