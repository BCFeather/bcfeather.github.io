---

Federal Reserve chair Jerome Powell used the annual symposium at Jackson Hole on 23 August to say that the time had come for policy to adjust, which was the clearest signal of a September cut that the committee had given. He did not say what size the reduction would be, and the markets spent the following week arguing about it. Two governors had already voted for a cut at the July meeting, which is a rare public split inside the committee.

His reasoning was about the balance of risks. Inflation had fallen to a rate close to the target by most measures, and the labour market had cooled from an overheated condition to a merely tight one, which means the cost of waiting had risen relative to the cost of moving early. A committee that shifts its attention from prices to employment is a committee that has decided the first problem is largely solved. A committee that had spent two years fighting inflation needed a reason to stop, and the reason was that wages had stopped accelerating.

Cooling is visible in the data. Hiring has slowed, the number of people quitting has fallen and the unemployed are taking longer to find work, and none of those measures has reached a level that suggests a recession. A labour market that is loosening without breaking is the outcome that a soft landing requires. Job openings have fallen from two for every unemployed person to a little over one, which is the measure the chair cites most often.

Speech was deliberately non-committal about the pace. A quarter point is the standard increment and a half point is what a committee does when it believes it is behind, and the chair said the decision would depend on the data that arrives before the meeting. A chair who leaves the size open is a chair who has not yet seen the payroll report. The payroll report due in early September would settle the argument, because a weak number would make a larger move the obvious choice.

Political setting was unavoidable. The symposium was held weeks before an election, and the committee's independence was being questioned by one of the candidates, which is a pressure that a central bank cannot acknowledge and cannot avoid. A decision taken in September will be described by both campaigns as political whatever its content. The candidate who appointed him had said that a cut before the election would be political, and the chair's answer was that the data sets the calendar.

What the speech establishes is that the direction is settled and the timing is not. A committee that has announced its intention is a committee that the market will hold to the promise, and the promise is now the thing that the next two reports will test. Expectations moved to a quarter point after the speech and back to a half point after the report, which is the volatility that an open question creates.
