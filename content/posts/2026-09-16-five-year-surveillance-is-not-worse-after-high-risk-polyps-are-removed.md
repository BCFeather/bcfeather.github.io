Colonoscopy programmes run on a schedule that nobody enjoys and everybody attempts to follow. Remove a polyp and the patient enters surveillance, with the next examination set by the size, number and pathology of what was taken out, and the interval is usually three years for anyone whose adenomas were large, numerous or villous. The schedule was built from observational data about how quickly cancers appear after polyps, and it has never been tested in a randomized trial of intervals, partly because the trial would need thousands of patients and a decade of patience.

EPoS II took on both costs. The trial randomized 10,799 patients with high-risk adenomas across eight European countries to surveillance colonoscopy at three years or at five, with the three-year group also examined at five, and the primary endpoint is the cumulative incidence of colorectal cancer at ten years. The interim analysis reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 16 September covers 5.5 years of follow-up, which is long enough to see whether the two schedules diverge.

They did not. Cumulative colorectal cancer incidence was 0.77 per cent in the five-year group and 0.82 per cent in the three-year group, a difference of minus 0.05 percentage points, and the upper boundary of the one-sided 99.12 per cent confidence interval was 0.68, inside the prespecified noninferiority margin of 0.7. Stage at diagnosis was similar in both arms, and five patients died of colorectal cancer, three in the five-year group and two in the three-year group.

The numbers are small enough that interpretation has to be careful. A difference of five cases per ten thousand is not evidence that the schedules are identical, and the confidence interval still permits a real if modest advantage for the shorter interval. What the trial shows is that any such advantage is smaller than the margin the designers judged clinically acceptable, which is a statement about policy and not about biology.

Intervals are also a resource question. Every examination removed from a patient's schedule frees capacity for someone who has never been screened, and programmes in most countries are constrained by the number of endoscopists and the number of slots. Moving high-risk patients from three years to five would release a substantial share of surveillance capacity, and the interim result suggests that release would cost almost nothing in cancer incidence.

Inverse probability weighting was used to account for patients who skipped their five-year examination, which is an admission that participation was incomplete. Surveillance depends on people attending, and a schedule that is easier to keep may produce better outcomes in practice than a denser schedule that patients avoid. The trial measures the effect of the offer and not the effect of the examination.

The final analysis at ten years will decide whether the interval can be widened with confidence, and the answer will depend on cancers that have not yet appeared. For now the trial gives programmes a defensible reason to relieve pressure on surveillance lists, and it gives patients one less appointment in a decade that is already full of them.
