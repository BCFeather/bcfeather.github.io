The new year shopping begins in the first fortnight of January, three weeks before the holiday, because the food that a family buys for the festival is the food that takes longest to make. The cured pork hangs in the doorway for twenty days, the salted fish takes a month, the rice cake is steamed and cooled and cut, and the sweets are bought in a box that will be opened on the first day of the year.

The market that sells it is a different place in January. The supermarket adds a corridor of gift boxes and the stalls outside sell the goods that the supermarket does not stock, the dried mushrooms from the mountains, the sausages from a particular town, the jujubes that a grandmother will inspect one by one. The trade is local and the reputation of a stall is the reputation of the family that has run it for twenty years.

The economics of the season are a mixture of the old and the new. The traditional market is still the place where a family buys the food, and the platforms have taken the gift boxes, the nuts and the alcohol, which are the items that a customer is willing to order online and carry to a relative. The two channels divide the shopping by what it is for, and the division has been stable for a decade.

The price of the goods rises in the week before the holiday, and the rise is a signal that the supply is local and the demand is fixed. A vegetable that travels from a greenhouse a thousand kilometres away is priced by the transport, and a sausage that is made in a village is priced by the number of pigs that the village had. The two prices move differently, and the family that shops early is the family that has understood this.

What the season means for the smaller producers is a month of income that carries the year. A workshop that makes a cured meat sells most of its output in three weeks, and the cash that arrives in January pays for the salt, the casings and the labour of the following spring. The new year is the harvest of a business that works all year for a single season.

The gift is the part of the shopping that has a grammar. A box of fruit, a bottle of oil, a pair of preserved ducks, the value of the gift is read by the receiver and the reading depends on the relationship. The gift that is too expensive embarrasses and the gift that is too cheap insults, and the range is narrower than a foreign observer would expect.

What the market shows is a festival that is still organized around food. The lights and the advertisements are modern, the shopping is done on a phone, and the centre of the holiday is a table that a family cooks for a week. The goods that are bought in January are the raw material for that table, and the market that sells them is the first place where the year turns.
