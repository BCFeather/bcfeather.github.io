description: "Playback experiments across three populations show giraffes in predator country stay vigilant longer after an oxpecker alarm call."

Animals listen to each other, and a species that lives among attentive neighbours can borrow their senses. Red-billed oxpeckers feed on the ticks and wounds of large mammals and call when something approaches, which makes them a candidate early-warning system for their hosts. Whether a giraffe acts on that call, and whether experience with predators changes the response, had not been tested with playback. Oxpeckers sit on the host for hours and call at anything unusual, which gives them a vantage point the host does not have.

*BMC Biology* published the experiment on 10 October 2025. The authors played oxpecker alarm calls to giraffes in three populations that differ in whether predators are present, and scored how long the animals stayed vigilant afterwards. The calls were also analysed acoustically, so the stimulus could be described as well as used. The three populations differed in predator presence, and playbacks were randomized within each group so that call order did not bias the response.

Response tracked risk. Giraffes in the reserve where predators live stayed vigilant longer than those in predator-free areas, which supports the idea that prior exposure sharpens the response rather than that the call works equally on naive animals. The acoustic analysis found low harmonic-to-noise ratios in the calls, the harsh broadband structure that is known to hold attention across species. That acoustic structure is what an alarm call shares with a scream, and it is the part of the signal that carries no information about the caller.

Two mechanisms could produce the pattern. One is learning, since an animal that has survived an ambush associates the call with danger. The other is a bias built into the signal, because broadband noise is hard to ignore and needs no learning to be salient. The experiment supports the first reading for the difference between populations and the second for the response itself. Experience and signal design are not exclusive explanations, and the observed pattern is consistent with both operating in the same animal.

Limits come from the design. Playback gives a call without a predator, so the behaviour measured is vigilance rather than escape, and the three populations are not replicates of one another. Group size and age structure are not summarized in the abstract, and habituation to repeated playbacks is a known confound that any such study has to control. Age structure matters because juveniles may not have learned the association, and a group of young animals would respond differently. Playback studies also differ in whether they mask the speaker's position.

Field tests can go further. Following individuals across seasons would show whether vigilance predicts survival, and playing calls to juveniles in both settings would separate learning from an innate bias. The result also bears on conservation, since a population that has lost its predators may lose the responsiveness that made the mutualism worth having. The response may be cheaper to preserve than to relearn, which is an argument for keeping predator populations where the mutualism still functions. That claim needs a test in a population that lost its predators recently.
