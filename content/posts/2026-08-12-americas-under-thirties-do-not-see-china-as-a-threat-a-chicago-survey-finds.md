The Chicago Council on Global Affairs published a report on 12 August with a title that reads like a provocation, saying that the generation born after 1996 does not worry about China. The finding rests on two surveys, one of 1,025 adults conducted with National Public Radio and Ipsos between 13 and 15 March, and another of 2,148 respondents carried out in 2025. Neither sample is large enough to settle a question of this size, and together they describe a direction of travel.

The headline number is striking. Fifty-nine per cent of Generation Z, and 58 per cent of millennials, said they favour cooperation and engagement with China. Older cohorts take a dimmer view, and the gap between the generations is wide enough to reshape the politics of the issue over a decade. Among the oldest respondents, the share favouring engagement falls well below half, and the share naming China as a critical threat rises. The report also breaks the answers down by party, and finds that young Republicans remain the most hawkish group in their own cohort, which complicates any simple story about a generation turning dove.

The report offers explanations that have little to do with geopolitics. Young Americans meet Chinese culture through social media, games and music in a way their parents did not, and their political attention is fixed on rents, student debt and the cost of living. A country that appears in a feed as a place where things are made and played differs from a country that appears in a briefing as a military threat. The researchers also note that younger voters came of age during a period when Washington's China policy produced few visible benefits for them. Respondents in that group were also more likely to say they had bought a Chinese-made product in the past year, which suggests that familiarity and trade have moved together.

The caveats matter as much as the finding. Public opinion does not set American policy on China; Congress, the Pentagon and the tariff bureaucracy do, and all three have moved in the other direction. An electorate that dislikes confrontation can still be governed by institutions that practise it, and the report's authors are careful to say that their data describe attitudes rather than voting behaviour.

Even so, the trend line is worth noting for both capitals. If the next cohort of American voters carries a cooler view of the competition, the political space for permanent confrontation narrows, and a future administration will find it easier to trade and harder to rally. Beijing has noticed, and its own outreach to American students and young professionals has grown in the past two years.

Surveys of this kind measure mood, not decisions. But a mood that has shifted this far among people who will be voting for the next forty years is a fact about the future, and both governments would be foolish to ignore it. The council has run this survey for years, and its own earlier reports show the shift beginning before the pandemic, when younger respondents first diverged from their parents on trade.
