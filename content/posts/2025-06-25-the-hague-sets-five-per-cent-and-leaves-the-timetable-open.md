A summit communique is usually a list of things that everyone has already agreed, and this one contained a number that nobody had agreed a year earlier. The allies meeting in The Hague on 24 and 25 June committed to investing 5 per cent of gross domestic product annually on defence and defence-related spending by 2035, which is two and a half times the target that had governed the alliance since 2014.

The split is the compromise. At least 3.5 per cent of GDP is to go to core defence requirements and to the capability targets that the alliance sets, and up to 1.5 per cent may be counted for infrastructure, networks, civil preparedness, innovation and the industrial base. A category that broad is a category that lets a finance ministry count a bridge.

The 2035 date is the part that critics seized. A pledge that takes a decade to fulfil is a pledge that two or three governments can outlive, and the summit text asks allies to submit annual plans showing a credible path, which is the mechanism that turns a headline into a budget line. Whether the plans are published is a national decision.

Spending has already risen. European allies and Canada increased defence expenditure by more than 90 billion dollars in 2021 prices in 2025, close to 139 billion in cash, an increase of nearly 20 per cent on the previous year, and most of that is procurement rather than payroll. A number that large is a number that the defence industry cannot absorb quickly.

Production is the binding constraint. Orders for artillery shells, air defence interceptors and drones have run into capacity that was allowed to shrink for thirty years, and a pledge of 5 per cent of GDP does not create a factory. The declaration says as much, and asks for cooperation on industrial capacity, which is the part that will take longest.

Ukraine was reaffirmed without a new commitment. The text repeats that Ukraine's future is in the alliance and that support will continue, and it does not offer a timetable or a membership invitation, which is the gap that the Ukrainian government has been pressing on since last year's summit in Washington.

What would settle the pledge is whether the 2026 plans show the first two points of the increase. A budget that moves from 2 to 2.4 per cent is evidence and a communique is not, and the parliaments that have to fund it meet before the next summit does. A pledge of this size is a fiscal decision disguised as a security one, and the finance ministries that have to fund it have not yet said which taxes will pay for it. Whether the promise survives the next recession is the test that nobody at the summit wanted to discuss, because a pledge made in 2025 is easiest to keep in a year when growth is paying for it.
