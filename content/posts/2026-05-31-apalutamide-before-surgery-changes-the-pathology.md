Surgery for high-risk prostate cancer removes the gland and then finds, in the pathology report, that disease had already spread beyond the capsule. Adjuvant radiotherapy and hormone therapy follow, but the operation has by then delivered its verdict without having changed the outcome. Neoadjuvant strategies have failed in this disease before, and the failures were usually explained by the trial being too short or the drug too weak rather than by the idea being wrong.

Perioperative androgen deprivation has been tried for decades with mixed results. The trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 31 May added apalutamide, an androgen receptor antagonist, to deprivation around radical prostatectomy in high-risk localized disease, and compared it with deprivation plus placebo. Apolutamide is already used in non-metastatic castration-resistant disease, and the trial moves it into an earlier, potentially curative setting.

Pathological complete response or minimal residual disease was 8.9 per cent with apalutamide against 1.0 per cent with placebo, an odds ratio of 10.17. That endpoint is a measure of what the drug does to the tumour before the knife arrives, and it is a proxy rather than a patient outcome, which is why the survival data carry the weight. A pathological response also tells the surgeon something the imaging did not, which is that the tumour was sensitive to androgen blockade and may respond again if it returns. The endpoint is also cheap to measure, which is why it became the first readout and why it should not become the last.

Metastasis-free survival at five years was 78.2 per cent against 73.5 per cent, a hazard ratio of 0.80. Event-free survival, time to first subsequent treatment and time to distant metastasis all favoured the apalutamide arm. The effect sizes are modest and consistent, which is what a perioperative strategy looks like when it works: a shift in the curve rather than a transformation of it.

Toxicity was higher. Grade 3 or 4 adverse events occurred in 39.6 per cent of the apalutamide group and 31.0 per cent of controls, which for a drug given around surgery means fatigue, rash and the metabolic effects of androgen suppression landing in the same weeks as recovery. The duration of treatment in the trial is the design variable that other groups will change first, since a shorter course around surgery would be easier to deliver and might preserve the benefit.

The question a multidisciplinary team will ask is who benefits enough to justify that. A pathological response in 9 per cent of patients who would otherwise have residual disease suggests a subgroup, and identifying it before surgery is the missing piece. Until a biomarker exists, the trial supports the strategy in high-risk disease as a whole, and leaves the individual decision to the conversation between urologist, radiation oncologist and patient. Shared decisions are where this evidence belongs.
