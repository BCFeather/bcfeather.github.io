---

Airline consolidation is usually explained by scale economies, and in this market the explanation is slots. The acquisition announced this month combines a regional carrier with 42 aircraft and a competitor with 180, on a set of routes where landing and take-off permissions at the main airport are the binding constraint.

Slot scarcity means a new entrant cannot start a route even if it wants to, because the airport has no capacity to allocate. The rules governing slots at congested airports allow an incumbent to retain them by using them 80 per cent of the time, which converts a permission into a quasi-property right.

That property right is what the buyer is paying for. The target's slot portfolio at the main airport is 38 pairs, and the purchase price is roughly 1.4 times the value of its aircraft fleet, which reflects slots rather than metal.

Competition review is the hurdle. The regulator will examine overlap on specific routes, and the remedy in previous cases has been a divestiture of slots to a new entrant. The buyer has pre-emptively offered to release eight slot pairs at the main airport, which is roughly the number that a review would require.

Consumer effects are ambiguous and measurable. Fares on routes where two carriers operate are typically 12 to 18 per cent higher than on routes with three, and the merged entity will operate as a duopoly on eleven of the target's routes. The regulator's model will estimate that effect and weigh it against the efficiency claim.

Employees are the other constituency. The two carriers have overlapping maintenance operations, and the buyer's stated synergy figure of 60 million euros implies about 400 job losses, mostly in engineering and ground handling. The regional carrier's staff are represented by a union with a consultation right.

Regional connectivity is the political question. A small market's secondary airports depend on a carrier willing to fly thin routes, and consolidation usually removes them because a larger airline's cost base cannot support a 40-seat aircraft. The buyer has committed to maintain six routes for three years, which is a short horizon against a route's usual life.

Slot reform is the structural fix that nobody has adopted. The rule that grandfathers a slot after 80 per cent use has been reviewed twice, and both reviews recommended a secondary market with a use-it-or-lose-it threshold. Airlines oppose it because slots are their balance-sheet assets, and airports oppose it because they collect the congestion rent.

Route-level data is what the review will turn on. The regulator publishes passenger numbers by city pair, and on four of the eleven overlap routes a single carrier already holds more than 60 per cent of seats, which is the threshold at which previous remedies were imposed.

For a reader, the pattern is that in slot-constrained markets, consolidation is a purchase of permissions rather than of businesses, and the regulation that matters is the slot rule. Where slots are auctioned or leased, entry is possible and consolidation is slower; where they are grandfathered, the incumbents trade among themselves.
