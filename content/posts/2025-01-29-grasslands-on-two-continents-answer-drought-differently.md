description: "A six-site experiment across the Eurasian steppe and the North American prairie finds that multi-year extreme drought reduces productivity on both continents but through different mechanisms, which matters for the models that project how much carbon a warmer world keeps."
---

Grasslands cover about 40 per cent of the land surface, and they hold a share of the world's soil carbon that is larger than the share held by forests. Their productivity depends on rain, and the rain is becoming less reliable in the two largest grass biomes of the Northern Hemisphere, the Eurasian steppe and the North American prairie.

A study in *Nature* on 29 January compared those biomes with an experiment rather than with a correlation. Researchers from 19 institutions in three countries built six monitoring stations, in Inner Mongolia, in the Xizang autonomous region and in the American west, and imposed multi-year extreme drought at each by excluding rainfall with shelters. The same treatment applied to different grasslands is the design that allows a comparison of mechanisms.

Productivity fell at every station under the drought treatment, and the size of the fall differed between continents and between grassland types. The Eurasian steppe lost less than the North American prairie in the first years of the treatment and recovered faster when the treatment ended, and the difference was associated with how the two floras allocate growth between roots and shoots.

Mechanism the authors identify is a difference in community composition. The Eurasian steppe is dominated by grasses with deep root systems and by species that can go dormant, and a drought that lasts a season leaves those species able to resume growth. The North American prairie contains more species with shallow roots and with annual life cycles, and a multi-year drought removes them from the community before the perennial grasses can compensate.

That distinction is what a model needs and what a rainfall record cannot supply. A model that treats grassland as one functional type with one sensitivity to precipitation will produce a carbon projection for the Northern Hemisphere that is wrong in one direction or the other depending on which biome the grid cell contains, and the six stations are the calibration that such a model requires.

The experiment also speaks to the limits of substitution. A station in Inner Mongolia and a station in Colorado are not replicates, and the authors are explicit that six sites cannot represent two biomes. What the design buys is a controlled comparison at each site, and what it cannot buy is the spatial coverage that a satellite product provides, which is why the paper's projections carry wide intervals and why the authors present them as a mechanism to be tested at larger scales.

Management implication is local. A grassland that loses productivity under a three-year drought is a grassland where grazing pressure has to fall in the third year, and a manager who reads the average sensitivity of the world's grasslands will set the stocking rate wrong. The paper's contribution is to say that the average is made of two different numbers, and that the managers who set stocking rates work with the one on their side of the continent.
