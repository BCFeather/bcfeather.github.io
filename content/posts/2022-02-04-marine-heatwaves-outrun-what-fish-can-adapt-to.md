Marine heatwaves are periods of abnormally warm surface water that last for days to months, and they have bleached corals, killed kelp and pushed fish out of their usual ranges. Whether such events will become more frequent is a question that global climate models have answered badly, because the models resolve the ocean at a scale too coarse for coastal features.

A study used an eddy-resolving Earth system model to simulate heatwaves over large marine ecosystems, the 66 coastal regions that cover about 22 per cent of the ocean and account for 95 per cent of fisheries catches. The analysis applied a future threshold, counting a heatwave as warming above the long-term mean rather than above a fixed baseline. It appeared in *Nature Climate Change*, 3 February 2022.

Under that definition, the intensity and the annual number of heatwave days over most large marine ecosystems remain higher than in the present climate, even after allowing organisms to adapt fully to the mean warming. Resolving mesoscale eddies produced more realistic heatwaves than coarse models, which had smoothed away the features that drive them. The future threshold is the methodological move that makes the result interpretable. A fish population may shift its tolerance as the average temperature rises, and the question of interest is whether the extremes will still exceed that shifted tolerance, which is what the threshold measures. Eddy resolution matters because heatwaves are shaped by ocean fronts, coastal currents and mesoscale structures that a 100-kilometre grid cannot represent. A model that misses a front also misses the pool of warm water that forms behind it, so its heatwaves are both weaker and less frequent than the observed ones. Fisheries management is the sector with the clearest exposure. A stock that is already fished at its limit has less capacity to absorb a heatwave, and the regions where heatwaves intensify most are not always the regions with the best-managed fisheries.
