Microsoft reported revenue of 61.9 billion dollars for the quarter to the end of March, up 17 per cent from a year earlier, and said its cloud platform had grown 31 per cent. The company also said that demand for the artificial intelligence services it sells was ahead of the capacity it had installed. The quarter was the first in which the company disclosed the revenue from the assistant it sells to businesses, and the figure was small relative to the total.

Capacity constraint is the theme. A customer who wants to run a model on rented computing has to be given a machine that is already built, and the company has been adding data centres as fast as it can obtain the chips and the electricity. A service that is sold out is a service whose growth is limited by construction rather than by demand. Electricity has become a constraint as well, and the company has said that some of its sites are waiting for a connection rather than for equipment.

Partnership is the arithmetic. The company has invested in a model developer and sells its output through its own cloud, and the arrangement gives it a product to sell before it has built one of its own. An investment in a supplier is a way of buying time that the balance sheet can afford. The relationship gives the company a share of the model developer's revenue and a seat on its board, which is an arrangement that regulators in two continents have begun to examine.

Productivity software is the annuity. The subscriptions that companies buy for email and documents renewed at higher prices, and the assistant that the company added to them is sold as an extra charge per user. A price rise on a product that nobody can remove is the most reliable revenue in the business. The subscriptions renew on annual terms, and a price increase that is announced a year ahead is one that customers accept because switching costs more than paying.

Capital spending is the bill. The company spent more than fourteen billion dollars on property and equipment in the quarter, and it said the figure would rise again, which is a commitment that the market has accepted so far. A cost that is guided upward while growth is visible is a cost that investors tolerate. The company has been signing leases for capacity that will not be ready for a year, which means the spending arrives before the revenue it is meant to serve.

What the quarter shows is that the resale of other companies' models can be a bigger business than building models, and the constraint on it is now the supply of machines rather than the supply of customers. Reselling a model is a business with thin margins and high volume, and the question is whether the company can keep the customers once they can buy the same model elsewhere.
