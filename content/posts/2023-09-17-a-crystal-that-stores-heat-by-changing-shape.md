A refrigerator removes heat by compressing a gas and letting it expand somewhere else, and the cycle has served for a century because the gas changes phase at a convenient temperature. A solid that changes phase under pressure can do the same work without a gas, and the class of solids that has the right properties is one in which the molecules are nearly spherical and begin to rotate at a temperature close to ambient.

The transition is a change of orientational order rather than a change of density. Below the transition the molecules sit on a lattice with their axes pointing in fixed directions, and above it they spin while staying on the same sites. The spinning costs entropy and absorbs latent heat, and the entropy change is large because the molecules have many more orientations available once they are free to rotate. A material that absorbs a kilojoule per kilogram per kelvin in a narrow temperature range is a material that can be used as a refrigerant.

Pressure shifts the transition because the ordered phase is denser than the disordered one. A compressive stress of a few hundred bars is enough to move the transition by tens of degrees, and the shift is what makes a cycle possible: the material is compressed at a temperature where it orders and releases heat, and the pressure is released at a temperature where it disorders and absorbs heat. The work done in compressing the material is the input, and the heat moved is the output.

The materials that work best are the plastic crystals of the neopentyl glycol family and the ammonium salts, and the reason is the shape of the molecule. A molecule that is close to a sphere can rotate without colliding with its neighbours, and a molecule with a long tail cannot. The same property that makes the rotation easy makes the crystal soft, and a soft crystal is one that flows under pressure and loses its order along the grain boundaries.

Engineering problems are the ones that any solid-state cooler faces. A device has to move the material between a hot and a cold end, or has to apply pressure to a bed of it in alternation, and either design adds losses that a gas cycle does not have. The temperature span that a single material covers is a few tens of degrees, which is enough for a domestic refrigerator and not enough for a freezer, and the span would have to be covered by a cascade of two or three materials. A domestic unit needs a span of forty degrees and a freezer needs seventy, so the cascade is not optional.

What the work establishes is the size of the effect and the shape of the phase diagram under pressure. Whether the effect survives ten million cycles is a question about the grains, and the answer will come from devices rather than from samples in a pressure cell. The samples that have been tested are a few grams, and the pressure cell that holds them is the part of the apparatus that has to be redesigned for anything larger.
