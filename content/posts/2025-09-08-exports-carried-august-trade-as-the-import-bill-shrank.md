China's customs administration published the August trade figures on 8 September, and the headline was steady. Goods trade for the month came to 3.87 trillion yuan, 3.5 per cent above the same month a year earlier, and the cumulative total for the first eight months reached 29.57 trillion yuan. The release separates goods from services, and the goods figure is the one that moves markets because it appears monthly on a fixed date. The cumulative figure includes both months and years, so the monthly number is the one to read.

Exports did the work. They rose 4.8 per cent to 2.30 trillion yuan while imports rose 1.7 per cent to 1.57 trillion yuan, so the surplus widened on the month. In dollars the figures were 321.81 billion for exports and 219.48 billion for imports, which is the same trade at an exchange rate near seven yuan to the dollar. In yuan terms the month's surplus came to 0.73 trillion, the arithmetic difference between the two figures.

The year-to-date picture is more one-sided than the month. Exports for January to August reached 17.61 trillion yuan, up 6.9 per cent, and imports came to 11.96 trillion yuan, down 1.2 per cent. A year in which exports grow and imports fall is a year in which the external balance absorbs the difference. The gap between the two growth rates is the year's story, and it reverses the pattern of 2024, when imports grew faster for much of the period. Prices explain part of the import fall, and the volume series tells the rest.

The import number needs care. A decline in value can come from lower prices, from lower volumes, or from both, and the monthly release does not separate them. Commodity prices fell through much of the period, which flatters the volume picture, and the customs release does not give the breakdown that would settle it. Customs publishes a volume series for selected commodities with a lag, and that release is where the price question gets answered.

Why it matters is arithmetic. Net exports contributed to growth in the first half, and a surplus that keeps widening adds to gross domestic product at the margin while adding to the trade friction that partners have already begun to legislate against. The August numbers keep that contribution in place instead of reducing it. Whether that contribution repeats in the second half depends on demand in the markets that buy Chinese goods, and those markets have been slowing.

Monthly customs data are noisy, subject to revision and distorted by the timing of holidays, and the Lunar New Year makes the January and February figures hard to read. The trend across eight months is firmer than any single month, and even that trend is a value measure in a year when prices moved. A three-month moving average smooths the holidays, and the direction it shows has been flat since the spring.
