Single-atom alloys place isolated reactive atoms in a host metal that does not bind well on its own, and the isolation is what makes each site identical. A paper in Nature Chemistry on 23 January 2024 reports a rule for how adsorbates bind to those sites. A counting rule is a compressed model, and compression is what makes a rule usable by people who do not run the calculation themselves. Stopping is what a screening pipeline needs a criterion for, and the criterion the paper supplies is an electron count. A rule that predicts geometry also predicts which adsorbate will displace which, and displacement is the competition that decides a reaction pathway.

Identity across sites is what makes a counting rule possible, since a distribution of sites cannot be described by a number. The rule counts electrons, and the count is the one organometallic chemistry uses for coordination. Organometallic counting assumes a discrete molecular orbital scheme, and a surface site supports that assumption only because the isolated atom is weakly coupled to the host. A criterion is the deliverable a computational paper can hand to an experimental group, and handing it over is how the loop closes. Competition between adsorbates is where selectivity is actually decided, and deciding it by counting is what makes the rule attractive.

Ten electrons is the number that fills the frontier orbitals of the site, and filling them is what determines the binding geometry. Geometry is what a catalyst needs to predict, and prediction is what a rule supplies. Weak coupling is the design feature of a single-atom alloy, and the feature is why the molecular analogy works at all. Attractive rules are adopted quickly and then tested hard, and testing hard is how their limits are found.

Adsorbates differ in how many electrons they contribute, and a molecule that contributes more shifts the site towards a different coordination. Shifting coordination is what changes the adsorption energy, and energy is what a microkinetic model needs. Adsorption energy is the quantity a catalyst screen needs, and a rule that gives it cheaply replaces a calculation per candidate.

Selectivity follows from geometry, since a site that binds one adsorbate weakly leaves another pathway open. Cheap screening is how a combinatorial search becomes feasible, and feasibility is the practical argument for the rule. A limit that is published is a limit that a later paper can move, and moving a limit is how a rule becomes a model.

An open pathway is a selectivity, and selectivity is the reason single-atom alloys attract interest. Host metals differ in how strongly they couple to the isolated atom, and coupling weakens the analogy at the strong end.

Transferability of the rule is the claim that matters, and a rule that works for several adsorbates on several hosts is one a screening study could use. A rule with a domain of validity is more useful than a rule without one, since a domain tells a user when to stop trusting it.
