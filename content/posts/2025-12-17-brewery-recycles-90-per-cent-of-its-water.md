Brewing is water-intensive in a way its product hides: a litre of beer takes four to six litres of water, most of it for cleaning. A brewery has brought its intake down by 90 per cent with a closed loop, and the report describes a plant where the constraint was the water bill rather than the engineering.

The loop treats the plant's effluent with a membrane bioreactor followed by reverse osmosis, and returns 70 per cent of the permeate to the cleaning circuit. The concentrate goes to the municipal sewer under a discharge permit.

Quality was the risk that had to be managed. Cleaning water with a high dissolved solid content leaves residue on a tank's surface, and the brewery's specification limits conductivity to 40 microsiemens. The reverse osmosis stage achieves 12.

Energy is the loop's cost. The treatment consumes 1.8 kilowatt-hours per cubic metre, against 0.3 for the municipal supply, and the plant's electricity bill rose 40,000 dollars a year while its water bill fell 190,000. Conductivity is the specification a brewery can measure continuously, and a probe in the return line is what makes the loop's quality claim auditable.

Payback is five years at the local industrial tariff, which is among the highest in the region. A brewery in a city with cheap water would wait 15 years, which is why the case is sited where it is.

The regulator's role was material. The discharge permit originally barred reuse for food contact surfaces, and the brewery's petition argued that a treated stream meeting a potable standard is not different from the supply. The permit was amended after 18 months.

Scale is what separates a plant's loop from a city's scheme. A municipal scheme treats sewage for 200,000 people and its cost per cubic metre is half; a brewery's loop is expensive because it is small. The technology pays at a plant scale and not at a city scale.

A brewery's water splits into product, cleaning and cooling. Cleaning is 60 per cent of the intake and it is the stream that a loop can recover, because the water's job is to rinse rather than to become beer.

A membrane bioreactor's biology is the plant's most fragile component. A cleaning chemical's residue can kill the biomass, and the plant's protocol isolates a tank's discharge before it reaches the reactor. That isolation is a valve and a procedure, not a technology.

A brewery's loop is a regulatory achievement as much as an engineering one. A food-safety regulator's acceptance of recycled water for a food-contact surface is a decision that a plant cannot make for itself, and a plant that operates before the decision is a plant that operates on an interpretation.

Constraints that a plant can solve and a market cannot are the subject here. A brewery with a water bill and a permit can close its loop, and the arithmetic depends on prices that a utility sets and not on the membranes.
