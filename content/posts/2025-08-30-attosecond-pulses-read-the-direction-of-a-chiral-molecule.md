Chirality is a property that shows up in the interaction with circularly polarized light, because a left-handed molecule and its mirror image absorb and emit different amounts when the light field turns one way and not the other. Photoelectron circular dichroism measures that difference in the electrons knocked out of a molecule, and it is large enough front to back to be a practical signal. The effect is large in the forward and backward directions and vanishes sideways, which is why the geometry of the measurement decides what can be seen.

A paper in *Nature* on 27 August 2025 adds the time coordinate. Its authors used circularly polarized attosecond pulses, alone and in combination with a near-infrared field turning the same way, and measured the emitted electrons in three dimensions, which means resolving both the direction and the delay at which they leave. Three-dimensional detection records an electron's momentum in all three components instead of as a time of flight along one axis.

The amplitude result is the first: with the co-rotating attosecond and near-infrared fields, the dichroism nearly doubled relative to the attosecond pulse alone, and its sign could be reversed by changing the relative timing or the handedness of the fields. That controllability is what makes the effect a measurement, not an observation. The doubling describes the field's influence on the departing electron, not a change in the molecule, and that distinction lets the effect serve as a probe of the emission itself.

The timing result is where the resolution sits. Forward and backward emission carried a chiral delay of up to 60 attoseconds, and the delay resolved against emission angle reached 240 attoseconds. The authors also identify an asymmetry of about 60 attoseconds that comes from the interaction between the outgoing electron and the remainder of the field, a continuum-to-continuum effect that has to be separated from the chiral signal itself. Delays of tens of attoseconds are short against a vibrational period and long against the time an electron spends leaving a molecule, which puts the measurement in a useful window.

Separating it is the technical achievement. The chiral delay and the continuum delay overlap in time, and a one-dimensional detector would sum them, so the three-dimensional momentum measurement is what allows the two to be told apart. The paper reports the separation as a modelling step checked against the angular dependence. A one-dimensional detector keeps only the momentum along its axis, so the chiral and achiral contributions arrive together. The modelling is therefore as important as the apparatus.

Limits are those of the sample. Gas-phase molecules have no environment to speak of, and a chiral solvent, a surface or a protein would add scattering that the present analysis does not carry. The measurement also needs an ensemble, so it returns an average over orientations unless the molecules are aligned first. Alignment of the molecules would remove the orientational average and turn the measurement into a probe of a single geometry, which would sharpen the comparison with theory.

What the method offers is a clock for chiral dynamics: whether a molecule keeps its handedness as it changes shape, and how quickly an excited state loses it. Light-induced chiral switching is a candidate application, and it needs exactly this kind of timing to be demonstrated, not assumed. A switch that completes within a vibrational period is what a femtosecond experiment cannot resolve and an attosecond one can. Whether such a switch exists in a molecule simple enough to model is the open question.
