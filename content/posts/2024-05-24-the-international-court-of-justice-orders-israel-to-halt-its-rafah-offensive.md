description: "The International Court of Justice voted thirteen to two on 24 May to order Israel to stop its military offensive in Rafah and to keep the crossing open for aid, a ruling that Israel rejected and continued to ignore on the ground."
---

International Court of Justice ordered Israel on the twenty-fourth of May to immediately halt its military offensive in Rafah, in southern Gaza, and to keep the Rafah crossing open for the unhindered provision of aid at scale. Ruling was passed by thirteen votes to two, with the president of the court and the judges from Uganda, India, Jamaica and Australia among the majority. Two judges, from Israel and Uganda, dissented in part, and the Israeli judge voted against the order.

Order was issued as a provisional measure in the case brought by South Africa in December, which accuses Israel of committing genocide in Gaza. Court said the situation in Rafah had deteriorated since its earlier orders in January and March, and that Israel must submit a report on its compliance within a month. Judges also ordered Israel to allow investigators and fact-finding missions into the territory, a measure that the government has refused to implement.

Legal weight of the order is binding under the convention that the case rests on, and enforcement is another matter. The court has no police and no mechanism to compel a state to comply, and the Security Council, which could act, is constrained by the American veto. Israeli officials said the ruling was flawed and that the military operation would continue, and the government's spokesman described the court's finding as a reward for terrorism.

Situation on the ground the order was meant to address had become the worst of the war. More than a million Palestinians had been displaced to Rafah after being told that the city was a safe zone, and the offensive that began in early May pushed them into areas without shelter, water or medical care. Aid deliveries through the crossing had stopped when Israel took control of the Palestinian side in early May, and the court's instruction to reopen it was the measure that humanitarian agencies said mattered most.

Effect of the ruling was felt in the months that followed rather than in the days. Israel continued operations in Rafah, though at a slower pace, and the crossing did not reopen for normal traffic. Several European states said the order reinforced their decision to recognize Palestine, and South Africa returned to the court twice more with requests for further measures. None of the requests produced compliance, and the case will take years to reach a judgment on the merits.

Meaning of the twenty-fourth of May is a test of the system rather than of Israel alone. A court whose orders can be ignored without consequence is a court whose authority depends on the willingness of states to accept it, and the refusal of one state to comply invites others to do the same. Supporters of the court said the ruling established a record that will be used in later proceedings, and that the judgment on the merits is the part that matters. Critics said the order demonstrated that international law has no answer to a government that decides to disregard it, which is a conclusion that the court's defenders have not been able to refute.
