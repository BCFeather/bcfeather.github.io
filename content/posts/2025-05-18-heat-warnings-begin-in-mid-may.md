The meteorological service issued a yellow heat warning on 18 May, with temperatures forecast above 35 degrees Celsius in the plains for three days. The warning is the lowest of the three levels, which means the forecast is for discomfort rather than for danger, and it arrived two weeks earlier than the average first warning of the past decade. Two weeks early is within the range that climatologists call normal variation, which is the least comforting form of normality.

Early heat is not the same as a hot summer. A single warm spell in May comes from a ridge of high pressure that sits over the north of the country for a few days and then moves east, and the soil is still moist from spring rain, so the surface temperature does not build the way it does in July. The forecasters are careful about this distinction, and the public is not. The public is not, and the first heat warning of the year always produces more anxiety than the forecast justifies.

City hall treats a May warning as routine now. Construction sites shift their working hours, outdoor markets close in the afternoon, and the electricity grid prepares for the first big air-conditioning load of the year. The grid's summer plan is written in March, with maintenance scheduled so that no large unit is offline in July, and a May warning is a rehearsal. A May warning is a rehearsal, and the grid treats it as one rather than as a crisis.

Hospitals see the effect before anybody else. Heat illness arrives in the emergency departments on the second day of a spell rather than the first, because the body's response is delayed and the people most at risk are the ones who do not notice they are dehydrated. Elderly residents living alone, outdoor workers, and people taking medication for blood pressure are the three groups that the public health guidance names. The guidance is printed on posters at every community clinic, and it is largely ignored until the second week of July.

The urban heat island makes the warning uneven. A station in a park can read three degrees lower than a station in a dense district with dark roofs and little shade, and the difference is largest at night, when the concrete releases the heat it absorbed. Cities that have planted trees along their streets measure the effect, and the measurement is usually disappointing: a young tree cools its immediate surroundings and not the block.

What the warning does politically is legitimate the summer. Once the first alert is issued, the municipal departments can activate plans that were written months earlier, from cooling centres to water distribution points. A warning in May is therefore useful beyond its three days: it tests the machinery before the season when the machinery matters. It also gives the public a vocabulary for the season, which is useful when the alerts become serious.
