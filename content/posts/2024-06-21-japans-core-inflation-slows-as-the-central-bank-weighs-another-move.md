Core consumer prices in Japan rose 2.2 per cent in the year to May, slower than the month before, while the headline rate was 2.8 per cent. The figures were published on 21 June, a week after the central bank ended its bond purchases. The core measure excludes fresh food and has been the bank's preferred gauge since the pandemic, and the figure was in line with the forecast. The figure was a tenth lower than the month before, and the economists attributed the fall to the base effect in the energy line.

Prices have now been above the target for three years, the longest run since the 1990s, and the bank's argument is that inflation is finally driven by domestic demand rather than by imported energy. A measure that stays above the target and falls slowly is one the bank treats as a success. Services prices rose 2.1 per cent, the fastest in the series, and the increase came from transport and restaurants. The bank's governor has said the target is now in sight for the first time since the 1990s, and that the policy of negative rates is finished.

Wages are the evidence it wants. The spring negotiations produced the largest increases in three decades, and the bank has said it will raise rates if those increases show up in the prices of services. A wage negotiated centrally is a signal that arrives once a year. The negotiations covered the large employers, and smaller firms have followed with increases that the surveys report but the statistics do not yet capture.

Currency is the complication. The yen is at its weakest since 1990, and the imported prices that follow are the reason the bank has been willing to move even while growth is weak. A currency that falls is a tightening the bank did not choose. The authorities have intervened in the market twice this year, and the effect of each intervention has lasted about a month.

Government debt is the constraint. The bank holds a large share of the government debt, and a rise in the policy rate raises the cost of servicing it, which the finance ministry has described as the main risk to the budget. A balance sheet that is the market cannot be reduced quickly. The bank has said it will reduce its holdings gradually, and the plan implies a sale of about a tenth of the portfolio each year.

What the month shows is that a central bank exiting a policy of twenty years has to move in steps the bond market can absorb, and the inflation data are now strong enough to justify the next one. A country that has spent two decades fighting deflation has to relearn how to raise rates, and the relearning is the slow part. The next meeting is in July, and the market has priced a small chance of a rise, which is higher than it was a month ago.
