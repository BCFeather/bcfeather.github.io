title: "A third dose restores efficacy to 95 per cent with no new safety signal"
date: "2022-03-24T09:10:00+08:00"

description: "In a placebo-controlled trial of 10,125 adults vaccinated at least six months earlier, a third BNT162b2 dose showed 95.3 per cent relative efficacy against COVID and no cases of myocarditis or pericarditis."
---

Booster policy in late 2021 rested on immunogenicity data and on observational estimates of waning, and both are weaker forms of evidence than a randomized trial. Regulators wanted a controlled comparison of a third dose against placebo in people who had completed the primary series months earlier. A trial funded by the manufacturer, which is normal for a vaccine programme and which shifts the burden of independent replication onto national surveillance.

The manufacturer's phase 3 trial provided one. Participants who had received two 30-microgram doses at least six months before were randomized to a third dose or placebo, and 5,081 received the vaccine while 5,044 received placebo. Efficacy was assessed from seven days after the injection. The paper appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 23 March 2022.

Median interval between the second and third dose was 10.8 months in the vaccine group and 10.7 months in the placebo group, and median follow-up was 2.5 months. Among participants without evidence of prior infection, COVID with onset at least seven days after the third dose occurred in 6 vaccinated and 123 placebo recipients, a relative efficacy of 95.3 per cent with an interval from 89.5 to 98.3. The efficacy estimate is relative rather than absolute, and with a placebo event rate of 123 in 5,044 over 2.5 months the absolute reduction is a few cases per thousand, which is the number a patient would want to know.

Safety was the other half of the question. Local and systemic reactogenicity after the third dose was generally low grade, no new safety signals emerged, and no cases of myocarditis or pericarditis were reported in either group during the follow-up.

Absence of myocarditis in 10,125 participants is reassuring and not conclusive, because the event is rare and the follow-up is short. A rate of a few cases per million doses cannot be excluded by a trial of this size, and the surveillance systems that detected the signal after the second dose remain the appropriate instrument. Myocarditis after mRNA vaccination clusters in young men after the second dose, and whether a third dose reproduces that clustering will be answered by the same passive surveillance rather than by this trial.

The population was selected for people who had already accepted two doses and who were healthy enough to enrol, so the trial says nothing about the people most likely to be hospitalized. It also ran before Omicron became dominant in most countries, and efficacy against a variant that escapes neutralization is lower than the figure reported here.

For a clinician, the result supports a third dose for adults six months after the second, with the caveat that the benefit measured against Delta may not transfer unchanged to later variants. For a regulator, it supplies the randomized evidence that the emergency authorization had lacked. A third dose given at ten months is one schedule, and the immune response to a dose given at six months or at twelve may differ in both titre and breadth.
