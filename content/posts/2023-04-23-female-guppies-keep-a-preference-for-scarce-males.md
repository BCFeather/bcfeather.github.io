Mate choice is usually modelled as a female choosing the best male, and a preference for rarity is a puzzle. A male who is rare is rare because few others share his trait, and a female who chooses him is choosing a quality that is scarce rather than a quality that is good.

The study in *Science*, 21 April 2023 explains why the preference persists. In Trinidadian guppies, females prefer males whose colour pattern is uncommon, and the preference is maintained by indirect selection: a choosy female's sons inherit the preference and her daughters inherit the attractiveness.

The genetics is the mechanism. The preference and the trait are linked in inheritance, so that a female who chooses a rare male produces sons who are choosy and daughters who are attractive. The two effects reinforce one another across generations.

The rarity's dynamics are the interesting part. A common pattern becomes less attractive as it becomes common, and a rare one becomes more attractive, which is a negative feedback that keeps the population's variation alive.

The alternative explanation is condition. A rare male may be rare because he is in poor condition, and a female who chooses him would be choosing badly. The paper's design separates the rarity effect from the condition effect.

The field setting is the evidence's strength. Wild guppies in Trinidadian streams have been studied for decades, and a laboratory preference would be a weaker claim than one measured in the population where the behaviour evolved.

The theory's relevance is general. Indirect selection is a mechanism that maintains a preference even when the preference's direct benefit is absent, and the guppy case is one of the cleanest demonstrations.

The limitation is the system's specificity. A live-bearing fish with a colour-based preference is a special case, and the mechanism's generality across taxa is an extrapolation.

What the paper does not measure is the fitness consequence over generations. A preference that persists is a preference that pays, and the population's trajectory is the evidence that the model predicts.

For behavioural ecology, the value is a resolution of a paradox. A preference for rarity looks maladaptive and is maintained by the inheritance of both the preference and the trait, and the paradox dissolves when the two are considered together.

The experimental manipulation is the test. The authors alter the frequency of colour patterns in mesocosms and measure the females' responses, and the change in preference with the change in frequency is the rarity effect isolated.

The sensory basis is a further layer. A female's preference is mediated by the visual system, and a male's colour is a signal that the eye has to detect against a stream bed's background. The paper's measurements of contrast address that.

The conservation reading is indirect. A preference that maintains variation is a mechanism that keeps a population's genetic diversity, and a population stripped of its variation by habitat change loses that mechanism with it.
The preference's stability is the model's prediction. A rare-male advantage that is strong would drive the rare pattern to commonness and erase itself, and an advantage that is weak would let the pattern drift. The observed equilibrium is between the two.
