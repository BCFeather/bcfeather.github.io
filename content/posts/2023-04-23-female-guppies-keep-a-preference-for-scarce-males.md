Mate choice is usually modelled as a female choosing the best male, and a preference for rarity is a puzzle. A male is rare because few others share his trait, so a female who chooses him chooses a scarce quality rather than a good one.

In *Science* on 21 April 2023, the preference is explained. In Trinidadian guppies females prefer males whose colour pattern is uncommon, and the preference is maintained by indirect selection, because a choosy female's sons inherit the preference and her daughters inherit the attractiveness.

Linked in inheritance, the preference and the trait reinforce each other across generations: a female who chooses a rare male produces choosy sons and attractive daughters. The dynamics keep variation alive. A common pattern becomes less attractive as it spreads and a rare one becomes more so, a negative feedback that stops the population settling on a single design.

Because a rare male may simply be in poor condition, choosing him could be choosing badly, and the design has to separate rarity from condition before the preference means anything. Manipulation does the separation: the authors alter the frequency of colour patterns in mesocosms, measure the females' responses, and read the rarity effect off the change in preference as the frequency changes.

Fieldwork makes the claim stronger, since wild guppies in Trinidadian streams have been studied for decades and a laboratory preference would be the weaker version of the result. A female's choice also runs through her visual system, and a male's colour has to be detected against a stream bed's background, which is why the measurements of contrast matter.

Stability is where the model earns its keep. A strong rare-male advantage would drive the rare pattern to commonness and erase itself, a weak one would let the pattern drift, and the observed equilibrium sits between the two. What remains unmeasured is the fitness consequence over generations: a preference that persists is a preference that pays, and the population's trajectory is what the model predicts. A live-bearing fish with a colour-based preference is a special case, so generality across taxa is an extrapolation. Behavioural ecology gets a resolution of a paradox, since a preference for rarity looks maladaptive and is maintained by the inheritance of both preference and trait, and the paradox dissolves when the two are taken together. A population stripped of its variation by habitat change loses that mechanism with it.

What the mechanism predicts for a population under pressure is the test that fieldwork can supply. A preference maintained by indirect selection should persist while variation persists, and a population that loses its colour patterns to predation or pollution should lose the preference with them, which is a prediction a long-term study could check. Whether the preference survives a collapse in numbers is the version of that prediction a conservation biologist would test, and the answer would say how much of the mechanism depends on the habitat holding still.
