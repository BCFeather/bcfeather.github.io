description: "A phase 3 trial finds weekly oral islatravir plus lenacapavir as good as daily triple therapy at keeping the virus suppressed."

Antiretroviral therapy works best when it is taken every day, and the gap between what a regimen can do and what a patient can sustain is where most treatment failure lives. Long-acting injections have narrowed that gap for some patients, but they require clinic visits and skilled administration. A once-weekly oral regimen would sit in a more convenient place: less frequent than daily pills, less dependent on the health system than an injection.

Islatravir and lenacapavir are both long-lived molecules with different mechanisms, and combining them was the obvious way to build a weekly pill. The phase 3 trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 29 July 2026 enrolled people with virologically suppressed HIV-1 and randomised them to the weekly pair or to continuing daily bictegravir, emtricitabine and tenofovir alafenamide. The primary comparison was noninferiority at 48 weeks on the share of participants with viral load at or above 50 copies per millilitre. Both arms were also compared on the share of participants who completed the schedule, since a weekly pill only helps if it is actually taken.

No participant on the weekly regimen had a viral load at or above that threshold, against one, 0.3 per cent, on daily therapy. Viral load below 50 copies was reported in 93.4 per cent and 92.4 per cent respectively, a difference of one percentage point with a confidence interval from minus 3.2 to 5.2. The mean change in CD4 count at 48 weeks was similar in the two arms, and noninferiority was declared.

The result is a genuine convenience gain, and the safety questions are the ones that matter for a weekly drug. Islatravir had previously run into trouble at higher doses, where it affected lymphocyte counts, and the trial's dose and monitoring schedule reflect that history. Lenacapavir brings its own interactions and injection-site issues in its long-acting form, though the oral version avoids the latter entirely. Those safety questions are the reason the trial's exclusion criteria were written around previous nucleoside exposure.

Noninferiority is not superiority, and a trial of this size cannot detect rare virological failures. Resistance is the real risk: a weekly regimen forgives fewer missed doses in terms of drug levels but punishes a failure with the same class-wide consequences, and the trial's follow-up is short relative to a lifetime of treatment. Adherence in a trial is also better than adherence in a clinic.

The population that benefits most is the one that struggles with daily pills for reasons of schedule, stigma or housing rather than preference, and that population is not well represented in a registration trial. The next question is implementation: cost, cold chain is unnecessary for a pill, and whether a weekly regimen can be delivered through the same clinics that already manage injections. A pilot phase with monthly viral-load checks would be the cheap way to find out whether the convenience survives contact with real clinics.
