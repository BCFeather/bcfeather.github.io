Psychedelic research has a measurement problem. The acute effects of psilocybin are described in metaphors, questionnaires and interviews, and the neuroimaging that accompanies them usually reports a single summary statistic such as the entropy of functional connectivity. A statistic of that kind says that brain activity became less predictable and cannot say whether the unpredictability was organized, which is the question a person under the drug would recognize.

A study by Stoliker and colleagues collected the largest single-site acute-phase dataset of its kind: 62 adults who had never taken a psychedelic were imaged on a baseline day and on a day when they received psilocybin. On both days they experienced four sensory and motor contexts, resting, guided meditation, music listening and film watching, which impose different demands on internally and externally directed processing. Roughly five hours after dosing they completed questionnaires on the quality of the experience. The work appeared in *Nature* and was highlighted in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* on 24 September.

Analysis looked for latent temporal structure in the brain activity, and found it. Activity under psilocybin was better aligned with the current context than baseline activity was, which means the drug did not simply scramble the signal; it made the signal more responsive to what the environment was asking for. The strength of that alignment covaried with subjective ratings, so volunteers whose brains tracked the setting most closely also reported the richest experience.

That result reframes the pharmacology. The classical account treats psychedelics as a temporary loosening of priors, in which the brain stops imposing its learned expectations on ambiguous input. A brain with weaker priors should be more sensitive to context, and the paper measures exactly that sensitivity while showing that its degree varies between people and predicts how the session felt.

Design has a limit that no psychedelic study has solved. Participants know what they have been given, expectations are strong, and the questionnaires are completed while the drug is still active, so the correlation between neural alignment and reported quality may run through expectation. A blinded design is difficult with a drug that announces itself, and the authors use within-subject comparisons to control for stable traits.

The therapeutic implication is speculative and worth stating as such. If the lasting psychological change that follows a session depends on how well the acute experience integrates the environment, then the setting is not a courtesy but a variable, and trials that standardize the setting may be throwing away the effect they are trying to measure. Music and guided meditation are already part of most protocols, and the paper suggests they are doing mechanistic work.

Clinical question remains open, and this study does not answer it. What it supplies is a measurable property of the psychedelic state that is neither entropy nor a questionnaire score, and a way to test whether the property differs between people who benefit and people who do not.
