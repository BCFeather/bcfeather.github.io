Two particles built from the same gold, the same coating and the same size can differ only in the twist of their surface lattice, and that difference is enough to change what a dendritic cell does. Chirality is a routine property of molecules, and the mirrors that drug regulators separate before approval, but for inorganic nanoparticles it had been a problem in synthesis rather than a question in immunology. Regulators have separated mirror-image drugs for decades, and the assumption that an inorganic surface could carry the same distinction had not been tested in a living system.

A group at the Chinese Academy of Sciences made achiral, left-handed and right-handed gold nanoparticles by irradiating a growth solution with circularly polarized light, which biased the deposition of the surface lattice one way or the other. Optical anisotropy factors, the g-factor that measures how differently the particles absorb the two circular polarizations, reached 0.4. The particles were then put in front of mouse bone-marrow-derived dendritic cells and, separately, injected into mice. The paper appeared in *Nature*, 19 January 2022.

The immunological readout separated cleanly by handedness. Binding to two adhesion G-protein-coupled receptors, CD97 and EMR1, opened mechanosensitive potassium-efflux channels, assembled inflammasomes and drove dendritic cell maturation. Both the in vitro and the in vivo responses depended monotonically on the g-factor, which is the strongest kind of evidence that the twist itself, not a batch difference in size or charge, was doing the work. Achiral particles sat between the two extremes, which is what a monotonic dependence requires. The particles shared diameter and capping chemistry, so the comparison isolates the twist of the lattice.

Why a potassium channel should care about handedness follows from the geometry of the receptor. These adhesion receptors present long, flexible stalks that experience the particle surface as a mechanical environment, and a surface that winds one way presents a different local curvature to a protein that winds the other way. The resulting tension opens the channel, and the ion flux is the signal that recruits the inflammasome.

The experiment establishes a mechanism and not a therapy.Mice were observed for days rather than months, so chronic exposure and repeated dosing remain unmeasured, and the paper reports no challenge experiment with an actual pathogen. The particles were tested in mice over short windows, the receptor pair is one of many that a particle encounters in serum, and the g-factors achieved in a cuvette may not survive the protein corona that forms within seconds of injection. The monotonic relation is also measured across a small set of particles rather than across a designed series.

Chirality as a design parameter is the durable claim. If a particle's handedness can be tuned during synthesis and read optically, then the same chemistry can be screened for the immune tone a vaccine adjuvant or a tolerizing therapy needs, with the twist rather than the composition carrying the instruction.
