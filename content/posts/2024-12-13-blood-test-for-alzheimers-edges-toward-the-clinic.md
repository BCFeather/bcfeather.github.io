A blood test for the protein fragment p-tau217 has performed as well as lumbar puncture and brain imaging in several large cohorts, and the results published this year have moved it from a research tool to a candidate for routine use. A test that requires a vial of blood is a test that a family doctor can order, which changes who gets diagnosed. A test that replaces a lumbar puncture is a test that removes the fear that keeps patients away.

Biology is specific enough to be useful. Phosphorylated tau accumulates in the brain in Alzheimer's disease, and the fraction that carries a phosphate at position 217 rises early and tracks the pathology rather than the symptoms. A marker that changes before dementia appears is a marker that a trial can use to select participants. A marker that rises before symptoms is a marker that a prevention trial can enroll against. Comparison studies are the evidence. Cohorts in Sweden and elsewhere compared the blood test with the results of positron emission tomography and with cerebrospinal fluid, and the agreement was high enough that a blood result could replace the first step of a diagnostic workup. A test that rules out the disease is as valuable as one that confirms it. A cohort of patients who already have symptoms is a cohort that tests the test less than a screening population would.

Practical obstacles are reimbursement and interpretation. A test that costs a few hundred dollars has no billing code in many systems, and a physician who orders it has to know what to do with a positive result in a patient without symptoms. A physician who orders a test without a treatment pathway is a physician who has to explain a probability.

Treatments changed the stakes. Two antibody drugs that clear amyloid have been approved for early disease, and both require confirmation of the pathology before use. A cheap test that identifies who is eligible is a test that makes the drugs usable at scale, and the drugs are the reason the test matters. What the test does not do is tell a patient what will happen. A positive result in a person with mild symptoms raises the probability of progression and does not date it, and a clinician who presents the result as a prediction is misusing it.
