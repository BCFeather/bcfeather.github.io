---

Federal Reserve left its benchmark interest rate unchanged at 5.25 to 5.5 per cent on the twentieth of March, the fifth consecutive meeting at that level, and its projections continued to show three quarter-point cuts before the end of the year. Median estimate of the nineteen officials on the committee was for a rate of 4.6 per cent in December, which is the same figure they had published in December. Markets had expected the projection to fall to two cuts after two months of stronger inflation data.

Economic forecasts published with the decision were revised in ways that pointed in both directions. Committee raised its estimate of growth for 2024 to 2.1 per cent from 1.4, and it lowered its forecast for unemployment to 4.0 per cent from 3.7. It raised its projection for core inflation to 2.6 per cent from 2.4. Combination of stronger growth and higher inflation is what makes the decision difficult, since it suggests the economy is running hotter than the committee expected without producing the slowdown that would justify cuts.

Comments from the chair were interpreted as a signal that the committee had no intention of moving at this meeting. Jerome Powell said inflation had come down substantially but that the committee needed more confidence before it began to reduce rates, and he repeated that the timing would depend on the data rather than on a calendar. He also said that the labour market was gradually coming into better balance, a phrase that officials use when they mean that wage growth is slowing without a rise in unemployment.

Market reaction was a rally that faded. Stocks rose to records during the afternoon, and bond yields fell as traders held to their expectation of a first cut in June. By the end of the following week, stronger-than-expected inflation data had pushed the expected date of the first cut to September, and the probability of three cuts in the year fell below fifty per cent. Federal Reserve officials said in subsequent speeches that they were in no hurry, which the market heard as a warning.

Divergence from other central banks became a subject in itself. The European Central Bank and the Bank of England signalled that they were closer to cutting, and the Swiss National Bank cut its rate on the twenty-first of March. The American economy has grown faster than the European one, which gives the Federal Reserve more room to wait, and the difference in timing has pushed the dollar higher against most currencies.

What followed from the twentieth of March was a commitment that the committee may not be able to keep. Three cuts in 2024 were the basis on which markets had priced assets since December, and the projections kept that expectation alive for another quarter. If inflation remained above target through the summer, the committee would have to choose between cutting with inflation above target and disappointing the markets that had believed its forecast. What the meeting did was to postpone that choice, and to leave the year's outcome dependent on data that had not yet arrived.
