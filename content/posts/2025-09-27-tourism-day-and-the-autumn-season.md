World Tourism Day falls on 27 September, the anniversary of the statute that created the organization now known as UN Tourism, adopted in 1970. The day has been marked since 1980 with a theme, a message from a secretary-general and a host country, and the ceremony is one of the more harmless rituals of the international calendar: a sector that employs roughly one in ten workers worldwide takes an afternoon to say so, and to ask for a policy that suits it.

Timing is deliberate and, in the northern hemisphere, sensible. Late September is when the harvest is in and the heat has broken, and the tourist trade moves from the coast to the interior. In China the calendar has its own logic, with the National Day holiday beginning on the first of October and the travel rush starting a week before it. The last days of September are the interval in which people book, compare prices and complain about them, and in which the booking platforms publish their forecasts for the week.

Numbers released this year described a recovery that has become ordinary. Domestic trips have passed their pre-2020 level, outbound travel has returned unevenly, with short-haul destinations in Asia ahead of long-haul ones, and inbound tourism has grown more slowly than the policy discussion assumes, with visa changes announced faster than the flights that would use them, a mismatch the industry complains about every autumn. The pattern is not a Chinese one: everywhere the industry is busier than it was and less confident than it sounds, because costs rose faster than prices during the recovery and because the staff who left in 2021 have not all come back.

What the day argues about more interestingly is what tourism is for. A country that markets itself to visitors builds the things visitors need, and those things are usually also what residents wanted: transport that runs on time, clean waterfronts, signs in two languages, a museum open on a Monday. The reverse is also true, and the literature on overtourism is a list of places where the visitors' needs displaced everyone else's, and a shorter list of places that managed the traffic without losing the street.

A quieter version of the same argument runs through the domestic trade. Guesthouses in villages that had no visitors a decade ago, homestays run by families who converted a floor of their house, the small restaurants that appear where a scenic road has been built: these are the ordinary forms the industry takes, and they are what the day's photographs rarely show, because a guesthouse with six rooms is not a postcard.

A week from now the moon will be full, the trains will be full before that, and the last weekend of September brings the year's largest movement of people. Tourism day is an anniversary; the thing it names is a habit, and the habit is now the largest single peace-time displacement that any species undertakes.
