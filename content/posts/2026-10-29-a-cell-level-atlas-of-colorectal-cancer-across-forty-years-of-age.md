Colorectal cancer in a 29-year-old and colorectal cancer in a 79-year-old look the same under a microscope and behave differently in a clinic. A paper in *Nature Medicine* on 29 October reports a single-nucleus RNA sequencing atlas of 189 colorectal tumours drawn from patients aged 20 to over 90, built to find what the age difference actually is at the level of individual cells.

Bulk sequencing averages. A tumour sample ground up and sequenced returns one expression profile per gene, which is a blend of epithelial cells, fibroblasts, immune cells and blood vessels. When the question is whether a young patient's tumour has a different immune infiltrate or a different stromal response, the blend hides exactly the answer that is wanted, and the age signal disappears into the noise. Sorting cells before sequencing is the standard remedy, and it has become cheap enough to run at atlas scale.

Single-nucleus sequencing keeps the cell identities. Each nucleus is counted separately, so the analysis can ask which cell types are present, in what proportions and with which programmes switched on. Across the age continuum the authors report features that track with age, in the tumour cells themselves and in the neighbourhood they construct around themselves. Composition, not just gene expression, carries the signal: which immune cells are present and which are absent is as informative as what the tumour cells are doing.

Early-onset colorectal cancer has been rising in incidence for two decades in richer countries, and the cohorts that could answer why are only now large enough to study, and the explanations offered, diet, obesity, antibiotics, microbiome, have not converged. An atlas does not settle causation, but it changes the questions: if young tumours carry a particular immune or stromal configuration, then the search for an exposure can be aimed at the mechanism rather than at the epidemiology alone.

The clinical use of such an atlas is in matching treatment to biology. Immunotherapy works in tumours that are inflamed and fails in tumours that are not, and mismatch-repair status explains only part of that. A cell-level description of who is in the tumour gives a way to ask whether the difference between a young and an old patient is the tumour or the host.

Two hundred tumours is a large atlas and a small sample. Age is one variable among many, and stage, site, treatment history and ancestry all move the same cells, so the age-associated features the paper reports will need replication in cohorts built for the question. The dataset is the contribution either way: an atlas is a resource that outlives the paper that introduced it. Whether age turns out to be the organising variable, or only one axis among several, depends on groups who will reanalyse the same cells with different questions. An atlas earns its place by the number of questions it makes answerable, and that count is not known on publication day.
