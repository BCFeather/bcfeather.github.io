A notice went up on the website of Poland's Constitutional Tribunal shortly after dusk on Friday, and it announced more than a change of office. It said the court's president, Bogdan Swieczkowski, and his deputy, Bartlomiej Sochanski, had been suspended from their duties for a month. It said Judge Anna Korwin-Piotrowska, the longest-serving judge on the bench, would run the institution in the meantime. By Saturday morning riot police were holding the gate of the building in central Warsaw, and several hundred supporters of Law and Justice were gathered at it with flags and a demand that the two men be reinstated.

Control of the Tribunal is the substance of the quarrel, because the court decides whether laws passed by the Sejm are constitutional and the government's programme depends on those laws surviving its review. Korwin-Piotrowska took her seat under the parliament elected in 2023, the one that gave Donald Tusk his majority, and the seven judges who issued a statement on Saturday, Swieczkowski and Sochanski among them, called her move 'completely unlawful'. That statement carried the argument the opposition has made all year: a judge sworn in without the president's signature holds no seat at all. The argument is why eight nominations have become a crisis of the state.

Karol Nawrocki, the president, was in the air on his way to Senegal when the notice appeared, and he ordered the aircraft to turn back over Europe. He reached the court before dawn on Saturday and walked into the building, and the two sides have told different stories about the minutes that followed ever since. His office says he was looking for the keys to the deputy president's office. The judges say he pushed past Korwin-Piotrowska, pressed against her, and told the judges appointed by the current parliament to their faces that they had no right to sit.

Outside, in a statement read by an aide, Nawrocki described 'a breach of public order in the Republic of Poland and a forcible takeover of a constitutional body'. He spent Saturday in talks with party leaders, and Tusk attended none of them, which left the consultations without the one participant who could have settled anything. By evening there was no breakthrough, and the president's chief of staff had described the prime minister as a man setting Poland on fire. The president's office said it would complain to prosecutors that the inviolability of the head of state had been breached.

Tusk answered on television late on Saturday, quieter than the day around him. He said he had no doubt that the Tribunal would work as it should, independently of him and in line with the constitution. The duty of the executive, he said, is to guarantee the court's safety and its conditions of work, and he meant to discharge it. On whether he would give ground, his answer was that in certain matters there can be no compromise.

The quarrel is a decade old and has outlived two governments. Since March the Sejm has elected eight new judges; Nawrocki has sworn in three, and the other five took their oaths in the chamber of the parliament instead, a procedure Swieczkowski refuses to recognize. On Saturday the European Commission urged the president to sign the outstanding nominations so that the court could operate under Polish and European law. Poland votes in a parliamentary election in less than a year, and each camp now has its own account of a coup to take to the country.
