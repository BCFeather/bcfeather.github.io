Consumer prices rose 11.1 per cent in the year to October, the Office for National Statistics reported on 16 November. The reading was up from 10.1 in September and above the 10.7 forecast. It was the highest since October 1981 and the highest of any large economy in the group of seven.

Energy was the largest contributor. The price guarantee had capped the average bill at 2,500 pounds from October, and the cap was set at a level above the previous year's prices. Gas rose 130 per cent over the year, and electricity 66. The government said the cap would cost 60 billion pounds in the first six months. The cap replaced a guarantee that would have set the average bill at 3,549 pounds.

Food rose 16.4 per cent, the fastest since 1977. The prices of milk, cheese and eggs rose 27 per cent, and of bread and cereals 16. The statisticians said the increases were broad, and that the weak pound had raised the cost of imported food. Supermarkets said they had absorbed part of the increase and could not absorb more. The price of a pint of milk had risen 40 per cent since January, and a dozen eggs 30.

Core inflation was 6.5 per cent, and services inflation was 6.6. The bank's target is 2 per cent, and its forecast had inflation falling to 5 per cent by the end of 2023. The October figure was 1.6 points above the forecast it had published in August, which is a large miss. The bank raised rates by 75 basis points on 3 November and said it expected inflation to peak at 11 per cent. The October figure was in line with that, and the bank said at its December meeting that the peak had arrived. It raised rates by 50 points in December and by 50 in February. The bank had raised rates to 3 per cent, and the real rate was minus 8 per cent.

Real wages were falling at the fastest rate since 2001. Regular pay rose 5.7 per cent in the three months to September, and prices rose 11, so the real fall was 5 per cent. The government said it would protect the poorest households through the benefit system, and it uprated benefits by 10.1 per cent in April. The bank said it expected a recession of eight quarters, and the forecast was published with the November meeting.

Inflation fell to 10.7 in November and 9.2 in December, and to 4 per cent by December 2023. The figures were published a day after the budget statement, which raised taxes and cut spending. The chancellor said inflation was the reason for the consolidation, and the opposition said the government had caused part of it. The independent forecast published with the statement showed inflation at 9.1 per cent for 2023. Food inflation stayed above 10 per cent until the autumn, and it was the last component to fall. The bank's forecast of a long recession was wrong, and output was flat in 2023.
