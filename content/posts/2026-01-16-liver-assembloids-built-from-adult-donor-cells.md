A liver model has to do several things at once to be useful: make albumin, metabolize drugs, secrete bile and survive for long enough to be studied. Cell lines do the first two badly, pluripotent stem cells do them inconsistently and organoids grown from a single cell type do them partly. The organ that performs all four has three main cell types working together, and reconstructing that arrangement from adult tissue is a harder problem than growing a ball of one cell type.

Yuan and colleagues built periportal liver assembloids from primary cells taken from adult donors, combining hepatocytes, mesenchymal cells and cholangiocytes, the epithelial cells of the bile duct. The periportal label refers to the region of the liver lobule nearest the portal vein, which is where the tissue was derived from and where bile secretion begins. The work was reported in *Nature* and highlighted in *Nature Biotechnology* on 16 January.

Getting adult hepatocytes to expand was the technical obstacle. The authors analysed the signalling pathways involved in hepatocyte proliferation and found that a combination of WNT and YAP activation supported long-term expansion of human adult hepatocytes as organoids, which is a result that many groups had pursued without success. They obtained organoids from samples taken from 28 patients. Expansion for months rather than days is the difference between an experiment and a cell bank, and the 28 donors give the bank some genetic breadth.

Maturation brought the model close to tissue. The hepatocyte organoids showed apical-basal polarity, a structural feature that distinguishes a functional epithelium from a clump of cells, and they reproduced patient-specific variability in bile canaliculus morphology and metabolic profiles. That variability is an asset, because it means the model can be used to ask why one patient metabolizes a drug differently from another.

The assembloid format adds the missing cell types. Mesenchymal cells supply the stroma and the signalling that hepatocytes expect, cholangiocytes form the duct structures that carry bile away, and the three together produce a tissue with the architecture of the periportal region. Architecture matters because bile secretion is a directional process, and direction requires a duct.

Limitations are those of any ex vivo system. There is no blood flow, no immune system and no nervous input, and the assembloids are grown from tissue that came from donors who had diseases and treatments that the model does not reproduce. A drug tested in the model is being tested against a liver that is partly a reconstruction and partly a memory of a patient.

Where this is going is drug-induced liver injury, which is the most common reason a drug is withdrawn and the hardest toxicity to predict from animal studies. A model built from human cells that retains patient-specific metabolism is the closest thing to a preclinical test that a pharmaceutical company could use, and the paper's patient diversity is what makes that plausible. Regulators have begun to accept human cell models as supporting evidence for some toxicity questions, and a model with this architecture is a candidate for that role.
