The Yellow River delta near Dongying is flat, green in patches and white in others, and the white is salt. The soil there was formed by sediment deposited at the river's mouth, and when the river changed course or the sea intruded, the salt stayed. Rice is the crop that tolerates the problem best, because it is grown in standing water, and flooding leaches salt from the topsoil. The varieties that do well on saline land are not the ones that yield most on good land: they are the ones that keep a reasonable yield when the electrical conductivity of the soil is high, and breeding for that trait is a specialist business. The trait is not a single gene but a set of responses: the plant excludes sodium at the root, sequesters it in older leaves, and keeps its stomata working when the osmotic pressure rises.

Breeders have three routes. One is conventional crossing between tolerant landraces and high-yielding varieties, which takes a decade. One is the introduction of genes for salt tolerance from wild relatives, which is faster and less predictable. The third is selection under field conditions, which is slow but is the only way to know whether a line will perform in a delta rather than in a greenhouse. The delta's trial stations grow a few hundred lines each season on plots that are deliberately marginal. Agronomists measure the salt in the soil before planting and after harvest, and the lines that hold their yield across a range of salinity are the ones that go forward. Most fail. A line that yields brilliantly at low salinity and collapses at moderate salinity is worthless here, however good its grain.

Economics are the constraint here. A saline field yields perhaps two-thirds of what a good field yields, and the rice from it sells at the same price. The farmer's margin is thin, and the subsidy that keeps the land in production is a policy choice about land use rather than about food. A village that loses its fields loses its school and its shop within a decade, and the land does not recover on its own.

What the work produces is optionality. A country with 100 million hectares of salt-affected land does not need all of it to grow rice, but it needs the knowledge of how to, because the alternative for those districts is abandonment. The trial plots near Dongying are the visible part of a programme that will be judged in twenty years.
