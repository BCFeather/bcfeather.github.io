A temporary truce between Israel and Hamas took effect at seven in the morning on 24 November, after weeks of negotiations mediated by Qatar, Egypt and the United States. Under the agreement, Hamas would release fifty hostages held in Gaza over four days, all of them women and children, and Israel would release a hundred and fifty Palestinian women and teenagers from its prisons. The deal also allowed more aid into the strip and paused Israeli air operations over the south.

Thirteen came out that afternoon. Thirteen Israeli hostages were handed to the Red Cross and crossed into Egypt before being flown to hospitals in Israel, and a number of foreign workers were released in the same exchange. Israel released thirty-nine prisoners, and the process repeated each evening, with the counts verified by the International Committee of the Red Cross and the two sides trading lists through the mediators.

Mechanics were fragile. Each side accused the other of violating the terms within the first two days, and the pause held because both wanted the exchanges to continue. Qatar and Egypt served as guarantors, and the United States pressed Israel to keep the air pause in the south, where most of the displaced population had moved. Aid convoys entered through Rafah at a larger scale than before, and fuel deliveries resumed under a limited quota.

A formula for extension was written into the deal. Every ten additional hostages released would buy another day of pause, and Hamas and Israel used that clause twice, extending the truce to seven days in total. By the end of the pause, more than a hundred hostages had come out, and roughly three times as many Palestinian prisoners had been freed. The remaining hostages included soldiers and adult men, categories that the original deal had excluded.

It ended on the morning of 1 December, when both sides accused the other of firing first, and Israel resumed its air and ground campaign. The mediators in Doha said they had been close to a further extension when the shooting started again. The collapse was expected by the mediators, who had designed the arrangement as a series of discrete steps rather than a ceasefire. Each extension required a fresh negotiation, and the room for agreement narrowed as the easier categories of hostages were exhausted.

For the families, the four days were both a relief and an ordeal. Some learned that relatives had been killed during the pause's negotiation, and others waited through each night for a list that arrived late. The hostages who returned described being held in tunnels and apartments, with little food and limited medical care, and Israeli hospitals reported malnutrition and untreated injuries among the released. The political consequences will take longer to settle than the truce did. The negotiations continued through the pause, with the heads of the CIA and Mossad meeting in Doha, and the mediators said publicly that they expected the framework to be tested again within days of its expiry, a prediction the first week of December confirmed.
