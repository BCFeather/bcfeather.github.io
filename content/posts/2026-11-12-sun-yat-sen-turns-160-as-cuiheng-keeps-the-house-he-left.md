Sun Yat-sen was born on 12 November 1866 in Cuiheng, a village in Xiangshan County, Guangdong, which was renamed Zhongshan in his honour in 1925. The 160th anniversary of his birth falls on a Thursday in 2026, and memorial sites connected with his life have been preparing exhibitions and commemorative programmes since the summer. Cuiheng lies about 20 kilometres south of the city centre, and one of the roads leading to it is lined with banyan trees he is said to have climbed as a boy. His father was a farmer with a small plot of land, and part of the family moved to Honolulu when Sun was thirteen to join his elder brother. Those early journeys between the Pearl River Delta and the Pacific helped shape his later outlook as a reformer who drew on both Chinese tradition and overseas experience.
The former residence in Cuiheng remains the centre of the memorial landscape. The two-storey brick-and-timber house, designed and built under Sun's supervision in 1892, has been preserved as a museum since 1956, and much of the furniture inside belonged to his family. The site now manages roughly 160,000 square metres of the historic village and is classified as a national first-class museum and the core of a five-star scenic area. Sun received his medical degree from the Hong Kong College of Medicine for Chinese in 1892, and the practice he opened in Macau closed within a year. In 1894 he founded a society in Honolulu that became one of the earliest organisations to support his revolutionary movement, and its branches gradually spread through overseas Chinese communities. In 2025, the museum's permanent exhibition, 'Sun Yat-sen, the Great Pioneer of China's Democratic Revolution', was renewed with more than 410 historical photographs and over 120 cultural objects, presenting his life, thought and political practice in five thematic sections.

Large numbers of visitors continue to arrive at the memorial museum. In 2025, the site received 4.277 million visitors, making it one of the most visited museums in Guangdong, while its single-day attendance reached 49,088 on 1 October, the highest figure since it opened in 1956. Staff members say that visitor numbers peak during national holidays and around major anniversaries, when queues at the entrance extend down the lane. Admission is free, and guided tours are often fully booked at weekends. In recent years the museum has also introduced mixed-reality glasses, audio-theatre guides, school programmes and family activities, turning the former residence and the surrounding village into a wider educational space rather than a single exhibition hall.

The museum's archival collection is one of its main attractions. It holds letters Sun wrote from Honolulu and Tokyo, drafts associated with the Three Principles of the People, and objects connected with his public life in 1912. Curators say that the collection has been built up through donations from his family and from overseas Chinese communities, and some photographs date back to his student years. The reading room is open to researchers by appointment, and parts of the archive are also available in digital form. For the 160th anniversary, Cuiheng has presented joint exhibitions and travelling displays that bring together documents and objects from memorial institutions in Beijing, Nanjing, Shanghai, Wuhan, Guangzhou, Hainan and other cities.

The commemoration extends well beyond Cuiheng, linking several key sites associated with Sun Yat-sen. The mausoleum in Nanjing, where his remains were interred in 1929, the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall in Guangzhou, which opened in 1931, and his former residence in Shanghai are all marking the anniversary with free entry, lectures and exhibitions. Xiangshan County was renamed Zhongshan in 1925, the year of his death, and the city has carried the name ever since. The Nanjing mausoleum covers about 80,000 square metres, and the approach to the tomb includes 392 steps. In 2026, commemorative events have also been held in Hong Kong, Macao, Xiamen, Wuhan, Hainan, Jinan and overseas communities, while a national memorial meeting in Beijing on 12 November underlined his place in modern Chinese history.

This anniversary recalls the generation that helped found the republic. Sun died in 1925 at the age of 58, leaving behind a republic that had not yet become stable, yet the house in Cuiheng has remained as a tangible link to his early life. The Three Principles of the People were set out in a series of lectures in 1924, and the collected version runs to about 400 pages. His life began in a small Pearl River Delta village, but the ideas he helped spread continue to connect local memory, national history and the wider Chinese diaspora.
