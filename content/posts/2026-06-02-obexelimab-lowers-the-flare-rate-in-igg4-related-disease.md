IgG4-related disease is a fibro-inflammatory condition that can enlarge the pancreas, the salivary glands, the orbits and the aorta, and it is treated with glucocorticoids because nothing else has been convincingly shown to change its course. Relapse on tapering is common, and cumulative steroid dose becomes the main source of harm. The diagnosis is made on histology and on the pattern of organ involvement, and it is frequently mistaken for cancer until a biopsy settles the question. Organ enlargement responds to steroids within weeks, and the difficulty is what happens after the dose is reduced.

Obexelimab is an antibody that binds CD19 and Fc gamma receptor IIb simultaneously, a design intended to dampen B-cell activity without depleting the B cells entirely. That distinguishes it from rituximab, which removes them, and the rationale is that IgG4-related disease needs modulation rather than ablation. It also avoids the prolonged B-cell depletion that leaves patients with low immunoglobulin and repeated infections.

INDIGO, reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 2 June, randomized patients with active disease to weekly obexelimab or placebo and found a significantly lower risk of flare with the drug, along with significantly less glucocorticoid exposure. The trial enrolled patients with active disease and measured flare over a treatment period that the report defines alongside glucocorticoid use.

Flare is the endpoint that matters in this disease, because the organ damage accumulates with each episode and the treatment's purpose is to prevent it. A drug that reduces flares while allowing the steroid dose to fall is doing both things that patients need at once. Steroid exposure is itself an outcome, because the cumulative dose predicts osteoporosis, diabetes and infection better than any single measurement. A flare usually means imaging, a biopsy and a return to high-dose steroids, so preventing one saves more than the drug costs.

Mechanism matters beyond this disease. Engaging an inhibitory Fc receptor while targeting a B-cell surface marker is a way of modulating antibody production without wiping out the cells that make antibody, which is the direction that autoimmune therapy has been moving. Modulation without depletion is the strategy that the field has been moving towards for autoimmune disease.

What the trial does not answer is durability. IgG4-related disease can smoulder for years, and a drug given weekly for a defined period either has to hold after it stops or has to be continued, and the trial's follow-up is too short to distinguish those. The next phase will also have to justify the cost against a steroid that works and costs almost nothing. Weekly dosing is also a commitment, and a subcutaneous injection that a patient gives at home is a different proposition from an infusion every six months. Whether payers accept that arithmetic is the question that decides how widely the drug is used. A single trial rarely changes a guideline, and this one is the first with a positive randomized result.
