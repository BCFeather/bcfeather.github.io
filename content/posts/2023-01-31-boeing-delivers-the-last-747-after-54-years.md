Boeing handed over the last 747 on 31 January, a freighter for Atlas Air, in a ceremony at the widebody factory in Everett, Washington. It was the 1,574th of the type to be built since the first one rolled out in September 1968, and the line that produced it will now be used for the 767 and the 777. The first aircraft had 100 seats in a single aisle, and the upper deck was added later to carry more passengers on the same airframe.

Design work began in 1965 at the request of Pan American, which wanted an aircraft twice the size of the 707 for routes it expected to grow. The first flight came in February 1969, and the first commercial service, from New York to London, in January 1970, after a delay caused by problems with the engines. Orders peaked at 1,000 in the 1980s, and the type carried more people across the Atlantic than any other aircraft for two decades.

Economics decided the outcome. Four engines of the era burned fuel at a rate that made sense while fares were regulated and airports were congested, and the type never carried as many passengers as its builders had expected. The 747-400, the version sold from 1989, became the workhorse of long-haul travel, and its upper deck remained the image people had of a large aeroplane. Fuel burn per seat was better than any four-engine aircraft of its generation, which is why it survived the first oil shock.

Freight kept the line open for two more decades. The cargo version carries about 100 tonnes and loads through a hinged nose, which is why it remains common on routes where the payload matters more than the schedule, and why the last orders came from cargo carriers rather than airlines. Freight carriers in the Middle East and Europe took most of the last twenty aircraft, and they expect to fly them for thirty years.

Ending the line was a matter of arithmetic. Boeing had 155 unfilled orders for the passenger version at the start of 2017 and delivered them over six years, and it booked no new passenger orders after 2017. Wider twin-engine aircraft with two rather than four engines took the long-haul market, and the 777 and the A350 do the work the 747 once did. The 777X, the aircraft meant to replace the passenger version, has been delayed until 2025, so smaller jets are filling the gap.

Atlas Air will fly the last aircraft for several years, and the cargo fleet as a whole will outlast the passenger one. Plans to convert retired passenger 747s to freighters have kept workshops busy, and the type will remain at airports well into the 2040s, long after the building where it was assembled has been turned over to something else. Boeing's workforce at Everett fell by half over the decade, and the factory now builds the 767 tanker for the air force.
