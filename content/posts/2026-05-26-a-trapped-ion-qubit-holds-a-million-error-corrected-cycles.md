A team working on a trapped-ion quantum computer reported on 20 May that a single logical qubit, encoded across thirty-two physical ions, completed more than a million rounds of error correction with a logical error rate of roughly three in a million per round. The result appeared as a preprint and has not yet been refereed. It is the longest continuous demonstration of a corrected qubit published so far, and the figure worth reading is the ratio rather than the million: the encoded qubit made fewer errors than any of the physical ions that composed it.

Error correction is the difference between a laboratory demonstration and a machine that can run an algorithm. A physical qubit decoheres in microseconds, and every operation on it introduces noise, so a useful computer must spread one logical qubit across many physical ones and repeatedly measure the parities between them, correcting the errors faster than they accumulate. The threshold theorem says this works if the physical error rate is below a certain value; the hard part is building a system with enough qubits, enough connectivity and enough speed to reach the regime where correcting helps rather than hurts.

The trapped-ion approach has advantages that show up in exactly this kind of experiment. Ions held in an electromagnetic trap are identical, they can be moved and re-ordered, and their coherence times are long by the standards of the field. Their weakness is speed: the gates that manipulate them are slower than those on superconducting circuits, so a million rounds takes hours rather than minutes, and a real algorithm needs many logical qubits interacting rather than one held in isolation.

That is the caveat that the team itself emphasized. Demonstrating one well-corrected logical qubit falls short of building a computer, because the next requirement is that several logical qubits perform operations on each other while each remains corrected, and the error budget for that step is tighter. The apparatus also relies on optics and vacuum systems that would be difficult to replicate at the scale of thousands of ions, and the group's own estimate is that a machine capable of a commercially useful calculation is several years and one architecture change away.

Still, a threshold crossed is a threshold crossed. The field spent a decade arguing about whether error correction would work in practice at all, and the past three years have produced a steady sequence of results that answer the question in the affirmative and move the argument to engineering. The interesting number in this preprint is the ratio of logical to physical error, because that ratio has to keep falling while the qubit count rises, and it is the only number that predicts whether the machines being built now will ever be useful. The cycle count also matters for a mundane reason that is easy to miss: a machine that holds a logical qubit for hours can be calibrated, diagnosed and repaired in the way that industrial equipment is maintained, and one that holds it for milliseconds cannot. Reliability at that level of tedium is what separates an experiment from a product, and the group's next milestone is a two-qubit gate between corrected qubits rather than a larger register.
