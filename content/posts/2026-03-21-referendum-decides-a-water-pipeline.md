A referendum on a 90-kilometre water pipeline passed this month with 54 per cent in favour, on a turnout of 61 per cent. The engineering case was uncontested: the region's aquifer is dropping 1.2 metres a year and the pipeline would carry surplus water from a river basin 90 kilometres away. The vote turned on who pays and who benefits.

Distribution is the mechanism. The pipeline would serve four municipalities, two of which hold the aquifer that is being depleted and two of which would receive water. The cost is borne by all four through a regional tariff, and the benefit accrues unevenly.

The campaign divided along that line. The upstream municipalities argued that they would pay for water they do not need and that the transfer would reduce their own allocations; the downstream ones argued that their wells are failing and that they have no alternative.

Legal structure shaped the choice. The regional authority can build the pipeline without a vote, and the referendum was advisory, called by the authority's chair after a protest. A yes vote gives political cover; a no vote would not have stopped the project, which both campaigns knew.

Turnout geography is the interesting data. The two downstream municipalities voted 71 and 68 per cent in favour, the two upstream 63 and 58 per cent against, and the margin came from the region's largest city, which is downstream and votes in proportion.

Cost is the contested number. The authority's estimate is 340 million, and a citizens' group commissioned an independent estimate of 520 million, including the pumping energy over thirty years. Both are plausible and neither has been tested against a tender.

The comparison with other infrastructure votes is the analytical point. Referendums on water projects in three other regions turned on the same question, and the ones that passed included a transfer payment to the upstream municipalities. This one did not, and passed anyway on the strength of the downstream majority.

The authority's next step is procurement, and the vote changes the terms. A tender issued after a yes vote can specify a 30-year operating contract, which the authority argues lowers the price; the opposition argues that it locks in a cost the upstream municipalities will subsidise.

Hydrology is the constraint the vote could not settle. The donor basin's surplus is estimated at 60 million cubic metres a year in a wet year and 20 in a dry one, and the pipeline's capacity is 45. A dry decade would make the transfer contentious again.

The turnout is the legitimacy question. A 54 per cent yes on a 61 per cent turnout is a clear majority and not an overwhelming one, and the losing municipalities have asked for a second vote after the tender is priced. No legal route exists for one.

For a reader, the case shows what a referendum does and does not decide. It decides legitimacy, not hydrology, and a project that passes on a distributional argument will still be built to a specification that engineers control.
