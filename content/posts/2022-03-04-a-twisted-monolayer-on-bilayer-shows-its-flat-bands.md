Moiré materials became interesting when twisted bilayer graphene produced flat electronic bands whose bandwidth could be tuned by the twist angle. A flat band concentrates many states at the same energy, and that concentration is the ingredient that turns a weak interaction into a correlated insulator or a superconductor. A flat band is easiest to detect when the states are close in energy and hardest when they are spread by disorder, which is why the quality of the sample determines whether the feature appears at all.

Twisted monolayer-bilayer graphene offers more knobs, because the two layers are not equivalent and an electric field can tilt the bands without changing the twist. Theory predicted flat bands in this stack and predicted that they would be narrower than in the bilayer case, and the prediction had not been checked by a measurement that could see the bands.

A group fabricated a single sample containing both stacks at exactly the same twist angle of about 1.13 degrees, which removes the variability that makes comparisons between samples unreliable, and imaged both with scanning tunnelling microscopy and spectroscopy. The work appeared in *Physical Review Letters*, 1 March 2022. Fabricating both stacks on one chip is the methodological advance, because twist angle varies by a fraction of a degree between samples and the bandwidth depends exponentially on that angle.

The tunnelling spectrum showed a sharp peak in the density of states near the Fermi energy, which is the signature of a flat band, and the width of that peak was slightly narrower than the corresponding feature in twisted bilayer graphene. That confirms the theoretical expectation and does so on the same substrate, with the same tip and in the same session. Spatially resolved spectroscopy added a feature that theory had predicted but not visualized. The flat-band states in the monolayer-on-bilayer stack are localized in one layer and extended in the other at the same time, a coexistence that arises from the different coupling of the two layers to the moiré potential. A state that is localized in the top layer can be tuned by a gate that couples to the bottom layer, which gives an experimentalist an electrical knob on a correlation that would otherwise be fixed by geometry.

That coexistence is useful. A state that is localized can host strong correlations, and a state that is extended can carry a current, so a single band that does both offers a way to couple a correlated phase to a metallic reservoir without adding a contact.

Scanning tunnelling microscopy measures the surface, and the buried bilayer contributes to the spectrum only through its influence on the top layer. The interpretation therefore relies on the continuum model that the authors use to assign the peaks, and a model-dependent assignment is not the same as a direct observation of the wavefunction. The natural next step is to dope the stack and look for the insulating and superconducting states that a narrow band supports, and the same sample geometry makes those measurements possible.

The result matters for the programme of moiré engineering, because it establishes that the monolayer-bilayer stack is a flatter and more tunable platform than the bilayer one. The next experiments will look for the correlated states that a narrow band is supposed to produce.
