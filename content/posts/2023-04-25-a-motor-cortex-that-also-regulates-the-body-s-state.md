---

The motor cortex has been mapped as a set of regions, each controlling a body part. A hand area moves the hand, a foot area moves the foot, and the organization has been a textbook diagram for a century.

The imaging in *Nature*, April 2023 complicates the diagram. The authors identify a network that alternates with the effector regions, responds to internal state as well as to movement, and integrates the two in a way that the classical map does not represent.

The network's position is the surprise. It sits between the effector areas rather than beside them, so that a map of the cortex by function alternates between action and regulation.

The response profile is the evidence. The network activates during tasks that involve effort, arousal and the coordination of breathing with movement, and its activity correlates with measures of the body's state rather than with a limb's displacement.

The functional interpretation is a control loop. A movement is not only a motor command; it is a command issued by an organism that has a blood pressure, a breath and a level of arousal, and the network is where those variables enter the command.

The clinical relevance is the map's use. A surgeon who plans an incision with a functional atlas relies on the atlas's categories, and a region that subserves both movement and regulation is a region whose damage produces more than weakness.

The comparative anatomy is suggestive. The network's location corresponds to areas that in other primates have been associated with visceral control, and the human map may be a specialization of a general plan.

The method's limitation is the correlation. Functional imaging shows where activity changes and not what the circuit computes, and the network's causal role is an inference.

What the paper cannot resolve is the development. A network that integrates action and state could be innate or learned, and the developmental data are not in the study.

For neuroscience, the value is a correction to a metaphor. The cortex is not a switchboard of muscles, and a map that says so is a map that misleads the physiologist and the surgeon alike.

The naming is deliberate. A somato-cognitive action network is a label that describes a function rather than an anatomy, and the authors choose it to signal that the region does not fit the old categories.

The task battery is broad. Effort, breath-holding and arousal are different manipulations, and the network's common response across them is what supports a single integrative role.
The boundary with the premotor areas is a second complication. A region that prepares a movement and a region that sets the body's state for it are adjacent, and the imaging cannot fully separate preparation from regulation. The authors' analysis of timing is the tool they use to try.
The atlas's revision has a practical edge. Deep-brain stimulation and surgical planning use coordinates referenced to the classical map, and a region whose function is broader than its label changes the risk calculation for an electrode placed beside it.
