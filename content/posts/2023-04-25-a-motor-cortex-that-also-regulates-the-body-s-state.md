Mapped as a set of regions, each controlling a body part, the motor cortex has been a textbook diagram for a century: a hand area moves the hand, a foot area moves the foot.

In *Nature* in April 2023, the diagram is complicated. The authors identify a network that alternates with the effector regions, responds to internal state as well as to movement, and integrates the two in a way the classical map cannot represent.

Position is the surprise. The network sits between the effector areas rather than beside them, so a map of the cortex by function alternates between action and regulation. The response profile carries the evidence, since the network activates during tasks that involve effort, arousal and the coordination of breathing with movement, and its activity correlates with measures of the body's state rather than with a limb's displacement.

Because a movement is not only a motor command but a command issued by an organism with a blood pressure, a breath and a level of arousal, the network is where those variables enter the command, which makes the interpretation a control loop rather than a switchboard. Naming reflects the mismatch: a somato-cognitive action network describes a function rather than an anatomy, a label chosen to signal that the region does not fit the old categories. The task battery is broad, and effort, breath-holding and arousal are different manipulations whose common response is what supports a single integrative role.

Surgical use gives the revision a practical edge. Deep-brain stimulation and operative planning reference coordinates from the classical map, and a region whose function is broader than its label changes the risk calculation for an electrode placed beside it. The boundary with premotor cortex complicates the reading further, because a region that prepares a movement and one that sets the body's state for it sit adjacent, and imaging cannot fully separate preparation from regulation, which is why the analysis of timing is the tool the authors use. Comparative anatomy is suggestive, since the network's location corresponds to areas associated with visceral control in other primates, and the human map may be a specialization of a general plan.

Correlation is the method's limit, because functional imaging shows where activity changes and not what the circuit computes, and the network's causal role stays an inference. Development is unresolved, since a network that integrates action and state could be innate or learned and the developmental data are absent from the study. The cortex is not a switchboard of muscles, and a map that says so misleads the physiologist and the surgeon alike, which is the correction on offer. A surgeon planning an incision with a functional atlas relies on the atlas's categories, and damage to a region serving both movement and regulation produces more than weakness.

Task design will decide how far the network's account travels, because a region defined by effort and arousal depends on manipulations that a clinical protocol cannot reproduce at a bedside.
