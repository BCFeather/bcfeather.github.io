Born into the autumn, an infant faces a virus for which no vaccine is licensed until the age of six months, and that gap between birth and the first immunization is where respiratory syncytial virus does most of its damage. Protection has to come from somewhere else in the meantime, and the mother is the obvious source.

In a trial reported by *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 5 April 2023, part of that window is closed from the mother's side. A prefusion-stabilized fusion protein vaccine given during pregnancy induces antibodies that cross the placenta, so the infant is born carrying a passive protection that lasts for the first months.

Timing carries the biology. Immunoglobulin G crosses the placenta actively in the third trimester, and a dose given in that window produces the highest cord blood titres, which makes the schedule a design variable rather than a detail. Those titres then fall over months, and the protection's duration follows the mother's response and the infant's catabolism, so a higher titre buys a longer window.

Because a vaccine given to a pregnant woman is a vaccine given to two patients, safety is the question that maternal immunization always carries, and the trial's monitoring of birth outcomes matters as much as its efficacy number. What the programme measures is severe lower respiratory tract infection in the first ninety days, the outcome a paediatric ward would notice.

The vaccine is a delivery choice rather than a rival to monoclonal antibody. An antibody given to the newborn works and needs an injection at birth, while a maternal dose works and needs a visit before birth, and the two are complementary. Seasonality complicates the choice, since a vaccine that protects for the first months has to be given at the right point in the year, and a pregnancy spanning the wrong season may deliver an infant after the virus has passed.

Three questions remain open. Protection is strongest against severe disease and weaker against mild illness, which is the trade a vaccine's economics has to accept. A pregnancy carries its own vaccine schedule, and whether the RSV dose interferes with the others is unsettled. Price and supply decide the rest, because an infant vaccine that protects in rich countries but not in poor ones has its benefit rationed by purchasing power. Sites in both hemispheres tested it against different seasonal curves, so a result that holds across them is not an artefact of one country's epidemic.

Implementation decides the arithmetic. A maternal dose reaches an infant only if the pregnancy is booked early enough for the visit to happen, and the coverage a trial achieves is not the coverage a health system delivers. Measuring the effect in a delivery suite and in a district clinic are different exercises, and only the second tells a ministry what an infant's first winter will actually cost, and that is the number a ministry budgets against.
