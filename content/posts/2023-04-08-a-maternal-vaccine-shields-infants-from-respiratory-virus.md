An infant born in autumn faces a virus for which no vaccine is licensed until the age of six months. The gap between birth and the first immunization is the window in which respiratory syncytial virus does most of its damage.

The trial reported by *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 5 April 2023 closes part of that window from the mother's side. A prefusion-stabilized fusion protein vaccine given during pregnancy induces antibodies that cross the placenta, and the infant is born with a passive protection that lasts for the first months.

The biology is placental transfer. Immunoglobulin G crosses the placenta actively in the third trimester, and a vaccine given in that window produces the highest cord blood titres. The timing of the dose is therefore a design variable rather than a detail.

The endpoint is infant illness. Severe lower respiratory tract infection in the first ninety days is the outcome that the trial measures, and a reduction in severe cases is the benefit that a paediatric ward would notice.

The safety question is the one that maternal vaccines always carry. A vaccine given to a pregnant woman is a vaccine given to two patients, and the trial's monitoring of birth outcomes is as important as its efficacy number.

The comparison with monoclonal antibody is the delivery question. A monoclonal antibody given to the newborn works and requires an injection at birth, and a maternal vaccine works and requires a visit before birth. The two are complementary rather than competing.

The seasonality is the implementation's problem. A vaccine that protects for the first months has to be given at the right point in the year, and a pregnancy that spans the wrong season may deliver an infant after the virus has passed.

The limitation is the efficacy's variation. The protection is strongest against severe disease, and the effect on mild illness is smaller, which is what a vaccine's economics has to accept.

What the trial cannot establish is the interaction with other vaccines. A pregnancy carries a schedule of vaccines, and whether the RSV dose interferes with or is compatible with the others is a question that the programme has to answer.

For obstetrics, the value is a new indication. A vaccine given in pregnancy for the infant's benefit is a category that has existed for tetanus and influenza, and RSV extends it.

The equity question is the price and the supply. A vaccine that protects infants in rich countries and not in poor ones is a vaccine whose benefit is rationed by purchasing power, and the global allocation is the next negotiation.

The antibody's decay sets the window. Cord blood titres fall over months, and the protection's duration is a function of the mother's response and the infant's catabolism. A vaccine that produces a high titre buys a longer window.

The trial's geography matters. Sites in both hemispheres test the vaccine against different seasonal patterns, and a result that holds across them is a result that is not an artefact of one country's epidemic curve.
