Flows of Russian gas through Ukraine stopped on 1 January, when a five-year transit agreement expired and Kyiv declined to renew it. The route carried about 15 billion cubic metres in 2024, against more than 80 billion in 2018, and the compressor stations that moved it are now idle. What ended was the last pipeline link between Russian fields and European buyers other than the two lines that run under the Black Sea, and the event was less a shock than the final settlement of a route that had been shrinking since 2022.

Slovakia, Hungary and Austria are the countries most exposed, because each of them sits at the end of the same corridor. Slovakia bought the largest share, and its contract with Gazprom had been renewed only after a political argument in Bratislava. Hungary imports most of its gas under a long-term agreement signed in 2021, and Austria's utility has said it expects to replace the volumes with LNG and with Norwegian deliveries. Czech buyers, who had already switched to German hubs, watched the expiry with little at stake.

Storage is the instrument that buys time. European reservoirs stood at about three-quarters full at the start of January, which is comfortable for a normal winter and thin for a cold one. A cold February would push withdrawals above the seasonal average and leave the continent entering the refill season with a deficit that the summer would have to close, and the summer is when LNG cargoes are contested with Asia.

Prices have not repeated the panic of 2022. Dutch benchmark contracts traded near forty-five euros per megawatt hour in the first week of the year, a level that hurts energy-intensive industry but does not ration household heat. The market's calm rests on the assumption that LNG supply continues to grow, and that assumption has been tested before.

Transit fees were a real source of revenue for the Ukrainian pipeline operator, worth several hundred million dollars a year. Losing them removes income that also paid for maintenance, and the network still has to be kept in a condition that would allow it to work again if a future settlement required it. Repair crews have been working on the system through three winters of war, and the pipes that are now idle are the ones they know best.

Moldova felt the change first. Gas to the breakaway region of Transnistria stopped, the power plant that serves both sides ran short of fuel, and households on the right bank were told to cut consumption. Moldova now buys on the European market at European prices, which is a solvency problem for an economy of that size. Europe's gas balance has been rebuilt around ships, storage and demand reduction, and the end of the Ukrainian route does not change the direction of that rebuild. It does change the arithmetic of a cold snap, because the cushion that a pipeline provides is measured in hours while the cushion that a ship provides is measured in weeks.
