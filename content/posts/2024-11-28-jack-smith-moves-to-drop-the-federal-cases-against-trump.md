Special counsel Jack Smith asked the courts on the twenty-fifth of November to dismiss the two federal prosecutions he had brought against Donald Trump, and the judge in the election case granted the request the same day. Filings cited the justice department's long-standing position that a sitting president cannot be prosecuted, which the department's legal counsel office set out in a memo in 2000 and which the supreme court endorsed in part in its ruling on immunity in July. Move ended two cases that had shaped American politics for eighteen months.

First case concerned the events of the sixth of January 2021 and the effort to overturn the 2020 election. Trump was charged in August 2023 with conspiracy to defraud the United States, obstruction of an official proceeding and conspiracy against rights, and the case was delayed for a year while appeals on immunity ran to the supreme court. Ruling in July held that presidents have broad immunity for official acts, and Smith's office subsequently filed a superseding indictment that removed some allegations and reframed others.

Second case concerned classified documents held at Mar-a-Lago after Trump left office, and it had been dismissed in July by a federal judge in Florida on the ground that the special counsel had been appointed unlawfully. Smith appealed that ruling, and the appeal was dropped with the other case. Charges against two co-defendants, including a property manager at the club, were also dismissed, which closed the only federal prosecution that had reached trial stage.

Timing of the request follows the calendar rather than the evidence. Incoming administration takes office on the twentieth of January, and the justice department's rule means the prosecutions could not continue once the president was sworn in. Smith's office said in its filing that the dismissal was required by the constitution and did not reflect the merits of the cases, which is the formulation officials have used in previous transitions. Report on the investigations, which the special counsel is required to produce, is expected before the inauguration, and its publication is a decision for the attorney general.

State cases are unaffected and remain the only criminal exposure the president-elect faces. Prosecutions in Georgia and in New York proceed on their own schedules, and a conviction in New York on charges relating to payments to a former adult film actress is scheduled for sentencing, though the judge has signalled that no custodial sentence will be imposed before the term begins. Federal pardons cannot reach state convictions, and the power to pardon federal offences does not extend to cases brought by a state.

Legacy of the two prosecutions is now a question for historians and for the institutions that brought them. Special counsel was appointed to remove political interference from the cases, and the outcome has been that the cases ended because of an election. Argument in the filings, that the rule protects the office rather than the man, is the same argument that critics of the rule have rejected for years, and the practical result is that a president can end a prosecution against himself by being elected. Whether that is a defect in the constitution or a deliberate feature of it is a question the courts have declined to answer.
