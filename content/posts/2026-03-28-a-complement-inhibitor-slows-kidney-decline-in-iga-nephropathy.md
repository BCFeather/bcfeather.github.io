IgA nephropathy is the most common primary glomerular disease in the world and it has been treated with supportive care for decades, because nothing targeted its mechanism. The mechanism is known: immune complexes containing galactose-deficient IgA1 deposit in the glomerulus and activate the alternative complement pathway, and the activation damages the filtration barrier. A drug that blocks that pathway should slow the decline, and until this trial the evidence for such a drug was limited to proteinuria.

Iptacopan is an oral inhibitor of factor B, the amplification enzyme of the alternative pathway, and the trial randomized 477 patients with IgA nephropathy to iptacopan or placebo for 24 months. The primary measure was the annualized slope of estimated glomerular filtration rate, which is the endpoint regulators now accept because it predicts progression better than a single proteinuria value. The results appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 28 March as final 24-month data.

The slope was minus 3.10 millilitres per minute per 1.73 square metres per year with iptacopan against minus 6.12 with placebo, a difference of 3.02 with a confidence interval from 2.02 to 4.01. The composite kidney failure endpoint occurred in 21.4 per cent of the treated group against 33.5 per cent of the placebo group, a hazard ratio of 0.57 with a confidence interval from 0.40 to 0.81.

Halving the rate of decline is the kind of result that changes a disease's trajectory if it holds. A patient losing six millilitres a minute per year reaches dialysis in a decade from a starting filtration rate of sixty, and a patient losing three reaches it in two decades, which is the difference between a working life interrupted and a working life completed.

Safety was consistent with the mechanism's risk. Complement inhibition predisposes to encapsulated bacterial infection, and any trial of a complement drug has to show that the rate of serious infection is not unacceptable. Adverse events occurred in 87.0 per cent of the treated group and 89.1 per cent of the placebo group, and serious adverse events in 12.2 and 11.7 per cent, which is a profile that a nephrologist can accept.

The endpoint is a slope, and a slope measured over two years in a trial of this size is a statistical construct rather than an outcome that a patient experiences. The composite endpoint of kidney failure is the harder number, and it moved in the same direction, which gives the slope credibility. Long-term data on dialysis and transplantation are the numbers that a health system will eventually want.

Where this leaves treatment is a choice between supportive care and a targeted drug that costs a great deal. The supportive standard includes blood pressure control, renin-angiotensin blockade and, in recent years, a dual endothelin-angiotensin receptor antagonist, and the new trial's placebo arm received that care. Patients will be offered a combination, and the question becomes how many can afford it.
