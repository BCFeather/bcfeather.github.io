Fixture congestion is usually discussed as a fairness issue between clubs and a scheduling issue for broadcasters. A study published this month in a sports medicine journal treats it as a physiology problem: it measured recovery in 180 players across a competition where three matches fall within eight days and two of them require long-haul travel.

The measurement is practical rather than laboratory. Players wore GPS units and completed a daily questionnaire scored on sleep, soreness and perceived recovery, and the study measured creatine kinase, a marker of muscle damage, from capillary blood on the morning after each match.

Recovery time is the finding. After a match with no travel, creatine kinase returns to baseline in 48 to 60 hours. After a match followed by a flight of more than six hours, the return takes 84 to 100 hours, which does not fit into an eight-day schedule with three matches.

Injury incidence follows. The rate of soft-tissue injury in the third match of a congested block was 2.1 per 1,000 hours, against 0.9 in matches played after a full week. The excess concentrates in hamstrings and calves, which are the muscles the study's load data show remain fatigued longest.

Travel is the mechanism the paper isolates. A long flight causes fluid shifts, disrupts sleep and reduces the range of motion on landing, and the study's data show that players who flew business class recovered 12 hours faster than those who flew economy. That is a finding clubs can act on.

Mitigation is limited by the calendar. A team can rotate players, which the study's data support: squads that rotated at least five starters in the third match had half the injury rate. Rotation is the only lever available once the dates are fixed.

The governing body's role is the structural question. The competition's dates are set by a broadcast contract, and the study's authors argue for a minimum recovery standard, 72 hours between matches plus travel time, to be written into the competition regulations.

The competition's own data support the argument. Its medical staff recorded 240 soft-tissue injuries last season and found that 38 per cent occurred in a match played fewer than 96 hours after a previous one with travel.

Broadcast scheduling is the structural cause. A match window that spans continents for a television audience determines the kick-off times and therefore the travel, and the study's authors note that no medical standard can override a contract. That is why their recommendation is addressed to the competition's regulations rather than to clubs.

The players' association is the actor with leverage. Its collective agreement specifies a minimum interval between matches, and the study's 96-hour figure is above the contractual minimum of 72. A renegotiation is the instrument that would change the schedule.

For a reader, the interesting number is 40 hours. That is the effective recovery window in a congested block, and it is less than the time a hamstring needs to heal microdamage. The schedule is not merely demanding; it is shorter than the physiology.
