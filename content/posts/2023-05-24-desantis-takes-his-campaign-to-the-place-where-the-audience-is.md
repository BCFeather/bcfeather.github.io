Ron DeSantis, the governor of Florida, announced on 24 May that he would seek the Republican nomination for president, doing so in a live audio conversation on Twitter with Elon Musk rather than in a speech in front of a crowd. The event started twenty minutes late and lost its audio for a while, in a technical failure that produced more commentary than anything the candidate said. When the sound returned, the governor described his record and his intentions in the flat, list-based manner that his supporters call discipline and his critics call absence of warmth.

The choice of venue was the message. DeSantis built his national reputation on the argument that Florida was what the country could be if it were governed by someone who would fight, and he has spent two years fighting institutions that he regards as politically hostile: the federal government over pandemic rules, Disney over its special district, the College Board over a course in African American studies, and the press. Announcing on the platform of the world's richest man, in the company of a man who has become a symbol of refusal, was a way of locating himself in that fight rather than in the party that he joined.

His record in Florida is the substance of the campaign and a mixed document. He signed a law in April that bans abortion after six weeks, which is one of the strictest in the country and a position that most Republican donors want and most general-election voters do not. He sent migrants to Martha's Vineyard. He banned instruction on gender identity in schools and took control of a small liberal arts college. He also governed through the pandemic with an approach that kept businesses open, which is the part of his record that travels best.

Its problem is structural. Trump's hold on the party's base has not weakened after the indictments and the verdict of the civil trial, and his supporters treat competition as disloyalty. DeSantis enters a field in which the second place is occupied by a man who is also running against the same enemy, and where the money and the media attention are concentrated at the top. His likely strategy is to survive the early states, to present himself to the party's donors as the only alternative who can win a general election, and to hope that the frontrunner's legal troubles become decisive.

Party rules make that strategy uncertain. Several states have changed their primary calendar and their delegate allocation in ways that reward loyalty and punish deviation, and the Republican National Committee has set a debate threshold that will keep the field on a single stage. A campaign built around a governor who has never held federal office has to demonstrate that it can raise money from people who have already given it to the incumbent, and the first filings of the quarter will tell whether that is happening.

What the launch demonstrated is how far the mechanics of American politics have moved. A candidacy announced on a social platform, in a conversation with its owner, watched by hundreds of thousands of people and heard by almost none of them, is a description of a country where attention is the first primary. DeSantis will spend the summer trying to convert that attention into a coalition, and his opponents will spend it arguing that attention is exactly what he does not have.
