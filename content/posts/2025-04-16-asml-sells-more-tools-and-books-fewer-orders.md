A lithography company is a leading indicator with a lag. ASML sells the machines that print the smallest features on a chip, and its order book reflects decisions that chipmakers take years before the capacity arrives. Sales in a quarter describe tools ordered long ago, while bookings describe what customers commit to now. The two numbers therefore disagree regularly, and the disagreement is the interesting part of any ASML report.

The first quarter of 2025, reported on 16 April, kept that pattern. Total net sales came in at 7.7 billion euros, inside guidance, with a gross margin of 54.0 per cent and net income of 2.4 billion euros. Installed base business contributed 2 billion euros of the sales, the recurring revenue from service and field upgrades. Net bookings were 3.9 billion euros, of which 1.2 billion was for extreme ultraviolet systems, against 7.1 billion in the fourth quarter.

Margin came in above guidance for two reasons, and both were mix. More of the EUV shipments were the newer NXE:3800 rather than the NXE:3600, and the configurations of those tools were richer than planned, so the average selling price rose without a price increase. Customers also took delivery earlier than the schedule assumed. A 54 per cent margin on a guided 52 to 54 per cent range is a small beat produced by arithmetic and not by demand.

Guidance held. Second-quarter sales were guided to 7.2 to 7.7 billion euros with a margin of 50 to 53 per cent, and the full year stayed at 30 to 35 billion euros with a margin of 51 to 53 per cent. Keeping a range that wide tells investors the second half depends on decisions customers have not yet taken. The bookings number is the evidence for that caution.

Why bookings halved is a question about memory and about China. The fourth quarter had carried a large volume of logic orders, some of them pulled forward, and the first quarter of a year is seasonally thin. Memory makers had been buying for a recovery in high-bandwidth memory, and that buying is lumpy. Chinese customers had also been stocking tools ahead of export controls, and the pace of that stockpiling depends on rules and not on demand.

The tariff question sits over the whole calculation. ASML does not make chips, so it is not directly exposed to a tariff on chips, but its customers are, and a customer facing an uncertain price for the thing it sells delays the machine that makes it. The company said as much without putting a number on it, which is the standard way a capital-goods supplier discusses a demand shock it cannot measure.

What would settle the year is the second and third quarter bookings, and specifically the split between logic and memory. A recovery in memory orders would show that the artificial intelligence build-out has reached the part of the industry that supplies it with ordinary DRAM. A further decline would show that the build-out is concentrated in a few customers, and that the rest of the industry is waiting.
