date: "2022-08-17T23:40:00+08:00"

description: "The July figure is the highest since 1982, driven by food and by fuel, and the forecast for October is worse."
---

Consumer prices rose by ten point one per cent in the year to July, the first double-digit figure since February 1982, and the monthly increase was point six per cent. The office that produces the numbers said food prices had risen by twelve point seven per cent, which is the fastest since 2008, and that the price of bread, milk and vegetables had all contributed. The price of motor fuel rose again after a fall in June, and the cost of gas and electricity began to reflect the cap that was set in April. The figure was above the ten point one that the economists had expected and it was the seventh consecutive month of acceleration.

The Bank of England had raised its rate by half a point in early August, the largest increase in twenty-seven years, and it said at the time that it expected inflation to reach thirteen per cent in October. The forecast was based on the assumption that the cap on energy bills would be raised again in the autumn, and the regulator is due to announce the new level at the end of the month. The bank's governor said in a press conference that the country was facing a real shock and that the bank could not prevent it, only reduce the second-round effects.

The distribution of the increase is the part that matters for the politics. The poorest households spend a larger share of their income on food and energy, and the increases in those two categories are the largest. The think tanks that model the effect said a household in the bottom tenth would be spending about eighteen per cent more than a year ago on the same basket, and that the rise for the top tenth would be closer to twelve. The government has announced a payment of four hundred pounds to every household and a larger one to those on benefits, and the payments began in the spring.

Wages have not kept pace. The figures published the same week showed that regular pay had fallen by about three per cent in real terms, which is the largest fall since the records began in 2001. The labour market is still tight, with more vacancies than unemployed people, and the bank has said that the tightness is the reason it expects inflation to last. The employers' organizations said that they could not raise pay further without raising prices, and the unions said that their members were taking a cut.

At the same time, the political consequence is a government that has to act and a campaign that has to wait. The prime minister is leaving office in September and the two candidates to replace him have promised different things, one of them a package of tax cuts and the other a package of payments. The treasury has said that either would require borrowing, and the markets have begun to price a higher level of debt. The household bills that arrive in October will be the first test of whichever policy is chosen.

What the number does is make the autumn a season of arithmetic. The energy cap, the inflation figure and the interest rate are now three numbers that decide the budget of every household, and none of them is set by the government alone. The bank expects the peak in October and a slow decline after that, and the government expects the voters to notice the decline before the next election. Both forecasts assume that the war ends and that the price of gas falls, and neither of those is in anybody's control.
