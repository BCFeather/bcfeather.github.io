description: "In 1,306 patients with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer, adding darolutamide to androgen deprivation and docetaxel cut the risk of death by 32.5 per cent."
---

Metastatic prostate cancer used to be treated with androgen deprivation alone, and the arrival of docetaxel and then of potent androgen receptor inhibitors turned a single drug into a combination question. Which agents to give together, and whether adding a third helps, has been settled trial by trial by principle rather than by precedent. Each addition was tested against the standard of its own era, which is why the trials are difficult to compare with one another.

Darolutamide is an androgen receptor inhibitor with a structure that keeps it out of the brain more than earlier drugs of its class, which matters because dizziness and seizures limit the others. The ARASENS trial randomized 1,306 patients with metastatic hormone-sensitive disease to darolutamide at 600 milligrams twice daily or placebo, both with androgen deprivation and docetaxel. The paper appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 17 February 2022. Docetaxel was given for six cycles and androgen deprivation continued throughout, so the comparison isolates the contribution of the new drug.

At the primary analysis, with a cutoff of 25 October 2021, the risk of death was 32.5 per cent lower in the darolutamide group, a hazard ratio of 0.68 with an interval from 0.57 to 0.80. Of the 1,306 patients, 651 received darolutamide and 655 placebo, and 86.1 per cent had metastatic disease at initial diagnosis after treatment rather than at diagnosis. The secondary endpoints, including time to castration resistance and time to pain progression, also favoured the triplet.

The design answers a specific question that earlier trials left open. Adding a third agent to a two-drug backbone is worthwhile only if the benefit is large enough to justify the toxicity and cost, and a hazard ratio of 0.68 in this setting is the kind of effect that changes a standard of care. An absolute difference in survival at the cutoff is harder to quote than a hazard ratio, and the hazard ratio is what the trial was powered to measure.

Adverse events were similar between the groups, which is the claim that distinguishes darolutamide from its predecessors. The earlier generation of receptor inhibitors carried a higher burden of fatigue, seizures and falls, and a patient population that is often elderly notices those effects. The similarity in adverse events held for fatigue, hypertension and fractures, which are the events that matter most in this population.

The trial does not report quality-of-life outcomes in the abstract, and overall survival is the endpoint that regulators and clinicians weight most. The control arm also received docetaxel, which is not universal practice in every country, so the comparison is with a specific standard and not with all standards.

Follow-up continues, and the question of sequencing remains. A patient who progresses on this triplet has few options left, and the next trials will ask whether the same benefit can be obtained by giving the drugs in a different order. Whether the triplet should be given to patients whose disease is hormone-sensitive but not yet metastatic is a separate question.
