Two astronauts reached the International Space Station on 6 June in a capsule built by a private contractor, a day after launching from Florida. The vehicle docked despite two helium leaks in its propulsion system that were detected during the approach. The launch had been delayed three times, twice by a valve in the rocket and once by a leak in the capsule itself, and the managers said they had accepted a level of risk they would not accept for a longer mission.

Certification is the context. The capsule had been cleared to fly a crew once, with the defects that were found in an earlier unmanned test to be reviewed afterwards, and the agency accepted the risk on the understanding that the problems were understood. A vehicle that flies once before the review is a vehicle whose second flight depends on the first. The earlier unmanned flight produced a list of defects that the contractor fixed, and the reviewers concluded that the remaining ones did not threaten the crew on a short trip.

Leaks are a design issue rather than an accident. Helium is used to pressurize the propellant, and a seal that leaks under vibration is a seal that will leak again, which is why the engineers described the problem as one that has to be fixed rather than managed. A seal that is the wrong material is a fix that requires a redesign. The propellant system has to work for the manoeuvres that bring the capsule out of orbit, and a leak that empties a tank would leave the crew without the margin to perform them.

Crew's return is the open question. The capsule is the lifeboat for the station while it is docked, and the agency has to be confident that it can bring the crew down before it can certify the next flight. A return that is delayed is a schedule that slips for everything downstream. The agency has said it will not certify the next flight until the leaks are understood, and the contractor has said the fix may require a change in the material of the seal that holds the helium.

Contract is the political economy. The agency pays for the service rather than for the vehicle, and the fixed-price arrangement means that the delays cost the contractor rather than the taxpayer. A contract that transfers the risk is a contract that changes the incentives to report problems. The fixed-price contract has already produced losses for the contractor, and the company has said it will not bid for the next round of missions on the same terms.

What the docking shows is that a commercial crew programme can deliver a vehicle and still not deliver a routine, and the routine is what the agency needs before it can stop paying for its own rockets. A programme that is safe and slow is the one the agency chose, and the choice is visible in the schedule that the crew's return now depends on.
