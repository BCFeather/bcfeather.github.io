Barrier films are judged by what gets through them. Packaging for food, displays and solar modules relies on polymers that slow the passage of oxygen and water vapour, and the standard materials achieve that by being thick and semi-crystalline: the crystals force a tortuous path, and thickness makes the path long. A polymer that is amorphous and nanometres thin should, by that logic, be a poor barrier.

The films reported in *Nature*, 13 November 2025 invert the logic. They are made from two-dimensional polyaramids, polymers whose backbones are designed to lie in a plane, and they stack into layers a few nanometres thick. The measured gas permeability is the lowest reported for any polymer, lower by orders of magnitude than the semi-crystalline barriers, and the films are not crystalline at all.

The reason is geometry. In a conventional polymer, gas diffuses through the free volume between tangled chains, and the only way to reduce that volume is to pack the chains more tightly or make the path longer. In a planar polymer, the chains stack face to face, and the free volume between the planes is not a network of channels but a set of narrow gaps that a molecule cannot traverse without breaking the stacking.

The second consequence is mechanical. A film a few nanometres thick that carries a pressure difference is a membrane, and membranes vibrate. The group uses that: the same films serve as nanomechanical resonators, where a vibrating membrane's frequency reports its mass and tension. A barrier and a sensor made of the same material is an unusual combination, and it suggests that the films are stiff enough to be useful as structures rather than only as coatings.

Processing is where the claim will be tested. Two-dimensional polymers are difficult to make in solution and difficult to transfer without defects, and a single pinhole dominates the permeability of a thin film. The paper reports films that are continuous over the areas measured, which is the result that matters, but the areas are small compared with a display panel.

The comparison with existing barriers is also a comparison of thickness. A 20-nanometre film with the permeability of a 20-micrometre one would replace a coating with a monolayer, which changes the economics of encapsulation: less material, less weight, and no drying step. Whether the films survive bending, thermal cycling and the solvents of a manufacturing line is the engineering question that follows.

For now the result is a materials argument: impermeability is a matter of how the chains are arranged, not of how many of them there are. A polymer that is thin because it does not need to be thick is a different starting point for every application that has been designing around diffusion.

There is a chemical question behind the stacking. Aramid bonds are stiff and planar because the hydrogen bonds between chains hold them in register, and the same interaction that makes a fibre strong makes a film dense. The group's synthesis controls where those bonds form, so that the sheets align across the whole film rather than forming domains that leave gaps at their boundaries.
