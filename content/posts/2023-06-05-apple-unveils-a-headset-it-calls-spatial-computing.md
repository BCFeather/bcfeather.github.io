Apple used the opening of its developer conference on 5 June to announce Vision Pro, a headset that it describes with a phrase it has spent months rehearsing: spatial computing. The device covers the eyes, tracks them, and overlays applications on the room in front of the wearer, and it is controlled by gaze, by the movement of the hands and by the voice, with no handheld controller at all. Apple put the price at three thousand four hundred and ninety-nine dollars and said it would go on sale in the United States early next year, with other markets later.

The hardware is unusually dense for a first-generation product. Two chips do the work, an M2 for the computing and a new R1 for the sensor processing, twelve cameras and five sensors map the room and track the body, and the whole assembly is machined from aluminium with a curved glass front. The battery sits outside the headset, connected by a cable, and provides about two hours of use, which is the detail that most clearly marks the device as a first attempt rather than a finished one. Apple's answer to the obvious objection is that a battery on the head is heavier than a battery in the pocket.

Apple chose demonstrations that make the case for a work device as much as an entertainment one. A virtual desktop of windows floating in a room, a three-dimensional model of a product, a film watched on a screen the size of a wall, a video call in which each participant sits in a spatial frame. The most striking demo was a photograph that became a life-sized scene, and the feature is part of the pitch: the headset records three-dimensional video, and Apple is selling the idea that families will want to keep their memories in that format.

Scepticism is warranted and has been rehearsed already. Headsets have been on sale for a decade and have not become a mass market, the price is that of a used car, and the use cases that justify wearing a device on the face for hours have not been demonstrated by anyone. Apple's own presentation leaned on solitary experiences, which is an odd way to launch a product whose promise is presence. The developers who build for it will need a reason that consumers can explain to themselves, and the company has given them a good camera to record with while it waits for them to find one.

Still, the announcement is consequential for the industry, because Apple has a record of entering a category late and defining it anyway. The watch was dismissed as a phone accessory and became a health device; the tablet was dismissed as a large phone and became the default computer for a great many people. Vision Pro may follow the same path or it may not, and the answer will be decided by what developers do with it in the next eighteen months. What is already clear is that the company has committed to a platform, that the components inside it are the best available, and that the price is high enough to make the first version a statement rather than a product.
