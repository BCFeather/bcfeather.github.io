Breathing on land required a new machine. A fish extracts oxygen from water with gills, and an animal that leaves the water has to bring air into a lung and then move it, and the two ways of moving it are by pumping the throat or by expanding the ribcage. Throat pumping is cheap and inefficient, ribcage breathing is expensive and efficient, and the fossil record of when the second replaced the first has been almost empty, because cartilage and soft tissue decay.

Fossils from a cave system in what is now Oklahoma preserve what usually decays. The specimens, dated to between 289 and 286 million years ago, were encased in fine clay and saturated with oil, and the combination sealed the tissue before bacteria could consume it. Robert Reisz, Ethan Mooney and colleagues describe them in *Nature* on 9 April.

The animal is *Captorhinus*, an early reptile of the early Permian, and what survived is remarkable: three-dimensional skin, cartilage around the ribs and shoulders, and traces of protein in cartilage, bone and skin. Protein surviving for nearly 290 million years is at the edge of what is possible, and it is the oldest such trace known from a land vertebrate.

Anatomically, the specimens carry a cartilaginous sternum in several parts, at least four pairs of sternal ribs, intermediate ribs, long cervical rib extensions and further cartilage. The arrangement connects the ribcage to the shoulder girdle and produces a ribcage that could expand and contract as a unit, which is the geometry of a bellows and the geometry that living reptiles use.

What that means for the history of breathing is that the rib-based system was already in place early. The earliest amniotes are often described as throat breathers that later evolved rib ventilation, and these fossils place a functional ribcage within a few tens of millions of years of the group's origin, which shortens the transitional period considerably.

The find also bears on the invasion of land more generally. A ribcage that ventilates efficiently supports a higher metabolic rate, and a higher metabolic rate supports sustained activity, which is the capacity that allowed amniotes to occupy dry habitats where amphibians could not. The breathing system and the body plan are not separate stories.

Interpretation beyond the anatomy requires care. The cartilage is preserved as a mineral replica whose original composition has to be inferred, and the protein traces are identified by chemical signature rather than by sequence. Both are strong evidence of what the tissue was and weaker evidence of exactly how it functioned, and function is reconstructed by comparison with living reptiles. Further collecting is the obvious next step, and the site appears to have more to give. A cave that preserves cartilage once may preserve it again, and each additional individual adds the kind of variation that one specimen cannot supply. For a question that has been answered by inference for a century, that is the most useful thing a new site can offer.
