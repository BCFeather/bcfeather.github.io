Three-dimensional printing usually works by accumulation. A nozzle deposits material at a point, a laser cures a point in a vat of resin, and the object grows because the tool visits every location in turn, which is why printing time scales with volume. Direct sound printing replaced the tool with ultrasound, using cavitation bubbles that collapse violently enough to heat the liquid around them and trigger a chemical reaction, and it still moved a focal spot through the material point by point.

A group led by Derayatifar took the next step, replacing the focal spot with an acoustic hologram. A hologram is a phase pattern that shapes a wavefront so that the pressure field at an image plane matches a chosen picture, and in this case the picture is a cross-section of the part being printed. Cavitation bubbles then appear across the whole plane at once, and the material polymerizes in a single exposure. The work appeared in *Nature Communications* and was summarized in *Science* on 17 September.

Speed is the headline, and it is measured against the scanning method it replaces. Holographic direct sound printing runs about an order of magnitude faster than the point-by-point version, because the number of exposures no longer scales with the number of voxels in the object. The authors also demonstrated printing through a physical barrier, curing an object behind a layer of porcine tissue, which is the kind of capability that makes a technique interesting to surgeons rather than only to manufacturers.

Penetration is the reason sound was chosen in the first place. Light cannot reach far into an opaque medium, and ultrasound can, so a printing method based on cavitation can work inside materials and behind obstacles that would block a laser. The cost is resolution, since the acoustic wavelength sets the smallest feature the hologram can address, and the pressure field has to be shaped precisely enough that cavitation occurs where intended and nowhere else.

Control of cavitation is the fragile part of the method. Bubbles that collapse in the wrong place damage the material, and the threshold between polymerization and disruption is narrow, so the hologram has to hold pressure within a band across the whole plane. The paper reports the tolerances it achieved, and those numbers will decide whether the technique moves beyond demonstration parts.

Applications are speculative and plausible. A printer that cures a whole layer at once and works through tissue could place material inside a body during surgery, repair a structure that cannot be opened, or manufacture in environments where a nozzle cannot reach. Each of those requires biocompatible chemistry, and the chemistry is a separate problem from the acoustics.

What the result establishes is a principle: the tool does not have to be a point. Holography turned a scanning method into a parallel one, and the same trick that made optical projection lithography fast has now been applied to sound.
