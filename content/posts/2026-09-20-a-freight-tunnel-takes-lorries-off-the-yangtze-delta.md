A railway tunnel under the Yangtze opened to freight traffic this month, connecting the container yards on the north bank near Nantong with the rail network that feeds the ports around Shanghai. The tunnel is a little over ten kilometres long and carries only freight, which makes it unusual: most crossings of the river in the delta are bridges shared with road traffic or tunnels designed for passenger trains. Its purpose is to move containers between the north bank and the deep-water terminals on the south side without sending lorries through the bridges that already saturate at peak hours.

The delta's geography explains the investment. The Yangtze widens into an estuary tens of kilometres across, and the two banks have developed as separate economies: the south side with the ports, financial districts and the dense manufacturing of Suzhou and Kunshan, the north side with cheaper land, chemical plants and a growing share of the region's container stuffing. Freight between them has had to cross by road, on bridges that were designed to carry passenger traffic, and by ferry, which is slow and weather-dependent. A dedicated rail link replaces a road trip of ninety minutes with a rail haul of twenty.

The engineering is the sort that goes unnoticed when it works. A tunnel under a major river has to be built without interrupting navigation, and the water above it carries some of the busiest shipping in the world. The line was constructed with a shield machine working from the north bank, and the railway was laid with a grade steep enough to clear the river bed but shallow enough for freight trains to climb with a full load. The operators say the design capacity is several tens of thousands of containers a day once the terminal connections are complete.

The effect on the roads is the immediate gain. Container lorries are heavy and slow, and they are the traffic that a bridge carries least efficiently. Removing even a modest share of them from the crossings improves the journey for everyone else, and the provincial transport department expects the tunnel to take a significant slice of the north-south container flow during the autumn peak. The longer-term effect is on the ports, which can now treat the north bank as part of their hinterland rather than as a separate market reached by road.

The costs and the limits are familiar from every large infrastructure project in the delta. The tunnel's capacity is finite, and the terminals it serves are themselves being expanded, so the link will be saturated within a decade if the traffic grows as forecast. Rail freight in China is cheap for bulk and awkward for containers, because the last mile usually needs a lorry anyway, and the tunnel solves the river crossing without solving the yard. And the money spent on the tunnel could have been spent on the barges that already move containers along the river at a fraction of the energy per tonne.

None of that diminishes what opened this month. A delta that moves containers by rail under its own river is a delta with one fewer bottleneck, and bottlenecks are what decide whether a port's costs are competitive. The tunnel will be judged in the accounts of the shipping lines that use it, which is a quieter test than a ribbon-cutting and a more honest one.
