Microsoft announced a chip on 19 February that it describes as the first topological qubit, built from indium arsenide nanowires and aluminium films grown on top of them. The company says the device hosts Majorana zero modes, quasiparticles that are their own antiparticles and that store information non-locally. The announcement was accompanied by a paper and by a claim about a material the company calls a topoconductor. The material stack was grown layer by layer in a laboratory the company built for the purpose, and the nanowires are its own design.

Topological qubits are attractive because they would be tolerant of noise by construction. Information encoded in a pair of Majorana modes is stored in the relation between them rather than at a point, so a local disturbance does not destroy it. That would remove much of the error correction overhead that superconducting machines carry, and it is the reason Microsoft has pursued the approach for nearly twenty years. The alternative approaches buy noise tolerance with error correction, which costs qubits, and the two strategies are competing for the same budget.

The evidentiary problem is old. A Majorana mode shows up as a zero-bias conductance peak in a tunnelling measurement, and disorder, Andreev states and poorly understood interfaces can produce peaks that look the same. A series of experimental papers in the 2010s was retracted or reinterpreted for that reason, and the field has been cautious since. A peak that appears at zero bias can also come from a quantum dot, and the experiments that distinguish the two are delicate.

What the new chip adds is a device rather than a signature. The company reports measurements on a qubit that can be operated and read out, with a parity lifetime that it says is long enough to be useful, and it reports the fabrication of a stack that can be made on a wafer. A working device is a stronger claim than a conductance peak, and it is not yet the same as a demonstrated topological qubit. The reported device operated at millikelvin temperatures, which is the same regime as superconducting qubits and no easier to engineer.

Independent verification is the step that will decide the argument. The paper reports internal measurements, and the materials stack is proprietary, which limits replication. A claim that cannot be checked by a rival laboratory is a claim that has to be accepted on the reputation of the group making it, and physics has a long history of resisting that. Publishing the fabrication recipe would let rivals check the claim, and it would also hand them the process the company spent a decade developing.

What it changes is the roadmap conversation. If the approach works, the path to a useful machine runs through growing nanowire arrays with uniform quality rather than through building larger dilution refrigerators. If it does not, the field has spent another decade on a beautiful idea, and the superconducting teams will have been right to bet on engineering over elegance. A useful topological machine would also need gates, and the company has published less about how two of these qubits interact.
