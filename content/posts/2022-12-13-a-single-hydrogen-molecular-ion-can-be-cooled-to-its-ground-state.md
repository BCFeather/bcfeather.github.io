Molecular hydrogen is the simplest neutral molecule, and its ion, \(\mathrm{H}_{2}^{+}\), is the simplest molecular ion, with two protons and one electron. Spectroscopy of that ion is a test of the theory of molecular structure, and the theory has been tested with beams rather than with single ions, since a beam averages over velocities. Benchmark status comes from the ratio of masses, since two protons and an electron give a rotational and a vibrational structure that no other light molecule reproduces as cleanly.

A single ion in a trap is the setting that removes the averaging. The ion is held in a Paul trap at cryogenic temperature, and its motion is cooled until it occupies the ground state of the trap, which makes its spectrum sharp. Work posted to arXiv on 13 December 2022 reaches that state for \(\mathrm{H}_{2}^{+}\), co-trapping it with a beryllium ion. Confinement is what makes the experiment possible at all, and the electrode geometry sets the frequency of the motion that then has to be cooled.

Co-trapping is the technique. A single \(\mathrm{H}_{2}^{+}\) is too light to be cooled directly by the usual methods, and a neighbouring \(\mathrm{^{9}Be}^{+}\) ion provides the electric field and the cooling that the light ion lacks. The pair is then a two-body system in a shared trap, and the modes of motion are collective rather than individual. Collective modes are the ones that the beryllium ion can cool, and the light ion inherits the cooling through the Coulomb interaction between them.

Chemical lifetime set the first limit. \(\mathrm{H}_{2}^{+}\) reacts with residual gas and with the trap surfaces, and the authors measure the lifetime as a function of apparatus temperature, reaching \(11^{+6}_{-3}\) hours at 10 K. Hours rather than seconds is what makes a spectroscopy experiment feasible, since the state has to be prepared and read before it decays. Reaction with residual water is the dominant loss channel, and the measured temperature dependence is the evidence that it is.

Sideband cooling brought the motion to the ground state. Two modes of translational motion reach average phonon numbers of 0.07(1) and 0.05(1), which correspond to 22(1) and 55(3) microkelvin. A phonon number below one in ten means the ion spends most of its time in the lowest level, which is the condition for a resolved spectrum. Microkelvin temperatures are the regime where the Doppler effect stops mattering, and reaching them is the whole point of the cooling effort.

What the paper does not do is perform the spectroscopy, which it prepares the ground for. Quantum logic spectroscopy is the method that would follow, transferring information from the molecular ion to the beryllium ion through their shared motion. The transfer needs a shared mode of motion, and the co-trapping already supplies one, which is why this cooling result doubles as the preparation for spectroscopy.

The same scheme is expected to work for other light ions, and the authors name \(\mathrm{HD}^{+}\), \(\mathrm{H}_{3}^{+}\) and \(\mathrm{He}^{+}\) as candidates, which makes the result a platform rather than a single measurement. A platform claim is worth making only if the scheme is transferable, and the authors argue that nothing in the cooling is specific to hydrogen.
