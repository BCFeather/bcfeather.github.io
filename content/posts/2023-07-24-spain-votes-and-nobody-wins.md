Feijoo claimed victory on the night of 23 July with thirty-three per cent of the vote and 136 seats, a gain of more than forty seats, and he was right about the arithmetic and wrong about what it means. His People's Party won the election and could not form a government. The Socialists of Pedro Sanchez held 122 seats with thirty-one point seven per cent, an increase of two, and the far-right Vox took thirty-three, less than it had expected. Sumar, the new leftist alliance, held thirty-one. Between them nobody had 176.

The campaign had been fought as a referendum on the coalition that governed Spain since 2020. Sanchez had governed with Unidas Podemos, presided over a rising minimum wage and record employment, and presided as well over inflation that made groceries expensive and housing impossible for young people. Its record on employment was the best in the European Union and its record on rents was the worst in living memory, and both were true at once. Feijoo offered competence and the promise of a single-party government with Vox outside the cabinet, and found in the closing week that a large share of his voters wanted him to depend on the far right less than he was willing to say.

Turnout reached sixty-six per cent, up slightly on the last election, and the result was close enough that both sides spent the next morning explaining why the other had lost. Nationally the Senate flipped to the People's Party with an absolute majority, which matters for the next government's ability to legislate and for the opposition's ability to delay. Catalonia delivered seven seats to Junts and seven to the Republican Left, and both of those parties have a price.

Sanchez's path runs through a party he has fought for six years. Junts, led by Carles Puigdemont from exile in Brussels, would have to support a government in Madrid in exchange for concessions on amnesty for the people prosecuted over the unilateral independence attempt of 2017. The alternative is another election in the winter, which every party except the far right would rather avoid and none can rule out.

The economics of the stalemate are plain. Spain holds the presidency of the European Union's council until December, its economy has outperformed its neighbours this year, and it has committed to a budget and to the disbursement of European recovery funds that require a government with authority. Caretaker administration is possible and limits what can be decided. A budget left unpassed in the autumn would roll over the previous year's spending, and the recovery fund payments Brussels has scheduled depend on milestones that need a minister with authority to sign them.

A country that has spent five years being told it faced a choice was told on 23 July that it had chosen a stalemate. The negotiations begin with arithmetic and will end, if they end, with concessions that the campaign did not mention. Neither leader has ruled out a repeat election, and both have reasons to fear one.
