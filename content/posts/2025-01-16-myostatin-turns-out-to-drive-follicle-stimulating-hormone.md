title: "Myostatin turns out to drive follicle-stimulating hormone"

description: "A muscle-derived protein best known for limiting muscle growth also drives the pituitary to release follicle-stimulating hormone in mice and rats, which raises a question for the myostatin inhibitors now in trials for muscle wasting."
---

Follicle-stimulating hormone is released by the pituitary and acts on the gonads, and its release has been attributed to activins, members of the transforming growth factor beta family that the gonads produce. The attribution has held for thirty years and has been the basis of the assays that drug developers use to screen compounds that act on the pathway.

A paper in *Science* on 16 January reports that myostatin, another member of the same family, drives follicle-stimulating hormone expression in mice and rats. The authors measured hormone levels after deleting the myostatin gene, after blocking its receptor and after adding the protein, and the hormone moved with myostatin in each experiment. The effect appeared in animals in which the activin pathway was intact, so the two drivers operate in parallel, and blocking one did not abolish the hormone, which is what a single-driver model would have predicted.

Myostatin is familiar for a different reason. It is produced in muscle and it suppresses muscle growth, and the observation that cattle with a defective myostatin gene grow unusually muscular has supported two decades of work on inhibitors for sarcopenia, cachexia and muscular dystrophy. Those programmes have produced antibodies and soluble receptors that are in clinical trials, and their designers have watched muscle mass and strength as the endpoints, with little attention paid to the pituitary and none to the gonads, because the trials were designed when the pathway was believed to be muscle-specific.

A new result adds a reproductive endpoint to that list. If myostatin drives follicle-stimulating hormone in humans the way it does in rodents, an inhibitor given for months could change the hormonal signal that controls gonadal function, which is a side effect that a trial focused on grip strength and lean mass would not look for, and would not report even if it happened. The authors state the cross-species question explicitly and do not claim an answer.

Activin retains its role, and the discovery does not remove it. Activins still regulate the hormone, and the demonstration that myostatin does too means the pathway has two inputs where the textbooks describe one. That redundancy is why deleting a single gene in a mouse often produces a smaller effect than blocking both, and it is the kind of result that revises a model without overturning it, which is how most of endocrinology has advanced since the radioimmunoassay made hormone measurement routine.

What a reader should take from the paper is a caution about specificity. A drug designed to block myostatin signalling does not necessarily leave the rest of the family untouched, and a drug designed to block activin signalling has been tested in humans for other indications. Whether either changes fertility is a question for trials with the right endpoints, and the paper supplies the reason to include them early, before an inhibitor reaches a patient who might want children and before a regulatory file is built on muscle endpoints alone.
