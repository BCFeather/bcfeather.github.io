A protostellar disk has to shed angular momentum before material can reach the star, and in the cold, weakly ionized interior of the disk the coupling between gas and magnetic field is too weak for the classical magneto-rotational instability to operate. Disk winds launched by magnetohydrodynamic forces from the ionized surface layers are the alternative that models have favoured, and the difficulty has been observational: a wind of that kind has to be resolved within tens of astronomical units of a young star.

A preprint posted to arXiv on 28 February 2024 reports such a measurement for L1448-mm, a well-studied Class 0 source. The observations are from ALMA at a resolution near 30 astronomical units, and the dust continuum at 0.9 millimetres shows a disk with a radius of about 23 astronomical units, small enough that the wind region sits outside it on the sky. The source sits at a distance of about 300 parsecs, so 30 astronomical units is a resolution that a single pointing can deliver.

Rotation in the infalling gas fixes the mass. The \(\mathrm{H}^{13}\mathrm{CO}^{+}\) line shows infall with a rotational signature whose centrifugal radius matches the dust radius, and the fit gives a central mass of 0.4 solar masses with an uncertainty of 0.1. A centrifugal radius that matches the dust radius is the geometric statement that the infalling envelope and the disk meet where the rotation stops rising.

Above the disk the wind appears in three lines. \(\mathrm{H}^{13}\mathrm{CN}\), \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{OH}\) and \(\mathrm{SO}\) all show rotation signatures in the outflow, and the pattern matches what a magnetohydrodynamic disk wind should produce, which makes this the first source in which the wind has been traced in the first two of those molecules.

The numbers are consistent with the models. The magnetic lever arm comes out near 1.7, in the range that recent non-ideal simulations produce, and the mass-loss rate is about four times the accretion rate of 2 millionths of a solar mass per year, which is the ratio needed for the wind to carry away the angular momentum that accretion removes. A lever arm near 1.7 means the wind removes angular momentum about 1.7 times more efficiently than a spherical outflow would.

The limits are those of a single object. One source cannot establish a class, the molecular emission may trace cavity walls as well as a wind, and the lever arm is derived from cold magnetohydrodynamic theory rather than measured directly, so the fitted value is a consistency check more than a determination. Inclination is the confounder that a survey would have to handle, since a wind seen face-on shows rotation where one seen edge-on shows only expansion.

What would make it a class is a survey. The same three lines in a sample of Class 0 sources, at the same resolution, would show whether the rotation signature and the mass-loss ratio recur, and whether the sources that lack them are those with no wind or those whose inclinations hide it.
