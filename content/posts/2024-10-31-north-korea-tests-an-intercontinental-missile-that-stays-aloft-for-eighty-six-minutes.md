North Korea fired an intercontinental ballistic missile on the thirty-first of October that flew for about eighty-six minutes and reached a maximum altitude of more than seven thousand kilometres, both records for the country's missile programme. Missile was launched on a lofted trajectory from an area near Pyongyang and travelled about a thousand kilometres before falling into the sea west of Okushiri island, in Japanese waters. South Korean and Japanese militaries tracked it and issued warnings to shipping and aircraft.

State media named the weapon the next day as Hwasongpho-19, and it said the test had confirmed the reliability of the country's strategic deterrent. Leader Kim Jong Un attended the launch and said the missile demonstrated that the country could respond to any threat from outside. The launch was the first of an intercontinental missile in almost a year, and it came after a period in which the country had concentrated on short-range weapons, drones and satellite launches.

Lofted trajectory is a choice rather than a limitation. Firing a long-range missile almost straight up keeps it within the country's own airspace and waters and avoids overflying Japan, and it allows engineers to test the full burn of the stages without the range that would otherwise be required. Analysts said the altitude and duration suggest a missile with a larger first stage and a heavier payload than earlier designs, and that the ability to reach the American mainland with a nuclear warhead remains unproven but is closer.

Timing was read by governments in the region as a political act. Launch came five days before the American election and hours after the American defence secretary met his South Korean counterpart in Seoul. North Korea has also sent troops to Russia to fight in Ukraine, according to American, South Korean and Ukrainian officials, which has added a new dimension to the security argument in north-east Asia and made contacts between the two Koreas even more distant than they were.

Responses were immediate and familiar. Japanese authorities briefly instructed residents of Hokkaido to take shelter, the second such order in a year, and the coast guard warned vessels away from the splashdown area. South Korean officials said the launch had been detected about an hour before it landed and that the data had been shared with the American and Japanese militaries in real time. South Korea, Japan and the United States condemned the launch and coordinated a trilateral response, and the South Korean military conducted a live-fire exercise of its own within hours. American officials said the launch violated multiple Security Council resolutions and that they would work with allies on a response. China called for calm and said the situation was complex, which is its standard formulation and stops short of criticism of the launch.

Meaning of the test lies in what it implies about the programme rather than the missile alone. North Korea has now demonstrated that it can put a large missile into space on a steep trajectory, and it has tested enough to learn from failures. What it has not demonstrated is a re-entry vehicle that survives the heat of a return from that altitude, which is the technical problem that separates a launch from a weapon. Verification is impossible without access, and the country has shown no interest in providing it.
