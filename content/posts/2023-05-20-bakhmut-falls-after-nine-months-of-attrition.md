The Russian private military company Wagner announced on 20 May that its units had taken the last blocks of Bakhmut, the industrial city in the Donetsk region that had been the object of the war's longest battle. The Russian defence ministry followed with a statement, and President Vladimir Putin congratulated the fighters. Ukraine's deputy defence minister said the situation was critical and that Ukrainian troops held a small area; by 22 May the general staff had effectively conceded the city, describing its positions as being on the outskirts.

Fighting began in August 2022, when Russian forces moved west from Popasna and Lysychansk. It became an infantry grind of the kind that the First World War made familiar: assaults across open ground, artillery duels at ranges of a few kilometres, and a front that moved by streets rather than by provinces. Wagner's owner, Yevgeny Prigozhin, recruited prisoners with promises of pardons and complained publicly and repeatedly that the regular army refused to supply him with ammunition. His complaints were the earliest sign of the factional fight that would break into the open a month later.

The cost is the number that will define the battle. Neither side publishes its casualties, and the estimates differ by an order of magnitude. Western officials have put Russian dead and wounded in the battle at tens of thousands, and Ukrainian losses at a comparable figure, with the ratio disputed. What is agreed is that the exchange rate was terrible for both sides, and that Russia spent months of munitions on a city whose strategic value, once the surrounding heights were taken, was mostly symbolic.

Symbolism is why both sides made the bet. Bakhmut sits on a road junction that leads to Kramatorsk and Sloviansk, the remaining Ukrainian-held cities of the Donetsk region, and its capture would allow Russia to claim that the annexation of the region had been completed. For Ukraine, the city became the demonstration that the country would not retreat from its own territory, and President Zelensky said so at every opportunity, in a phrase about the war being fought in the east and decided everywhere.

Militarily, the question is what the battle did to the coming offensive. Ukraine committed some of its best brigades to the defence and used the time to train and equip the formations that were held in reserve for the counterattack. Russian forces, having spent their ammunition and their most aggressive infantry on the city, were left holding a ruin with no operational reserve behind it. The American and European officials who urged Ukraine to withdraw argued that the city did not justify the cost; the Ukrainian command argued that it was worth the time.

What Bakhmut demonstrated is the shape of this war: a front where neither side can break through, and where every square kilometre is bought with casualties that would have been unthinkable in the wars of the 1990s. The city is now a name in a news bulletin and a set of ruined streets that no one will rebuild for years. The battle for the rest of the region has already begun, and the ground that Ukraine will try to recover will be measured against the ground it has just lost.
