She Came to Me, a comedy by Rebecca Miller, had its world premiere on 16 February and opened the festival. The film stars Peter Dinklage as a composer and Marisa Tomei as a tugboat captain, and it played to a full house at the Palast. The opening night is a formality in Berlin, and the audience is famously polite rather than enthusiastic. Miller wrote the screenplay during the pandemic, and the film was shot in 30 days in New York. Ticket sales for the opening were sold out within an hour, and the cinema held 1,800 people. Dinklage learned to play the piano for the role, and the singing was recorded on set rather than in a studio.

Programmers selected 400 films from 100 countries, and 18 competed for the Golden Bear. Documentary made up a quarter of the competition, more than in previous years. The directors of the festival said the selection reflected the war in Ukraine and the protests in Iran, and several films addressed both. Films from Iran were submitted without their directors, several of whom are banned from travelling. A Ukrainian documentary about the first weeks of the war screened in the forum section. The competition included three films by first-time directors, the highest number in a decade.

Kristen Stewart led the jury, the youngest president in the event's history at 32. The other members included a Iranian director working in exile and a cinematographer from Portugal. The jury's decisions are usually political in the sense that they reward subject matter, and this year was no exception. Stewart had served on a jury in 2018, and she said she had accepted the presidency to see the films early.

Attendance recovered to about 320,000 tickets, close to the 2019 level, and the industry side was busier than expected. The European film market reported 600 exhibitors and 9,000 visitors, with deals announced for several titles before the awards. Streaming buyers were the most active, and they paid more than the theatrical distributors for two of the competition films. The market reported 1,500 buyers, and the festival's own fund gave 1.5 million euros to projects in development. The market's largest deal was for a Scandinavian drama series, sold to a streaming service for a reported 20 million euros.

Weather stayed cold and the queue for evening screenings ran around the block. The festival's sidebar for new German cinema sold out, and the retrospective of 1930s musicals drew a younger audience than usual. Security was tighter than in previous years, with bag checks at every entrance. The retrospective programme of musicals included prints restored by the archive in Berlin.

Awards were announced on 25 February, and the top prize went to a French documentary. The festival also gave a prize for the best first feature, which went to a film shot in 16 millimetres. The next edition was scheduled for February 2024, and the director's contract was extended to cover it. The prize money for the Golden Bear is not paid to the director, which is why the winners often complain. The documentary prize went to a film about a psychiatric clinic in Paris, shot over two years.
