Chinese retail sales rose 3.7 per cent in May from a year earlier, slower than the 4.5 per cent that economists expected, while industrial output grew 5.6 per cent. Investment in property fell 10.1 per cent in the first five months of the year. The data were published on 17 June. The figures were published a week after the trade data that showed exports rising, and the combination describes an economy supported by selling abroad rather than by spending at home. The figures cover the month before the second round of property measures was announced, which means the effect of the down payment cut is not yet in any of them.

Household demand is the weak part. The households that own flats have seen the value fall and the ones who work in the industry have seen their incomes fall with it, and the saving rate has risen as a result. A household that is poorer on paper is a household that spends less, whatever the shops do. The restaurants and the travel services did better than the goods, which is the pattern of a consumer who is trading down rather than stopping.

Factory output is the strong part. The output grew faster than the sales, which means the inventory is being built rather than sold at home, and the difference is exported. An economy that produces more than it consumes is an economy that depends on the trade it is now being denied. The inventory of unsold flats has been rising, and the developers that are still building are the state-owned ones that the local governments have asked to continue.

Fiscal support has gone into investment rather than into consumption. The local governments have been told to build and the banks have been told to lend, and the result is capacity that the domestic buyer has not appeared to use. A stimulus that adds supply is a stimulus that deflates prices. The government has announced a trade-in programme for appliances and cars, and the subsidies are small relative to the price of the goods they are meant to move.

Prices are falling, which is the measure that the authorities watch most closely. The producer prices have declined for more than a year, and the consumer prices are barely rising, which is the pattern of an economy where the demand is the constraint rather than the supply. A deflation is harder to reverse than the inflation it replaced. The producer prices have fallen for eighteen months, and the profits of the industrial firms have fallen with them, which is why the investment has not followed the output.

What the month shows is that a property slump is a consumption tax, and the measures that the government has taken have supported the builders without restoring the households' willingness to spend. A policy that supports the producers while the consumers retrench is a policy that produces more of what nobody buys, and the correction is the question the year now poses.
