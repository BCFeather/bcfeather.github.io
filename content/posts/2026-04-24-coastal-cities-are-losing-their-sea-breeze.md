A sea breeze is a daily thermal circulation. Land heats faster than water through the morning, the pressure difference drives air inland from the sea, and by afternoon a coastal city has a natural air conditioner that predates air conditioning. The breeze ventilates, it cools, and in a city it disperses pollution, which makes it a piece of infrastructure that nobody built and nobody maintains.

Climate models have generally treated the ocean surface as a smooth boundary, with a temperature that varies slowly and in a pattern set by the large-scale circulation. A modelling study reported as a research briefing in *Nature Climate Change* on 24 April resolves the sea surface temperature at a much finer scale, and includes the eddies and filaments that make the real ocean patchy.

Ocean warming has already reduced the number of sea breeze days in most large coastal cities. Warm water reduces the temperature contrast that drives the circulation, and a smaller contrast produces a weaker and less frequent breeze, which is the physical mechanism and is simple once stated.

Magnitude is what matters and the numbers are regional. Cities on coasts where the offshore water has warmed fastest lose the most days, and cities on eastern ocean boundaries, where upwelling keeps the surface cool, lose fewer. The pattern is not uniform, which is a reason to distrust any single global average.

The consequence for cities is a summer that is hotter than the thermometer records, that is hotter than the thermometer suggests. A city without its breeze loses ventilation during exactly the hours when heat accumulates, and the urban heat island and the loss of the breeze reinforce one another, so that a day without wind is a day with a higher heat load, which is why the effect on human health is larger than the effect on mean temperature.

Adaptation is constrained by physics. A city cannot restore a temperature contrast, and it can change what depends on it: building orientation, street ventilation corridors, tree cover along the coast, and the siting of the facilities that emit heat. Each of those is a planning decision with a decades-long lifetime, and the modelling result is the argument for making them now, while the contrast is still large enough to be worth designing around.

Model resolution is the uncertainty. A fine grid resolves the sea breeze and it also requires boundary conditions that are themselves uncertain, and different parameterizations of convection and of land surface exchange can shift the answer by a meaningful fraction. The paper reports the spread, and the direction of the change is more robust than its size. The finding is a reminder that climate change acts on circulations and not only on averages. A warming planet is not a planet that is uniformly a few degrees hotter, and a coastal city that loses a daily wind loses something that a thermostat cannot replace.
