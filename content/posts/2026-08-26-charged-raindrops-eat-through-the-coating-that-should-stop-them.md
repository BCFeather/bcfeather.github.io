Corrosion is usually taught as chemistry: oxygen, water and salt, with acid rain as the accelerated case. The physical part of the process, the impact of a drop on a surface and the film it leaves behind, is treated as erosion when it is treated at all. A paper in *Nature* on 26 August argues that the physics includes an electrical step, and that the step is what defeats a hydrophobic coating. Industrial coatings are qualified with salt spray and ultraviolet exposure, and neither test includes the charge a draining surface generates.

Rüdiger Berger, Hans-Jürgen Butt and colleagues dropped water onto four common surfaces: a plant leaf, a polyvinyl chloride foam board, a polystyrene glass and a coating known as PFOTS, which is used to make surfaces water-repellent. The drops slid off and landed on copper that had been coated with Teflon. Each drop carried a tiny charge, measured between 0.2 and 2 nanocoulombs, which is small per drop and not small in aggregate.

After 3,000 drops had passed from the surfaces to the Teflon-coated copper, the coating broke down and the copper beneath began to corrode. When the drops carried no charge, no damage was observed. That control is the paper's central argument, because it isolates the electrical variable from the mechanical and chemical ones that a rain test would otherwise confound. Nanocoulombs sound negligible until one calculates the field they produce across a micrometre-thick film, which is where the breakdown begins.

Where the charge comes from is contact electrification, the same process that makes a balloon stick to a wall. Water sliding over a polymer or a leaf separates charge at the interface, and the amount depends on the material, the sliding speed and the humidity. The implication is that a coating's performance depends on the surface it drains from, which is not how coatings are tested. Contact electrification is well studied for solids and poorly studied for a moving drop, which is why the measurement had to come before the mechanism.

The numbers put the effect in a practical range. Three thousand drops is a few hours of rain on a car roof or a bridge span, and the charge accumulated in that time was enough to degrade a fluoropolymer. The authors do not claim that every coating fails this way, and they do not model the field strength that would break a carbon-fluorine bond, which is the mechanism a reader would want quantified. Fluoropolymers are chosen for inertness, and an electrical route to degradation is a finding about the material class rather than about one product.

What follows is a materials problem rather than a chemistry one. A coating that dissipates charge as it forms would resist the mechanism, and a surface that produces less charge when water slides across it would remove the source. Ships, buildings and cultural heritage objects all sit in the relevant conditions, and none of them are tested with charged drops today.
