Sea ice is not a solid sheet so much as a habitat with a geography. Brine channels run through it, narrow and salty, and inside those channels live diatoms, single-celled algae with silica shells, which photosynthesise in light that has already passed through a metre of ice. A study in *PNAS* on 9 September reports that these cells can still move at temperatures low enough to stop almost every other eukaryotic cell, and the measurement is a record.

Motility matters more than it sounds. A diatom that cannot reposition itself cannot find the light or the nutrients it needs, and in ice the gradients are steep and close together, and a cell a millimetre out of place may sit in darkness for a week. The cells glide by secreting a polysaccharide through a slit in the shell, and the mechanism is temperature-sensitive because the secretion depends on the membrane staying fluid enough for the polymer to pass through it. The question the authors asked was how far down the thermometer that mechanism could be pushed.

The answer, from cells extracted from Antarctic ice and filmed under controlled conditions, is further than expected: movement continued at several degrees below the previous limit reported for any eukaryote. The experiments cooled the samples in steps and tracked individual cells, measuring speed and the fraction still active rather than relying on a single observation, so that the record is a curve rather than a point on a thermometer. The cells slowed, and many stopped, but a measurable share kept gliding at the coldest step.

The adaptation is not a curiosity of one species but a property of a community that has to be able to work in the coldest months or lose its place in the ice. The authors point to changes in the membrane lipids, which stay fluid at temperatures that would stiffen a temperate diatom's membranes into something closer to wax. Whether that is a general trick or a specialization of polar species is an open question, and one that a comparison of temperate and polar strains could answer within a season.

The implications run in two directions. For polar ecology, the result extends the range in which ice algae can actively position themselves, which affects estimates of primary production at the base of the food web. For the search for life elsewhere, the relevant point is that ice is not automatically a barrier. Europa and Enceladus both carry ice, and both are candidates for liquid water beneath it; if single cells can operate in brine at these temperatures, the habitable envelope of an ice-covered ocean is wider than the models assumed.

That is not an argument for life on Europa, and the authors do not make one. It is an argument about limits, which is a more modest and more useful thing. The coldest place a known organism can still swim is a fact about biology, and facts of that kind tend to be quoted for a long time.
