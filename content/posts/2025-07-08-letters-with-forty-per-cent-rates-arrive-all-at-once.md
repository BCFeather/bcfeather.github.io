Fourteen letters went out in a single afternoon, each addressed to a government and each setting a rate that would take effect on 1 August, and the instrument changed from a threat to a schedule. Rates ran from 25 per cent for Japan and Korea to 40 per cent for Myanmar and Laos, with South Africa at 30, and a separate announcement on 9 July put Brazil at 50 per cent.

Recipients had four weeks. A letter that arrives on 7 July and takes effect on 1 August gives a trade ministry twenty-five days to decide whether to concede on the issue named in the letter, retaliate, or accept the rate, and no ministry can do any of those things quickly, which is the point of the deadline.

Coverage was partial by design. The letters named countries rather than products, which shifts the tariff from a line item to a national rate, and left the European Union, China and Mexico under separate frameworks, so the same administration is running three different instruments at once and each has a different legal basis. A national rate is easier to announce and harder to administer than a product list, because customs officers still have to classify the goods and firms still have to prove origin, and the paperwork is where a letter becomes a cost.

Markets treated it as noise. Equities fell modestly on the day and recovered within a week, because the rates were close to what had been announced in April and because the deadline looked like a negotiating device, and the currency market moved more than the stock market, which is the usual pattern when the story is about trade flows.

Legal risk is the part that economists underrate. A rate set by letter and justified by a statute that has been read narrowly in court is a rate that can be suspended by a judge, and the administration has already lost two cases at the trial level, so a company planning a supply chain has to price a tariff and a stay of the tariff at the same time. A company that has already built a plant in a country named in a letter has to decide whether to move it, and a decision taken under a legal cloud is a decision that gets delayed, which is a cost that shows up in investment data and not in tariff revenue.

The economics are straightforward and unwelcome. A 25 per cent rate on a country's exports is a 25 per cent tax on its producers or on American buyers, the incidence splits according to who has alternatives, and the letter does not say which side of the split will bear it, which is why importers were renegotiating contracts within hours of the announcement.

What would settle the month is how many of the fourteen concede before 1 August. A handful of concessions would let the administration call the letters a success, and none would turn them into a rate schedule that survives only until the next court hearing. A schedule that survives to the end of the year is a schedule that has been tested in court and in a supply chain, and the fourteen letters are now the test.
