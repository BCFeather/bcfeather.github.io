The security conference in Munich ran from 14 to 16 February and produced the usual mix of speeches, bilateral meetings and a final declaration that nobody reads. What it also produced was a number: European governments now accept that defence spending has to rise to a level most of them have not sustained since the cold war, and the debate has moved from whether to how. Attendance was the largest in the conference's history, and the corridors were busier than the main hall.

Its arithmetic is awkward. A commitment to spend three per cent of output on defence, from a base near two, is an increase of roughly one hundred billion euros a year across the union, at a time when budgets are already stretched by pensions and by the energy transition. Borrowing jointly would lower the cost, and it would require a treaty change that several capitals reject. A common borrowing instrument would spread the cost over twenty years and would let smaller states buy equipment they cannot finance alone.

Industrial capacity is the binding constraint rather than money. European ammunition production rose sharply after 2022, and it is still below the rate that a sustained conflict would consume. Shell production is a chemical industry problem, artillery barrel production is a forging problem, and both take years to expand because the plants are specialized and the workforce is trained on the job. A plant that makes propellant for artillery shells needs a chemical supply chain that Europe mostly imports, and that chain is the real bottleneck.

American messaging shaped the mood. A delegation that questioned the durability of the alliance's commitments, and that argued publicly about European domestic politics, made the case for autonomy more persuasive than any European speech. A guarantee that is questioned by its guarantor is a guarantee that has to be replaced. European leaders responded by repeating the word autonomy, which is a description of a goal rather than a plan for reaching it.

Defence spending is also a fiscal transfer between countries. A country that buys equipment abroad exports the demand, and a country that builds it at home captures the industrial base. The procurement rules that decide where a system is made are therefore the real subject of the spending debate, and they are negotiated one contract at a time. Offset agreements, local assembly and technology transfer are the instruments governments use to keep the industrial base at home.

What Munich settled is the direction and not the mechanism. Rearmament will happen, it will be financed by a mixture of national budgets and common instruments, and it will run into the same shortages in every country that tries to expand faster than its suppliers can deliver. The money will be spent, the plants will be built, and the gap between the promise and the delivery will be measured in years. A promise repeated at a conference is a promise that has to survive the budget cycle of every member state.
