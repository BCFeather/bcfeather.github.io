Tropical forests hold nearly half of the above-ground carbon stored in the world's forests, and the accounting of how much they lose has been done at a scale that hides the mechanism. Satellite products report deforestation in patches of a hectare or more, national inventories report by land-use class, and global models average over continents. A clearing of half a hectare made by a farmer with a chainsaw falls between all three.

The RECCAP-2 analysis published in *Nature* on 15 January reconstructed carbon gains and losses across all tropical forests from 1990 to 2020 at a resolution of thirty metres. The authors integrated sub-hectare Earth observation data with biomass recovery curves that vary by disturbance type and local climate, which is the part that previous bookkeeping approaches lacked. The headline number is that humid tropical forests lost \(15.6\pm3.7\) petagrams of carbon over the three decades.

The distribution of that loss is the finding that changes policy. Clearings smaller than two hectares accounted for only five per cent of the disturbed area and fifty-six per cent of net carbon losses, because small clearings are rarely abandoned and are usually converted to farmland, pasture, roads or settlements. A large clearing is often logged and left to regrow; a small one becomes a field, and a field does not return carbon for decades.

Dry tropical forests behaved differently, staying roughly carbon-neutral over the period. Fire drove much of their loss and post-fire recovery offset much of it, and repeated burning has gradually reduced the fuel available so that each subsequent fire does less damage. That distinction between humid and dry forests is important because the two are often lumped together in global assessments.

Africa dominates the small-clearing story, where such disturbances account for over 97 per cent of net carbon losses. Smallholder expansion is the mechanism, and it is a development story as much as an environmental one, since the same clearings support households that have few alternatives. Policy that treats the farmer as the problem tends to fail, and policy that pays for carbon has to reach a household that may not have title to the land.

Undisturbed tropical forests remained a net carbon sink over the period and offset part of the loss elsewhere. The overall balance for the tropics was close to neutral, which sounds reassuring and is a statement about a ledger in which the sink is shrinking as the disturbances move into denser, more carbon-rich forest. Each hectare lost now carries more carbon than one lost in 1990.

What the paper contributes methodologically is a thirty-metre reconstruction that distinguishes disturbance type and size, and its limitation is that recovery curves are models informed by plots rather than measurements everywhere. The direction of the result is unlikely to be wrong and the magnitude will be revised. What the paper makes plain is that the smallest events are the ones that count.
