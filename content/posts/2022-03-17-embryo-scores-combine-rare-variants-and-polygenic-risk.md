Preimplantation genetic testing began with single-gene disorders, where a couple who both carry a pathogenic variant can select an embryo that does not. Common diseases are polygenic, and the question of whether an embryo's polygenic score should inform selection has been argued on principle and not tested on data. Single-gene testing is uncontroversial because the variant causes the disease with near certainty, while a polygenic score shifts a probability and the ethics of selecting on a probability are unsettled.

A study combined molecular and statistical methods to infer the inherited genome sequence of 110 embryos and to model susceptibility across twelve common conditions. Genotype accuracy at sites relevant to polygenic scoring reached 99.0 to 99.4 per cent for day-5 biopsies and 97.2 to 99.1 per cent for day-3 biopsies. The work appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 21 March 2022.

The interesting result is the interaction between the two kinds of risk. In a couple where one parent carries a pathogenic BRCA1 variant, the predicted odds ratio across sibling embryos differed by a factor of 15 when rare variant and polygenic score were combined, against 4.5 with the variant alone and 3 with the score alone. The comparison is within one couple, which controls for the parents' genomes and isolates the contribution of the two risk models, and that is the cleanest way to show the effect of combining them.

Multiplying the two sources of risk is statistically simple and clinically unfamiliar. A polygenic score is a population-level predictor that is poorly calibrated for an individual, and a rare variant is a strong predictor for a single disease, so the product describes a risk that neither component alone conveys.

Embryo biopsy is the constraint on accuracy. A day-3 biopsy samples one or two cells and a day-5 biopsy samples five to ten, and the inference fills the rest from the parents' genomes, which means that errors in parental phasing propagate into every embryo from that couple. A polygenic score built from European ancestry data performs worse in other populations, and a clinic that offers the test without saying so would widen an existing inequity.

Selection also has limits that the statistics do not address. Embryos are few, the score explains a small fraction of variance for most conditions, and a couple selecting on a polygenic trait is trading one set of risks for another in ways that the model does not quantify. A couple with two unaffected embryos is choosing between risks and not between a sick child and a well one.

The authors frame the result as information for a policy debate rather than as a recommendation. Clinics that offer the test will need counselling standards, and regulators will need a view on whether a polygenic score is a diagnosis or a preference. The paper is a proof of principle, and the distance between a modelled odds ratio and a live birth is the distance that a clinical service would have to cross.
