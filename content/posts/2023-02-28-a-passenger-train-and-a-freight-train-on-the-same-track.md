A passenger train travelling from Athens to Thessaloniki collided head-on with a freight train near the town of Tempi shortly before midnight on 28 February. The first two carriages of the passenger train were crushed and the third caught fire, and the fire was hot enough that the identification of the dead took weeks and required DNA. Fifty-seven people died, most of them in their twenties, and eighty-one were seriously injured. The wreckage was spread over a kilometre, and the fire burned for two days, which is why the identification took weeks.

Both trains had been routed onto the same track, and the signalling that should have prevented it was partly manual. The stationmaster at Larissa, who was on duty alone, was arrested the following morning and charged with negligent homicide and with disrupting transport. He said the switch had been set wrongly and that the automatic system had not been working, and the union that represented the staff said the system had been known to be unreliable for years.

Infrastructure became the subject of the investigation that followed. The line had been rebuilt with European funding and with modern signalling installed on part of it, and the section where the collision happened was still controlled by a system that depended on a person throwing a switch. The government had promised to complete the signalling by 2020, and the contract had been delayed and revised, and the delay had been reported by the state auditor in 2019.

The political consequences came quickly. The transport minister resigned on 1 March, and the prime minister accepted the resignation and promised that the causes would be established. Strikes by railway workers and by doctors followed, and the unions that had warned about the line for a decade described the accident as a predictable result of underinvestment and of contracting out. An election that had been due in July was brought forward to May, and the campaign that followed was conducted almost entirely about the crash.

The public response was one of the largest in the country's recent history. Protesters gathered in Syntagma Square in Athens and in Thessaloniki, and the banners referred to the dead by their first names and to the contract that had been delayed. The prime minister, who had been expected to call an election in the spring, brought it forward to May and framed it as a choice about the state's competence.

What the accident revealed was a gap between the country's infrastructure and its institutions. Greece had built a motorway network and an airport and a metro in the previous two decades, and it had not built the supervisory capacity that a railway requires, and the gap was invisible until a switch was set wrongly at eleven in the evening. The crash became the event that the year in Greece was organized around, and the argument about the state's capacity became the election. The railway had been sold to a private operator in 2017, and the state kept the track and the signalling, which is the split that the inquiry examined.
