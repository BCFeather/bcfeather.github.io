Cinema in China has become a holiday business. The eight days around the lunar new year took 9.5 billion yuan in 2025, a record, while the full year of 2024 finished near 42.5 billion, down sharply from the 54.9 billion of 2023. A market that earns a fifth of its annual revenue in a single week is a market where the calendar matters more than the marketing, and the calendar is fixed in November, when distributors claim release dates. A distributor that misses the window waits a year for the next one, which is why the date is negotiated before the film is finished.

Distribution works through a small number of chains that control screens rather than through a studio system. A distributor negotiates a revenue split, typically with the exhibitor keeping a little more than half after the first week, and the split improves for the cinema as a run lengthens. That structure rewards a film that holds its audience, because the exhibitor keeps a larger share of a long run than of a crowded opening weekend. Minimum ticket prices are set by the distributor, and a cinema that discounts below them pays the difference out of its own share.

Pre-sales have become the instrument that decides screen allocation. A film that sells tickets before release can show an exhibitor a number, and the number translates into showtimes on the first day. The 2025 holiday set records for advance sales, and the pattern that emerged was a single title taking nearly half the screens in the largest cities by the third day. A cinema with eight screens has to choose, and the choice is made on the advance number rather than on the reviews.

Animation has an advantage in this system that live action does not. A family film sells four tickets per transaction rather than two, and it plays best in the afternoon and early evening, which are the slots a holiday schedule has to fill. The sequel to a 2019 animated hit arrived on 29 January with a pre-sold audience, and its share of the week rose every day as word of mouth spread. Merchandise and licensing add a revenue line that the box office does not capture, and animation travels better abroad than comedy does.

The concentration is a problem for everyone else on the slate. A title that opens against a runaway leader loses screens by the second weekend, and a film with a production budget of several hundred million yuan cannot recover that budget in the residual slots. Two films in the 2025 holiday passed a billion yuan, and the rest split a smaller pool. Marketing spending is concentrated in the two weeks before release, and a film that opens badly has no budget left to correct.

The structural question is whether the holiday can carry the year. A market that depends on one week has to fill the other fifty-one, and the 2024 decline suggests it has not. Cinemas have responded with concert recordings, repertory screenings and event programming, and none of those has yet replaced the holiday blockbuster as the reason to leave the house.
