On paper, a lithium-carbon dioxide cell would combine energy storage with the capture of a greenhouse gas. In practice the chemistry offers several routes, and which one runs decides what the cell produces and how long it lasts, which is why an electrolyte that steers the route is worth more than a new catalyst.

The route, reported in *PNAS* on 4 April 2023, follows the electrolyte. The solvation shell around the lithium ion, and the solvent's donor number, determine whether the reduction of carbon dioxide proceeds through one intermediate or another.

Donor number is the handle. It measures how strongly a solvent binds a cation, and a strong binder leaves fewer molecules in the ion's first shell, so the composition of that shell is what carbon dioxide meets when it is reduced. Carbon dioxide can bind to the lithium ion, to the solvent or to the electrode, and the shell's size shifts that competition; spectroscopy and calculations trace the shift, and a titration across a series of solvents turns the correlation into a number.

What the cell deposits is the readout. One pathway gives lithium carbonate, another lithium oxalate or carbon, and the two differ in reversibility, so a cell that deposits carbonate is a cell that struggles to recharge. Because solvation is a variable rather than a fixed property of a material, the design rule is one a battery chemist can apply without a new theory: a solvent with a donor number in a particular range favours the reversible route.

Working against that rule is the rest of the cell. Carbon dioxide is a gas, and a gas electrode needs a porous support with a catalyst whose surface chemistry interacts with the electrolyte's shell; the same solvent that changes the shell also changes how the intermediate binds to the carbon surface, and the two effects compound, which is why the control experiments matter. A lithium metal anode brings the other constraint, since the reactivity that makes the system high-energy is the reactivity that makes it hard to cycle.

Temperature narrows the window further, because a solvent that works at room temperature can freeze or decompose at the temperatures a practical cell reaches, and measurements at several temperatures show how the shell responds. Full-cell cycle life is the test still missing: half-cell coulombic efficiency is a first screen, and a cell with a limited electrolyte is what the chemistry has to survive. Parallels with lithium-oxygen, which shares the architecture but not the intermediates, carry the lessons about solvation from one system to the other.

Cell-level experiments cannot settle the electrode's behaviour at scale, because a carbon-dioxide cathode that works in a half cell has to survive the impurities, the pressure and the packing of a full cell. The gap between a coulombic efficiency and a product is where most battery chemistries have failed, and that gap is the one that matters.
