---

The Federal Reserve left its target range for the policy rate unchanged at the meeting that ended on 18 June, at four and a quarter to four and a half per cent. It was the third consecutive hold, and the statement that accompanied it repeated a phrase the market has heard for months, which is that the committee wants more evidence before it moves. The evidence it wants is about tariffs.

This is an unusual position for a central bank. The standard argument is that a tariff is a tax on imports, that some of the cost passes through to consumer prices, and that a central bank facing higher inflation should lean against it. The complication is that a tariff also slows demand and unsettles business confidence, which argues for easing. When a single policy pushes inflation and growth in opposite directions, the correct response is often to do nothing and see which force dominates.

The data gave the committee room to wait. Inflation had come down substantially from its peak and was still running a little above the two per cent target. Unemployment was low by historical standards. Growth had slowed but not stalled. None of that demanded an immediate move in either direction, and the risks were genuinely two-sided, which is the situation in which a committee prefers to sit still and say so.

The projections published alongside the decision pointed to a modest path of cuts later in the year, and the market read them as a signal that the easing cycle had been delayed rather than abandoned. That reading has been wrong before. The projections are forecasts, not commitments, and the committee has spent three years demonstrating that it will abandon a forecast when the data changes.

For households the practical consequence is that borrowing costs stay where they are for a while longer. Mortgages, car loans and credit card rates are tied to the same benchmark, and a hold means the pressure does not lift this quarter. For the currency and for emerging markets, a higher-for-longer American rate keeps the dollar firm and makes imported debt more expensive, which is the channel through which American monetary policy reaches places that have no vote in it.

The political context is the part that cannot be ignored and cannot be fully analysed from outside. An independent central bank making a technical judgement about pass-through effects is a different institution from one making that judgement under public pressure from an elected government. The committee's language has become noticeably careful about this, and careful language is what an institution uses when it is defending a position rather than describing one.

Waiting is a decision, and it has costs. Every month of delay is a month in which the borrowers who are most exposed to the rate continue to pay for it, and the benefit of waiting accrues later and to everyone. The committee has decided that the balance favours patience this quarter. It will be judged on whether the patience was vindicated or merely expensive.
