Shipping a solid-state battery inside a phone is less dramatic than the announcement implies and more important than the sceptics allow. The handset a Chinese manufacturer launched this month carries a 4,500 milliamp-hour sulfide-electrolyte cell in a 7.9 millimetre case, which is 400 milliamp-hours more than the same model's previous generation at the same thickness. The chemistry has been in laboratories for a decade; what changed is that a factory can now produce it at a yield that a phone company will accept.

Electrolyte is the whole story. A liquid electrolyte conducts lithium ions between the electrodes and, being flammable, is the reason a punctured cell can catch fire. A solid sulfide electrolyte conducts comparably at room temperature, does not burn, and permits a lithium metal anode instead of a graphite one, which is where the extra energy comes from.

Manufacturing is where the difficulty sits. Sulfide electrolytes react with moisture to produce hydrogen sulfide, so every step from powder mixing to stacking happens in a dry room at a dew point below minus 40 degrees, and the equipment is not the equipment a conventional line uses. Stacking the thin electrolyte without pinholes is the yield-limiting step, and a pinhole produces a short that shows up weeks later.

Numbers in the launch materials are modest. Energy density is 720 watt-hours per litre against roughly 650 for the best conventional cells in the same package, and the cycle rating is 1,000 cycles to 80 per cent capacity rather than the 1,600 a graphite cell would last. The phone's charging rate is capped at 45 watts, well below what the liquid-electrolyte model accepts.

Cost is the constraint that decides whether this spreads. Industry estimates put the cell at roughly three times the price per kilowatt-hour of a conventional one, which a flagship phone with a high retail price can absorb and a mid-range handset cannot. Volume will follow cost, and cost depends on yield, which depends on the dry-room equipment that only two suppliers currently make.

Safety claims need a fair reading. A sulfide cell does not vent a flammable solvent, but it can still produce hydrogen sulfide if the casing is breached, and the phone's firmware reports a fault rather than continuing to charge when the pressure sensor trips. The gain is a smaller fire risk and a larger usable capacity, not an end to battery failure.

Second sourcing is the quiet constraint. The sulfide electrolyte powder comes from two suppliers, and qualifying a third takes about eighteen months because the particle size distribution affects how the electrolyte presses into a film. A phone maker that depends on one supplier for a chemistry it has just committed to is exposed to a single point of failure, and the launch schedule assumes the supplier's yield holds.

Recycling is also different: a sulfide cell cannot be shredded in air, because the electrolyte reacts with moisture, so end-of-life handling needs a dry process that no recycler currently operates at scale.

What to watch this year is whether the second product, a tablet with a 6,000 milliamp-hour cell, reaches the same yield. A single launch can be a hand-built line; two product lines at volume is the test of whether the chemistry has become manufacturing.
