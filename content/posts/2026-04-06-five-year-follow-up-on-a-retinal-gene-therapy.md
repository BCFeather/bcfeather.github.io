Gene therapy for inherited retinal disease delivers a functional copy of a gene into photoreceptors with a viral vector, and the question after the first trials was how long the effect lasts. A five-year follow-up published this month reports on 14 patients treated in one cohort, and the answer depends on the construct rather than on the disease.

Trial design was a single subretinal injection at three dose levels. Visual acuity, contrast sensitivity and retinal thickness were measured every six months, and the primary outcome was the change in acuity at five years.

Results are 12 of 14 holding their gain. The average improvement at one year was 12 letters on an acuity chart, and at five years 10 letters, which is a small decline within the measurement's variability. Two patients lost the gain entirely.

The exceptions are informative. Both were in the lowest dose cohort, and both showed a decline in retinal thickness that suggests the transduced cells turned over. The vector's promoter was the same in all patients, and the difference was dose.

Immunology was monitored and unremarkable. None of the patients developed antibodies against the vector's capsid at levels that would preclude a second dose, which matters because the contralateral eye is a candidate for retreatment.

Safety findings are the usual ophthalmic ones. Four patients had a transient rise in intraocular pressure, and two developed a cataract that was removed. No patient developed a sight-threatening inflammation after the first month.

Durability depends on the promoter. A constitutive promoter drives continuous expression, which is what these patients received; a cell-specific promoter can reduce expression and extend the life of the transduced cell. Two second-generation constructs are in trials.

Vector shedding was monitored and absent after the first month. The trial measured vector DNA in tears and blood at each visit, and no patient showed persistence beyond 30 days, which is the data a regulator needs for a repeat dose.

Cost is the deployment question. The therapy is priced at 850,000 dollars per eye, and the five-year durability is what a payer needs to justify it. A therapy that lasts five years and one that lasts twenty have very different budget impacts.

The surgical route is the delivery constraint. The vector is injected under the retina in a procedure that takes 90 minutes and requires a vitreoretinal surgeon, and the number of surgeons who can perform it limits how many patients can be treated in a year.

The outcome's shape is what the paper emphasizes. Improvement was largest at year one and then held rather than growing, which suggests the vector's expression reached a plateau. A plateau is acceptable for a degenerative disease and is not a cure.

For a reader, the result is the kind of evidence gene therapy needs. A one-year gain is a proof of mechanism; a five-year gain is a proof of durability, and the field's unresolved question is whether a construct can deliver an effect that lasts the patient's lifetime.
