On the fourteenth of August the World Health Organization declared the mpox outbreak in central Africa a public health emergency of international concern. It was the second time in two years that the disease has earned the organization's highest alarm. A director-general announced the decision after an emergency committee failed to reach a consensus. Declaring an emergency has become the easy half of the work. The world is skilled at the announcement and slow at the funding.

Centre of the storm is the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where the disease has been endemic for decades. This outbreak is not the one that circled the globe in 2022. The country has counted more than twenty thousand suspected cases this year, in provinces where laboratory capacity to confirm them is thin.

Strain in question, clade 1b, passes more readily through close contact. It has appeared in countries that had never reported mpox. It has struck the young hardest, and in the Congo more than half of suspected cases have been in children under fifteen. An earlier outbreak, concentrated among adult men in Europe and North America, never had that profile.

Response follows from the difference. This is no longer a disease of one network, manageable with vaccines aimed at one population. It is an outbreak moving through crowded households and clinics in a region where the vaccine supply is counted in thousands and the need in millions. Vaccinating a household takes a week of logistics and a cold chain that many clinics do not have.

Record on such alarms is not encouraging, which is why the declaration deserves the useful kind of scepticism. An emergency declared for mpox in July 2022 produced a wave of vaccines and attention that ended as soon as the outbreak in rich countries subsided, leaving the disease to smoulder in the Congo where it had always smouldered. This emergency is in large part the bill for that attention span. The African Union's health agency had asked for the declaration weeks earlier, and its plea for vaccines, tests and research money has been answered, so far, with pledges rather than deliveries.

Reasons for hope are buried in the failure. Vaccines developed in a hurry in 2022 exist and can be shipped. Countries now affected learned from the last round what surveillance and community outreach look like. What is missing is distribution, a matter of money and political will rather than of science. A dose that costs a dollar to make costs several to move to a clinic that has no refrigerator.

Overdue and insufficient is the honest reading of the declaration. Treat it as a promise that the world intends to do better this time, and the difference between this emergency and the last will be counted in the weeks ahead, in doses crossing borders, in tests reaching clinics, and in whether the children of the Congo are treated as part of the world the alarm is meant to protect. The alarm has sounded. Hearing it has never been the hard part.
