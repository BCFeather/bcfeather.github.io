---

The disinflation of 2023 and 2024 was the reason the Federal Reserve could cut three times starting in September, and the January report published on 12 February was the first evidence that the process had stalled. The consumer price index rose 0.5 per cent in the month, against a forecast of 0.3, and the annual rate moved up from 2.9 to 3.0 per cent.

Details were as uncomfortable as the headline. Shelter rose 0.4 per cent and accounted for nearly 30 per cent of the monthly increase, which is the component that has been the slowest to fall and the one that matters most for the 12-month rate. Energy rose 1.1 per cent on a 1.8 per cent rise in petrol prices, and food rose 0.4 per cent, so the increase was not confined to a single volatile category.

Core inflation, which excludes food and energy, rose 0.4 per cent in the month and 3.3 per cent over the year. That is the number that the committee watches, and it moved further from the 2 per cent target rather than closer to it. A core rate above 3 per cent with an unemployment rate below 4.2 per cent describes an economy in which demand is still strong enough to carry price increases.

Market reaction was immediate. Equity indices fell, Treasury yields rose and the probability that the committee would cut at the March meeting dropped towards zero. A single report does not change a policy path, and this one changed the market's expectation of the path, which is the channel through which the report reaches the economy before the committee meets.

Composition of the increase matters for the forecast. Goods prices had been falling through 2024 and services were the residual problem; in January the goods component turned positive, which suggests that the tariff announcements of the new administration were already reaching prices through importers who were building inventory ahead of them. A tariff is a tax on the importer, and whether it appears in the consumer price index depends on how much of the cost the retailer absorbs.

What the report did to the committee's position was remove the option of a cut in the near term. The January statement had already dropped the language about progress towards the target, and the data confirmed the deletion. A committee that said it was not in a hurry in January now had a reason to stay unhurried, and the next meeting in March would be judged on whether the February report showed a reversal.

Risk is the one that every inflation episode carries. A single month of 0.5 per cent is noise; three months of it is a trend, and the committee has said it will look at the trend. The February report, published in March, was 0.2 per cent and 2.8 per cent, which relieved the pressure without restoring the case for a cut, and the year would go on to be one in which the rate did not move at all.
