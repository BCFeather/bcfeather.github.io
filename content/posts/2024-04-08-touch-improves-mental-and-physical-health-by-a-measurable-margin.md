Receiving touch is a basic social signal, and its health effects have been studied in small trials for decades. A paper in Nature Human Behaviour on 8 April 2024 pools those trials. Anxiety and depression are measured with scales that differ between trials, and a pooled estimate has to place them on a common axis. Advice is what a health service can act on, and acting requires knowing who should deliver the touch and for how long. Touch is cheap and universal, and a cheap intervention with a moderate effect is one a clinic can adopt without a budget. Heterogeneity is the honest description of a literature that measures relationships with questionnaires. A clinic that adopts touch adopts a practice rather than a drug, and a practice is what a nurse can deliver without a prescription. A practice that a nurse can deliver is a practice that scales, and scaling is the only route from a meta-analysis to a ward.

Pooling is what turns a literature of small effects into an estimate with a margin. Mental health outcomes include anxiety, depression and stress, and physical outcomes include pain and weight gain in infants. Placing them requires standardization, and standardization is a judgement that the paper documents rather than hides. Duration is rarely reported consistently, and inconsistency is the moderator that the literature cannot yet resolve. Clinics adopt what is cheap, and cheapness is why the result matters more than its effect size suggests.

Grouping them is a decision about what counts as health, and the decision shapes the pooled number. Touch from a familiar person differs from touch delivered by an object, and the comparison is what the analysis separates. Infants are a separate population, and a separate population is where touch has been studied as a growth intervention rather than as a comfort. A moderator that cannot be resolved is a question for the next generation of trials rather than a defect of this one. A protocol for touch is a protocol for a relationship, and relationships are not standardized in the way a trial standardizes a drug.

A familiar hand is a social stimulus, and a weighted blanket is not. The effect is real and moderate for mental health, and smaller for physical outcomes. Growth interventions have a physical endpoint, and the endpoint is weight, which is measurable without a questionnaire. Standardization of a relationship is the design problem, and the problem is why the literature is heterogeneous.

Moderate effects matter at population scale, and scale is where a health service operates. Questionnaires are self-reported, and self-report is the weakest link in any mental health meta-analysis.

Heterogeneity across trials is large, which is what a meta-analysis of social interventions expects. A weak link does not invalidate a pooled effect, and the paper's robustness checks are directed at the link.

Large heterogeneity limits generalization, and the paper reports it. Familiarity of the toucher is a moderator that changes the effect, and a moderator is what turns a general claim into advice.
