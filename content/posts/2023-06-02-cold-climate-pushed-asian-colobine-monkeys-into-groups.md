A primate's social system is usually explained by ecology, with group size following food distribution and food distribution following climate. The causal chain is plausible and rarely tested across a whole clade.

In *Science* on 2 June 2023, it is tested. The authors compare social organization across Asian colobine monkeys, relate it to climatic and dietary variables, and ask whether cold climates are associated with particular social arrangements.

Association is the result. Species living in cold, seasonal habitats tend to form larger groups with more complex structure, and the association survives phylogenetic correction.

Mechanism supplies a plausible link. A cold winter removes leaves and forces a monkey to range widely and rely on fallback foods, and a wide range with a scarce resource favours a group that can defend a territory. Physiology adds the other link, since a colobine's gut ferments leaves and fermentation generates heat, which makes a large body and a long gut advantageous in the cold.

Social consequence is the interesting part. A group that must travel far and share scarce food has an incentive for cooperation, and the analysis of group composition suggests the cold-climate species have more of it. Fallback diet sets the seasonal bottleneck, because a tropical forest supplies leaves all year and a temperate one does not, so a cold-climate colobine must survive a period without its preferred food, and the social system answers that period.

Comparative method is the study's strength. A trait appearing once can be an accident, and one appearing repeatedly in cold lineages is a pattern. Group structure is what the authors measure, since a one-male group and a multi-male group differ in how many adults cooperate, and the classification follows observed composition rather than a theoretical scheme. Inference sets the limitation, because a correlation between climate and social structure is not an experiment and a third variable such as food quality could carry the causal direction. Sequence stays unestablished, since a species' climate changed and its social system changed, and the order of the two belongs to the fossil and biogeographic record.

Behavioural ecology gains a clade-level test: the field's hypotheses are usually tested within a species, and a comparison across the colobines asks whether they scale. Phylogenetic correction is the necessary step, because close relatives share traits through ancestry and an uncorrected correlation can be an artefact of the tree, while body size is a covariate carried through the analysis since a larger monkey loses heat more slowly and the social signal must be separated from the size signal. Conservation reads it directly, because a cold-climate colobine has a narrow range and a specialized diet, its habitat moves uphill as the climate warms, and a social system resting on a seasonal bottleneck is one a changed season can disrupt. A behavioural test would strengthen the argument further, because cooperation in a cold-climate group should be observable as sharing and as tolerance at a feeding site, and those are measurements a field study can make.
