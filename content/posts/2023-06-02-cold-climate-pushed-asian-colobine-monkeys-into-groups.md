description: "Adaptations to cold climates are linked to the evolution of larger and more complex social groups in Asian colobine monkeys, according to a comparative study published in *Science* on 2 June 2023."
---

A primate's social system is usually explained by ecology: group size follows food distribution, and food distribution follows climate. The causal chain is plausible and rarely tested across a whole clade.

The study in *Science*, 2 June 2023 tests it. The authors compare social organization across Asian colobine monkeys, relate it to climatic and dietary variables, and ask whether cold climates are associated with particular social arrangements.

The association is the result. Species that live in cold, seasonal habitats tend to form larger groups with more complex structure, and the association survives phylogenetic correction.

The mechanism is the plausible link. A cold winter removes leaves and forces a monkey to range widely and to rely on fallback foods, and a wide range and a scarce resource favour a group that can defend a territory.

The physiology is the other link. A colobine's gut ferments leaves, and fermentation generates heat, so that a large body and a long gut are advantages in the cold.

The social consequence is the interesting part. A group that must travel far and share scarce food has an incentive for cooperation, and the authors' analysis of group composition suggests that the cold-climate species have more of it.

The comparative method is the study's strength. A trait that appears once can be an accident, and a trait that appears repeatedly in cold lineages is a pattern.

The limitation is the inference. A correlation between climate and social structure is not an experiment, and the causal direction could run through a third variable such as food quality.

What the paper cannot establish is the sequence. A species' climate changed and its social system changed, and the order of the two is a question for the fossil and the biogeographic record.

For behavioural ecology, the value is a clade-level test. The field's hypotheses are usually tested within a species, and a comparison across the colobines asks whether the hypotheses scale.

The dietary fallback is the seasonal bottleneck. A tropical forest supplies leaves all year, and a temperate one does not, so a cold-climate colobine must survive a period when its preferred food is absent. The social system is a response to that period.

The group's structure is what the authors measure. A one-male group and a multi-male group differ in how many adults cooperate, and the paper's classification follows the species' observed composition rather than a theoretical scheme.

The phylogenetic correction is the necessary step. Closely related species share traits for reasons of ancestry, and a correlation without correction can be an artefact of the tree. The authors' comparative model is what separates the two.

The body size is a covariate the analysis carries. A larger monkey loses heat more slowly, and the cold-climate species tend to be larger, so the social signal must be separated from the size signal.

The conservation implication is direct. A cold-climate colobine has a narrow range and a specialized diet, and a warming climate moves its habitat uphill. A social system that depends on a seasonal bottleneck is a system that a changed season can disrupt.
