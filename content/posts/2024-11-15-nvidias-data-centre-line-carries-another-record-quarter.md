---

NVIDIA reported revenue of 35.1 billion dollars for the quarter ended in late October, an increase of 94 per cent on the same quarter a year earlier, and 30.8 billion of it came from data centre products. Gross margin was 74.6 per cent, and the company told investors that the next quarter would be about 37.5 billion, which is a number that assumes the supply chain delivers. A quarter that doubles on the year before is a quarter that has to be compared with a market that was already large.

Demand comes from a small number of customers. A handful of hyperscale buyers account for a large share of the data centre line, and each of them is building clusters that take tens of thousands of accelerators. A market that is concentrated in four buyers is a market that is sensitive to one of them pausing a build. A cluster that takes ten thousand accelerators is a purchase that a buyer plans two years in advance.

Constraint is manufacturing rather than design. The newest generation of accelerators is built on a process that only one foundry can run at volume, and the memory that goes beside it is also in short supply. A company that can sell everything it can make is a company whose growth is set by its suppliers. A memory stack that has to be bought with the accelerator is a second constraint that the chip designer does not control.

Margin is the part that competitors study. A gross margin near three quarters is a margin that pays for the software stack, the reference designs and the field engineers who make a cluster work. A rival that matches the silicon but not the support does not match the margin, and the margin is what funds the next generation. A software stack that a developer already knows is a margin that a competitor has to spend years to reach. A reference design that ships with the chip is a design that a customer's engineers do not have to invent.

Customer's arithmetic differs from the vendor's. A buyer that spends a billion dollars on a cluster has to earn a return on it, and the return depends on how many tokens the cluster can serve and what a token sells for. A price war in inference would compress the return and slow the buying, which is the risk that the guidance does not mention. A token that sells for less than it costs to serve is a token that stops the build.

What the quarter shows is that the accelerator business has become the most concentrated part of the computing industry. One company's supply chain is the bottleneck for the entire build-out, and the customers who depend on it have begun to design their own alternatives. A customer that designs its own chip is a customer that has decided the margin is too high.
