title: "British inflation holds above 10 per cent as the food prices bite"
date: "2023-04-19T09:20:00+08:00"
categories: [economy]
description: "The consumer price index fell less than expected to 10.1 per cent in March, with the food inflation at 19.1 per cent, the highest since 1977."
---

British consumer prices rose by 10.1 per cent over the year to March, down from 10.4 per cent in February and above the forecast of 9.8. The monthly change was 0.8 per cent. The core measure, which strips the energy and the food, was 6.2 per cent, unchanged from the previous month. The figure was the seventh month above 10 per cent, and the peak of 11.1 per cent came in October. The services inflation was the part the bank watches, and the 6.2 per cent core was unchanged for a third month.

Food was the reason for the miss. The prices of the food and the non-alcoholic drinks rose by 19.1 per cent over the year, the fastest since August 1977, and the bread and the cereals rose by about 19. The salad and the vegetable shortages of the winter have eased, and the prices of the fresh vegetables fell by about 2 per cent on the month. The prices of the milk fell by about 2 per cent on the month, and the eggs rose by about 4 as the supply stayed short. The food and the drink have added about 2.5 percentage points to the headline over the year, which is more than the energy.

Services were firm. The prices of the services rose by about 6.6 per cent, and the transport by about 9 as the rail fares were raised in March. The restaurants and the hotels rose by about 11, which is the part that the wage growth feeds. The air fares rose by about 12 per cent over the year, and the package holidays by about 20 as the demand for the summer returned. The rents rose by about 5 per cent, and the measure the bank watches for the housing costs is running at about 6.

Energy helped a little. The cap on the household bills was raised in April, and the effect shows in the April figures rather than in these. The petrol prices fell by about 6 per cent over the year, and the electricity rose by about 40 before the cap moved. The regulator's cap was set at about 2,500 pounds for the typical household from April, which is about 1,000 below the level of the winter. The wholesale gas has fallen by about 60 per cent since the summer, and the effect on the bills will show from July.

Markets moved. The traders raised the odds of another increase in May to about 90 per cent, and the two-year yield rose by about 15 basis points. The pound strengthened against the dollar, and the gilts fell. The market now expects the rate to reach about 5 per cent by the autumn, and the bank's own forecast had been for 4.3. The mortgage fixes that expire this year are about 2 percentage points higher than the ones they replace, and the repayments are the channel through which the tightening reaches the households.

This report leaves the bank in a difficult place. The inflation has been above 10 per cent for seven months, and the growth is close to zero. A central bank that faces 10 per cent inflation and no growth is a central bank whose next move will please nobody. The bank has raised the rate eleven times since the end of 2021, and the next meeting is in early May. A food inflation of 19 per cent is the one that the voters feel in the shops, and the politics of the price is the part the bank cannot answer.
