title: "Fish grew new kinds of teeth and then multiplied"
date: "2025-02-26T14:05:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "A comparative study of fish dentitions published in the *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* finds that lineages which evolved a new kind of tooth more easily diversified faster, which supports the idea that a key innovation can open a niche."
---

Teeth are the part of a vertebrate that fossilizes best and that records diet most directly, and fish have the widest variety of them. A fish may carry teeth on its jaws, on the roof of its mouth, in its throat and on its gill arches, and the number and the arrangement differ between groups to a degree that a palaeontologist can use to identify a species from a fragment.

Study asked whether the ability to produce teeth in a new position is a property that a lineage acquires once and keeps, which would make it a key innovation in the classical sense. The measure was the ease with which the developmental programme for a tooth can be redeployed, which the authors inferred from the anatomy of the tooth-bearing bones and from the phylogenetic distribution of the arrangements.

The groups that could redeploy the programme diversified more. The rate of species formation was higher in lineages where the dental pattern was flexible, and the effect held when the analysis controlled for the age of the lineage and for the size of the body, which are the two variables that most often produce a spurious correlation in this kind of comparison.

Mechanism is developmental. A tooth forms where a field of cells expresses a set of genes, and a bone that acquires a tooth is a bone that has acquired the field. A lineage in which the field can be induced at a new site can add teeth without changing the ones it has, which is a change that does not disturb the existing function, does not have to be compensated elsewhere and can be tested by selection one site at a time.

That flexibility is what makes the innovation a key one. A trait that improves survival in a fixed way raises the fitness of a lineage; a trait that makes further change easier raises the rate at which the lineage can explore, and the second is the one that shows up as a difference in diversification rather than in abundance.

Result is a statement about evolvability, which is a concept that has been difficult to test. A trait that is easy to vary produces more variants, and if the variants are selectable, the lineage diversifies. The fish dentition provides a case in which the ease of the change can be estimated from the anatomy and the rate of diversification can be counted from the tree, and the two correlate across several hundred million years of fish history.

What the study cannot show is the direction of causation. A lineage that is diversifying may encounter more habitats, and a lineage in more habitats may evolve more dental arrangements, so the correlation could run the other way. The authors argue that the anatomy precedes the diversification in the groups where the sequence can be dated, and the argument is plausible without being decisive.
