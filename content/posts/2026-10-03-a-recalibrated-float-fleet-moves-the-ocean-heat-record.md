The Argo programme is one of the quiet successes of oceanography. Roughly four thousand autonomous floats, spread across every ocean, sink to two thousand metres, rise, measure temperature and salinity on the way up, transmit by satellite and repeat the cycle every ten days. The fleet has been running for more than two decades, and the global ocean heat content record rests largely on it. A paper published in *Nature* on 1 October reports that the fleet has been measuring slightly wrong, in a consistent direction, and that the error is now correctable.

The culprit is a pressure sensor. Each float estimates its depth from a piezoresistive pressure reading, and those sensors drift as they age, by a few decibars over years. A drift of that size sounds trivial, and for many purposes it is. For heat content it is not: the ocean is stratified, and a float that thinks it is twenty metres deeper than it is samples water from a colder layer and records it at the wrong depth. Across the fleet, the effect accumulates into a systematic bias in the integrated heat content, which is the quantity everyone uses.

The correction came from cross-calibration. A subset of floats carried reference-grade sensors, and a larger subset were compared against shipboard conductivity-temperature-depth profiles taken during deployment and recovery cruises. By tracking how each float's pressure reading diverged over its lifetime, the authors built a drift model and applied it retrospectively to the archive. The revised series shows the ocean has absorbed roughly eight per cent more heat since 2005 than the uncorrected record suggested.

Eight per cent is not a rounding error in the context of the planetary energy imbalance. The ocean takes up more than ninety per cent of the excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases, so an eight per cent revision to that reservoir is a revision to the total imbalance of a comparable order. It matters for estimates of how fast the planet is warming, for the thermal expansion component of sea level rise, and for the calibration of satellite measurements of the Earth's radiation budget, which are compared against ocean heat content as an independent check.

The paper is careful about what the correction does not fix. Argo samples the upper two thousand metres, and the deep ocean below that is measured by a much sparser set of instruments, so the total heat content is still a lower bound on the true value. The correction is also global; regionally, the drift varies with sensor batch and deployment, and the authors flag several basins where the sampling is too thin to constrain the adjustment well.

The methodological point is the one worth keeping. Ocean heat content is a derived quantity, built from millions of individual measurements, each with an instrument history attached. The record is not a ruler that stays the same length; it is a network that has to be recalibrated against itself as its components age. Papers like this are how a long-term climate record is maintained, and they rarely make headlines because the headline is that yesterday's number was slightly wrong.
