Anti-poverty programmes are evaluated on income, and their effects on behaviour are usually left out. A transfer that raises a household's consumption also changes its position relative to its neighbours, and that change can alter how people treat one another.

In *PNAS* on 7 March 2023, the behavioural effect is measured. Wenchao Li, Zhiming Leng, Junjian Yi, Songfa Zhong and colleagues exploit a programme in China whose eligibility followed a threshold, and compare households just above it with households just below.

Design is a fuzzy regression discontinuity. Eligibility is a function of a score, and households close to the cutoff are similar in everything but their treatment status, which makes the comparison close to causal.

Because games that measure trust, altruism and reciprocity are played by participants, the outcomes are social preferences, and the treated and untreated groups differ in their choices, so an effect on income is accompanied by an effect on how the money is shared. Components complicate attribution, since a multifaceted programme combines cash, training and services and the package makes it hard to credit the behavioural change to any one part, which the paper discusses openly.

Interpretation is contested, because a richer household may give more because it has more to give or give less because it no longer depends on neighbours, and the paper's measures distinguish the channels. Gender deserves a note, since transfers that go to women change household bargaining, and a preference measure that is not disaggregated by gender can miss the mechanism the programme actually uses.

Measurement is the weak point in two ways. A laboratory game is a stylized interaction, so a preference measured in it is a proxy for the behaviour a household exhibits with a neighbour, and a participant who has just completed a programme may answer differently because of the attention the survey brings, while a control group that knows it was not selected may respond in kind.

External validity sets the limit, since a programme in one province with one design is one data point and a cash transfer in a different setting may produce a different response. Long-run change stays invisible, because a preference that shifts during a programme may revert, and a follow-up survey after several years would show whether the change is durable. Development economics gains a reusable design, since a threshold-based programme is a natural experiment and the method applies wherever eligibility has a cutoff, while policy gains a reminder that an evaluation counting income and ignoring behaviour misses part of the effect, and that a programme eroding social capital carries a cost no budget line shows. Attrition is a further threat, since households that migrate out of the study area are missing from the follow-up and their absence may be correlated with the transfer, which is the kind of bias a panel has to correct. What the design fixes is the cutoff, and a programme that changed its formula midstream would break the comparison, which is why the stability of eligibility is the assumption worth checking first.
