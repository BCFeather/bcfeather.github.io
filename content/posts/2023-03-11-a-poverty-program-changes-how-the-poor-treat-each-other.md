---

Anti-poverty programs are evaluated on income, and their effects on behaviour are usually left out. A transfer that raises a household's consumption also changes the household's position relative to its neighbours, and that change can alter how people treat one another.

The study in *PNAS*, 7 March 2023 measures the behavioural effect. Wenchao Li, Zhiming Leng, Junjian Yi, Songfa Zhong and colleagues exploit a program in China whose eligibility followed a threshold, and compare households just above and just below it.

The design is a fuzzy regression discontinuity. A household's eligibility is a function of a score, and households close to the cutoff are similar in everything but their treatment status. The comparison that the design permits is close to causal.

The outcomes are social preferences. Games that measure trust, altruism and reciprocity are played by participants, and the treated and untreated groups differ in their choices. The program's effect on income is accompanied by an effect on how the money is shared.

The interpretation is contested. A transfer that makes a household richer may increase its generosity because it has more to give, or decrease it because it no longer depends on neighbours. The paper's measures distinguish the channels.

The external validity is the limitation. A program in one province with one design is one data point, and a cash transfer in a different setting may produce a different behavioural response. The design's strength is internal, and the paper is careful about the external claim.

The policy use is a reminder. An evaluation that counts income and ignores behaviour misses part of the effect, and a program that erodes social capital has a cost that a budget line does not show.

The measurement's limitation is the game. A laboratory game is a stylized interaction, and a preference measured in a game is a proxy for the behaviour that a household exhibits with a neighbour.

What the paper cannot see is the long run. A preference that shifts during a program may revert, and a follow-up survey after several years would show whether the change is durable.

For development economics, the value is a design that can be reused. A threshold-based program is a natural experiment, and the method that this paper applies is available wherever eligibility has a cutoff.

The program's components are multiple. A multifaceted intervention combines cash, training and services, and the package makes it hard to attribute the behavioural change to any one part. The paper's discussion of that ambiguity is honest.

The gender dimension is worth noting. Transfers that go to women change household bargaining, and a preference measure that is not disaggregated by gender can miss the mechanism that the program actually uses.

The behavioural measure is the experiment's weak point in a different way. A participant who has just completed a program may answer a game differently because of the attention the survey brings, and a control group that knows it was not selected may respond in kind.
