A gut bacterium is a difficult target for a gene editor, because the tools that work in a cell culture do not survive the trip through the stomach. A phage can make that trip, since a filamentous phage is a protein coat around a single strand of DNA and it is stable at the pH of the stomach for long enough to be swallowed in a buffered solution. The delivery problem and the editing problem are separate, and the work that combines them packages an editor inside a phage and lets the phage do the targeting, which is where the specific work of this study sits. A phage also has to survive the bile and the mucus layer, and the coating proteins that protect it from the stomach do not protect it from everything.

What does the editing is a base editor, which is a Cas9 that has been mutated so that it no longer cuts and a deaminase that changes one base into another. A cytidine deaminase fused to a nickase converts a C to a U within a window on the non-target strand, and the repair machinery resolves the mismatch in favour of the edit. The advantage over a nuclease is that no double-strand break is made, so the chromosome is not put at risk of a rearrangement, and the edit can be a single base change in a codon, which is the smallest change that a codon needs. A base editor also has a size problem, since the Cas9 and the deaminase together are larger than a plasmid can comfortably carry. A deaminase that is fused to the nickase is the part that decides the window, and the window is set by the linker between the two domains.

Delivery falls to a phage, and the payload is a plasmid and not the phage genome itself. A filamentous phage packages a single-stranded genome that is a few kilobases long, and a base editor is larger than that, so the editor was split across a plasmid that the phage carries into the cell and a protein that the phage displays. The delivery works because the phage attaches to a pilus on the surface of the bacterium and injects its DNA, and a plasmid that carries the editor then replicates in the recipient. The split into two parts is a design constraint that the phage genome imposes, and the protein that is displayed on the coat is the part that has to fold inside a bacterial membrane.

The animal experiment is what makes the claim. Mice with a human-derived bacterial strain in their gut were given the phage by mouth, and the edit was scored in the bacteria recovered from faeces. The fraction of edited cells rose over the days of dosing and fell when the dosing stopped, which is what a payload that does not integrate should do, and the fall is also the reason the approach is not a one-off cure. The bacteria that were not edited outgrew the ones that were once the selection pressure was gone. The dose is the other number that matters, since a phage that is cleared by the immune system delivers fewer copies than the same number of phage given to a mouse without antibody.

Specificity is the open question. A base editor changes every C in its window, and a window placed near a codon can change neighbouring bases, so the off-target edits are counted by sequencing the whole genome of the recovered bacteria. The reported rate was low against a background of the mutations that a lab strain already carries, and the comparison has to be made in the same strain and not across strains. The other risk is the one that phage therapy has always had, which is that a bacterium can lose the receptor the phage uses. A window that covers a codon is designed by choosing the guide sequence, and the guide is the part that has to be specific across the bacterial population.

Any route to a treatment runs through the pathogens and not through the commensals. A strain that carries a toxin gene or a resistance gene is a target whose edit would be therapeutic, and the same phage could be given to a patient with a chronic infection. The obstacles are regulatory, since a phage that has been engineered is a biological product with a genome that can be sequenced, and the trials that would show a benefit are the trials that have not yet been run. A delivery system that works for one strain can be defeated by a single mutation in the receptor, and no phage has been shown to be durable across a patient population. A trial would need a strain that is both a pathogen and tractable to phage, and the strains that cause chronic gut disease are not always the ones that a phage can enter.
