Metamaterials get their properties from structure and not from chemistry, and the structures that engineers can build have a recurring problem: the motions they produce are coupled. A lattice that twists also shortens, and a lattice that shortens also twists, so a device that needs one motion has to accept the other. Earlier chiral designs also worked within a strain of about 2 per cent, which is a demonstration and not a mechanism.

A paper in *Nature* on 23 April 2025 reports a design that separates the motions. The structure combines flat tessellations of rotating squares, which are auxetic in the sense that they expand in both directions when pulled, with arrays of columns folded in the Kresling pattern, the tubular origami that collapses by twisting. The two components respond to the same input but produce different deformations, and their combination is what decouples the response.

Numbers describe a large motion, not a small one. Under a single degree of freedom, the assembly twists between 0 and 90 degrees, contracts in plane by up to 25 per cent, and shrinks out of plane by more than 50 per cent. Those are numbers a device can use: a 90 degree rotation is a switch, a quarter of the footprint is a stowage volume, and half the height is a lift.

The mechanism is geometric and therefore scale-free. Because the behaviour comes from the arrangement of rigid panels and hinges and not from the elasticity of a material, the same design can be built in millimetres for a medical stent and in metres for a deployable structure. The experiments and simulations in the paper show that the predicted deformation matches the measured one, which is the test a metamaterial has to pass before anyone builds with it.

Manufacturing is the constraint the paper does not solve. A structure with hundreds of hinges needs either a precise assembly or a process that prints the hinges in place, and the tolerances at each hinge accumulate across the array. The demonstrations are small, the materials are stiff polymers, and a version that survives a thousand cycles of deployment has not been reported. Scale-up is where most metamaterials stop.

Applications that come to mind are deployable. A solar array that folds into a quarter of its area, a radiator that changes its projected area on command, an implant that expands in one direction while shortening in another. Each needs exactly the property the paper provides: one input, two independent outputs, and a geometry that does not depend on the material.

What would settle the design is a fatigue test and a manufacturing route. A structure that twists 90 degrees and returns, ten thousand times, without a hinge failing, would move the work from a demonstration to a component. Until then the contribution is a design principle, and design principles are the part of metamaterials research that lasts.
