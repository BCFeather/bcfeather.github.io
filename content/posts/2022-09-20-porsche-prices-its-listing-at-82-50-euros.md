Volkswagen announced the price on 20 September after a bookbuilding that had been oversubscribed several times. The shares were offered at 82.50 euros each, the top of a range that started at 76.50. The listing valued Porsche at 75 billion euros, which made it the largest flotation in Europe since 2011. Demand from retail investors was the strongest part of the book, and the allocation was capped at 20 shares each.

Volkswagen sold 12.5 per cent of the ordinary shares to the Porsche and Piech families, who already controlled the parent, and 12.5 per cent of the preference shares to the public. The structure was unusual. The families paid a premium for the voting stock, and the deal gave them a blocking minority. Volkswagen kept the rest and consolidated the company.

Analysts estimated that Porsche earned about half of the group's profit before the split. The proceeds were about 9 billion euros, and Volkswagen said it would spend 5 billion on electric vehicles and 4 billion on a special dividend. The company had been criticized for its complexity, and the listing was presented as a way to unlock value. Volkswagen's own net debt fell by 4 billion euros after the transaction, and its credit rating was upgraded.

Qatari and Norwegian sovereign funds took large allocations, and several German institutions said they had been given less than they asked for. The order book was covered multiple times on the first day, and the retail tranche was 20 times oversubscribed. The shares started trading on 29 September at 84 euros. Investors had questioned the governance. The family holding controls the company through the ordinary shares, and the free float is small enough to limit liquidity. The company also has a supply agreement with Volkswagen that gives the parent control over some production decisions. The prospectus disclosed the arrangement and the risk. The first day's turnover was 1.8 billion euros, and the stock closed 3 per cent above its issue price.

Two other large flotations in Europe had been postponed in the spring, and the Porsche deal was seen as a signal that the window had reopened. The listing was a test of the market for new issues in a year when volumes had fallen by 80 per cent. The market took two more deals before the end of the year.

Porsche's first results as a public company were published in March and beat the forecast. Its operating margin was 18 per cent, which is the highest in the industry, and it said it would raise prices. The shares rose 30 per cent in the first six months and fell back with the rest of the sector in 2023. The flotation changed the ownership of German industry. The families that had been forced to sell Volkswagen's shares to the public in 2009 bought back a stake in the sports car maker, and the two companies became separate again. Volkswagen's own market value fell below Porsche's within a year.
