India will have about 1.4286 billion people by the middle of 2023, according to the World Population Prospects published by the United Nations on 19 April, and China will have about 1.4257 billion. The crossing took place during the year rather than on a date, and the report made the point that the two figures are projections rather than counts. The world total is 8.045 billion, and the growth is concentrated in a shrinking list of countries.

What matters is the shape of the two populations more than the ranking. The median age in India is 28, and in China it is 39, which means that India has several decades in which a large share of its people are of working age. China's population fell in 2022 for the first time since the famine of the early 1960s, and its fertility rate is now about 1.2 children per woman, well below the level at which a population replaces itself, and the country is now debating how to pay the pensions of a generation that will be smaller than the one before it.

The Indian number comes with its own arithmetic. Fertility has fallen from about 3.4 in 1990 to just below 2.0, which is replacement level, and it is below 1.7 in several southern states and in the cities. The growth that remains is in the north, in states where incomes are lower, schools are weaker and the workforce is less able to move, and that is where the next two decades of Indian demography will be decided.

Migration has become the third force in the numbers. India sends more migrants abroad than any other country, about 18 million people live outside it, and their remittances were 108 billion dollars in 2022. It also receives internal migrants on a scale that no census has measured properly, and the report notes that the countries gaining population fastest in the coming decades will be those with the youngest age structures, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa. The report expects the world to reach 9.7 billion in 2050 and to peak at about 10.4 billion in the 2080s.

Ageing is the part that governments have to pay for. China has 280 million people over 60, its pension system covers a minority of them adequately, and its provinces are already running deficits that the central government has to cover. India has no such burden yet, and it also has no system that would carry one, which means the dividend it is being offered depends on building one before the window closes.

The milestone will be remembered as a headline and it describes a slow turn. Half a century ago, India and China had populations of similar size and both were poor; one then restricted births and grew rich enough to pay for its own old age, the other grew faster in people than in income. Which of the two arrangements turns out to be more durable is the question that the next twenty years of the report will answer.
