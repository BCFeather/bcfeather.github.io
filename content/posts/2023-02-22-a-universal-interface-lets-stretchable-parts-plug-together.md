Stretchable electronics have a common failure point, and it is not the material. A soft conductor can stretch, a soft substrate can bend, and the joint between them is where the device breaks.

In *Nature* on 16 February 2023, an interface that survives strain is reported, designed for assembly rather than for a single device. The authors describe a universal interface that bonds stretchable components of different chemistries, so that a circuit can be built from modules and reconnected when a module is replaced.

Mechanical requirements come first. Two soft materials in contact must carry current while their interface accommodates deformation, and a rigid solder joint concentrates stress at its edges, whereas a soft joint distributes strain and a distributed strain does not crack. Chemistry follows, because elastomers and hydrogels bond through different mechanisms and an interface that works for one pair does not work for another, so a universal interface is one whose bonding mechanism does not depend on the pair.

Because a device built from modules can be repaired, upgraded and tested in parts, modularity is the consequence for assembly, and a wearable assembled from a sensor, a conductor and a power source can have one component replaced without discarding the rest. Resistance is a design variable too, since a joint that conducts well but stiffens the device is worse than one with slightly higher resistance that lets the whole assembly flex, and the characterization reports both numbers.

Demonstrations supply the evidence, with stretchable devices assembled through the interface and operated under strain while electrical performance holds rather than degrades. Comparison with rigid connectors is instructive, because a pin-and-socket is reliable and imposes a rigid patch on a soft system, while this interface trades some of that reliability for compliance, a trade worth taking in a device that has to move with a body. Medical use is the nearest application, since a patch that monitors a signal must conform to skin that moves, and a device that fails at its joints is one a patient notices.

Fatigue is the question a product would ask, because an interface that survives a hundred cycles is a laboratory result while a wearable experiences thousands, and the cycling data indicate a direction rather than a lifetime. Encapsulation follows, since a soft device in contact with skin or tissue must be sealed and a seal stiffer than the interface moves the failure to its edge, so the materials have to be co-designed. Manufacturing stays unresolved, because assembly by hand is not a process, and a universal interface that can be printed or laminated would be the version industry adopts. A standard part is what the field gains, since stretchable electronics have been a collection of bespoke devices, and an interface shared across devices is what turns a collection into a platform.

Standardization is the step that would turn the interface into a component, since a specification for resistance, stretch and bond strength is what lets two manufacturers' modules meet. A module that fails can be cut out, which is the maintenance argument in its simplest form, and the interface makes the cut cheap enough to be routine.
