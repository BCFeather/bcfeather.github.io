title: "A second booster cuts hospitalisation and death in the over-60s"
date: "2022-04-20T09:10:00+08:00"
categories: [science]
description: "Among 563,465 members of an Israeli health fund aged 60 to 100, a second booster was associated with a 64 per cent reduction in COVID hospitalisation and a 78 per cent reduction in death, relative to a single booster."
---

Israel offered a second booster to people over 60 in January 2022, four months after the first, and the decision was again taken ahead of the evidence. A cohort study within one health fund supplied the estimate within weeks. A fourth dose given four months after the third is the shortest interval that any regulator has allowed, and the schedule was chosen to match the interval that the first booster had used.

The cohort included 563,465 members aged 60 to 100 who were eligible on 3 January 2022, of whom 328,597 received a second booster during the 40-day study period. Hospitalisation and death were compared between those who received it and those who did not, with Cox models and time-dependent covariates. The paper appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 25 April 2022.

Hospitalisation due to COVID occurred in 270 recipients of a second booster and in 550 who had received one, an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.36 with an interval from 0.31 to 0.43. Death occurred in 92 and 232 respectively, a hazard ratio of 0.22 with an interval from 0.17 to 0.28. A hazard ratio of 0.36 for hospitalisation and 0.22 for death are the kind of estimates that a health ministry needs in order to decide, and they were produced within six weeks of the policy.

Both endpoints move together, and the difference in magnitude reflects the fact that a fourth dose restores neutralizing antibody against a variant that the third dose no longer matches well. Hospitalisation is a lower bar than death, so protection against the milder endpoint falls short of protection against the severe one. A fourth dose given to a population that has already had three is a booster of a booster, and the marginal benefit is smaller each time, which is what the interval between the estimates shows.

Its cohort is a health fund's membership, which is large and reasonably representative of older Israelis, and the analysis adjusts for demographic factors and coexisting illness. It does not adjust for behaviour, and people who seek a second booster may be more careful or more worried than those who do not. The comparison is between people who took the fourth dose and people who were eligible but did not, which is the comparison that a population actually makes.

A fourth dose for younger adults to younger adults is not answered. The absolute risk of death in a healthy 40-year-old is low enough that a 78 per cent relative reduction is a small number of events, and the benefit has to be weighed against the small risk of myocarditis.

That result was one input to the decision that several countries made in the following months, and it supports a second booster for people over 60 and for those whose immune response to vaccination is impaired. A country that has not yet offered a first booster to its older population should not read this as a reason to skip it, because the marginal benefit of a fourth dose depends on the level of the third.
