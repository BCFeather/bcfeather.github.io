Alphabet said on 20 January that it would cut about 12,000 jobs, roughly 6 per cent of its workforce and the largest reduction in the company's history. The chief executive, Sundar Pichai, told staff in a memo that the firm had expanded faster than its revenue growth during the pandemic and that the reductions would touch every product area and region. The memo went out at 6.30 in the morning California time, and the shares rose 3 per cent when the market opened.

Announcements like this one closed a month of retrenchment across the industry. Microsoft had said on 18 January that it would remove 10,000 roles, Amazon had begun a round of 18,000 that it disclosed earlier in the month, and Meta had added 11,000 in November. Trackers that count public announcements put technology layoffs above 50,000 for January alone. Four large technology firms together announced more than 50,000 cuts in the quarter, and none of them had done so in the previous decade.

Advertisers explain much of the pressure. Growth in search revenue had fallen to single digits in the fourth quarter, and the company had reported its first annual decline in advertising income since becoming Alphabet. Cloud, which had grown above 30 per cent through 2022, slowed as clients cut discretionary spending and renegotiated contracts. Headcount had nearly doubled since 2018, to 190,000, and the increase was concentrated in cloud and hardware.

Severance terms were reported as sixteen weeks of pay plus two weeks for each year of service, with health cover continuing through the notice period. Employees in some countries learned of the decision through calendar invitations that disappeared, the same pattern that had upset staff at other firms, and the company said it would keep a smaller number of roles open for people who wished to reapply. The company also closed some smaller projects, including a division working on augmented reality glasses for consumers.

Cost of the programme will show up in the first-quarter accounts. Alphabet expects a charge of between 1.9 and 2.3 billion dollars, most of it in the March quarter, and the savings run at roughly 2.5 billion dollars a year once the reductions are complete, according to calculations by analysts who cover the firm. Analysts put the annual saving at about 2 billion dollars against the 28 billion the firm spends on research.

Investors treated the cuts as a signal about discipline rather than distress. The shares rose in the days before the announcement, when reports of the plan first appeared, and the company had already announced a 70 billion dollar buyback and a change to its reporting segments. What remains unclear is whether the smaller headcount changes the amount spent on the projects that produced the growth in the first place. The buyback was larger than the previous year, which is how firms signal confidence while cutting costs.
