Negotiators for a global plastics treaty adjourned in Busan in the early hours of 1 December without an agreed text, after a week in which the divisions narrowed on waste and stayed fixed on production. The session will resume next year, and the delay is the outcome that the producers wanted. A session that was meant to be the last is a session that the chairs had described as the moment for a text. A session that was meant to conclude the negotiation is a session whose failure had been predicted by the chairs.

Fault line is familiar from climate talks. A group of countries that make polymers, and a group that imports waste, want an agreement that governs waste management and recycling, and a coalition of island states and African countries wants one that limits how much plastic is made. A treaty that caps production is a treaty that changes the economics of the petrochemical industry. A country that makes polymer is a country whose budget depends on the export.

Waste provisions are the easier half. A ban on some single-use items, a scheme for extended producer responsibility and rules on chemicals of concern are measures that most delegations can accept, and the text that emerged was close on those. Recycling argument is the producers' case. A treaty that sets targets for recycled content and for collection creates demand for a recycling industry, and the industry would need investment. A cap on production would strand the assets that the industry already has, and the countries that own them are not willing to write that off. A recycled-content target is a target that requires collection, sorting and a plant.
