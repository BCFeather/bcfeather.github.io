description: "A single oral dose of suraxavir marboxil shortened the time to symptom relief and cut viral shedding in adults and adolescents with uncomplicated influenza, a phase 3 trial in *Nature Medicine* finds."

Oseltamivir has been the standard antiviral for influenza for twenty years, and its limits are well documented. It has to be started within two days of illness, it is taken twice a day for five days, and resistance emerges readily in treated children, who shed virus for longer than adults do. Baloxavir marboxil, approved in 2018, showed that one dose of a polymerase inhibitor could do the same job, and the class has been the focus of antiviral development since then.

Suraxavir marboxil is a polymerase acidic endonuclease inhibitor developed in China, and the trial reported in *Nature Medicine* on 7 January was its phase 3 test. The design was multicentre, randomized, double-blind and placebo-controlled, and it enrolled adults and adolescents with acute uncomplicated influenza. Participants received one oral dose or placebo within 48 hours of the onset of symptoms, and the trial followed them for symptom resolution and for the amount of virus detectable in the upper airway.

The symptom endpoint was the time to alleviation of influenza symptoms, measured on a patient diary that scores fever, cough, sore throat, headache and muscle ache twice a day. The virological endpoint was the change in viral load and the duration of detectable virus, measured by culture and by molecular assay. Both endpoints moved in the treated group, and the paper reports the differences against placebo as statistically significant, with the effect appearing early in the follow-up and persisting through it.

Resistance is the part of the class that matters for public health. The endonuclease inhibitors select for substitutions in the polymerase acidic protein, and the experience with baloxavir showed that such variants can appear during treatment, can reduce the antiviral effect and can transmit within households. The suraxavir trial looked for treatment-emergent substitutions in paired samples and reported a low rate, which the authors present as a consequence of how the compound holds the active site.

What a one-dose oral drug changes in practice is adherence and logistics. A five-day course is easy to abandon once the fever breaks, and an abandoned course leaves virus replicating under drug pressure. A single observed dose removes that failure mode and fits stockpiling for a pandemic, where the constraint is the number of courses that can be distributed and the number of people who can be reached in the first days of an outbreak, and where a drug that needs five days of dosing is a drug that a strained clinic cannot finish.

The caveats are the ones that any phase 3 influenza trial carries. Uncomplicated influenza in otherwise healthy adults is a mild illness, so the trial cannot say what the drug does in patients who are hospitalized, pregnant or immunocompromised. The comparison was against placebo, which leaves open how the drug compares with oseltamivir or with baloxavir on the endpoints that clinicians use. Approval, price and prescribing habits will decide what the result means outside the trial, and those are decisions that the data cannot make.
