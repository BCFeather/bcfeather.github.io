A cloud is a population of supercooled droplets, and how each freezes sets the ice that follows. Freezing is fast and small, and the crystal forming inside a micrometre-sized drop has been hard to observe. In *Nature* on 17 August 2023, it is observed. The authors use X-ray scattering from a stream of droplets, and the pattern resolves the phases appearing as the drop solidifies.

Sequence is the result. A drop first forms a metastable phase and stable ice appears later, so freezing is a two-step process rather than a single transition. Latent heat explains the order, because a freezing drop releases heat and warms itself, and that warming can arrest the transition between the steps.

Nucleation is the first step. A droplet's nucleation rate depends on volume and temperature, and statistics over many drops give the rate. Microstructure is the second. Crystal order and defect density depend on cooling rate, and scattering shows the difference between fast and slow freezing; cooling rate is the control variable, since a quench favours a disordered solid while a slow ramp favours a crystal.

Atmospheric consequence supplies the motivation. A cloud's ice fraction depends on how its droplets freeze, and a two-step process changes the models predicting it. Radiative property is the consequence, because an ice cloud reflects differently from a liquid one, and the fraction of each is a number climate models need.

Ensemble is the method's advantage. A single drop's freezing is stochastic while a population's statistics are reproducible, and the experiment measures the population; the X-ray probe enables it, because a drop is small and scattering averages many of them.

Pressure and purity set the limitation. A laboratory drop is pure water at ambient pressure, and a cloud droplet carries aerosol at lower pressure. Aerosol is the atmospheric application, since a droplet with mineral dust freezes at higher temperature, and the pure-water case is the baseline against which that effect is measured.

Nucleation on a particle stays untested, because a droplet freezes on a particle or on a fluctuation, and pure water isolates the second mechanism. Atmospheric science gains a mechanism at the drop's scale, since models parameterize freezing with a rate, and a measurement of its dependence on volume and temperature is what a parameterization needs. A droplet containing a known mineral dust would show how the two-step sequence changes when a particle provides the nucleus, since heterogeneous freezing happens at a higher temperature and the metastable phase may be skipped, and that comparison is the one cloud models would need. A slower cooling rate would let the metastable phase transform completely, and the resulting crystal's defect density is what an ice cloud's optical properties would depend on, which is why the rate matters beyond the laboratory. A drop smaller than the X-ray beam's resolution limit would freeze at a rate the experiment cannot follow, which is the technical frontier the method would need to cross for cloud-relevant sizes.
