---

A cloud is a population of supercooled droplets, and how each one freezes sets the ice that follows. The freezing is fast and small, and the crystal that forms inside a micrometre-sized drop has been hard to observe.

The measurements in *Nature*, 17 August 2023 observe it. The authors use X-ray scattering from a stream of droplets, and the scattering pattern resolves the phases that appear as the drop freezes.

The sequence is the result. A drop first forms a metastable phase, and the stable ice appears later, so that the freezing is a two-step process rather than a single transition.

The nucleation is the first step. A droplet's nucleation rate depends on its volume and on its temperature, and the authors' statistics over many drops is what gives the rate.

The microstructure is the second. The ice's crystal order and its defect density depend on the cooling rate, and the scattering shows the difference between fast and slow freezing.

The atmospheric consequence is the motivation. A cloud's ice fraction depends on how its droplets freeze, and a two-step process changes the models that predict it.

The method's advantage is the ensemble. A single drop's freezing is stochastic and a population's statistics are reproducible, and the authors' experiment measures the population.

The limitation is the pressure and the purity. A laboratory drop is pure water at ambient pressure, and a cloud droplet contains aerosol and sits at a lower pressure.

What the paper does not show is the nucleus. A droplet freezes on a particle or on a fluctuation, and the authors' pure water isolates the second mechanism.

For atmospheric science, the value is a mechanism at the drop's scale. Cloud models parameterize freezing with a rate, and a measurement of the rate's dependence on volume and temperature is what a parameterization needs.

The metastable phase is the interesting intermediate. A phase that appears first and transforms later is one that a model with a single transition would miss, and the scattering pattern identifies it by its reflections.

The cooling rate is the control variable. A fast quench favours a disordered solid and a slow one favours a crystal, and the authors' range of rates is what maps the boundary.

The X-ray probe is the enabler. A droplet is small and a scattering experiment averages many of them, and the averaging is what turns a stochastic process into a measurable one.

The aerosol question is the atmospheric application. A droplet with a mineral dust particle freezes at a higher temperature, and the paper's pure-water case is the baseline against which the aerosol effect is measured.

The latent heat is the physics of the sequence. A freezing drop releases heat and warms itself, and the warming can arrest the transition between the two steps.

The cloud's radiative property is the consequence. An ice cloud reflects differently from a liquid one, and the fraction of each is a number that climate models need.
