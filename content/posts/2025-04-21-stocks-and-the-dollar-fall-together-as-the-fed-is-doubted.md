description: "American stocks fell more than 2 per cent and the dollar hit a three-year low on 21 April, after the president renewed his attacks on the Federal Reserve chair."
---

A market that sells stocks usually buys dollars, because the two move in opposite directions when the news is about growth. On 21 April both fell at once. The S&P 500 lost 2.36 per cent to close at 5,158.20, the Dow fell 971.82 points, or 2.48 per cent, to 38,170.41, and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 2.55 per cent to 15,870.90. The dollar index slid to its lowest level in three years, gold set a record, and the ten-year Treasury yield rose.

Trigger was political and not macroeconomic. The president had spent the weekend criticising the Federal Reserve chair, Jerome Powell, for not cutting rates, and on Monday he called him a major loser and urged an immediate cut. Powell had said the previous week that tariffs were likely to move the central bank further from its goals, probably for the rest of the year, which is a statement about inflation and about patience. The two positions are not compatible, and the market priced the conflict and not either view.

What is unusual is the combination. Yields rising while the dollar falls means investors are demanding more compensation for holding American assets and less for holding the currency, which is what a risk premium looks like when it is applied to a country and not to a company. The S&P 500 ended the day 16 per cent below its record, set two months earlier, and the losses were concentrated in the large technology names that had carried the index.

Removing a central bank's independence is a slow-acting threat with a fast-acting price. Investors can hold a view about tariffs and adjust positions; they cannot easily price a monetary policy that responds to a president's preference, because the distribution of outcomes widens. A wider distribution raises the discount rate applied to every cash flow, and a higher discount rate is a fall in the value of the index, which is the mechanism behind a two per cent day.

The legal question sits underneath. The Federal Reserve Act allows a president to remove a governor for cause, and the courts have never defined what cause means for a policy disagreement. A president who says he is considering dismissal has therefore created an option that the market has to value, even if the option is never exercised. That is why the move showed up in the dollar and in gold as much as in stocks.

Nothing in the day's data justified the fall. There was no inflation report, no employment report, and no decision from the committee. The move was a repricing of institutional risk, and repricing of that kind tends to reverse slowly, because the underlying question is unresolved by design. A tariff can be negotiated down in a week; a norm about central bank independence is either kept or broken.

What would settle it is a nomination, a court case, or a statement from the committee that it will not be moved. Until one of those arrives, the market will trade the headline, and the headline will arrive on the president's schedule. The lesson for a reader is that the cost of a political attack on an institution is paid in the currency before it is paid in anything else.
