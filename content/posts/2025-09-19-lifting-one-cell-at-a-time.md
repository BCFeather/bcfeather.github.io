---

Magnetic levitation of living cells is an old trick with a new refinement, and the refinement is what turns a sorting method into an instrument. The old version placed a population in a paramagnetic medium and let density do the sorting: cells of different types settle at different heights, and a photograph of the column reads as a profile of the sample's density, which is a useful measurement and a crude one. The new version, described in *PNAS* on 8 September, works at the scale of one cell, steering it in three dimensions without contact and without the forces that ordinary tweezers bring.

The trick is to give the cell something to respond to without giving it something that changes its behaviour. A small amount of magnetic material, attached to the cell surface or carried inside it, couples the cell to a field that can be shaped by coils. Change the current and the field gradient moves; the cell follows, with a lag of milliseconds that the authors measure and report. Because the forces are small, the cell survives, and because the control is electronic, the position can be changed in milliseconds rather than by replacing a sample and refocusing the microscope.

Levitation has an obvious advantage over adhesion. A cell stuck to a dish is a cell in an unnatural posture, flattened against plastic and unable to move. A cell held in suspension keeps its shape, and can be brought alongside another cell and then separated again, which is what experiments on cell-cell contact and on immune recognition actually need. The authors demonstrate pairing and controlled approach, and report that the cells remain viable through repeated manipulation, with membrane integrity and division unaffected over the hours the experiments lasted.

There are limits, and the authors list them. Throughput is low by the standards of flow cytometry, the magnetic label is a perturbation of its own however small, and the medium that makes levitation possible is not the medium a cell would choose. The method is a microscope technique rather than a screening tool, suited to questions where a handful of cells observed closely is worth more than a million counted quickly.

That is a real category of question. Immune synapses, the moment a T cell recognizes a target, occur in seconds and in a geometry that a dish distorts. A method that can hold two cells at a chosen angle and watch what follows is a way to study that moment directly, and to repeat it with the parameters changed by a fraction, which is how a phenomenon turns into a measurement.

The paper is a methods paper, and should be judged as one, which means on reproducibility and on whether the apparatus fits on an ordinary bench. Whether other laboratories adopt it depends on how easy the apparatus is to build, which the authors address at length. The appeal is plain enough: biology has spent a century learning to look at cells, and is still short of ways to hold them.
