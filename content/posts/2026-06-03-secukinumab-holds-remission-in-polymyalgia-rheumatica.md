Polymyalgia rheumatica is treated with glucocorticoids and has been since the 1950s. Patients start at a moderate dose, taper over a year or two, and a large fraction relapse on the way down, which leaves them on steroids long enough to acquire the bone, glucose and blood pressure problems that steroids cause. The disease affects people over fifty, women more often than men, and its symptoms of shoulder and hip girdle pain and morning stiffness are common enough that diagnosis is often delayed.

Secukinumab blocks interleukin-17A, a cytokine that sits in the signalling pathway of several inflammatory diseases including psoriasis and ankylosing spondylitis. The rationale in polymyalgia is that the disease behaves like a steroid-responsive inflammatory condition with an IL-17 component, and the trial tested whether the antibody could replace part of the steroid exposure.

In the phase 3 trial reported in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 3 June, sustained remission at 52 weeks was observed in 41.2 per cent of patients receiving secukinumab, with a confidence interval that the report gives in truncated form. The endpoint was defined as remission maintained without rescue therapy, which is the clinically meaningful version rather than a point measurement. Reporting a truncated interval is unusual, and the reader should treat the point estimate as the finding while waiting for the full publication.

Comparison was against placebo on a glucocorticoid taper, which is the design that asks whether the biologic allows the steroid to come down faster without relapse. That question matters more than a comparison against another biologic, because the harm being avoided is the steroid itself. A taper schedule that is protocol-driven rather than left to the clinician is what makes the comparison interpretable, since steroid decisions are otherwise a source of noise. Fifty-two weeks is also the horizon at which most relapses in a steroid taper occur, which is why the endpoint was chosen.

Safety of IL-17 blockade is known from other indications, with candida infections and inflammatory bowel disease as the two signals that regulators watch. In an older population, which polymyalgia largely is, infection risk carries more weight than it would in a trial of young adults. Candida infections in this population are usually mucosal and treatable, and the bowel signal is more serious because it can unmask colitis in patients who already have gastrointestinal symptoms.

A genuine option now exists for a disease that has had none, and it is not a cure. Forty per cent in sustained remission means that most patients still need steroids, and the trial does not establish whether the responders keep their remission after the drug stops. The next trial has to answer that question, because a biologic that has to continue indefinitely trades one chronic treatment for another. Steroid-sparing also changes the economics, because the cost of long-term prednisolone is measured in fractures and diabetes rather than in pharmacy bills.
