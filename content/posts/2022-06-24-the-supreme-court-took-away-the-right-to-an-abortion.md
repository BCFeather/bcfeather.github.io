The Supreme Court overruled Roe v Wade on the morning of 24 June, in a decision written by Justice Samuel Alito and joined by five other members of the court. The case, Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization, concerned a Mississippi law that banned abortion after fifteen weeks, and the majority held that the Constitution contains no right to the procedure. Within hours, clinics in states with laws designed for this moment stopped performing abortions.

Roe had stood since 1973, when a court led by Harry Blackmun found a right to privacy that covered the decision to end a pregnancy, and it was reaffirmed in 1992 in a case from Pennsylvania. The new opinion argued that the earlier rulings had invented a right that appears nowhere in the text of the Constitution, and that the question belonged to the states and to their voters. It compared the reasoning of 1973 unfavourably with the court's treatment of other unenumerated rights.

Dissent came from the three liberal justices, who wrote that the majority had removed a right that women had relied on for two generations and that the consequences would fall hardest on those with least money to travel. They read parts of their opinion aloud from the bench, which is a sign of deep disagreement, and they described the decision as one that would damage the court's standing.

Mechanics of the change depend on state law. Thirteen states had passed laws before the ruling that were designed to take effect automatically, and several more had older statutes still on the books that had been unenforceable while Roe applied. Courts in some of those states blocked enforcement within days, and the map that emerged had large regions where the nearest clinic was hundreds of miles away.

Support for abortion rights in polls had been stable for decades at about sixty per cent, with most respondents favouring legal access in at least some circumstances. The leak of the draft opinion on 2 May had produced protests outside the court and a fence around the building that stayed up for weeks, and the final decision matched the draft almost exactly, which intensified questions about how the court's deliberations had been kept.

Other consequences extend beyond abortion. Justice Thomas wrote separately to say that the reasoning of the decision should be applied to cases protecting contraception and same-sex marriage, and although no other justice endorsed that view, the statement became the basis for arguments about what the court might do next. Companies began to announce policies on travel for employees, and states on both coasts prepared for an increase in patients arriving from elsewhere. What the ruling does is return a question to politics, where it had been removed by a court fifty years earlier. The result will be decided state by state, election by election, and the country now has two regimes rather than one, which is what the majority said the Constitution required and what the dissent said would be a catastrophe for the women who live in the wrong one.
