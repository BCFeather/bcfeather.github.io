Insurance is priced on the expected loss across many events, and a single event can invalidate the expectation for a decade. A hailstorm that crossed a metropolitan area in the spring produced 40,000 claims and a loss that two regional insurers could not absorb, and both have withdrawn from the homeowners' line.

The loss's structure explains the severity. Hail damaged roofs, gutters and vehicles in a 40-kilometre band, and the claims clustered in the subdivisions built between 1995 and 2010, whose shingles were rated for a smaller stone. Reinsurance absorbed the first layer and repriced the rest. The insurers' cat programs covered the event, and the renewal that followed raised the cost of the layer above by 60 per cent, which is the number that made the line unprofitable.

Model risk is the underlying problem. The catastrophe model's estimate for the region was a 1-in-50-year loss of 400 million dollars, and the actual event was 1.2 billion. That is a factor of three, and a model that errs by three can be paid for only by capital rather than by premium.

Underwriting changed in response. Two insurers now require a roof inspection above a certain age, and three have introduced a separate hail deductible that is 2 per cent of the dwelling's value. A homeowner with a 20-year-old roof in the affected band faces a 40 per cent premium rise. Building codes are the mitigation that works. A class 4 shingle costs 25 per cent more and resists a 5-centimetre stone, and one municipality has amended its code to require it for new construction. The amendment applies to new roofs and not to existing ones. Flood insurance supplies the policy lesson. Flood is a peril with a public program, and hail is a peril the private market prices. When a private line withdraws, the homeowners have no public backstop, and the state's regulator has opened a proceeding on whether one is needed.

A reinsurance treaty is the mechanism that turns a local storm into a market exit. An insurer cedes a share of its catastrophe risk to a reinsurer and keeps a retention, and a storm that exhausts the retention in one event leaves the insurer paying from its own capital.

Price is the signal that follows. A market that loses two insurers has 30 per cent less capacity, and the premium for a roof policy rises until the remaining capital earns its cost. The rise is what makes the exit visible to a homeowner. A claims surge is also a labor problem. An insurer that handles 200 claims a week receives 40,000 in three days, and its adjusters' capacity is the reason a settlement takes a year. A regulator's response is usually a deadline rather than a capacity.

Diversified risk and correlated risk are different objects, and hail is the second. A thousand small fires are insurable; one hailstorm over one city is a single bet, and the market's response to a single bet is to leave.
