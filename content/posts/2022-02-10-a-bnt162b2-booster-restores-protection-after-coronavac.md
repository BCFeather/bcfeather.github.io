Countries that used an inactivated vaccine faced a question in late 2021 that the original trials could not answer: how fast protection wanes, and whether a different vaccine can restore it. Brazil had administered CoronaVac widely and had linked databases covering tests, vaccinations and outcomes. The answer mattered for vaccine policy in every country that had chosen an inactivated product for its first campaign.

A test-negative design used those links to estimate effectiveness over time in nearly 14 million people and about 16 million tests. The comparison was between people who tested positive and people who tested negative, with vaccination status and prior infection adjusted in the model. The study appeared in *Nature Medicine*, 9 February 2022. The design compares people who tested positive with people who tested negative on the same day, which controls for the changing epidemic curve.

At 14 to 30 days after the second dose, CoronaVac was 55.0 per cent effective against confirmed infection, with an interval from 54.3 to 55.7, and 82.1 per cent effective against hospitalisation or death, with an interval from 81.4 to 82.8. Beyond 180 days the figures fell to 34.7 per cent against infection and 72.5 per cent against severe outcomes. The decline is steepest in the oldest group, whose immune response to the primary series was weakest to begin with.

A BNT162b2 booster given six months after the second CoronaVac dose raised effectiveness against infection to 92.7 per cent and against severe outcomes to 97.3 per cent, measured 14 to 30 days later. People aged 80 or older had lower protection after two doses and protection similar to younger groups after the booster. The oldest group is also the group in which a third dose was first recommended, and this study supplies the numerical justification for that decision.

The heterologous schedule is the practical result. A country that began with an inactivated vaccine can finish with an mRNA one, and the immune system responds to the combination at least as well as to a third dose of the same product. Immunogenicity studies had suggested that heterologous schedules produce higher titres, and this effectiveness estimate is the clinical confirmation of that suggestion.

Test-negative designs have a known weakness, which is that people who seek testing differ from those who do not, and the adjustment cannot correct for differences in behaviour that are unmeasured. The Brazilian database is also large enough that small residual biases become statistically visible.

Waning against infection is faster than waning against severe disease, and the gap between 34.7 and 72.5 per cent at six months is the measure of that. Antibody titres fall quickly while memory B and T cells persist, so the vaccine continues to protect the lungs and the bloodstream after it stops preventing the nose from being infected. The practical implication is that protection against severe disease is the endpoint to track when deciding how often to boost.
