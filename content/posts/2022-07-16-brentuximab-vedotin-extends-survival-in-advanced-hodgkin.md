A regimen that cures most young patients is hard to improve, because the ceiling is close and the follow-up has to be long enough for the difference in survival to emerge. A trial that ran for six years is the trial that could see it. A ceiling that is already close to 90 per cent is the hardest place to demonstrate an improvement, because the events are few and the follow-up has to be long.

The ECHELON-1 trial randomized 664 patients to A+AVD, which replaces bleomycin with brentuximab vedotin, and 670 to ABVD, for up to six cycles in stage III or IV Hodgkin lymphoma. The paper appeared in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, 13 July 2022. An antibody-drug conjugate that delivers a microtubule poison to cells expressing CD30 is a targeted version of a cytotoxic drug, and the substitution is the design idea of the regimen.

At a median follow-up of 73 months, 39 patients in the A+AVD group and 64 in the ABVD group had died, a hazard ratio of 0.59 with an interval from 0.40 to 0.88. The six-year overall survival estimates were 93.9 per cent and 89.4 per cent. A hazard ratio of 0.59 is a relative reduction of 41 per cent, and the same data expressed as 4.5 percentage points at six years sounds much smaller.

Progression-free survival also favoured A+AVD, with a hazard ratio of 0.68 for progression or death, and fewer patients in that group received subsequent therapy including transplantation. Second cancers were reported in 23 patients on A+AVD and 32 on ABVD. A secondary endpoint that also favours the experimental arm is the corroboration that a survival difference is not a fluke of one analysis.

The absolute difference of 4.5 percentage points at six years is the number that a patient weighs, and the relative reduction of 41 per cent is the number that a statistician reports. Both are correct and they describe different things. A neuropathy that resolves is a toxicity that a patient will accept for a survival gain, and a neuropathy that persists is one that they may not.

Peripheral neuropathy was more common with A+AVD, and most patients in both groups had resolution or improvement by the last follow-up. Febrile neutropenia led to a recommendation for granulocyte colony-stimulating factor prophylaxis, which is a cost that the regimen carries. A regimen that replaces a drug with a targeted agent is the pattern of a decade of oncology practice, and the trial is the demonstration in a disease where the older regimen already worked.
