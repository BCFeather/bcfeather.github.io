Ethylene and ethane are separated in enormous quantities and the separation is one of the most energy-intensive steps in the chemical industry. The two molecules differ by a single double bond and by two hydrogens, their boiling points sit four degrees apart, and the standard way to separate them is distillation at a temperature low enough that the difference in boiling point can be exploited. A membrane that works at ambient temperature would replace a column the height of a building with a sheet of polymer, and the chemistry that makes it possible is a reversible bond.

The carrier is a silver ion. Ethylene forms a weak complex with silver through its pi electrons, and ethane does not, because a saturated molecule has no orbital to donate. The complex is reversible at the operating temperature, so the alkene is absorbed on one side of the membrane, carried across, and released on the other. The ethane is excluded at the first step, and the purity of the product comes from the selectivity of that bond and not from the size of the pore.

A carrier has to be immobilised without being destroyed. A silver salt dissolved in water would be washed out of the membrane, and a silver salt in a dry polymer is a salt that does not dissolve. The solution that has been developed puts the ion in an ionic liquid that is held inside a porous support, and the liquid keeps the ion mobile enough to bind the alkene while the support keeps it in place. The membrane is therefore a liquid in a sponge rather than a solid sheet.

The driving force is a pressure difference, and the flux depends on how fast the complex forms and breaks. A carrier that binds too strongly is a carrier that holds the alkene and does not release it, and the rate of release sets the flux the membrane can deliver. The temperature is the compromise: higher temperature speeds the release and also speeds the loss of the ionic liquid, and the operating window is a few tens of degrees wide.

Stability is the practical question. Trace contaminants in a real feed, such as acetylene or sulfur compounds, bind more strongly than ethylene and occupy the carriers, and a membrane that is tested on a clean mixture can fail on a plant stream within days. Water is the second problem, since the ionic liquid absorbs it and the silver complex changes its behaviour in the presence of water. A membrane that has been run for a thousand hours on a real feed is the evidence a plant would need, and that evidence is the part of the literature that is thinnest.

What the work shows is that a chemical difference of one bond is enough to sort two molecules that a distillation column struggles with. The membrane is not going to replace every column, since a feed that is mostly ethylene is cheaper to purify by distillation, and the case is the one where the alkene is a small fraction of the stream. A plant that separates a dilute stream is the plant that would pay for a membrane, and the economics decide whether the carriers are stable enough to be worth installing.
