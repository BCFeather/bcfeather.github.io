title: "Insects borrowed bacterial weapons for their own immunity"

description: "Insect genomes carry toxin genes acquired from bacteria, and the proteins they encode defend the insect against parasites, according to an analysis in *PNAS* on 18 April 2023 of how horizontal gene transfer builds immunity."
---

Horizontal gene transfer is usually discussed as a bacterial affair. A gene that jumps between species can also jump from a bacterium into an animal, and when the gene encodes a toxin the animal may find a use for it.

The analysis in *PNAS*, 18 April 2023 documents that use. The authors trace pore-forming toxin genes in insect genomes, show that the genes arrived from bacteria, and demonstrate that the encoded proteins act in the insect's defence against parasites.

The mechanism is a weapon turned inward. A pore-forming toxin inserts into a membrane and makes it leak, and a toxin that a bacterium uses against a host can be used by an insect against a parasite that enters its body.

The evidence is functional. The authors express the insect's toxin and show that it damages the parasite, and the loss-of-function experiments show that the insect's defence weakens without it.

The evolutionary story is the interesting part. A gene that arrives by transfer is a gene that has already been optimized by another lineage, and the insect inherits a solution rather than evolving one.

The scope is wider than one species. The analysis finds the genes across insect orders, which suggests that the acquisition happened more than once and that the function is general.

The immune context is the insect's lack of adaptive immunity. An insect defends with innate mechanisms, and a repertoire that includes borrowed toxins is an innate system with an expanded arsenal.

The risk is the toxin's specificity. A pore-forming protein that attacks a parasite's membrane may also attack the insect's own cells, and the paper's expression pattern explains why it does not.

What the paper does not establish is the ecological benefit in the field. A laboratory challenge is not a natural infection, and the fitness cost of carrying the genes is a separate question.

For evolutionary immunology, the value is a mechanism of innovation. Immunity is usually modelled as a host's own invention, and horizontal transfer makes it a market in which solutions are traded.

The genomic signature is the evidence of origin. Bacterial genes carry a composition that differs from the host's, and the paper's phylogenetic analysis places the insect copies inside bacterial clades.

Gene loss is the counterpoint. Some insects have lost the acquired genes, which suggests that the benefit depends on the parasite's pressure and that the genes are dispensable in its absence.
The comparison with plant immunity is instructive. Plants also deploy pore-forming proteins against pathogens, and the two kingdoms appear to have solved the same problem with the same class of molecule acquired from the same source. The convergence is a hint that the solution is general.
The expression pattern is the safety mechanism. The toxin is produced in the gut or in the fat body and released where a parasite is likely to be, and a protein that stays in those compartments does not reach the insect's own membranes in a damaging concentration.
