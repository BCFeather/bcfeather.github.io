---



Horizontal gene transfer is usually discussed as a bacterial affair. A gene that jumps between species can also jump from a bacterium into an animal, and when the gene encodes a toxin the animal may find a use for it, which turns a mechanism of bacterial competition into a source of animal immunity.

In *PNAS* on 18 April 2023, that use is documented. The authors trace pore-forming toxin genes in insect genomes, show that the genes arrived from bacteria, and demonstrate that the encoded proteins act in the insect's defence against parasites.

Originating in bacterial clades, the insect copies sit inside their phylogenetic trees, and a composition that differs from the host's marks them as foreign, which is how provenance is established. The mechanism inverts a weapon: a pore-forming toxin inserts into a membrane and makes it leak, so a toxin that a bacterium uses against a host becomes one an insect uses against a parasite entering its body.

Function is what the experiments establish, since expressing the insect's toxin damages the parasite and loss-of-function experiments weaken the defence without it. Economics explains why the arrangement is safe: the toxin is produced in the gut or in the fat body and released where a parasite is likely to be, and a protein confined to those compartments does not reach the insect's own membranes at a damaging concentration, so the expression pattern answers the objection that a pore-former would attack its owner.

Because a gene that arrives by transfer has already been optimized by another lineage, the insect inherits a solution rather than evolving one, which is the interesting part of the story. The scope is wider than one species, since the genes appear across insect orders, and that distribution suggests the acquisition happened more than once and that the function is general. Plants deploy pore-forming proteins against pathogens as well, and two kingdoms solving the same problem with the same class of molecule acquired from the same source is a hint that the solution is general.

Insects lack adaptive immunity, so they defend with innate mechanisms, and a repertoire that includes borrowed toxins is an innate system with an expanded arsenal. Gene loss is the counterpoint, because some insects have lost the acquired genes, which suggests that the benefit depends on parasite pressure and that the genes are dispensable in its absence. Ecology is what remains untested: a laboratory challenge is not a natural infection, and the fitness cost of carrying the genes is a separate question. Immunity is usually modelled as a host's own invention, and horizontal transfer makes it a market in which solutions are traded, which is the shift in view on offer.

What the transfer cannot explain on its own is why the same class of gene appears in some insect orders and not others, since opportunity and need are different conditions and only one of them leaves a trace in a genome.
