Ovarian cancer is the solid tumour where immunotherapy has most consistently failed, for a reason that is now well described: the tumours carry few mutations, so there are few foreign peptides for a T cell to recognise. A phase 1 trial reported in *Nature Medicine* on 14 October tests the obvious response, which is to manufacture the target rather than wait for it. Ovarian cancer is also the tumour where trials of checkpoint blockade alone have failed most consistently, which makes it the right place to test a combination.

Personalized neoantigen vaccines work from the tumour's own sequence. Each patient's tumour is sequenced, the mutations that could produce a peptide displayed on the surface are predicted, and a vaccine is built against the shortlist. The patient then receives a product designed for one person, which makes the logistics of the trial as much a part of the result as the immunology. Manufacturing a vaccine per patient takes weeks, and a trial that cannot deliver the product on schedule measures nothing.

Patients in the trial had high-grade serous ovarian cancer, the commonest and most aggressive subtype, and they received the vaccine with PD-1 blockade. Feasibility was the first question, and the answer was yes: the pipeline produced a vaccine for each participant, and the combination could be given. Tumor-specific immune responses appeared in many of the patients, which is the intermediate endpoint that a phase 1 trial can measure.

Resistance had a signature. Cell-free DNA analysis pointed to defects in antigen presentation in tumours that did not respond, which is the escape route a vaccine should be expected to provoke: a T cell that cannot see the peptide cannot kill the cell. That finding is more useful than a response rate, because it names the mechanism a combination trial would have to address next.

The setting matters for how the result should be read. A vaccine given to patients with recurrent disease faces a tumour that has already been selected for immune escape, and a vaccine given earlier, after surgery and before recurrence, faces a smaller and less evolved target. What the paper does not show is survival. Phase 1 trials in this field report safety, feasibility and immune response, and the history of the field is full of immune responses that did not become remissions. The next step is a randomised trial in a less advanced population, with antigen presentation status measured as a stratification variable rather than discovered afterwards. Until that trial runs, the result belongs in the file of immune responses that have not yet become survival. Cost is the other obstacle: a product made for one patient has no unit price until it is manufactured, and the price will be set before the benefit is known.
