Persistent pain frequently produces anxiety, and the two are usually treated as a pair of consequences rather than as two processes with separate circuits. A paper in *PNAS* on 20 November 2024 separates them in a mouse model of trigeminal neuralgia. A pair of phenotypes in one model is a design choice that trades specificity of the model for comparability of the circuits.

The model reproduces the facial pain of the clinical condition, and the animals develop anxiety-like behaviour alongside it. Two phenotypes in one preparation are what allow the circuits behind them to be compared. A single preparation carrying both phenotypes is what makes the comparison possible, since two models would confound the circuit with the model. Optogenetic manipulation is fast enough to separate a state from a trait, and the separation matters for a disorder that lasts months.

GABAergic neurons of the lateral habenula lose activity in the model. The habenula is a region implicated in both pain and negative affect, so a loss of inhibitory tone there is a plausible common origin. Loss of inhibitory tone in a region associated with both pain and affect is a common origin that the paper proposes rather than proves, and the proposal is testable in the same preparation. A manipulation that relieves one output and leaves the other is the strongest evidence that the outputs have separate controllers.

Optogenetic and chemogenetic tools were used to raise and lower the activity of those neurons. The two directions produced different effects on pain behaviour and on anxiety behaviour, which is the result the design was built to detect. Opposite manipulations are the appropriate control, because a manipulation that relieves everything demonstrates nothing about specificity. Differential control is the claim a clinician would want, since treating pain and treating anxiety are different prescriptions in practice.

Differential modulation is the finding. One manipulation relieved pain without altering anxiety, and another altered anxiety without relieving pain, so the two outputs are not a single readout of one state. Specificity is the contribution, since a treatment that relieved both would leave the clinical problem of separating them unsolved. Translation is the weakest link in any mouse model of a facial pain syndrome, and the weakness is stated rather than assumed away.

The habenula therefore contains a population that can be steered towards one outcome or the other, and the steering is what a clinical strategy would need if the two are to be treated separately. A population that can be steered is a target for a therapy that has to choose, and choosing is what a clinician does when a drug treats pain and not mood. A circuit target is a target for a intervention that has to choose, and choosing is what a clinician already does by prescribing two drugs.

On the modelling side, the translation is uncertain, since a mouse model of trigeminal neuralgia is not the human condition, and the circuits that differ between species are not known in advance. Species differences in habenular circuitry are the obstacle to translation, and the obstacle is the reason the result is a mechanism rather than a treatment.
