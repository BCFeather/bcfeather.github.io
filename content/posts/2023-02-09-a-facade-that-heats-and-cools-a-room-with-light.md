Buildings spend more energy on keeping rooms at a comfortable temperature than on almost anything else, and most of that energy goes into fighting the sun. A window that admits light and rejects heat is a partial answer, and a static one.

In *PNAS* on 7 February 2023, the answer is made movable. Raphael Kay, J. Alstan Jakubiec, Charlie Katrycz, Benjamin Hatton and colleagues build a facade with several fluid layers, and the layers are reconfigured to change what the facade transmits, reflects and emits.

Optofluidics supplies the mechanism. A thin layer of fluid between transparent sheets has an optical property set by its composition, and pumping another fluid through the layer changes that property, so several layers, each independently addressable, give a set of optical states rather than a single one. The states are chosen for climate control: in summer a facade that reflects near-infrared light and emits in the atmospheric window rejects heat, in winter one that absorbs and re-emits inward keeps it, and the same hardware does both, which a static coating cannot.

Because a facade decides how much solar gain a room receives and solar gain is the largest term in a cooling load, the building's energy balance is the target, and a surface that tracks weather and occupancy can answer a grid signal as well as a thermostat. The case is made by simulation, with a room behind the facade compared against the same room glazed conventionally, so the saving is a function of climate and of control strategy rather than a single number.

Materials set the first limit, since the fluids must stay stable for years, stay non-toxic and remain compatible with the pumps and seals a building can service, and a laboratory demonstration with water and dye is a long way from a facade that a contractor will install. Control sets the second, because each layer has a switching time set by flow rate, and a facade that takes an hour to change state cannot follow a cloud; the trade-off runs between speed and pumping power.

Market comparison is with electrochromic glass, a mature product whose optical range is narrower, and whether the extra range justifies the extra complexity is a question for pilots rather than for papers. Durability is untested, because ultraviolet light, freeze-thaw cycles and pollution act on the fluids and the seals, and the lifetime claims come from accelerated testing. Maintenance is the hidden cost as well, since pumps, valves and reservoirs add components that an operator must service, and a facade that fails becomes a tinted window with a bill attached. What architects gain is a surface to design rather than specify, and the thermal performance of the building becomes a function of that surface rather than of glazing ratio alone.

Scale-up is the last unknown, since a facade of a square metre and a facade of a building differ in pumping distance, in the volume of fluid they hold and in the shading they cast.
