Buildings spend more energy on keeping rooms at a comfortable temperature than on almost anything else, and most of that energy is spent fighting the sun. A window that admits light and rejects heat is a partial answer, and it is a static one.

The device in *PNAS*, 7 February 2023 is not static. Raphael Kay, J. Alstan Jakubiec, Charlie Katrycz, Benjamin Hatton and colleagues build a facade with multiple fluid layers, and the layers can be reconfigured to change what the facade transmits, reflects and emits.

The mechanism is optofluidics. A thin layer of fluid between transparent sheets has an optical property set by its composition, and pumping a different fluid through the layer changes that property. Several layers, each independently addressable, give a set of optical states rather than a single one.

The states are chosen for climate control. In summer, a facade that reflects near-infrared light and emits in the atmospheric window rejects heat; in winter, a facade that absorbs and re-emits inward keeps it. The same hardware can do both, which is what a static coating cannot.

The building's energy balance is the target. A facade decides how much solar gain a room receives, and solar gain is the largest term in a cooling load. A reconfigurable facade can track the weather and the occupancy, and it can respond to a grid signal as well as to a thermostat.

The materials are the practical constraint. The fluids have to be stable over years, non-toxic, and compatible with the pumps and seals that a building can service. A laboratory demonstration with water and dye is a long way from a facade that a contractor will install.

The control problem is the second constraint. Each layer has a switching time, and a facade that takes an hour to change state cannot follow a cloud. The paper's design has switching times that are set by the fluid's flow rate, and the trade-off is between speed and pumping power.

The comparison with electrochromic glass is the market question. A tinted window is a mature product, and its optical range is narrower than a fluidic facade's. Whether the extra range justifies the extra complexity is a question for pilots rather than for papers.

What the study does not provide is a durability test. A facade is exposed to ultraviolet light, freeze-thaw cycles and pollution, and the fluids and the seals degrade. The paper's lifetime claims are estimates from accelerated testing.

For architects, the appeal is a surface that can be designed rather than specified. A facade that changes with the season is a facade that a designer can use as a variable, and the building's thermal performance becomes a function of the surface rather than of the glazing ratio alone.

The building simulation is where the paper's case is made. The authors model a room with the facade and compare its annual energy use with the same room glazed conventionally, and the saving is a function of the climate and of the control strategy rather than a single number.
Maintenance is the hidden cost. Pumps, valves and fluid reservoirs add components that a building's operator has to service, and a facade that fails is a facade that becomes a tinted window with a maintenance bill. The design's simplicity will decide whether it is adopted.
