Republican People's Party won Turkey's municipal elections on the thirty-first of March, taking about 37.7 per cent of the national vote against 35.5 per cent for the Justice and Development Party of President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, which is the first time since 2002 that the governing party has finished behind an opposition in a national ballot. Ekrem İmamoğlu was re-elected mayor of Istanbul with 51.1 per cent against 39.6 for his rival, and Mansur Yavaş took Ankara with 60.4 per cent.

Result was also a reversal of the previous local elections. In 2019 the Republican People's Party won Istanbul, Ankara and Izmir in what was then described as a protest vote, and the government recovered some ground in the years that followed. This time the party added 14 metropolitan municipalities and took about 35 per cent of the vote in the smaller cities, which is the part of the country where the governing party had been strong.

Inflation is the issue that dominated the campaign. Annual consumer price inflation was about 68 per cent at the time of the vote, and the government's response, which combined sharp increases in the minimum wage with an unorthodox monetary policy, had left households poorer in real terms. Pensions had been raised by less than inflation, and pensioners were among the most visible participants in opposition rallies. Government argued that the economy was growing and that the opposition had no plan to manage it.

Vote was also about the future of the presidency. İmamoğlu is the most likely challenger to Erdoğan in 2028, and his victory in Istanbul gave him a platform on which to build. He had been convicted in 2022 of insulting officials and sentenced to two years and seven months, which would have barred him from politics, and the case was on appeal. Government's control of the courts is the reason that analysts described his position as precarious, and the sentence was upheld in a revised form later in the year.

Consequences inside the governing party were visible within a week. Erdoğan appeared on the balcony of the party headquarters and said the result was a message from the voters that the party would hear, and he promised a period of self-criticism rather than a change of course. Several mayors from the party lost their posts and the party's coalition partner, the Nationalist Movement Party, did worse than in 2019.

Distribution of the largest cities was the clearest measure of the shift. The Republican People's Party took fourteen metropolitan municipalities, the governing party took twelve, and the pro-Kurdish party took three. In Izmir the opposition held the city with a comfortable margin, and in Bursa it took a city that the governing party had held for two decades. In the south-east, where the pro-Kurdish party fielded candidates, the result reversed several mayoralties that had been placed under state trustees after the 2019 vote.

What the thirty-first of March means is that Turkey's opposition can win an election on turnout and on the economy. Turnout was about 85 per cent, which is high by any standard, and the pattern suggests that voters who had supported the government in national elections were willing to punish it locally. Whether that transferable dissatisfaction extends to a presidential race is the question the result posed, and the answer depends on whether the economy improves and on whether the courts allow İmamoğlu to run.
