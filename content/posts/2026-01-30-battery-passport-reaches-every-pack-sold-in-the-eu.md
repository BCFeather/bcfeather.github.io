A traction battery is the most material-intensive component of an electric vehicle, and until now its history has lived in supplier spreadsheets. From this month, every battery above 2 kilowatt-hours sold in the European Union must carry a digital passport: an identifier, readable by a scanner, linked to its chemistry, its carbon footprint, its recycled content and its repair history.

The legal basis is a regulation adopted in 2023 with a staged timetable, and the passport is the first stage that touches every pack. The carbon footprint declaration came earlier; the recycled-content minimum, 16 per cent cobalt, applies from 2031.

The format is a data model rather than a document. The passport is a set of linked elements held by the manufacturer and exposed through a resolver, so a scanner reads an identifier and fetches the record. That keeps data with the party that owns it and makes the passport a pointer rather than a copy.

Interoperability is where standards bodies spent their time. The identifier scheme, the access protocol and the field definitions are specified across three standards, and a pack from a manufacturer that implements them differently still resolves. Conformance testing is voluntary, and the market is relying on it.

Enforcement runs through market access. A pack without a passport cannot be placed on the market, and customs can check the identifier at the border. Penalties are set by member states, so the deterrent varies by country.

Data quality is the weakness that will show first. The carbon footprint figure depends on the electricity mix at the factory, and a supplier reporting a grid average rather than a contract produces a number that is compliant and wrong. Auditors have a right to the underlying data, and audit capacity is thin.

Repair and second life are the purposes that pay. A battery that has been through a workshop has a record of the work, and a pack removed from a vehicle carries its state of health to a stationary storage buyer. That is the part of the regulation with a commercial return.

The identifier scheme has a second use that the regulation did not anticipate. A pack's identifier is being adopted by recyclers as the record that tells them the chemistry before disassembly, and two European recyclers have begun requiring it in their intake contracts. A regulation written for market access is becoming an operational standard.

Small manufacturers are the compliance risk. A pack assembler that buys cells and builds modules has to declare the carbon footprint of a cell it did not make, and the data must come from a supplier that may not have it. The regulation allows an estimate with a stated method, which is a loophole that will be used and audited.

For a reader, the notable feature is that the passport is a data-infrastructure obligation attached to a physical product. Governments have long regulated what a product contains; requiring a machine-readable history forces the supply chain to standardize its records, and that standardization is the durable effect.
