Two hundred and sixty-four Ukrainian defenders left the Azovstal steelworks in Mariupol in the early hours of 16 May, the first group of several thousand who had held the plant for eighty-two days. Fifty-three of them were severely wounded and were taken to a hospital in Novoazovsk, a town under separatist control, and the other two hundred and eleven went to Olenivka, where a former penal colony had been prepared to hold prisoners. Russian state media described the movement as a surrender, and Ukraine's deputy prime minister, Iryna Vereshchuk, described it as an evacuation operation and said the wounded would be exchanged.

Mariupol had been surrounded since the first week of March. A city of more than four hundred thousand people before the war was reduced street by street, its theatre, its maternity hospital and its water system hit, and by April the survivors were living in basements without heat, water or medicine, burying the dead in courtyards because the cemeteries were mined. The Red Cross had tried for weeks to reach the plant and failed, and the civilians who had sheltered in its bunkers left in small convoys at the start of May, after which only fighters remained.

The plant itself is a Soviet-era complex of forty square kilometres, with blast furnaces, rail yards and a network of tunnels and bunkers built to survive a nuclear strike, which is why it became the last redoubt. The defenders were a mixture of the Azov Regiment, marines of the 36th brigade, border guards and police, perhaps two and a half thousand in all by the time the order came, sharing ammunition, treating wounds with improvised dressings and broadcasting messages from a commander who appeared in a bunker in a series of videos.

The order to stop fighting was issued by the general staff in Kyiv, and it was given in language that avoided the word surrender. The commanders were told to preserve the lives of their men, and the men were told that the state would bring them home. Whether that would happen depended on an exchange that Russia had no reason to hurry, and by the following winter about two thousand of them were still in custody, a fact that Ukrainian officials cited every time they were asked about the plant.

Militarily, the fall of Mariupol finished the land bridge. Russia had held a corridor from the Donbas to Crimea since March, and the last Ukrainian position inside it had now been removed, which freed units for the fighting around Sievierodonetsk and gave Moscow the first city it could present as a prize. Politically it also gave Moscow a trial it intended to stage, with plans reported for a tribunal in the occupied city and charges that Ukrainian officials called illegal.

Eighty-two days of resistance had produced no relief for the city and no breakthrough elsewhere, and the plant fell when the ammunition ran out rather than when the politics changed. The defenders left in buses under Russian escort, their wounds treated by the side that had been shelling them, and the last footage from the bunkers showed men carrying the wounded down stairs in the dark.
