European Central Bank left its deposit rate at 4 per cent on 11 April and its president said the council would have enough information in June to decide on a cut. German two-year yields fell and the euro weakened within minutes of the statement. The statement dropped a reference to the risk of higher inflation and kept a reference to the risk of weaker growth, which is the order in which the council now ranks its worries.

Inflation path is the reason. Headline inflation had fallen to 2.4 per cent and the forecast for next year sat close to the target, while wages were still rising at around five per cent and services prices were sticky. A wage number that falls slowly is the last obstacle between the council and its first cut. The forecasts published with the decision showed inflation at the target by the end of next year, and the staff projections are the anchor the council uses to justify a change.

Divergence from America is the constraint. The Federal Reserve is waiting because its economy is growing faster and its labour market is tighter, and a cut in Europe before a cut in America would widen the rate gap and weaken the euro further. A currency that falls is an import price that rises, and the council knows it. The euro's fall has been modest because the market expects the American bank to cut as well, and the gap in expectations rather than the gap in levels is what the exchange rate tracks. The council's chief economist has said that the wage data for the first quarter arrive before the June meeting, which is why that date rather than an earlier one has become the consensus.

Banks are the transmission channel. Lending surveys show demand for credit falling and standards tightening, which means policy is already working through the financial system in places where the headline rate has not moved. A loan that is never made is demand that never appears in the statistics. The survey published a week earlier showed that small firms were paying more for credit than at any point in the previous decade, which is the transmission the council wanted.

Politics is the backdrop the council keeps denying. Growth near zero and a manufacturing sector in recession have made easing popular with governments, and the council has insisted its decisions are not political. Independence is tested when the argument is about jobs rather than about prices. The council's members have been giving interviews that describe the June meeting as live, which is a technique for preparing the market without committing the institution.

What the meeting shows is that a central bank can prepare a cut months in advance and still keep the decision formally open, and the instrument it uses for that is language rather than action. A first cut that the market has already priced is a cut that changes expectations rather than conditions, and the second cut is the one that will matter.
